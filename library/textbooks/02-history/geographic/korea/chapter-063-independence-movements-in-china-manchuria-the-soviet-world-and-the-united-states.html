Independence Movements in China, Manchuria, the Soviet World, and the United States

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On 15 August 1945, as news of Japan's surrender spread across Asia, Korean independence activists in Chongqing, Yan'an, Xi'an, Khabarovsk, Honolulu, and Washington each learned that the empire they had opposed for decades was ending. Each group had its own organization, its own sponsors, and its own claim to speak for Korea. None had been able to enter the peninsula as a government. This chapter asks how that situation came about.

After the annexation of 1910 and the repression that followed the March First Movement of 1919, open political organizing inside colonial Korea was tightly restricted. Newspapers were censored, associations were licensed or suppressed, and the colonial police and courts could punish political dissent severely. Yet the desire for independence did not disappear. It moved outward along the routes that Koreans had already opened through earlier migration: across the Amnok and Duman rivers into Manchuria, along the coast into the Russian Far East, across the Yellow Sea to Shanghai and the Chinese interior, and across the Pacific to Hawaii and the American mainland. Independence, in other words, was sustained not by one organization but by a dispersed set of communities living under very different legal and political conditions.

Those conditions shaped what each movement could do. In Manchuria, a large rural Korean population lived in a borderland where Chinese authority was weak and Japanese power was growing, and armed bands could form, recruit, and sometimes fight. In Shanghai, a small body of exiles sheltered in the French Concession and attempted to build a republican government. In the Russian Far East and later Soviet territory, Korean revolutionaries encountered a state that could supply weapons and training but that also pursued its own priorities, and that in 1937 deported the region's entire Korean population. In Hawaii and California, Korean laborers and students built churches, schools, newspapers, and fundraising associations in a society where they could organize openly but had little political leverage. Each environment enabled some forms of activism and ruled out others.

Shared opposition to Japanese rule did not produce agreement about almost anything else. Korean activists disagreed about whether independence would come through diplomacy, education, armed struggle, assassination, or Allied victory; about whether the future state should be a liberal republic, a socialist society, or something between; about who had the right to lead; and about which foreign power, if any, could be trusted. These disagreements produced rival governments, rival armies, and in some episodes violent conflict among Koreans. They also produced real achievements: sustained financial support from emigrant workers, military engagements in Manchuria, a government that survived for more than a quarter century, and a small Korean force that served within the Allied war effort in China.

Historians reconstruct these movements from a diverse and uneven body of evidence: provisional-government documents and newspapers published in exile, memoirs written by participants after the fact, Japanese police and military intelligence reports, Chinese Nationalist and Communist records, Soviet and Comintern archives opened after 1991, American diplomatic correspondence, and oral histories gathered from emigrant communities. Each of these sources has characteristic strengths and distortions. Japanese police files recorded activists closely but were written to suppress them. Exile memoirs preserve motives and experiences but are often shaped by later political loyalties, including, in the divided peninsula after 1945, by competing needs to legitimate one state or another. This chapter therefore treats evidence critically, and several features ask students to compare accounts of the same event.

The chapter follows five divisions. The first examines the creation and difficulties of the Provisional Government in Shanghai and the politics of direct action. The second turns to Manchuria and the armed struggle from 1910 to the 1940s. The third follows the Provisional Government and Korean military organizations through wartime China. The fourth compares the Soviet and Korean American environments, including Kim Il Sung's wartime experience and the diplomacy of Syngman Rhee and his rivals. The fifth examines competing visions of an independent republic, Korean participation in Allied operations, and the Cairo Declaration, ending at the threshold of liberation.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, a plural movement must not be collapsed into a single figure. Not every Korean communist was Kim Il Sung, not every nationalist was Kim Ku, and the Provisional Government was never an internationally recognized sovereign state. Second, a record of dedicated resistance must be distinguished from the claim that any exile organization won liberation by itself. Japan's defeat in August 1945 resulted chiefly from Allied military victory, and Korean activism and Allied power were related but distinct processes.

The chapter's central question is one that Chapters 64 through 66 will make urgent: when Japan's empire collapsed, who possessed the historical, organizational, and political authority to speak for an independent Korea, and why was there no universally accepted answer? The chapter ends before liberation itself, which Chapter 64 narrates, and it does not narrate the American and Soviet occupations or the formation of two states. Its task is to establish the claims, rivalries, and unfinished business that those later events inherited.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how annexation, migration, exile, and Japanese surveillance produced transnational networks of Korean resistance, and how conditions differed among China, Manchuria, the Russian Far East, Hawaii, and the American mainland.
  2. Trace the formation, consolidation, institutions, and limitations of the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea from 1919 to 1945.
  3. Distinguish among the major independence organizations, including the Provisional Government, Uiyeoldan, the Korean Patriotic Corps, the Korean Volunteer Corps, the Korean Volunteer Army, the Korean Liberation Army, the Korean Independence League, the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army, the Korean National Association, and the Korean Commission.
  4. Analyze the objectives and consequences of direct-action campaigns, including the 1932 attacks in Tokyo and Shanghai, and their effect on Chinese support for Korean exiles.
  5. Describe the borderland geography and social structure that made Manchuria a center of settlement and armed resistance, and evaluate competing claims about the battles of 1920.
  6. Interpret the Free City Incident of 1921 as a product of Korean factionalism, Soviet policy, and regional politics rather than a simple confrontation with Japan.
  7. Compare the Korean Liberation Army in Chongqing with the Yan'an-oriented Korean Volunteer Army, and explain how Chinese military and political authorities shaped each.
  8. Evaluate the experience of Soviet Koreans, including the 1937 deportation, as an episode of state repression rather than only a phase of revolutionary organizing.
  9. Assess Kim Il Sung's documented wartime activities independently of later North Korean state narratives.
  10. Compare the strategies and rivalries of Syngman Rhee, Ahn Chang-ho, and Pak Yong-man, and explain how emigrant communities financed the movement.
  11. Interpret the Cairo Declaration and its phrase "in due course," and explain why no major Allied government formally recognized the Provisional Government.
  12. Explain why, by August 1945, several Korean leaders and organizations held competing claims to legitimacy and none held universally recognized authority.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a movement for national independence survive the destruction of national sovereignty and the suppression of political activity inside the colony?
  2. What made a government in exile a government in any meaningful sense, and what did it lack?
  3. Why did movements that shared an enemy so often fight over leadership, ideology, and strategy?
  4. How did the political environment of each host region, whether Manchuria, China, the Soviet Union, or the United States, enable and constrain what Koreans could do?
  5. What was the relationship between civilian settlements and armed resistance in Manchuria, and what did it cost civilians?
  6. How should historians weigh participants' memoirs, enemy police files, and later state narratives when they describe the same events differently?
  7. In what ways did dependence on Chinese, Soviet, or American patrons both create opportunities and limit Korean command?
  8. What does it mean to distinguish Korean political agency from Allied military victory in explaining the end of colonial rule?
  9. Why did the question of who could speak for Korea remain unresolved in August 1945, and why must that outcome not be read as inevitable?

Historical Context

The chapter builds on earlier chapters and should be read with them. Chapters 53 and 54 explain how the Korean Empire lost its sovereignty between 1905 and 1910, including the righteous-army resistance that continued after the protectorate. Chapters 55 through 58 describe the colonial state and the March First Movement and its aftermath, including the founding of the Provisional Government in Shanghai, which Chapter 56 introduces. Chapter 59 describes the creation of nationalist, socialist, and guerrilla movements, and Chapters 61 and 62 trace the expansion of Japan's empire into Manchuria and the mobilization of Koreans for total war. The present chapter does not retell those histories. It reconstructs the institutions, geography, military operations, and diplomatic claims of the movements that operated outside the peninsula.

Overseas Korean communities were older than annexation. Famine and hardship in the northern provinces had pushed farmers across the Duman and Amnok rivers into what is now northeastern China and into the Russian Maritime region from the middle of the nineteenth century. Korean laborers began arriving in Hawaii in 1903, recruited for sugar plantations, and small numbers of students and political exiles reached the American mainland in the same years. After 1905 and especially after 1910, these communities received new arrivals who left for political reasons: officials, intellectuals, former soldiers of the disbanded Korean army, and participants in the righteous armies. Existing settlements gave exiles somewhere to go, schools and churches to use, and sympathizers to approach for money.

The international environment also mattered. Revolutionary Russia and then the Soviet Union offered an alternative to the imperial order, and the Comintern sought contacts with Asian revolutionaries. Republican China was politically fragmented through the 1920s and early 1930s, with regional militarists, a Nationalist government that consolidated slowly, and a Communist party that built its own territory; Korean exiles had to navigate all of them. The United States, whose missionaries and educational institutions had shaped many Korean leaders, was a place where Koreans could organize openly but where the government treated Japan as a legitimate power in Asia for most of this period. Wilsonian language about self-determination raised hopes in 1919 that the peace conference would address Korea, and the failure of that hope influenced the strategic debates traced in this chapter.

Finally, the chapter must be read against the geography of Japanese power. Japan's colonial police and intelligence services followed Korean activists across borders, pressed Chinese and Western authorities to restrict them, and after 1931 controlled most of Manchuria directly. The Korean movements' capacity to operate was never independent of the policies of the states that hosted them, and a recurring theme of this chapter is that Korean organizations depended on, negotiated with, and sometimes were restrained by Chinese, Soviet, American, and British authorities.

Section 63.1 · Division I — Government in Exile and the Politics of Independence

Korean Independence Beyond the Peninsula

When Japan annexed Korea in 1910, it did not eliminate Korean political life; it relocated much of it. The reasons were practical. Inside the colony, the Government-General controlled the police, press, and courts, and organizations with openly political aims could not survive. Outside it, activists could write, raise money, drill recruits, and negotiate with foreign governments, subject to the limits set by their hosts and to Japanese efforts to track and suppress them. Understanding the independence movement therefore requires a map as much as a chronology.

The overseas Korean population was not created by exile. Farmers crossing the northern rivers into Manchuria and the Russian Maritime region had been settling there since the middle of the nineteenth century, and by the 1910s they formed large agricultural communities with their own villages, schools, and religious associations. Exiles after 1905 and 1910 joined these communities rather than founding new ones. Some of them, including former officials and army officers, brought administrative skills and political connections; others, including intellectuals associated with the Sinminhoe (New People's Society), an association formed in 1907 to promote education, industry, and eventual independence, brought organizational models. Two organizations of the period deserve notice for later developments. In the United States, the Korean National Association (Daehan Gungminhoe) was formed in 1909 by the merger of earlier emigrant societies and became the principal organization of Koreans in the Americas. In California, Ahn Chang-ho founded the Young Korean Academy (Hungsadan) in 1913 to cultivate leaders through moral and civic training.

The communities differed sharply. In Manchuria's borderland districts, the Korean population was large, rural, and relatively concentrated, and it lived under Chinese sovereignty that local authorities enforced unevenly. These conditions allowed schools, self-governing associations, and armed groups to develop, although they also exposed villages to Japanese pressure. The Russian Far East also held a large Korean population, including naturalized subjects and recent arrivals, and its revolutionary politics after 1917 opened contacts with Bolshevik institutions. In China proper, Shanghai's international settlements gave a small number of exiles legal cover. The French Concession, in particular, offered protection from direct Chinese and Japanese police action, though not from Japanese consular pressure. In Hawaii, the Korean population consisted largely of plantation laborers and their families, and in California and elsewhere on the mainland it included students, small business owners, farm workers, and a few professionals. The Hawaiian and Californian communities could organize openly and legally, and they supplied a larger share of the movement's financing than their modest incomes would suggest.

Japanese surveillance linked these places in a different way. The colonial police maintained agents, informers, and cooperating consular officials across Manchuria, Shanghai, and beyond, and Japanese diplomats pressed Chinese and Western authorities to restrict or expel activists. The pressure was steady rather than uniform. Chinese local officials sometimes tolerated Korean groups and sometimes cooperated with Japan, as the 1925 agreement between the Japanese authorities in Korea and the Manchurian warlord government to control Korean activists shows (§63.7). Movement leaders responded by using aliases, moving frequently, and relying on couriers and sympathizers. Evidence for these networks, which survives largely in Japanese police files and in participants' later memoirs, therefore reflects both the activists' secrecy and their opponents' attention.

Three features of this transnational setting recur throughout the chapter. First, activists moved among regions: a leader might begin in Manchuria, work in Shanghai, travel to Moscow, and end in Chongqing, and a young person might begin in a Hawaiian church school and end in a Chinese military academy. Second, the host societies shaped the movements. Each region's authorities, social conditions, and political currents gave local organizations their particular character. Third, no single place held a monopoly on legitimacy, so competing claims to leadership were built into the geography of the movement from the start.

The remainder of Division I concentrates on Shanghai and on the politics of exile government, because the Provisional Government became the institution that claimed to unify the scattered movements. It did so with limited success, and its difficulties are essential to understanding why no single authority could claim recognition in 1945.

Section 63.2

The Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea, 1919

The March First Movement of 1919, narrated in Chapter 56, changed the political situation of exile politics. Demonstrations that involved large numbers of people across the peninsula showed that the independence aspiration was widely shared, and they encouraged activists abroad to believe that an organized body could claim to speak for the Korean people. The Paris Peace Conference and the language of national self-determination associated with Woodrow Wilson raised the possibility, which proved illusory, that the victorious powers would take up Korea's case. Korean representatives in Paris, Philadelphia, and other cities made appeals, and several groups decided that an organization with governmental form would strengthen them.

As a result, more than one authority was proclaimed in 1919. In the Russian Far East, Korean activists associated with the Vladivostok community organized a Korean National Assembly (Daehan Gungmin Uihoe) in March. In April, a plan for a government was publicized under the name of Hansung (Seoul), based on a claim that delegates from across the peninsula had agreed on it. In Shanghai, activists who had gathered after the March First Movement convened a provisional assembly and adopted a provisional charter on 11 April 1919, organizing a Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea. These efforts overlapped in membership, and over the following months negotiators worked to merge them. In September 1919 an integrated Provisional Government, based in Shanghai and claiming continuity with the Hansung plan, took shape under a revised constitution. It named Syngman Rhee, then in the United States, as president.

Historical Vocabulary
Provisional Government (Imsi Jeongbu) and Legitimacy

Definition: A provisional government is a body that claims governing authority on a temporary basis until a regular government can be established. The Korean Provisional Government claimed to be a temporary executive for a republic that would hold a national assembly once Korean territory was liberated.

Historical context: The word "provisional" signaled that the government expected to be replaced by a regularly constituted government after independence. Its legitimacy rested on several claims: the popular expression of March First, the participation of delegates from many regions, a charter that named the people as the source of sovereignty, and a claimed continuity with the Korean state that had existed before 1910. It lacked the usual attributes of a government: territory it controlled, a population it taxed, and diplomatic recognition by other states.

Why it matters: Legitimacy in 1945 would depend on how many of these claims other Koreans and foreign powers accepted. The Provisional Government held a strong symbolic claim and a weak practical one.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat the Provisional Government as an internationally recognized sovereign state in exile, as some governments displaced during the Second World War in Europe were. No major power formally recognized it (§63.19).

The Provisional Government developed institutions that, however modest, matter to the history of Korean republicanism. It adopted the Republic of Korea (Daehan Minguk) as its name, which connected it to the Korean Empire's Daehan name while rejecting monarchy. It organized an executive of ministries, and a provisional assembly with members drawn from the activists in Shanghai and from regional delegates who could attend. It issued a constitution and revised it repeatedly. It published a newspaper, the Independence (Dongnip Sinmun), which circulated among exiles and, through clandestine routes, in Korea. It also attempted to set up a network of contact offices and couriers (the yeontongje) to link Shanghai with the peninsula, and to raise funds through donations and independence bonds. The network operated imperfectly and was repeatedly penetrated by Japanese police.

Diplomacy was an immediate preoccupation. Kim Kyu-sik was sent to Paris, and the government sought contact with the Chinese authorities of the day, with the Soviet representatives in the Far East, and with American officials. In practice the outcomes were limited. The peace conference did not take up Korea, and the Allied governments had commitments and interests, including their alliance with Japan, that made them unwilling to challenge Japanese rule. Rhee, who had proposed in early 1919, with another Korean activist, that the Korean case be placed under a League of Nations mandate as a step toward independence, provoked sharp criticism. Opponents viewed a mandate petition as a concession to foreign control, and the disagreement became one source of the long conflict between Rhee and the more militant activists.

The consolidation of 1919 was more fragile than the formal unification suggested. The Shanghai government had a limited revenue base and relied on donations from communities in Manchuria, the Americas, and elsewhere. It had no army, though it recognized and sought to coordinate armed groups in Manchuria. It faced the problem of authority: ministers and assembly members disagreed with each other and with leaders in other regions, and the Russian Far Eastern and Manchurian groups were reluctant to defer to Shanghai. Syngman Rhee visited Shanghai from December 1920 to 1921 but left without resolving the quarrels, which concerned his use of funds in the United States, his mandate proposal, and his view of the government's priorities. These disputes, and the strategic argument they reflected, are the subject of §63.3.

Primary Source Window

The Provisional Charter of the Republic of Korea, April 1919

Creator: Delegates to the provisional assembly convened in Shanghai · Date: adopted 11 April 1919; revised later in 1919 and repeatedly afterward · Provenance: Preserved in published collections of independence-movement documents and in the exile press; students should consult a verified modern edition for exact wording · Type: Constitutional document

Context: The charter was drafted within weeks of the March First demonstrations by exiles who wanted to give the new government a constitutional basis. It was a short document of ten articles, and its drafters worked quickly. The text was also meant to be read abroad, where it could demonstrate that the Korean movement had a republican program.

Paraphrase: The following paraphrases the charter's general content; it is not a quotation.

The charter declares a democratic republic in which sovereignty belongs to the Korean people, and affirms equality among citizens regardless of sex, social class, or wealth. It guarantees freedoms of religion, speech, publication, assembly, and association, among others; recognizes the rights to vote and to stand for office; and commits the state to universal education and to the obligations of taxation and military service. It pledges the new state to international cooperation, and it includes progressive social provisions such as the abolition of corporal punishment and of licensed prostitution. It provides that, after the recovery of national territory, the government would convene a national assembly.

Analysis: The charter's rejection of monarchy and of hereditary status was significant. Many of its drafters had been born into a society where class distinctions had been legally entrenched until the Gabo reforms of the 1890s (Chapter 52), and the equality clauses reflect a deliberate break with that order. The charter also shows the influence of liberal and republican models then circulating in East Asia, though it was adapted to Korean conditions and aspirations.

Limitations and perspective: The charter records the intentions of a small body of exiles, many of them educated and some with Christian or foreign-school backgrounds. It cannot show how widely its principles were understood or accepted among Korean farmers, workers, or women, and it described a state that did not exist. It says nothing about practical authority, and the government's later experience shows how limited that was.

Questions for students:

Documentary reference: The provisional charter is available through the National Institute of Korean History, Constitutional History Database; its ten articles can be checked there against the paraphrase in this window.

  1. What does the charter say about who holds sovereignty, and why would that choice matter to a movement opposing a colonial empire?
  2. Who wrote the document, and under what circumstances? How might they have addressed both Korean and foreign audiences?
  3. What purposes beyond governing might a constitution serve for a government in exile?
  4. What can historians learn about the founders' ideas about society from the equality and rights provisions?
  5. What can the charter not tell us about the actual power, support, or legitimacy of the government that adopted it?
Section 63.3

Leadership, Ideology, and Divisions within the Independence Movement

The early Provisional Government faced a problem that would recur: it was the shared home of activists who disagreed fundamentally about means and ends. These disagreements cut across one another rather than falling into two neat camps. At the level of strategy, some leaders favored diplomacy, appealing to the League of Nations and the Western powers and waiting for an opportunity; others favored preparation through education and the cultivation of a capable citizenry, an emphasis associated with Ahn Chang-ho; others advocated armed struggle immediately and in earnest, as Pak Yong-man and the commanders in Manchuria did; and others, especially after 1920, embraced terrorist attacks and assassination as direct action. At the level of ideology, republican liberals, Christian reformers, socialists, communists, and anarchists all participated, and some came to doubt whether a liberal republic could address the grievances of Korean workers and farmers.

Personal and regional rivalries compounded these differences. Activists from the northwestern provinces, from the Seoul region, and from elsewhere brought different networks and expectations. Funding sources, whether the United States, Manchuria, or the Soviet Union, gave their patrons influence. Soviet funds that reached some Korean communists in the early 1920s reportedly became a source of controversy, and the extent and uses of those funds remain subjects of scholarly discussion rather than settled fact.

The government's problems culminated in 1923. After several years of declining resources and growing frustration, activists called a National Representatives Convention (Gungmin Daepyohoe) in Shanghai, which met from January to June 1923, with the aim of uniting the movement. The delegates, who came from several regions, divided into two main groups. The reformers (gaejo-pa) wished to keep the existing Provisional Government and revise its institutions. The creationists (changjo-pa) wished to abandon it and establish a new government that would better represent the whole movement and could assume a more radical course. The convention failed to resolve the dispute. The creationists withdrew and attempted to set up an alternative organization, which was not durable and which lost its base after Soviet authorities in the Far East declined to support it. The Provisional Government survived but in a weakened condition, deprived of many of its most active members and of some of its funding.

Leadership crises followed. Rhee, who remained in the United States, was impeached by the provisional assembly in 1925, and Pak Eun-sik succeeded him. Rhee and his supporters disputed the legality of the action, and the controversy left a durable rivalry. The government's constitution was revised several times in the following years, shifting between a presidential and a collective or committee-based structure, in part to prevent any single leader from dominating and in part to cope with the shrinking number of active participants. By the late 1920s the government was reduced to a small group in Shanghai, persisting through the efforts of a few dedicated leaders, including Kim Ku, who took on increasing responsibility for its survival and who also began to rely on direct action as a way to restore its prominence.

Attempts at unification continued through the 1920s and 1930s. Activists established party-like organizations to broaden participation, and in the late 1920s a movement for a single national party sought to bring nationalists and socialists together. In 1930, Kim Ku and associates formed the Korean Independence Party in Shanghai to provide a more stable political base for the government, and its leaders became central to the government's later development. These efforts had mixed results: they produced durable organizations but also new rivalries, since each initiative implicitly challenged other claimants to leadership.

The fragmentation of the exile movement should not be read as simple failure or as evidence that activists were unserious. The movement operated under severe constraints: its members were exiles with few resources, hunted by a powerful empire, dependent on foreign hosts and scattered across thousands of kilometers. Under such conditions, differences of strategy and ideology that might have been manageable in a normal political system became sources of lasting division. The historians who study the period generally emphasize both points: the divisions were real and damaging, and they arose in a setting that made unity extraordinarily hard to maintain.

Section 63.4

Direct Action, the Heroic Corps, and the Shanghai Crisis of 1932

One response to the weakness of diplomacy and of formal organization was direct action: targeted violence against colonial officials, police, and symbols of Japanese authority, intended both to damage the enemy and to demonstrate that Korean resistance continued. This strategy did not originate in a single organization, and it is important to distinguish the two principal groups with which it is associated, which pursued related goals under different leaders and with different political outlooks.

The first was Uiyeoldan, usually rendered the Heroic Corps, founded in November 1919 in Jilin, Manchuria, by Kim Won-bong and a group of young activists. Its members were drawn largely from young men in Manchuria and China, and its leaders were influenced by revolutionary and anarchist ideas, as well as by Korean nationalism. Uiyeoldan carried out or attempted a series of attacks. Members bombed police stations in Busan and Miryang in 1920, tried to kill a senior Japanese official in Shanghai in 1922, and attempted a bomb attack at the gate of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo in 1924. In December 1926, one of its members, Na Seok-ju, bombed the Oriental Development Company and the Chosun Industrial Bank in Seoul, institutions that Koreans associated with Japanese economic penetration. These actions caused limited material damage and, in several cases, failed outright. Their significance lay in their symbolism and in the arguments made for them.

Paraphrase: The following paraphrases the declaration's main arguments; it is not a quotation.

The declaration denounces Japanese rule as an enslavement of Koreans that has deprived them of political, economic, and cultural life. It rejects the options of cooperation with the colonial regime, of a limited autonomy under Japanese rule, and of reliance on foreign diplomacy and the great powers, and it criticizes advocates of cultural and educational activity as unwilling to confront the real source of Korea's condition. In their place it calls for a revolution made by the Korean people themselves through the use of force, directed against Japanese power and against the privileged classes that had cooperated with it, and it describes violent action as the instrument by which the people can recover their own destiny.

Analysis: The declaration shows how some activists responded to the apparent futility of diplomacy by emphasizing popular agency, and it demonstrates that the case for direct action rested on a theory of how national revival would occur, and not only on a desire for vengeance. It also shows the influence of anarchist and populist thought on the circle around Kim Won-bong, a current distinct from both liberal republicanism and organized communism.

Limitations and perspective: The declaration is a manifesto, written to persuade, and it presents its program in absolute terms. It cannot show how many Koreans accepted its arguments, and it cannot show how the group's actual operations related to its theory. It represents one current of exile opinion; the diplomats, educators, and organizers whom it criticizes are its objects rather than its authors.

The second group was the Korean Patriotic Corps (Hanin Aeguktan), organized by Kim Ku in Shanghai in 1931, when the Provisional Government was weak and Kim Ku sought a means of restoring its prestige. Kim Ku's organization was connected to the government and to the Korean Independence Party rather than being an independent revolutionary body, and its leaders were nationalists rather than anarchists. Its aim was to carry out a small number of dramatic attacks that would draw attention to the Korean cause and especially to win the support of the Chinese government.

The Patriotic Corps became prominent in 1932, within a few months of each other. On 8 January 1932 in Tokyo, Yi Bong-chang threw a grenade at a procession carrying Emperor Hirohito. The device failed to injure the emperor, and Yi was captured and later executed. On 29 April 1932, at a Shanghai ceremony in Hongkou Park celebrating the emperor's birthday and the Japanese military's recent operations in the city, Yun Bong-gil threw a bomb at the reviewing stand. The bomb killed General Shirakawa Yoshinori, the commander of Japanese forces in Shanghai, who died of his wounds weeks later, and Kawabata Teiji, the head of the Japanese residents' association; it also wounded several other officials, including Shigemitsu Mamoru, the Japanese minister to China, who lost a leg, and Nomura Kichisaburo, a naval officer. Yun was arrested and executed in Japan in December 1932. Kim Ku publicly claimed organizational responsibility, and Japanese police sought him.

The consequences were significant. Japanese authorities pressed the French Concession to arrest Korean exiles, and the Provisional Government's personnel scattered, beginning a westward movement that would carry the government across China (§63.11). Kim Ku evaded capture with the help of Chinese and Western friends, and the Korean cause gained sympathy among Chinese observers, particularly at a time when Chinese resistance to Japan had just been tested in the fighting at Shanghai in early 1932. Chinese Nationalist leaders, including Chiang Kai-shek, became more willing to support Korean activists, a change that helped Kim Ku obtain assistance in the following years, including military training for Korean cadets at a branch of the Central Military Academy at Luoyang in 1934. A remark attributed to Chiang, that a single Korean had accomplished what millions of Chinese soldiers had not, is widely repeated; students should treat it as a reported statement rather than as a verified quotation.

Whether direct action achieved its objectives is a question on which assessments differ. The attacks did not threaten Japanese rule, and they cost lives, both of the attackers and of Korean communities that Japanese authorities pressured in response. But they dramatically raised the profile of the Korean cause in China and brought the Provisional Government material support that it could not have gained by other means. The Patriotic Corps and Uiyeoldan should be understood as distinct organizations pursuing related tactics with different political affiliations, and the tactics themselves belong to a longer history of anticolonial violence in which Koreans, like many colonized peoples, debated the ethics and efficacy of terror. The attacks also illustrate a theme of the whole chapter: Korean exiles' ability to act depended on the policies of Chinese, French, and other host authorities.

Section 63.5 · Division II — Manchuria and the Armed Independence Struggle

Manchuria as a Korean Settlement and Resistance Region

Manchuria occupied a different place in the independence movement from Shanghai or Hawaii because it contained a large rural Korean population living directly across the border from the peninsula. Korean farmers had crossed the Duman and Amnok rivers for decades, often escaping famine, high rents, and taxes, and they cleared land, cultivated rice and other crops in river valleys, and built villages. By the early 1920s, Koreans in the borderland districts known as Jiandao (Gando in Korean) numbered in the hundreds of thousands according to commonly cited estimates, and Koreans in Manchuria as a whole were more numerous still. Exact figures vary by source and date, and Chinese, Japanese, and Korean counts differ, so students should treat all totals as approximate.

The political status of the region was ambiguous. Both Korea and China had claimed Jiandao in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In 1909, after Japan had established a protectorate over Korea, Japan and the Qing dynasty concluded an agreement that recognized Chinese sovereignty over the area in exchange for Japanese commercial and railway concessions in Manchuria, and the Japanese authorities withdrew the office they had established there in 1907. The result was that Koreans lived under Chinese sovereignty, administered by officials whose power was often limited, while Japan claimed an interest in protecting Korean subjects and extended its consular police into the region. Many Koreans had a complicated legal status: some had become Chinese subjects, others remained Japanese subjects, and the distinction mattered to who could arrest them.

Within this setting, Koreans built institutions. Schools, such as the Myeongdong School and the Seojeon Seosuk in the borderland, taught Korean language and history together with modern subjects and became centers of nationalist education. Self-governing associations, including the Ganminhoe formed in the 1910s, handled community affairs and provided a basis for political organization. Religious communities, including Christian churches and the Daejonggyo, a religion centered on the figure of Dangun (discussed in Chapter 6), contributed organizational networks, and the Daejonggyo in particular became closely linked with the armed movement. The Shinhung Military Academy, established in 1911 by exiles in southern Manchuria, trained officers, some of whom would later command independence forces.

The relationship between civilian settlements and armed groups was close and dangerous. Independence forces depended on villagers for food, shelter, recruits, intelligence, and funds. Villages, in turn, sometimes welcomed the protection and sense of purpose that armed groups offered and sometimes resented the demands for contributions, which could be heavy. Japanese authorities, understanding the dependency, treated villages as part of the resistance infrastructure. They pressured Chinese local officials to restrict Korean associations, used police and informers to monitor them, and, when they could, punished entire communities for aiding armed groups. The Mitsuya Agreement of 1925 between the Japanese police authorities in Korea and the government of the Manchurian warlord Zhang Zuolin committed the Chinese side to disarm and arrest Korean armed activists in return for payments for each capture, and it greatly reduced the ability of nationalist groups to operate.

Manchuria was not simply a Korean space. The population included large numbers of Chinese farmers, as well as Manchu, Mongol, and other communities, and conflicts between Korean and Chinese villagers over land and water occasionally occurred, which Japanese authorities sometimes exploited. Local warlord regimes had their own interests, and bandit groups added to insecurity. Korean armed bands often negotiated with local Chinese authorities, and some operated in cooperation with Chinese forces, while others clashed with them. The history of armed resistance in Manchuria is therefore a history of a multiethnic borderland, and the claim that Korean forces alone controlled it would be mistaken. Later sections show that some of the most important anti-Japanese forces in the region after 1931 were led by the Chinese Communist Party, with Koreans serving among them.

The borderland's geography contributed to its role. Forested mountains along the northern frontier, river valleys that allowed settlement, and the possibility of crossing quickly into Korea or retreating into Chinese or Russian territory made it a plausible place for guerrilla activity. But geography created possibilities rather than outcomes, and the decisive constraints were political: the attitudes of Chinese authorities, the ability of Japan to project force across the border, and the willingness of civilians to bear the consequences. The next sections examine two periods in which these constraints produced very different results.

Section 63.6

Bongodong, Cheongsanri, and the Independence Armies of 1920

After the March First Movement, armed activity in Manchuria expanded rapidly. Existing groups grew, new ones formed, and veterans of the Shinhung Military Academy, former soldiers of the Korean army, and volunteers from among the settlers organized units. The best-known commanders were Hong Beom-do, a former soldier and hunter from northern Korea who led the Korean Independence Army (Daehan Dongnipgun); Kim Jwa-jin, a Daejonggyo member who commanded the military forces of the Northern Route Military Government Office (Bukro Gunjeongseo); and Yi Cheong-cheon, an officer who had trained at a Japanese military academy before leaving to join the independence cause and who held senior command roles. These units were not a single army. They had separate organizations, supporters, and sometimes rivalries, and in 1920 they cooperated tactically rather than under one command.

The forces made cross-border raids into northern Korea, attacking police posts and government offices. In early June 1920, a Korean raid across the Duman River prompted a Japanese pursuit. On 7 June, in the valley of Bongodong, Hong Beom-do's combined forces, using positions in the surrounding hills, ambushed a Japanese detachment and inflicted losses on it. This engagement is celebrated in Korean historical memory as the first major victory of the independence forces over Japanese regular troops. Korean accounts reported well over a hundred Japanese dead. Japanese records acknowledged a defeat but reported far smaller casualties. Historians generally accept that the engagement was a genuine tactical success for the Korean forces, but they regard the larger Korean casualty claims as unverified and probably inflated, and the exact figures remain uncertain.

Japan responded with a much larger effort. Japanese authorities had long sought a justification for deploying troops in Manchuria. After an attack by Chinese bandits on the Japanese consulate at Hunchun in October 1920, which some Koreans and Chinese suspected had been tolerated or encouraged by Japanese agents, Japan sent thousands of troops, including units drawn from its forces then engaged in Siberia, into the Jiandao region, ostensibly to suppress bandits and Korean insurgents. The Chinese authorities protested but were unable to prevent the incursion.

Facing overwhelming force, the independence units withdrew northward, and between about 21 and 26 October 1920 they fought a series of engagements in the Cheongsanri area, in rugged country in the Helong district. Kim Jwa-jin's forces and others used terrain to ambush pursuing Japanese columns. Korean accounts describe a major victory with Japanese dead in the hundreds or even over a thousand, whereas Japanese reports acknowledged much smaller losses. Scholars generally credit the independence forces with real tactical successes in difficult conditions and doubt the larger numbers. The accounts illustrate a recurring problem with the evidence: each side had reasons to inflate or minimize casualties, and neutral observers were absent from the remote battlefield.

The strategic result was not a victory. The independence forces could not stand against the Japanese deployment, and they retreated, abandoning the bases they had built. The principal damage fell on Korean civilians. Between the autumn of 1920 and the spring of 1921, Japanese troops burned villages, schools, and churches across the Jiandao region and killed civilians in what is known in Korea as the Gyeongsin Massacre, named for the cyclical year 1920. The number of Korean civilians killed is debated: Korean and missionary accounts at the time reported thousands, and some later Korean estimates are considerably higher, while Japanese military reports gave much lower figures. Historians generally accept that the violence was extensive and deliberate, directed at communities that supported armed groups, and that the lower official Japanese numbers are unreliable. The destruction of villages and schools also damaged the settlements on which resistance depended.

The events of 1920 illustrate the structure of armed resistance in Manchuria. The forces could win engagements in favorable terrain but lacked the resources to hold ground, and their dependence on civilian communities exposed those communities to retaliation. For the surviving units, the question was where to regroup. Many moved north and east toward the Russian Far East, where new opportunities and new dangers awaited them.

Section 63.7

Retreat, the Free City Incident, and the Fragmentation of Armed Resistance

Following Japanese offensives against Manchurian independence forces in 1920, Korean units sought to preserve their fighting strength by retreating northward. Several gathered first near Mishan, in Chinese Manchuria, where a federation commonly called the Greater Korean Independence Corps (Daehan Dongnip Gundan) was organized. The fighters then crossed into the Russian Far East, hoping for sanctuary, arms, and opportunities to continue the anti-Japanese struggle. These successive locations matter: Mishan was not inside Soviet territory, and the later confrontation occurred near Alekseyevsk, renamed Svobodny (Korean Jayusi), on the Zeya River in the Amur basin. Russia was still affected by civil war and Japanese intervention. The Soviet-aligned Far Eastern Republic, created as a buffer, maintained its own military apparatus. Korean armed groups arriving from Manchuria encountered other Korean partisans already serving within revolutionary formations, and the resulting dispute over command could not be resolved merely by invoking a common national purpose.

Korean communist networks were themselves sharply divided. The Shanghai-aligned circle associated with Yi Dong-hwi competed with an Irkutsk-aligned circle that enjoyed connections to Comintern authorities. In the Svobodny region the Sakhalin Volunteer Army (Sahallin Uiyongdae) was associated with the Shanghai-oriented military network and with Park Il-ya (Park Ilia), rather than with O Ha-muk. O Ha-muk was linked to a rival revolutionary battalion and the Irkutsk-aligned Korean Revolutionary Military Council, which pressed to bring the armed detachments under its authority. This alignment is decisive: it was not a Sakhalin force led by O Ha-muk that disarmed a separate Korean Independence Corps. Rather, the key confrontation pitted the Park Il-ya-associated Sakhalin Volunteer Army against forces enforcing the rival council's claim to command. Some Manchurian veterans accepted the council's structure; other Korean detachments did not. Loyalties cannot be reduced to a clean division between all nationalists and all communists.

By June 1921 these conflicts had become a contest for authority over armed Korean units near Svobodny. A commander acting for the Irkutsk-supported council, generally rendered as Kalandarishvili, ordered the units under the Sakhalin Volunteer Army arrangement to submit. Park Il-ya resisted. On 28 June 1921, the Soviet-aligned 29th Regiment and supporting forces attempted to disarm the Sakhalin Volunteer Army near its encampment at Masanov. Fighting followed, and men were killed, scattered, captured, or subsequently transferred into other formations. A Korean historical reference records 864 prisoners after the fighting, while estimates of deaths differ substantially across records and later histories; that prisoner count is not a verified universal total for all armed Koreans in the region. The site should be situated within the Zeya-Amur river system rather than described as a battle on the Amur River itself. This episode, widely called the Free City Incident, was a devastating struggle over command and Soviet policy, not an encounter in which Korean soldiers were simply defeated by Japanese troops.

The chain of authority is especially important for interpreting responsibility. The Korean Revolutionary Military Council asserted jurisdiction through revolutionary political and military institutions, while the Sakhalin Volunteer Army's leaders disputed its legitimacy. The Far Eastern Republic and the Red Army supplied the military power that enabled the disarmament. In this setting, arguments over who had the right to lead Korean independence troops became inseparable from Soviet calculations about security, diplomatic exposure to Japan, and the practical control of armed foreign groups. Participants could view themselves as loyal to Korean liberation while reaching incompatible conclusions about which command they should obey.

Later memoirs sometimes present the catastrophe as the inevitable result of one faction's treachery, while accounts from the victorious side depict it as the suppression of insubordinate soldiers. Neither description alone adequately reconstructs the crisis. Historians compare reports of the June orders, the sequence of disarmament, factional political correspondence, and survivors' accounts. Counts of those killed, detained, and eventually reassigned must be separated; figures cited in commemorative narratives may refer to different populations. Students should therefore distinguish the facts of the June 28 attack and captivity from contested interpretations of motive, legality, and the exact human toll.

Section 63.8

Anti-Japanese Guerrillas after the Manchurian Crisis of 1931

The Japanese military conquest of Manchuria, which began with the Mukden Incident of September 1931 and was followed in 1932 by the creation of the puppet state of Manchukuo (Chapter 61), transformed the conditions of resistance. Japan now controlled the region's cities, railways, and eventually most of its countryside, and its military and police could operate throughout the area where Korean communities lived. Resistance therefore became anti-Manchukuo as well as anti-Japanese, and it involved Chinese, Korean, and other participants in varying combinations. Students should avoid assuming that anti-Japanese armed resistance in Manchuria was exclusively or even mainly Korean in leadership.

Korean nationalist forces continued to operate in the early 1930s. The Korean Independence Army (Hanguk Dongnipgun), commanded by Yi Cheong-cheon and linked to a nationalist party, fought alongside Chinese anti-Japanese forces in northern Manchuria. In southern Manchuria the Korean Revolutionary Army, led by Yang Se-bong, cooperated with Chinese volunteer units, including in operations around Yongneungga in 1932. These alliances produced local successes but could not compensate indefinitely for shortages of weapons, reliable supply lines, and recruits, or for Japanese and Manchukuo counterinsurgency. Yi Cheong-cheon later moved into China proper and became central to the Provisional Government's military project (§63.12). Yang Se-bong was killed in 1934 after being lured into a Japanese-directed trap by an informer; he and accompanying men were surrounded and died in the resulting armed confrontation. The event should not be described simply as his capture and execution. Surviving accounts differ in minor circumstances, but the involvement of an informer and armed attack is well established.

Communist organizations became the most durable guerrilla force. In 1928 the Comintern dissolved the Korean Communist Party, accusing it of factionalism, and directed Korean communists abroad to join the party of the country in which they lived. In Manchuria, many Korean communists therefore joined the Chinese Communist Party, and with the Mukden Incident they entered the party's guerrilla movement. Over the following years, Chinese Communist-led guerrilla bands were organized into larger formations and, from 1936, into the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army, a coalition of Communist and other units under commanders such as Yang Jingyu and Zhou Baozhong. Koreans formed a significant share of the rank and file in some areas, especially in eastern Manchuria, where Korean settlement was densest, though they served under a largely Chinese leadership structure and a Chinese party. Kim Il Sung was one such Korean guerrilla commander, and his career is discussed in §63.14.

The relationship between Koreans and the Chinese Communist Party was fraught. In the early 1930s the party became convinced that Japanese agents had infiltrated its Korean members, and a series of purges, known as the Minsaengdan affair after an organization supposedly created by Japanese-linked Koreans in Jiandao in 1932, led to the arrest, torture, and execution of many Korean communists. Estimates of the dead run from several hundred to well over a thousand, and the affair left a lasting mark on Korean participants, as well as on the party's later reassessments, which acknowledged that many of the accusations had been unfounded. The Minsaengdan purges show that Koreans in Chinese Communist forces were participants in a movement that did not always trust them.

Japanese counterinsurgency was systematic and brutal. The Kwantung Army and Manchukuo forces used mass roundups of villagers, the relocation of rural populations into guarded settlements to separate civilians from guerrillas, intelligence networks, and the formation of special units. One such unit, the Gando Special Detachment, was composed in part of Koreans employed by the Japanese, a reminder that Koreans fought on both sides of this conflict. Japanese forces also offered rewards and amnesties to encourage surrender. By the late 1930s, the strategy had severely reduced guerrilla strength, and in 1940 Yang Jingyu was killed. The surviving units withdrew into Soviet territory in 1940 and 1941, and the possibility of sustained guerrilla operations in Manchuria had largely ended by the early 1940s.

Military defeat was not the only consequence of the conquest. Armed groups depended on food, information, shelter, and recruits from Korean and Chinese villages. Once Japanese and Manchukuo authorities reorganized settlements and constrained movement, the social foundation of guerrilla warfare narrowed. Villagers faced incompatible demands: resisting patrols could bring reprisals, while cooperation with police or informants could expose relatives to retaliation from insurgents. Similar dilemmas troubled communities near the Duman border, where migration, smuggling, kinship, and armed movement crossed jurisdictions more easily than legal protection did. The result was not merely a shift in battlefield tactics but a transformation of civilian political life across a colonized borderland. [Documentary reference]

Section 63.9 · Division III — Wartime China, the Provisional Government, and Liberation Armies

Korean Revolutionaries and Chinese Nationalist and Communist Politics

By the mid-1930s the center of gravity of exile politics had shifted into China proper, where the Nationalist government under Chiang Kai-shek was consolidating authority, the Chinese Communist Party was building a separate territory, and Japan was extending its pressure southward from Manchuria. Korean activists could not operate in China without the tolerance or support of some Chinese authority, and their fortunes therefore depended on the shifting relationship among Chinese factions and on Chinese assessments of how Korean groups could serve Chinese interests.

The Nationalist government offered support to Korean exiles for reasons of strategy and sympathy. It saw Korean activists as potentially useful against Japan, as sources of intelligence and propaganda, and as a way to show that Japan's empire was resisted throughout Asia. The impact of the 1932 attacks (§63.4) made such support more practicable. But the government also set limits. It refused to treat Korean organizations as equal allies, it sought to keep their armed units under Chinese supervision, and it tended to favor groups whose leaders it considered reliable and anticommunist. Because Chinese officials distributed funds, housing, and military training, Korean leaders competed for access to them, and Chinese patronage became a source of influence within the Korean movement.

Kim Won-bong, whose Uiyeoldan had pursued direct action in the 1920s, changed course in the late 1920s and early 1930s. Korean cadets, including some Uiyeoldan members, had attended the Whampoa Military Academy in Guangzhou in the mid-1920s, and Kim and his associates came to emphasize military and political training as better preparation than individual terrorist acts. In the early 1930s, with Nationalist support, he organized a cadet training program near Nanjing for Koreans. In 1935, several left-nationalist groups, led by Kim Won-bong and including activists associated with other organizations, united to form the Korean National Revolutionary Party (Joseon Minjok Hyeongmyeongdang) at Nanjing. The party presented itself as a united front of the independence movement and sought to rival the Provisional Government in Kim Ku's circle, which it regarded as inadequately representative. The coalition was unstable, and by the later 1930s some participants withdrew or formed rival organizations, while others remained associated with Kim Won-bong's group.

The outbreak of full-scale war between China and Japan in July 1937 changed the context again. Japan's offensive drove the Nationalist government from Nanjing to Wuhan and then to Chongqing in the interior, and Korean groups were scattered along with Chinese institutions. The Nationalists and Communists, who agreed to a nominal united front against Japan, both sought assistance from Koreans, and some Korean leftists who had been reluctant to cooperate with Chiang found it possible to work within the united-front structures. Late in 1937, Kim Won-bong and others formed the Korean National Front League (Joseon Minjok Jeonseon Yeonmaeng), bringing together left-leaning organizations, and in 1938 the league created an armed organization, the Korean Volunteer Corps (§63.10).

The Korean left in China was therefore not a single organization but a spectrum. It included communists who identified with the Chinese Communist Party, nationalist socialists such as Kim Won-bong who cooperated with the Nationalists but sought a social revolution after independence, anarchists who had been central to the early Uiyeoldan circle, and others. Their relations with Kim Ku's right-leaning circle in the Provisional Government were sometimes hostile, and violence among Korean factions occurred, though the common enemy and Chinese pressure encouraged cooperation. Understanding this spectrum is important for resisting the assumption that Korean communists were a single bloc or that every Korean on the left looked to Moscow or to Yan'an.

Chinese priorities limited Korean autonomy throughout. Korean units were to be useful to the Chinese war effort, which meant propaganda, intelligence, translation, and interrogation of prisoners more than independent combat, and Chinese authorities rarely permitted Koreans to act as an independent army. This pattern, in which participation in Chinese or Soviet structures replaced independent Korean command, is one of the chapter's major themes and is essential to assessing what Korean forces in China could and could not claim.

Section 63.10

The Korean Volunteer Corps and the Yan'an Independence Movement

The Korean Volunteer Corps (Joseon Uiyongdae) was founded in October 1938 in Hankou, in the Wuhan region, as the military arm of the Korean National Front League. It began with about a hundred members, many of them students or former cadets, and it operated within the Chinese Nationalist government's wartime military structures, receiving funding and support from the Nationalist authorities and cooperation from sympathetic Chinese individuals, including Communist figures working in the Political Department of the Nationalist military in the period of the united front. Its role was not mainly to fight pitched battles. Its members worked in propaganda against Japanese troops, distributing leaflets and broadcasting messages in Japanese, in interrogating prisoners, gathering intelligence, translating documents, and making appeals to Korean conscripts in the Japanese army. These tasks were valuable, and Chinese authorities valued them, but they were auxiliary to the Chinese war effort.

After the fall of Wuhan in October 1938, the Corps moved to Guilin and later to the wartime capital Chongqing, and its members debated the best course. The debate was both strategic and political. Some wished to remain in Nationalist territory, with its access to the Provisional Government and the possibility of cooperating with the Allies. Others argued that the Corps should move to northern China, where Communist-led forces were operating behind Japanese lines and where large numbers of Koreans lived, with the aim of reaching Manchuria and the peninsula and of recruiting Koreans in Japanese-occupied zones. They also had political reasons to move: the Corps' leftists found the Nationalist environment restrictive and found Kim Won-bong's leadership increasingly tied to the Nationalist government.

In 1940 and 1941, the majority of the Corps' members, by many accounts a large majority, crossed Japanese lines and moved to the Taihang Mountains in Communist-controlled northern China. There they were joined by Korean communists who had served with the Eighth Route Army, and they reorganized under new names. Korean activists in the north formed the North China Korean Youth Federation in 1941 and the Korean Volunteer Corps' North China branch, and in 1942 the Korean Independence League (Joseon Dongnip Dongmaeng) in northern China, led by Kim Tu-bong, a linguist who had been prominent in studies of the Korean language before becoming an exile. The league's armed wing became known as the Korean Volunteer Army (Joseon Uiyonggun), with Mu Jeong, an artillery officer who had served in the Chinese Communist forces, as its commander. The league's political program called for the overthrow of Japanese rule and the establishment of a democratic republic, and it operated in cooperation with the Chinese Communist Party and its military.

The Volunteer Army saw combat. Korean fighters participated in operations against Japanese forces in the Taihang area, and at least one battle in late 1941, at Hujiazhuang, resulted in casualties among the Korean unit, which commemorative traditions treat as a founding sacrifice. The Korean communities in the Communist-controlled areas also supported schools, a training institution for cadres, and propaganda efforts directed at Korean soldiers in the Japanese army. The Yan'an region, the Communist capital, became a base for Korean organizers, and the independence movement associated with it is often called the Yan'an independence movement or, because of the leaders' later careers, the Yan'an faction.

The Hujiazhuang engagement is useful precisely because names assigned retrospectively to organizations can obscure the sequence of their formation. The fighting generally associated with the Korean Volunteer Corps' north-China elements preceded the 1942 consolidation of the Yan'an-oriented Korean Volunteer Army. Later memoir and literary treatments, including writings associated with Kim Hak-chul, preserve memories of participants but should be checked against contemporary orders, Chinese military records, and the date of the organizational change. In a textbook chronology, the earlier combat experience of volunteers must not be turned into an operation by a military body that had not yet formally assumed its later name.

The division of the Volunteer Corps left Kim Won-bong in Chongqing with a small fraction of his original force, and he moved toward cooperation with the Provisional Government, which in 1942 absorbed his remaining members into the Korean Liberation Army (§63.12). The result was two armed Korean traditions, derived from the same origin: one in Chongqing, under the umbrella of the Provisional Government and Nationalist China; the other in northern China, under the umbrella of the Communist-led movement. The two were never united, and students should not confuse the Chongqing-based Korean Liberation Army with the Yan'an-oriented Korean Volunteer Army or treat them as parts of one force.

The northern movement's relationship with the Chinese Communist Party mirrored that of the Chongqing groups with the Nationalists. Korean units operated within Communist military and political structures, relied on Chinese supplies and protection, and were expected to contribute to the Chinese war effort. The Korean Independence League declared independence for Korea as its goal, and there is evidence that its leaders wished to return to Korea and to form a government, but they could not act independently of their hosts. As the war approached its end, the Volunteer Army moved toward Manchuria with Communist forces, and its leaders would later return to a very different political situation in Korea. That story belongs to Chapters 64 and 65, and only its setup is needed here.

Section 63.11

The Provisional Government's Wartime Migration and Reorganization

While the Korean left reorganized in northern China, the Provisional Government itself moved from city to city. After the exodus from Shanghai in 1932, it relocated to Hangzhou and other cities of the Yangtze region, with Kim Ku and a small circle sustaining its institutions in difficult conditions. After the Japanese advance of 1937, it moved again: through Changsha and Guangzhou to Liuzhou in 1938, then to Qijiang in Sichuan in 1939, and finally to Chongqing in 1940, where it remained until the end of the war. The journey was long, with officials, family members, and supporters traveling by rail, river boat, and sometimes on foot, with Chinese Nationalist assistance. A government that had begun as a body of a few dozen in a foreign concession had become a refugee community moving across a country at war.

Kim Ku's leadership became central to this period. Having claimed responsibility for the 1932 attacks, he had won Chiang Kai-shek's attention and trust. In the following years he organized the Provisional Government's party, the Korean Independence Party, and managed relations with the Nationalist authorities. Under a revision of the government's constitution in 1940, the system was reorganized around a chairmanship, and Kim Ku became chairman, giving him greater formal authority than any leader had held for years. Other figures were also important. Jo So-ang, a political thinker who served as foreign minister, helped to develop the government's formal program, and Yi Cheong-cheon, who commanded the military forces, balanced Kim Ku's political leadership.

The government's finances were precarious. It relied on Chinese Nationalist subsidies, donations from overseas Koreans (especially in the Americas), and repeated emergency fundraising. Political support did not automatically produce military independence. In November 1941 the Chinese Military Affairs Commission imposed the Nine Rules Governing the Operations of the Korean Liberation Army, placing crucial aspects of its activities under Chinese military supervision. Korean ministers and legislators objected that the arrangement undercut the very claim to sovereignty the army was meant to embody. Their protests led to sustained negotiations during 1943–1944. The Nine Rules were cancelled in August 1944: a Chinese communication of 28 August recorded that the decision had been notified to Kim Ku and the army commander on 23 August. On 8 September Chiang Kai-shek ordered the army to be placed under the Provisional Government. Formal command authority thereby changed, although Chinese logistical and financial leverage remained strong. The parties subsequently concluded a replacement aid arrangement in April 1945. The rules therefore did not remain operative through the surrender.

The entry of the United States into the war following the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 gave the Provisional Government an opening. In December 1941, it formally declared war on Japan, and it sought recognition and aid from the Allies, a campaign that proved largely unsuccessful (§63.19). It also adopted a political program, the Fundamental Principles of National Reconstruction (Geonguk Gangnyeong), in November 1941, drawing on Jo So-ang's Three Equalities doctrine (§63.17).

The most significant political development was the broadening of the government to include the Korean left. In 1942, representatives of the Korean National Revolutionary Party and the Korean National Front League entered the provisional assembly, ending a period in which the government had been dominated by Kim Ku's party and its allies. A constitutional revision in 1944 reorganized the executive, with Kim Ku as chairman and Kim Gyu-sik, a veteran nationalist associated with the left-leaning coalition, as vice-chairman, and Kim Won-bong was appointed minister of military affairs. The 1944 government was thus a coalition of the right and the moderate left, in which the left held significant positions, though not control.

Institutional broadening should not be mistaken for the resolution of all political disagreements. The 1944 governmental reorganization gave left-nationalist figures greater participation, and some leaders who had previously opposed Kim Ku entered the administration. Yet the exile government's membership was necessarily limited: people inside occupied Korea could not freely elect representatives, and communist networks around Yan'an and the Soviet border retained separate loyalties. Its legitimacy was constitutional and historical rather than demonstrably electoral. That distinction became crucial when the wartime government sought international recognition and planned to transfer its institutions to Korea at the end of Japanese rule.

The coalition should be assessed with care. It represented real cooperation, motivated by the common enemy, the Chinese Nationalist government's interest in a unified Korean front, and the recognition that the Allies were unlikely to support a divided movement. But the underlying rivalries persisted: Kim Ku's circle and the leftists differed about social policy, about the role of the Chinese Communist Party, and about relations with the Soviet Union. The Yan'an-based Korean Independence League, with its own army, was not part of the Chongqing government at all. The Chongqing government could claim to represent a wide range of exile opinion, but it could not claim to represent all of it, and it did not control the movements in Yan'an, in the Soviet Union, or in the United States. Section 63.12

The Korean Liberation Army, 1940–1945

The Korean Liberation Army (Hanguk Gwangbokgun) was formally inaugurated at Chongqing on 17 September 1940, with a founding ceremony attended by Korean and Chinese representatives. The event realized a long-standing aim of Kim Ku and the Provisional Government: to create an armed institution explicitly associated with Korean sovereignty and to give Korean exiles an organized place in the Allied struggle. Yi Cheong-cheon, who had military experience in Manchuria, became commander in chief; Yi Beom-seok served in senior command and subsequently led its Second Detachment. Kim Ku exercised political leadership as head of the Provisional Government. The army's establishment was a diplomatic act as well as a military one, but the ceremony did not mean that a fully equipped field army already existed. In its first months the force was composed chiefly of a small headquarters cadre, and building combat units, transport, training systems, and reliable recruitment remained difficult.

Historical Vocabulary
Gwangbok (光復)

Definition: A Korean term meaning "restoration of light," used for the recovery of national sovereignty. It appears in the names of exile organizations, notably the Korean Liberation Army (Hanguk Gwangbokgun), and in the Korean name for the anniversary of 15 August 1945 (Gwangbokjeol).

Historical context: The term suggests restoring a state that had existed before it was lost, in contrast to the creation of an entirely new state. It reflects the conception of many exiles that the Provisional Government and its army were continuing a Korean state rather than founding a new one.

Why it matters: The terminology expresses a claim of continuity and legitimacy. Different organizations used different words, and North Korea would later employ a different vocabulary of liberation (Haebang, "liberation") for the same date, reflecting contrasting historical narratives.

Potential misunderstanding: "Liberation Army" in English can suggest a large, independent army that liberated Korea. The Korean Liberation Army was small, operated under Chinese supervision, and did not liberate Korea.

Organization developed unevenly. The headquarters moved toward Xi'an soon after the September 1940 founding, reflecting the military value of that western Chinese city and its proximity to fronts and possible Korean recruits. It returned to Chongqing in October 1942 under Chinese military direction; field detachments continued operating in other cities, including Xi'an. The distinction between headquarters and field units prevents contradictory accounts of the army's location. Detachments sought volunteers among Koreans living in Chinese-controlled territory and among Koreans escaping Japanese military service. Students and former conscripts could provide invaluable information about Japanese unit organization, but reaching Allied-held territory could require dangerous travel across controlled areas. Recruitment, training, and provisioning were constrained by the small exile population under Nationalist protection and by inflation and wartime disruption. Published totals for the army's strength vary by date and by whether headquarters staff, trainees, associated units, and active personnel are counted together; no single unsourced total should be treated as definitive.

A headquarters without logistical capacity could not immediately field large formations. The army needed instructors, barracks, medical supplies, political officers, translators, and routes for bringing recruits safely through contested territory. Military training was only one part of the work: propaganda aimed at Korean soldiers in Japanese service, screening of possible recruits, translation of enemy documents, and intelligence collection had practical value even when sustained combat operations were out of reach. The Chinese authorities wanted activities that served Chinese war objectives, whereas Korean ministers wanted public recognition of a national military institution. The resulting negotiations are evidence of Korean political agency, not merely proof of Chinese control: the exiles repeatedly pressed for command rights and eventually obtained the rescission of restrictive rules.

In 1942 the portion of the Korean Volunteer Corps that remained aligned with Kim Won-bong was incorporated into the Korean Liberation Army as its First Detachment, while other former volunteers belonged to the distinct Yan'an-oriented movement. Kim Won-bong became deputy commander, giving the Provisional Government's military structure a broader nationalist and left-nationalist character. Chinese oversight nevertheless constrained the army. The Nine Rules imposed in November 1941 subordinated important operational decisions to the Chinese Military Affairs Commission, which also controlled supplies and permissions to move through war zones. After Korean protests and negotiations, the rules were rescinded in August 1944; the Chinese decision was conveyed on 23 August and documented in a 28 August communication. A further instruction on 8 September 1944 confirmed the army's attachment to the Provisional Government. The April 1945 aid agreement addressed continuing cooperation and Chinese assistance. None of these steps gave the small army unlimited operational freedom or independence from Chinese military conditions.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of the general content of these documents, not a quotation.

The founding statements announce the establishment of a Korean army to fight Japanese imperialism in cooperation with China and other countries opposed to Japan, and they present its purpose as the recovery of Korean independence and the restoration of the nation. They address both Korean and international audiences, calling on Koreans to join and appealing to the Allies for recognition of the army as a legitimate belligerent force. The Chinese regulations, by contrast, set out terms under which the army would operate within Chinese military supervision, including provisions for Chinese guidance, supply, and control of its activities within China.

Operationally, the army's contributions were limited but real. Cause and Effect

How Wartime Alliances Shaped Organizational Power among Korean Exiles

Long-term causes

Korea's loss of sovereignty left activists without territory, taxes, or a standing army, so every exile organization depended on foreign hosts for money, safe haven, and weapons. Earlier factional divisions meant patrons could choose among rival groups.

Short-term causes

The Japanese attack on China in 1937 and on the United States in 1941 turned Korean exiles from nuisances into potentially useful auxiliaries for Chinese, Soviet, British, and American authorities.

Immediate context and event

Chinese Nationalists, Chinese Communists, the Soviet military, and the Allied intelligence services each offered resources to Korean groups on terms that matched their own priorities, as in the Nine Rules of 1941.

Immediate effects

Organizations aligned with particular patrons gained resources and survived, and others declined. The Chongqing army was bound to Nationalist authority, the Yan'an army to Communist authority, and Soviet-connected guerrillas to Soviet command.

Long-term consequences: By 1945 Korean organizations carried the structures, loyalties, and rivalries of their sponsors, which complicated efforts to form a single government once the war ended.

Historical complexity: The alliances were not simple impositions. Korean leaders chose their patrons, bargained with them, and sometimes switched. But the structure of dependence meant that organizational power derived at least as much from external sponsorship as from popular support in Korea itself, which exile groups could not measure.

Section 63.13 · Division IV — The Soviet World and the Korean American Independence Movement

Koreans in the Russian Far East, the Soviet Union, and Central Asia

Koreans had settled in the Russian Maritime region from the 1860s, soon after Russia acquired the territory, and by the early twentieth century the region held a substantial Korean population. Some became Russian subjects and converted to Orthodox Christianity; others remained foreign residents and migrant laborers. The community included farmers, laborers on railways and in mines and fisheries, merchants, and exiles. In Vladivostok, the quarter known as Sinhanchon (New Korean Village) became a center of Korean political and cultural life, with newspapers, schools, and associations. After annexation in 1910, it received new exiles, and the Russian Far East became one of the principal bases of the early independence movement.

The Revolution of 1917 and the civil war that followed opened new possibilities. Korean activists in the region organized socialist groups, such as the Korean Socialist Party founded in 1918 in Khabarovsk by Yi Dong-hwi and others, and thousands of Koreans joined Red partisan units against the Whites and Japanese interventionists. Korean communists divided into factions tied to different Bolshevik authorities: the Shanghai faction associated with Yi Dong-hwi and the Irkutsk faction, among others. The Comintern, the Bolshevik-led international organization, tried to mediate and to unify Korean communists, but the rivalries persisted. They contributed directly to the violence of the Free City Incident (§63.7) and they frustrated efforts to build a single Korean Communist Party, which was formed in Seoul in 1925 (Chapter 59) and dissolved by the Comintern in 1928 for factionalism.

Soviet policy toward Koreans in the 1920s and early 1930s was mixed. Korean-language schools, newspapers, and cultural institutions were permitted and promoted within the framework of Soviet nationality policy, and Koreans in the Far East created kolkhozes (collective farms), many of which grew rice. Korean communist cadres worked in party and administrative institutions. But Soviet authorities also restricted Korean political independence, discouraged the armed Korean units that had formed in the civil war, and treated Korean revolutionary organizations as subordinate to the Soviet and Comintern leadership. Soviet interest in Korean revolutionaries was driven by Soviet foreign policy, including its relations with Japan, not by a commitment to Korean independence for its own sake.

In the 1930s, the position of Koreans in the Soviet Far East deteriorated as the Soviet Union came to see the Far Eastern border as a security risk after the Japanese conquest of Manchuria. Soviet security officials suspected Koreans, who had family and cultural links across the frontier and a recent history of association with both Japan and China, of serving as Japanese spies. In August 1937, the Soviet government decreed the removal of the Korean population from the border districts of the Far East. Between September and November 1937, approximately 172,000 Koreans were deported by rail, mostly in freight cars, to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan in Central Asia. They were given little time to prepare, were forced to leave property and livestock, and were resettled in unfamiliar and often harsh environments, where many initially lived in improvised shelters. Mortality in the first years was high from disease, cold, and hunger; estimates of deaths vary and are disputed, and students should treat any single figure with caution.

The deportation was an act of state repression directed at an entire ethnic group. It was not a military assistance program or merely a disruption of revolutionary organizing. It occurred at the height of the Great Terror, when Soviet security organs also arrested many Korean political, cultural, and educational leaders, including Korean communists, on charges of espionage, and many were shot. The Korean-language institutions of the Far East were closed, and the Korean-language press was relocated and later reduced. The independence veterans among the deportees included Hong Beom-do, the Manchurian commander, who lived in Kazakhstan until his death in 1943. A community that had supplied revolutionary cadres and partisan fighters was thus subjected to forced relocation by the same state that had supported some of its political causes.

The deportees and their descendants developed a distinct identity, known as Koryo-saram, in Central Asia, rebuilding agricultural communities, schools, and cultural life in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. Over time many became prominent in Soviet agriculture and professions. Their history shows that the Soviet connection with Korean independence cannot be reduced to military assistance or to the story of a few guerrilla commanders. For most Soviet Koreans, it was an experience of settlement, political suspicion, and eventually forced migration, and their history also lies outside the story of independence in the narrow sense. Some Soviet Koreans would later play roles in the Soviet occupation of northern Korea, a development belonging to Chapter 65.

Section 63.14

Kim Il Sung: Guerrilla Warfare, Soviet Exile, and the Making of a Political Leader

No figure in this chapter has been more shaped by later state narrative than Kim Il Sung, who became the founder and leader of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (Chapter 77). The task here is to reconstruct his wartime career, so far as sources permit, independently of the official biography that the North Korean state later produced and made a foundation of its legitimacy. It is necessary to do this carefully because the documented record is substantial enough to show that he was a real anti-Japanese guerrilla and incomplete enough to leave many details uncertain.

He was born Kim Seong-ju in April 1912, near Pyongyang, into a family that, like many others in the northwestern provinces, emigrated to Manchuria while he was a child. He attended Chinese schools in Manchuria, including a middle school in Jilin, where he became involved in leftist circles. He was arrested briefly in the late 1920s for political activity and, in the early 1930s, joined the Chinese Communist Party and its guerrilla movement, reflecting the Comintern policy that Korean communists in China should join the Chinese party. He adopted the name Kim Il Sung during the 1930s. His guerrilla service took place in the eastern Manchurian borderlands, where he rose to become a commander of a unit in the Chinese Communist-led forces. He was caught up in the Minsaengdan purges (§63.8) and, according to accounts, was suspected and briefly detained before being cleared.

His best-known operation was the Pochonbo raid of 4 June 1937. A guerrilla force crossed the Amnok River into the northern Korean county of Pochonbo, attacked the police substation and other government offices, and withdrew. The raid caused limited damage and few casualties, but it was reported in the Korean-language press in the colony, which made it known to Koreans in the peninsula and gave Kim a reputation inside Korea. Japanese authorities responded with a large campaign against the guerrillas, backed by Korean and Manchukuo auxiliaries. In the following years, the Japanese counterinsurgency drove the guerrillas to the point of near destruction, and in about 1940 Kim retreated across the border into Soviet territory, as did other surviving commanders.

In Soviet territory, Kim and the other Korean and Chinese guerrillas were assembled, interrogated, and trained. In 1942 the Soviet command organized them into the 88th Separate Rifle Brigade, based near Khabarovsk, which included Chinese and Korean officers and men, with Zhou Baozhong as its senior Chinese commander. Kim served in the brigade as a captain and battalion commander and was part of a group of Korean officers who would later return to Korea. The brigade did not fight against Japan in significant operations before the end of the war, though it conducted reconnaissance and training. This was a Soviet military unit: Kim's participation in it was service within a Soviet command, not independent Korean command, a distinction that applies to the other Korean military organizations in this chapter.

Historical Debate

Kim Il Sung's Documented Anti-Japanese Activities and the Construction of Later Political Mythology

The question: How much of Kim Il Sung's wartime reputation reflects documented activity, and how much was built later by the North Korean state? Historians agree that he was a real guerrilla commander but disagree about the scale and independence of his activities and about the use of sources.

Interpretation A — A modest but genuine record, inflated afterward: Many historians outside North Korea conclude that Kim was one of several Korean commanders in Chinese Communist-led forces, that the Pochonbo raid was a small action whose importance was magnified, and that the later official biography expanded his role. They point to Japanese police records, Chinese Communist materials, Soviet files, and contemporary press reports, which together show a junior or mid-level Korean commander serving within Chinese-led and then Soviet-led structures.
Interpretation B — Defenders of the official narrative: North Korean state histories describe Kim as the independent leader of a Korean revolutionary army, with the guerrilla campaign as the origin of the state. These claims are made in an official context that rules out dissent, and their sources are largely official publications and memoirs produced under state supervision. Some of the memoir material, including the multivolume reminiscences published under his name, includes details that independent evidence does not corroborate and some that it contradicts.

Evidence: Relevant evidence includes contemporary Japanese police and military reports on the guerrillas, Chinese Communist Party documents, Soviet records of the 88th Brigade, Korean newspaper coverage of the 1937 raid, and later memoirs. Soviet-era records and most scholars place the birth of his son Kim Jong Il in 1941 at a Soviet camp near Khabarovsk, whereas the North Korean official account places it in 1942 on Mt. Paektu, which is one visible example of divergence between records and the official narrative.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale and interpretation. Few serious scholars deny that he fought Japanese forces. The issues are how large his unit was, whether he held independent command, how much his activities mattered strategically, and how much of the official account can be credited. A separate, now largely discredited story that the postwar leader was an impostor who had borrowed the name of an earlier legendary guerrilla has also circulated, and historians generally reject it, though it reminds students how rumor can enter a record already clouded by propaganda.

Why it matters: The North Korean state treats the guerrilla struggle as the founding event of the nation and the basis of the leader's right to rule. How historians assess the record therefore bears on how the legitimacy claims examined in Chapter 77 can be understood.

Student task: List the types of evidence for Kim Il Sung's wartime career and rank them by likely reliability, explaining the purposes that produced each. Then explain which claims in the debate can be considered established and which remain uncertain.

Kim Il Sung's wartime experience gave him several things: a record of anti-Japanese fighting that, even when its scale is discounted, distinguished him from Koreans who had collaborated or stayed passive; a network of comrades from the Manchurian guerrilla and Soviet brigade experience; and a relationship with Soviet military and political authorities. These were assets in 1945, when the Soviet army entered northern Korea. They did not make him the sole leader of the Korean communist movement, nor did they make Korean communists a single bloc. Other communists, including Korean veterans of the Chinese Communist forces in Yan'an and domestic communists who had stayed in Korea, had comparable or stronger claims, and the competition among them belongs to Chapters 65 and 77.

Section 63.15

Korean Communities in Hawaii and the Continental United States

The first organized group of Korean emigrants to the United States arrived in Hawaii in January 1903, recruited to work on sugar plantations, and over the next two years roughly 7,000 Koreans, most of them men, followed, until the Korean government, under Japanese pressure, ended emigration in 1905. Many had been Christians, and some had been soldiers, officials, or students. They worked long days in the cane fields for low wages, lived in plantation camps, and sent money to families at home. After 1910, a smaller group of Korean women, known as picture brides, came to Hawaii and the mainland to marry men whom they knew only from photographs, and families began to form. On the American mainland, the Korean community was smaller and more varied, including students, farm laborers in California, small business owners, and a few professionals.

The emigrants' legal and economic position helps explain the significance of their political organizing. Workers on plantation contracts lived under employers' rules and often faced deductions, company stores, and limited mobility, while mainland Koreans confronted exclusionary immigration laws and racial restrictions on landholding and naturalization. Few could rely on citizenship or electoral influence to press the Korean case. Nevertheless, a congregation, cooperative, newspaper office, or branch association could serve as a stable institution. Reading rooms preserved news from Korea and China; public meetings allowed organizers to explain appeals from Shanghai; and dues collected over many weeks created a predictable income stream. This was infrastructure for a national cause, built by people who rarely held formal office in the independence movement.

Churches were the central institutions. Korean Methodist and other Protestant congregations provided worship, social support, language teaching, and political organization, and ministers often became community leaders. Schools taught Korean to children of emigrants and sometimes trained young people in nationalist history. In Hawaii, Syngman Rhee established the Korean Christian Institute in 1918. Newspapers, including the Sinhan Minbo in San Francisco and Taepyongyang Jubo (Korean Pacific Weekly) in Honolulu, carried news from the peninsula, advocated independence, and publicized fundraising campaigns. Women formed associations of their own, such as the Korean Women's Relief Society and the Korean Women's Patriotic Society, which raised funds, supported relief efforts, and organized cultural and educational activities.

The Korean National Association, founded in 1909, coordinated communities across the Americas. It collected dues, maintained local branches, mediated community disputes, and issued certificates of identity at a time when Koreans' legal status was uncertain. Its membership and funding made it the principal financial backbone of overseas independence activity. Its contributions went to the Provisional Government in Shanghai, to Korean military training efforts, and to diplomatic and publicity work. Emigrants contributed regularly, even though their wages were low, and mobilization at times took the form of subscriptions, special collections, and the sale of independence bonds. Estimates of the total sums raised vary and are not firmly established, but historians agree that the contributions were large relative to the emigrants' incomes and that they sustained work that could not have been funded otherwise.

Women's organizing deserves distinct attention rather than being treated as an auxiliary to male-led associations. The Korean Ladies' Relief Society in Hawaii was organized in the wake of the March First Movement in 1919; women raised funds by selling food and organizing community events, promoted education and relief, and helped circulate independence publications. Scholarship on Korean picture brides shows that some women used these organizations to claim a public role as citizens of a nation that did not yet possess a sovereign state. Such activity could be constrained by family responsibilities, employment, church hierarchies, and community disputes, but women's associations also possessed officers, agendas, fundraising methods, and political objectives of their own. They were not merely spouses delivering their husbands' donations.

Money traveled through practical networks rather than abstractions. Dues and benefit proceeds could be counted at local meetings, remitted to a central association, converted to usable funds, and carried or transferred to representatives in Shanghai, Chongqing, or Washington. As organizations competed, donors sometimes questioned whether their subscriptions should support military training, diplomacy, schooling, or refugee relief. Korean-language newspapers publicized appeals and contested financial accountability. The movement's transnational reach thus depended on trust, bookkeeping, correspondence, and repeated voluntary contributions as much as on prominent leaders' speeches. Researchers reconstruct these patterns by combining association minutes, church records, newspaper advertisements, remittance reports, and family testimony, while remembering that published appeals do not prove the amount actually collected.

The communities were not unified. Disputes over leadership, religion, regional origin, and the use of funds recurred, and they became entangled with the rivalries among Rhee, Ahn Chang-ho, and Pak Yong-man (§63.16). They also had to contend with limited political power: Koreans in the United States were largely excluded from citizenship by naturalization law and from political influence, and American authorities treated Japan as a legitimate colonial power in Korea. In 1941, the main groups in Hawaii and on the mainland formed the United Korean Committee of America (Jaemi Hanjok Yeonhap Wiwonhoe) to coordinate their efforts, a step that reflected both the pressures of war and long-term efforts at unity.

Generation and language introduced further changes. Children raised in Hawaii and California experienced American schooling and local discrimination while inheriting memories of a country many had never visited. Community schools taught Korean language, history, and patriotism; families debated how to equip young people for life in the United States without abandoning the prospect of independence. After the United States entered the Pacific War, Korean organizers had greater opportunities to demonstrate opposition to Japan, but they also had to distinguish their community publicly from Japanese subjects at a moment of intense anti-Asian suspicion. The 1941 efforts at coordination should be understood against this longer struggle to reconcile local belonging, political vulnerability, and commitment to Korea. [Documentary reference]

Everyday Life and Social History

Supporting Independence on a Plantation Wage

A Korean laborer in Hawaii in the early twentieth century typically earned less than a dollar a day for a long working day in the cane fields, and he lived in plantation housing that the employers controlled. Out of such wages, he might pay church contributions, membership dues to the Korean National Association, a share of the cost of a Korean-language school for the community's children, and special levies for independence campaigns, while also sending money home. A family might budget for a newspaper subscription and for contributions solicited at gatherings on Korean national days. Some contributed regularly over decades.

Women's work was essential. Picture brides, who had come to marry men they had never met, often worked in the fields or took in laundry and boarders, raised children, and ran households, and they also joined women's associations that sponsored fundraising events, collected small donations, and supported schools. The associations gave women a public role and a role in nationalist politics that they would not have had under the Confucian social order in the homeland, and their organizing is an example of how social history and political history intersect. Evidence for this everyday world comes from community newspapers, church and association records, immigration documents, and oral histories gathered from descendants, and students should note that many of these sources record organization leaders more fully than ordinary members.

These small contributions, replicated across a community of several thousand, sustained an independence effort that no wealthy patron funded. They also carried costs: the emigrants' own economic advancement was delayed, community disputes over funds could be bitter, and the commitment was often passed to children who had never seen Korea.

Section 63.16

Syngman Rhee, Ahn Chang-ho, Pak Yong-man, and American Diplomacy

Three leaders dominated the early Korean American independence movement, and their differing strategies illustrate the movement's range. Syngman Rhee (Yi Seung-man), born in 1875, had studied at a mission school, been imprisoned in the 1890s for reformist activity, and gone to the United States in 1904, where he earned a doctorate at Princeton in 1910. His approach emphasized diplomacy and appeals to American and international opinion, and he believed that the Western powers, especially the United States, could be persuaded to support Korean independence. His mandate proposal of 1919 and his long residence in the United States reflect this orientation.

Ahn Chang-ho, born in 1878, was a reformer who had founded the Sinminhoe in Korea and organized the Korean community in California. His emphasis was on organization and education: he argued that independence required a capable and morally prepared citizenry and that the movement should concentrate on building institutions. He founded the Young Korean Academy, spent time in Shanghai as a leading figure in the early Provisional Government, and was arrested by Japanese authorities in 1932 after the Hongkou Park bombing and imprisoned in Korea. Released in 1935 and arrested again in 1937, he died in March 1938 after his health collapsed in custody.

Pak Yong-man, born in 1881, a journalist who had studied in the United States, argued that independence required armed force and organized military training. In 1909 he helped to establish a school for Korean youths in Hastings, Nebraska, and in 1914 he organized a military training corps in Hawaii. He later lived in northern China, where he advocated armed struggle and clashed with Rhee and others; he was assassinated in Beijing in 1928. His division with Rhee in Hawaii in the 1910s was bitter, and it split the Korean community there into rival camps, with disputes over funds, leadership, and policy.

The rivalry among these men was partly personal, partly strategic. Rhee's critics accused him of authoritarianism and of using funds for his own purposes, and of preferring diplomacy that produced no results. His defenders pointed to his persistence and to his access to American officials. Ahn's emphasis on organization and Pak's emphasis on military preparation each addressed what they saw as weaknesses in the other strategies, and historians generally regard these disagreements as reflecting real strategic debates rather than simple personal feuds, though both elements were present.

Diplomacy occupied much of Rhee's career. In 1919, an organization called the Korean Commission was established in Washington to lobby on behalf of the independence cause, supported by contributions from the Korean community and led by Rhee and others. Its activities included publicizing Korean grievances, petitioning officials and the legislature, and working with American churches and sympathetic organizations. In 1933, Rhee traveled to Geneva to present the Korean case to the League of Nations, which was then considering Japan's conquest of Manchuria. The appeal did not change League policy. In 1939 and after, Rhee revived the Commission as the Provisional Government's representative in Washington and lobbied the State Department and Congress for recognition. He published a book on Japanese expansionism in 1941, shortly before the United States entered the war, and he used the war to argue that the Korean cause served American interests.

The results were limited. The American government consistently declined to recognize the Provisional Government. The State Department doubted that it controlled anything in Korea, regarded the Korean exile movement as factional and divided, worried about possible Soviet influence, and was reluctant to commit the United States to a position before the postwar settlement was clear. These concerns were not baseless, since Korean exile politics were indeed divided, and Rhee's rivals in the movement undermined his claim to speak for all Koreans by writing to American officials themselves. But the American refusal also reflected a more general reluctance to challenge Japan's colonial rule before the war and a postwar vision in which Korea's future was to be settled by the Allies, possibly through a period of trusteeship, rather than by Korean exiles. Korean Americans did contribute more concretely to the war effort, including volunteers who served in the U.S. military and, in 1945, Korean Americans trained for possible missions in Korea under American intelligence programs (§63.18).

The overseas diplomatic effort thus produced publicity, advocacy, and a network of American sympathizers, but not recognition. Its significance in 1945 lay in what it gave Rhee: an American audience, a record of persistent advocacy, and a claim to represent a Korean government, which would be consequential when the United States occupied southern Korea (Chapter 65). It did not give him the authority to speak for all Koreans, and the existence of rivals in China, in the Soviet Union, and in Korea itself ensured that his claim would be contested.

Section 63.17 · Division V — Allied Victory, Competing Legitimacies, and the Approach of Liberation

Competing Visions of an Independent Korean Republic

Although all independence activists wanted Korea to be free, they imagined different Koreas. By the war years, the debates of earlier decades had matured into competing programs for the structure of an independent state, and these programs mattered because the movements that held them expected to take part in building it. The visions did not divide simply into left and right, and within each tradition there were moderates and radicals, but several clusters can be identified.

The programs differed on concrete policy, not just rhetorical labels. An exile favoring constitutional republicanism might prioritize a national assembly, civil liberties, and secure diplomatic recognition. Advocates of Jo So-ang's Three Equalities wanted political equality joined to economic and educational equality, confronting the possibility that ending colonial rule would leave concentrated property and unequal access to schooling intact. Communist-oriented movements were more likely to emphasize land redistribution, workers' and peasants' organizations, and a party-led revolutionary order, although their exact proposals varied by place and year. These distinctions mattered because landholding, policing, official collaboration, and the position of former Japanese-controlled industries would become urgent issues once the colony ended.

Republican nationalism, the tradition of the early Provisional Government, envisioned a democratic republic with civil liberties, universal suffrage, public education, and a national assembly. Its supporters included Christian reformers, former officials, intellectuals, and emigrants in the Americas. Within it, views on economic questions ranged from support for private property and moderate reform to openness to substantial land redistribution. Kim Ku's own program emphasized national unity, anticommunism in the sense of rejecting dictatorship by any party, a strong cultural nationalism, and a democratic government in which Koreans would decide their institutions. He distrusted foreign tutelage and, like many leaders, wished the Allies to recognize Korean sovereignty at once.

Jo So-ang's Three Equalities (Samgyun) doctrine provided the Provisional Government's most developed social program. It proposed equality among individuals, among nations, and in the distribution of wealth, which in practice meant political and civil equality, support for national self-determination, and a mixed economy under which land and major industries would be subject to state control or distribution, to prevent concentration of wealth. It was embodied in the Fundamental Principles of National Reconstruction of 1941, which committed the government to land reform, nationalization of key industries, and universal compulsory education. The doctrine aimed to place the movement between laissez-faire capitalism and Marxist revolution, and it provided a basis for cooperation between the republicans and the moderate left.

The left held several visions. Kim Won-bong's Korean National Revolutionary Party and the National Front League advocated a democratic republic with social reforms, including land redistribution and workers' rights, with a strong role for popular organizations, and they were willing to cooperate with the Nationalist government in China as a wartime necessity. The Korean Independence League in northern China, under Kim Tu-bong, called for a democratic republic with broad popular participation, and its leaders worked within the Chinese Communist framework while using the language of a national and democratic revolution rather than an immediate socialist one. Korean communists in the Soviet Union and Manchuria, including Kim Il Sung's circle, looked to Soviet and Chinese models, and communists who had remained in Korea had their own organization and priorities. These communists were not a single group and had histories of mutual hostility.

Multiple Perspectives

Envisioning an Independent Korea, c. 1944–1945

From the perspective of republican nationalists in the Chongqing government: Independence should restore Korean sovereignty under a constitutional republic, built on the continuity of the Provisional Government. They hoped for Allied recognition of that government as the legitimate authority and feared that foreign powers would impose a settlement. They could draw on the symbolism of March First and on years of survival in exile, but they depended on Chinese support and lacked a base in Korea.
From the perspective of Korean communists and left-nationalists connected with the Chinese Communist movement: Independence should coincide with social transformation, including land reform and a government of broad popular participation, and exile governments that excluded mass organizations and worked with conservative elites seemed inadequate. They benefited from the organizational strength and military experience of their Chinese allies, but they too operated under the authority of their hosts and had little knowledge of conditions inside Korea after years away.
From the perspective of Korean American activists and emigrants: Independence should secure the rights and dignity of Koreans, and recognition by the United States and its allies was the most practical route. Their long experience of organizing in a democratic society inclined many toward liberal republican institutions, and their financial contributions made them stakeholders. But they lived far from Korea, had limited access to American power, and were divided among themselves by the rivalries of their leaders.
From the perspective of Koreans inside the colony: Millions of Koreans living under Japanese rule, including the farmers, workers, and conscripted laborers whose wartime experience Chapter 62 describes, had little voice in these exile debates and limited information about them. Their expectations of independence were shaped by local conditions, and the exiles' claims to represent them were untested by any election.

The programs shared more than the debates suggest. Nearly all rejected monarchy, favored some land reform, endorsed universal education, and wanted a state that would end the privileges of the colonial order, and in the 1944 government left and right agreed on a common platform. Attempts at cooperation included the 1942 and 1944 broadening of the Provisional Government and talks between Chongqing leaders and representatives of the northern movement. But important matters remained unresolved: the extent of land redistribution and compensation, the role of the state in the economy, the treatment of Koreans who had collaborated with Japan, the relation to the Soviet Union and China, the place of the Communist Party, and above all the method by which a Korean government would obtain authority, whether through the Provisional Government's claim of continuity, through elections inside Korea, or through arrangements with the Allied powers. These unresolved issues would structure the political competition of 1945 and afterward, as Chapters 64 and 65 show.

Section 63.18

Korean Independence Fighters and Allied Wartime Operations

As the war turned against Japan, Korean organizations sought ways to contribute directly to the Allied effort, in part because participation might strengthen their claim to recognition. Their opportunities were constrained by Allied policies and by Allied doubts about Korean capacities. The history of these efforts requires careful separation of what was proposed from what was carried out.

In China, Korean units cooperated with Chinese forces of both kinds. The Korean Liberation Army served within the Nationalist military structure, carrying out intelligence, propaganda, and training roles, and the Volunteer Army in the north cooperated with Communist forces and fought in several local engagements. These roles were useful to their hosts, but they did not constitute independent operations against Japan. After 1943, escaped Korean conscripts joining the Liberation Army brought direct knowledge of Japanese military organization and increased interest in Korean recruits.

A small but well-documented Korean mission operated with British forces in the India–Burma theater. A group of nine Korean Liberation Army members under Han Ji-seong was dispatched in late August 1943. Its members underwent language, broadcasting, and intelligence training in India and subsequently worked near the India–Burma front, including the Imphal sector. Tasks included loudspeaker and leaflet propaganda, translating captured documents, interrogating prisoners, and reporting on Japanese forces. Some served during the Allied advance through Burma. A National Institute of Korean History document collection preserves a 1945 report on the team's work; modern scholarship reconstructs its assignments from Korean, Chinese, and British records. Its activities constitute real wartime service, not proof that the Korean Liberation Army as a whole fought a major independent campaign. The distinction matters equally in the opposite direction: small size does not make intelligence, translation, and psychological warfare historically nonexistent.

American contact with Korean groups was shaped by intelligence interests. The Office of Strategic Services (OSS), the wartime intelligence agency, was interested in using Koreans for operations against Japan and for information about the colony. In China, OSS officers discussed cooperation with the Provisional Government and the Liberation Army, though American commanders in China were cautious about commitments that might imply recognition, and Chinese Nationalist authorities were reluctant to lose control of Korean units. In 1945 a joint program, known as the Eagle Project, began training groups of Korean Liberation Army members at Xi'an for infiltration into Korea, with the aim of gathering intelligence and organizing resistance. Another OSS program, the Napko Project, planned to train Korean Americans and others for operations in Korea, with training in the United States, but it did not reach operational use. Korean Americans also served in the U.S. armed forces and in intelligence and propaganda roles.

The negotiations over joint operations reveal the distinction between contact, authorization, training, and deployment. OSS officers could regard Korean speakers as useful for clandestine work without committing Washington to recognize the Provisional Government as Korea's sovereign authority. Korean leaders, meanwhile, saw potential participation in an operation inside Korea as a way to improve their standing at the eventual peace settlement. Military planning therefore carried diplomatic expectations that the American side did not necessarily share. Recruitment and preparation required verification of identities, evaluation of Korean-language ability, selection of safe routes, equipment, communications training, and coordination with field commands. Progress in one part of this chain did not mean that an insertion mission had been approved or executed.

Proposals for operations inside Korea existed, but none had been carried out when Japan surrendered. The Eagle Project's trainees finished their course in the summer of 1945, and plans to send them into Korea were in preparation when the war ended; the Napko program was still developing. Kim Ku, who had traveled to Xi'an and met with American intelligence representatives in early August, reportedly agreed on joint plans. Soviet and Chinese Communist planning for operations in Korea involving Korean units also existed in outline. The speed of Japan's collapse after the atomic bombings and the Soviet entry into the war in August 1945 overtook all of them. A small mission that tried to reach Seoul days after the surrender is discussed in Chapter 64. Students should note that the independence movement's military contribution was thus largely preparatory, and that the repeated, accurate observation that no Korean exile army liberated Korea is compatible with an equally accurate observation that many Koreans fought and suffered for the cause.

The Eagle Project developed in China through OSS cooperation with the Liberation Army's Second Detachment near Xi'an. By the summer of 1945 Koreans were receiving training for clandestine intelligence work and possible contact with networks in the peninsula. The related but separate Napko Project involved prospective Korean personnel trained through American channels; it must not be treated as the same unit or a mission already operating inside Korea. In early August Korean and American officers discussed future deployments, yet events overtook their plans. Japan's surrender meant that the trainees could not demonstrate a Korean-led armed entry into the peninsula before the war ended. This distinction is not a judgment on their commitment: it differentiates a prepared capacity from an operation completed, exactly as historians must do when reading operational plans and later memoirs.

The larger map of cooperation also includes Korean units aligned with Chinese Communist forces and guerrillas associated with the Soviet military. Those forces did not belong to Kim Ku's chain of command, and claims about their operational achievements must be evaluated from their own records. The Soviet 88th Brigade, which included Korean veterans, was based in Soviet territory; its existence does not mean that Kim Il Sung personally commanded the Red Army's August invasion of Korea. Likewise, the Korean Volunteer Army's northern-China work does not establish that it entered the peninsula as the Provisional Government's force. All these histories became raw material for rival postwar claims, making exact names and institutional relationships a substantive issue rather than a terminological nicety. [Documentary reference]

Section 63.19

The Cairo Declaration, International Recognition, and the Uncertain Future of Korea

The Allied powers' public statement on Korea came late in the war. From 1942, the Provisional Government and Rhee's Commission pressed the American, British, and Chinese governments for recognition, and in the same period American planners began to consider Korea's postwar status. President Franklin Roosevelt, among others, doubted that Koreans were ready for immediate self-government after decades of colonial rule and suggested that a period of international trusteeship might be needed. This idea, discussed in conversations with British and later Soviet leaders, became a major source of anxiety for Korean leaders, who saw trusteeship as a continuation of foreign control. Chiang Kai-shek, by contrast, favored a clear commitment to Korean independence.

A useful way to read Allied planning is to separate a public promise from confidential policy proposals. The Cairo communiqué announced that Korea would eventually be free, but it did not designate a Korean government or set a timetable. American interdepartmental memoranda in 1944 considered forms of interim administration and the problem of who, if anyone, could represent Koreans. Some plans envisaged international trusteeship, reflecting assumptions about Korean political preparedness that Korean independence advocates firmly rejected. These were policy discussions, not a single settled plan shared without disagreement by every Allied government. Reading the draft memoranda alongside public declarations enables students to see both a commitment to ending Japanese rule and the uncertainty over how the end would be managed.

At the Cairo Conference of late November 1943, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Chiang discussed war aims, and the communiqué, made public on 1 December 1943, included a statement on Korea. It said that the three powers, mindful of the enslavement of the Korean people, were determined that "in due course Korea shall become free and independent." The declaration was the first public Allied commitment on Korean independence, and it carried real weight. But the qualifying phrase "in due course" left the timing unspecified, and Korean leaders feared that it signified delay, conditions, or the trusteeship the Americans had been discussing.

The declaration did not lead to recognition of the Provisional Government. None of the major Allied governments formally recognized it as the government of Korea, and the reasons are instructive. Allied governments doubted that the government controlled territory or commanded the support of Koreans inside the colony, and they were aware of its divisions. They wished to avoid commitments that might constrain postwar arrangements, including their relations with one another and with the Soviet Union, which had not joined the declaration. The Chinese Nationalist government, the most sympathetic, supported the government materially but did not grant formal recognition either, and Chinese officials had their own views about the future of the peninsula. The Soviet Union, for its part, pursued its own relationships with Korean communists. Potsdam in July 1945 reaffirmed the Cairo terms, and the Soviet Union adhered to the Potsdam Declaration when it entered the war on 8 August.

Chinese policy illustrates the problem particularly well. Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government hosted Korean exiles, financed portions of their administration and army, and supported the general objective of independence. Yet its foreign ministry and military commission distinguished such support from formal diplomatic recognition of a sovereign Korean government. The Chinese state also had to consider its relationships with Britain, the United States, and the Soviet Union, as well as its own interests in the future of Northeast Asia. Korean ministers could therefore obtain subsidies, audiences, military arrangements, and sympathetic statements without acquiring recognition of their authority over the peninsula. American records make a similar distinction between cooperating with Korean personnel and accepting a claim that one exile organization represented all Koreans.

The approaching end of the war introduced an additional problem: the organizations most visible to the Allies were often the ones whose papers and representatives could circulate abroad, not necessarily those with the widest domestic following. Japanese policing made ordinary political expression inside Korea difficult to measure. Meanwhile, domestic activists, religious and labor networks, and people with no connection to foreign exiles retained their own ideas of independence. As a result, a political claim based on decades of sacrifice was powerful but not equivalent to an election or effective governing authority. The chapter should close with that distinction rather than converting the multiplicity of exile movements into a foregone account of the two postwar states.

By the summer of 1945, therefore, Korean leaders in exile expected liberation but not the manner of it. The Chongqing government prepared to return and to claim the authority of a continuous republic, the northern movement prepared to move toward Manchuria and Korea with Communist forces, Soviet-connected Koreans waited in the Soviet Far East, and Rhee lobbied in Washington. Kim Ku, who learned of Japan's surrender while in China, later wrote in his memoir that the news brought dismay rather than joy, since it ended the war before Korean forces could take part and left Korea's standing in the postwar settlement uncertain; the memoir is a retrospective source, and students should read it as such. The American and Soviet armies, meanwhile, were preparing their own plans for the Korean peninsula, which were made without consulting any Korean exile organization. Continuity and Change Over Time

Independence Strategy from 1919 to 1945

What Changed

  • The expectation in 1919 that peace-conference diplomacy and the example of March First might secure independence gave way to emphasis on armed struggle in 1920, then to direct action in the 1920s and early 1930s, and finally to participation in the Allied war effort.
  • The geography of the movement shifted from Manchuria and the Russian Far East as principal armed bases toward wartime China, with Chongqing and Yan'an as centers.
  • The ideological balance moved from a predominantly liberal republican and Christian leadership toward a broader spectrum that included socialists and communists, culminating in the coalition of 1944.
  • Patron states changed: Soviet and Comintern influence in the early 1920s, Chinese Nationalist support after 1932, and Allied intelligence cooperation by 1945.

What Stayed the Same

  • The movement depended on foreign hosts and patrons throughout.
  • Factionalism over leadership, strategy, and ideology recurred in every phase.
  • Overseas Korean communities supplied funds and personnel across the whole period.
  • Japanese surveillance and pressure followed the movement across borders.
  • No exile organization held territory in Korea, and none could claim a mandate tested by election.

Timing and experience of change: Change was uneven and often forced by outside events, such as the Japanese conquest of Manchuria in 1931 and 1932, the war in China from 1937, and the Pacific War from 1941, rather than chosen by the movement. Activists in Manchuria experienced the collapse of armed resistance most directly, while those in the United States experienced greater continuity in their methods of fundraising and lobbying. Why the pattern matters: The continuities, especially dependence and division, help explain why the movement entered 1945 with plural claims to legitimacy.

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Korean independence was sustained, after 1910, by a transnational network of republican nationalists, socialists, communists, military organizations, religious communities, emigrant workers, students, and exiles. Annexation and colonial repression closed the space for open politics inside Korea, and activists moved along routes that earlier migration had opened: into Manchuria, the Russian Far East, China proper, Hawaii, and the American mainland. Their shared opposition to Japanese rule did not produce agreement about leadership, ideology, military strategy, international alliances, or the shape of a future Korean state.

The Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea, organized in Shanghai in April 1919 and consolidated in September of that year, gave the movement a republican constitution, a set of institutions, and a claim to continuity with the Korean state. It never held territory or secured recognition from a major power, and it was repeatedly weakened by quarrels over strategy, funds, and leadership, most visibly at the failed National Representatives Convention of 1923 and in the impeachment of Syngman Rhee in 1925. Direct action, pursued by Kim Won-bong's Uiyeoldan and Kim Ku's Korean Patriotic Corps as distinct organizations, produced dramatic attacks in 1932 that raised Chinese Nationalist support for Korean exiles at the cost of dispersing the government from Shanghai.

Manchuria was the principal armed theater. Large Korean farming communities in the borderland, living under uncertain Chinese sovereignty, supplied recruits and supplies to independence armies, and the battles of Bongodong and Cheongsanri in 1920 were genuine tactical successes whose casualty figures remain disputed. Japanese reprisals against Korean villages caused heavy civilian suffering and destroyed the settlements that sustained resistance. The Free City Incident of June 1921, shaped by disputes among Korean armed groups and Soviet military priorities, shattered an important coalition of fighters when the Sakhalin Volunteer Army was attacked and disarmed. After 1931, Japanese control of Manchuria made nationalist armies largely untenable, and the most durable guerrilla force was the Chinese Communist-led Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army, in which Koreans served under mainly Chinese leadership and suffered the Minsaengdan purges.

In wartime China, several Korean movements coexisted. The Provisional Government moved inland to Chongqing in 1940, established the Korean Liberation Army under Chinese supervision, and in 1942 and 1944 broadened to include the left. The Korean Volunteer Corps, formed in 1938, divided: its smaller part joined the Liberation Army, while most members moved to Communist-held northern China and became associated with the Korean Independence League and the Korean Volunteer Army. These organizations must be kept distinct. All of them operated within Chinese military and political structures and could not act as independent Korean commands.

The Soviet world and the United States presented contrasting environments. Koreans in the Russian Far East participated in the revolution and civil war but were subjected, in 1937, to the deportation of approximately 172,000 people to Central Asia, an act of state repression. Kim Il Sung's anti-Japanese guerrilla career was real, but its scale and independence were magnified by later state narrative, and his service in the Soviet 88th Brigade was within a Soviet command. Korean communities in Hawaii and California, organized through churches, schools, newspapers, women's associations, and the Korean National Association, supplied money and organization that sustained the movement, even as the rivalries among Rhee, Ahn Chang-ho, and Pak Yong-man divided them.

Competing visions of an independent republic ranged from liberal republicanism to Jo So-ang's Three Equalities to socialist and communist programs. They shared more than their disputes suggested, including rejection of monarchy and support for land reform and education, but they left unresolved the extent of economic redistribution, the treatment of collaborators, relations with China and the Soviet Union, and the question of who would establish a government. Korean participation in Allied operations, from the nine Liberation Army members in India and Burma to the OSS Eagle and Napko projects, was small and, in the case of the planned missions into Korea, unexecuted when the war ended.

The Cairo Declaration of 1 December 1943 made Korean independence an official Allied aim but qualified it with "in due course," and no major Allied government formally recognized the Provisional Government. In August 1945, Korean leaders in Chongqing, Yan'an, the Soviet Far East, and Washington each held claims to legitimacy grounded in different kinds of evidence: constitutional continuity, military service, organizational reach, wartime experience, or diplomatic advocacy. None held universally recognized authority to govern.

The chapter has also emphasized two distinctions. Korean political agency and Allied military victory were both consequential but were not identical processes, so that no single organization or leader independently secured liberation. And the plurality of the movement must be preserved: not every communist was Kim Il Sung, not every nationalist was Kim Ku, and the Provisional Government was not an internationally recognized sovereign state. The outcome of 1945 was not inevitable. It followed from the conditions of dependence, division, and Allied policy that this chapter has traced, and Chapters 64 through 66 explain how those conditions interacted with the American and Soviet occupations.

Key Terms

Provisional government
A body claiming governing authority until a regular government can be established; the Korean exile government claimed to hold this role for a Korean republic.
Legitimacy
The recognized right to govern, resting on claims such as popular consent, continuity, effective control, or foreign recognition; contested among Korean exile groups in 1945.
Diaspora
Communities of a people living outside their homeland, here Korean communities in Manchuria, Russia, China, Hawaii, and the mainland United States.
Jiandao (Gando)
Borderland districts north of the Duman River in Manchuria with a large Korean farming population and ambiguous status before 1909.
Uiyeoldan
The Heroic Corps, a direct-action organization founded in 1919 by Kim Won-bong, distinct from the Korean Patriotic Corps.
Korean Patriotic Corps
Kim Ku's direct-action organization of 1931, responsible for the 1932 Tokyo and Shanghai attacks.
Direct action
Targeted violence against officials and symbols of colonial power, intended to damage the enemy and demonstrate continuing resistance.
Mandate petition
Rhee's 1919 proposal that Korea be placed under League of Nations oversight as a step toward independence, criticized by militants.
National Representatives Convention
The 1923 Shanghai meeting that tried and failed to unify the exile movement, dividing reformers from creationists.
Bongodong and Cheongsanri
Engagements of June and October 1920 between Korean independence forces and Japanese troops in the Manchurian borderland.
Gyeongsin Massacre
Japanese reprisals against Korean civilians in Jiandao, autumn 1920 to 1921; the number of victims is disputed.
Free City Incident
The June 1921 disarmament of the Sakhalin Volunteer Army near Svobodny by Red Army forces acting for the Irkutsk-aligned Korean Revolutionary Military Council; a major rupture in Korean armed resistance.
Mitsuya Agreement
1925 arrangement between Japanese police authorities in Korea and Manchurian warlord authorities to suppress Korean armed activists.
Minsaengdan purge
Chinese Communist purges of Korean members suspected of ties to a Japanese-sponsored organization, in the early 1930s; many accusations were later judged unfounded.
Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army
Chinese Communist-led guerrilla coalition in Manchuria in which many Koreans served.
United front
A nominal alliance between rival political forces against a common enemy, such as the Chinese Nationalist-Communist cooperation after 1937.
Korean National Revolutionary Party
A left-nationalist coalition formed in 1935 under Kim Won-bong.
Korean Volunteer Corps
Armed organization founded at Hankou in 1938 that divided between Chongqing and northern China.
Korean Volunteer Army
Armed wing of the Korean Independence League in Communist-held northern China, distinct from the Liberation Army.
Korean Independence League
Organization founded in northern China in 1942 under Kim Tu-bong, aligned with the Chinese Communist movement.
Korean Liberation Army
The Provisional Government's army, founded 17 September 1940 at Chongqing under Chinese supervision.
Nine Rules
Chinese military guidelines of 1941 placing the Liberation Army under Chinese oversight.
Three Equalities (Samgyun)
Jo So-ang's doctrine of equality among persons, nations, and in distribution of wealth.
Gwangbok
"Restoration of light," a Korean term for recovery of national sovereignty.
Koryo-saram
Descendants of Koreans from the Russian Far East, especially those deported to Central Asia in 1937.
88th Separate Rifle Brigade
Soviet unit formed in 1942 from Chinese and Korean guerrillas, in which Kim Il Sung served as a captain.
Pochonbo raid
Small guerrilla attack of June 1937 on a police post in northern Korea, later magnified in North Korean narrative.
Korean National Association
Principal organization of Koreans in the Americas, formed in 1909, a major financial backer of the movement.
Korean Commission
Diplomatic office in Washington headed by Rhee, representing the Provisional Government to American officials.
Picture bride
A woman who emigrated to marry a man she knew only through photographs, important in forming Korean families in Hawaii and the mainland.
OSS
The U.S. Office of Strategic Services, wartime intelligence agency that planned operations with Koreans (Eagle and Napko projects).
Cairo Declaration
Allied statement of 1 December 1943 promising Korean independence "in due course."
Trusteeship
Proposed temporary international supervision of a territory before independence, feared by Korean leaders as continued foreign control.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the end of Japanese rule was met by a divided and dispersed set of Korean claims rather than by a single prepared government. The independence movement's endurance, across decades and continents, is a historical achievement in its own right. The movement's divisions, which the chapter has traced to dependence on foreign patrons, factional rivalry, and the absence of territory, are equally part of the record, and neither should be allowed to obscure the other.

The chapter also reveals how colonial and wartime geography shaped political outcomes. Korean organizations took on the structures and loyalties of the places where they operated: Nationalist China, Communist northern China, the Soviet Union, and the United States. Those loyalties gave them resources and survival, but they made unified action harder and gave outside powers influence over Korean politics. Participation in a foreign command and independent command are different things, and recognizing the difference protects historians from inflating any organization's role.

Later developments drew directly on this inheritance. The leaders who returned to the peninsula in 1945 and afterward carried the claims examined here, and the competition among them unfolded under the American and Soviet occupations, as the chapters that follow show. The debates over land reform, collaboration, and political representation that remained unresolved in 1945 became central issues of liberation. In North and South Korea alike, later states would select from this history, emphasizing some movements and neglecting others, and the memory of exile and guerrilla struggle would become a source of legitimacy for rival regimes.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter extends the argument made at its beginning: that Korean history is the history of a peninsula-centered civilization in continuous interaction with its neighbors, including its communities abroad. The independence movement was an expression of that interaction. It also prepares the question that Chapter 64 takes up, which is how liberation arrived and who stood ready to claim it.
