Yi Seong-gye and the Founding of Joseon

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the seventh lunar month of 1392, a general who had spent his career defending Goryeo's northern frontier and, more recently, dismantling Goryeo's government from within, accepted the throne his own allies had arranged for him. The Wang dynasty, which had ruled the peninsula since 918, was over. But the general who now sat on the throne, Yi Seong-gye, did not yet possess a settled dynasty. He possessed an army, a coalition of reform-minded officials, a half-built claim to legitimacy, and a capital city, Gaegyeong, saturated with the memory of the rulers he had just replaced. Everything else, the new dynasty's name, its capital, its ruling ideology, and even the question of who among Yi Seong-gye's own sons would eventually inherit it, remained to be settled, often violently, over the following eight years.

Chapter 24 explained how Goryeo's fall became possible: factional exhaustion, the catastrophic 1388 Liaodong campaign and Yi Seong-gye's withdrawal from Wihwado, the political maneuvering that produced a succession of contested and constrained Wang monarchs, the murder of the loyalist official Jeong Mong-ju, and the final removal of King Gongyang in 1392. That chapter answered a narrow but essential question: why did a dynasty of nearly four hundred seventy-five years collapse when it did, and how did one general and his allies acquire the power to replace it? This chapter takes up a different and in some ways harder question. Military and political victory in 1392 did not, by itself, create a stable or legitimate dynasty. A new ruling house needed a name, a capital, an ideological justification, an international position, a reorganized landholding elite, and, not least, a settled answer to the question of succession. None of these things existed automatically simply because Yi Seong-gye now occupied the throne.

The founders of Joseon were acutely aware of this problem. Jeong Do-jeon, the reformist scholar-official who had done more than almost anyone else to design the intellectual and institutional case for replacing Goryeo, understood that a dynasty founded on force alone would remain vulnerable to being overthrown by force in turn. He and his colleagues therefore worked to construct a positive case for the new dynasty's legitimacy: a case resting on claims about virtuous rule, the failures of the last Goryeo kings, ritual and cosmological correctness, and acknowledgment from the Ming court in China, whose approval mattered enormously within the diplomatic order of fourteenth-century East Asia. At the same time, the founders reorganized rights to land taxation and reshaped who held office, since a new political order needed a material foundation as much as an ideological one. And they built, from comparatively modest beginnings, an entirely new capital at Hanyang, on the site of the modern city of Seoul, whose walls, palaces, and street plan were meant to announce, in stone and timber, that a new dynasty now governed the peninsula.

Yet the founding coalition itself was neither unified nor stable. Jeong Do-jeon envisioned a monarchy substantially guided by a strong body of Confucian scholar-officials, a vision that limited the personal authority of the royal house even as it exalted the dynasty's ideological mission. Yi Seong-gye's own son, Yi Bang-won, who had played an indispensable and often ruthless role in bringing the new dynasty into being, held a very different view of where power ought to reside. These competing visions collided in 1398, when Yi Bang-won led an armed strike against Jeong Do-jeon and the princes favored for succession, an episode Korean historians call the First Strife of Princes. A second succession crisis followed in 1400. By the time Yi Bang-won ascended the throne as King Taejong that same year, the founding generation of Joseon had been transformed by two rounds of court violence, the deaths of some of the dynasty's own architects, and the emergence of a monarch determined to concentrate authority more directly in the throne itself.

This chapter traces that eight-year process, from Yi Seong-gye's accession in 1392 to Taejong's accession in 1400, as the true founding of Joseon: not a single triumphant moment but an extended and often violent negotiation over what kind of state the new dynasty would actually be. It examines the arguments the founders made for their own legitimacy, the diplomatic work of seeking Ming acknowledgment and securing approval of the dynastic name, the reorganization of land-tax rights that gave the new regime's officialdom a material base, the decision to move the capital to Hanyang and the meaning embedded in that new city's design, and the personal and ideological struggles that determined who would exercise power once Goryeo was gone. Readers should notice throughout how much continuity survived the change of dynasties alongside the genuine transformations under way, since many officials, families, administrative practices, and even portions of the old landholding order carried over from Goryeo into the new Joseon state.

By the chapter's end, Joseon will have survived its founding crises, but its mature governing institutions, the State Council, the Six Ministries, the censorial offices, the examination system, and the fully articulated Confucian political order, remain the subject of Chapter 26. This chapter is concerned with something that had to come first: converting a military and political victory into a dynasty capable of enduring.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Yi Seong-gye and his allies moved from controlling the Goryeo court to formally founding a new dynasty in 1392.
  2. Analyze the arguments the founders of Joseon used to justify replacing a dynasty that had ruled for nearly four hundred seventy-five years, including Confucian ideas about legitimate rule.
  3. Describe the diplomatic process by which the new dynasty secured the name "Joseon" and sought recognition from Ming China.
  4. Identify Jeong Do-jeon's role as political theorist and institutional architect of the new dynasty, and distinguish his vision from Yi Bang-won's.
  5. Trace the consequences of the 1391 Gwajeon land law for elite formation and political power in the new dynasty.
  6. Evaluate the degree of continuity and change between the Goryeo elite and the officials who served early Joseon.
  7. Explain the strategic, symbolic, and geomantic reasoning behind moving the capital to Hanyang in 1394.
  8. Describe how the construction of Hanyang, including Gyeongbokgung and the city's walls and gates, expressed the new dynasty's political legitimacy.
  9. Analyze the causes and consequences of the First Strife of Princes in 1398 and the Second Strife of Princes in 1400.
  10. Assess why Taejo's abdication and Jeongjong's brief reign demonstrate the instability of Joseon's first years.
  11. Distinguish the founding-and-legitimation problems addressed in this chapter from the mature institutional structures examined in Chapter 26.
  12. Contextualize the founding of Joseon within the broader pattern of dynastic transition in Northeast Asian history.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What separates a successful seizure of power from a legitimate founding of a new dynasty, and how did Yi Seong-gye and his allies try to close that gap?
  2. Why did the new dynasty's founders consider recognition from Ming China essential to their own legitimacy at home?
  3. How did land reform function simultaneously as an economic policy and a tool of political consolidation?
  4. Why might a new dynasty choose to keep many officials, administrative practices, and even landholding arrangements from the regime it replaced?
  5. What did the founders hope to communicate by building an entirely new capital rather than remaining in Gaegyeong?
  6. Why did the ideological vision of a Confucian bureaucratic monarchy come into direct conflict with the ambitions of the dynasty's own princes?
  7. What do the First and Second Strife of Princes reveal about the unresolved questions left over from the founding coalition of 1392?
  8. Why should the founding of Joseon be understood as a process extending to 1400 rather than as a single event in 1392?

Historical Context

Chapter 24 traced the final collapse of Goryeo: the disastrous 1388 order to invade Ming-controlled Liaodong, Yi Seong-gye's decision to turn his army back at Wihwado rather than proceed with the campaign, the political purge that followed, the installation and removal of a rapid succession of Goryeo kings, the killing of the loyalist scholar-official Jeong Mong-ju in 1392, and the final removal of King Gongyang later that year. By the time Gongyang was deposed, Yi Seong-gye held overwhelming military power, controlled the capital, and led a coalition of reform-minded officials who had already spent several years dismantling the political and economic foundations of the old aristocratic order, most consequentially through the 1391 Gwajeon land law.

What Yi Seong-gye did not yet possess in the summer of 1392 was a dynasty in the fuller sense: a name, an accepted place within the East Asian diplomatic order, a capital city untainted by association with the ruling house he had just displaced, a stable resolution of who would succeed him, and a governing philosophy capable of holding together the coalition of soldiers, reformist officials, and remaining aristocratic families who now had to work within a new political order. Fourteenth-century Northeast Asia offered both dangers and opportunities for a newly founded state. The Ming dynasty, established in China in 1368 after the collapse of the Mongol Yuan, was still consolidating its own authority and remained deeply attentive to the political behavior of its neighbors, including whether a new government on the Korean peninsula would present itself as legitimate, cooperative, and properly deferential within the tributary framework that structured much of East Asian diplomacy.

Within the peninsula, decades of Mongol-era political interference, aristocratic landholding abuse, military factionalism, and repeated foreign incursion, examined across Chapters 22 through 24, had left many officials receptive to the argument that Goryeo's ruling house had lost the capacity, and therefore in Confucian political terms the right, to govern. This argument, developed most fully by Jeong Do-jeon and like-minded reformers, provided the intellectual scaffolding for dynastic replacement, but it also committed the new regime to demonstrating, through its own conduct, that it represented genuinely better government rather than merely a change of ruling personnel.

Section 25.1

From General to Founder, 1392

By the summer of 1392, Yi Seong-gye no longer needed to govern through a constrained and contested Wang monarch. King Gongyang, the last ruler of the Wang dynasty, had reigned since 1389 as a figure installed with Yi Seong-gye's coalition's support and dependent on it for continued rule, though his court retained real capacity for resistance, as Jeong Mong-ju's own political counteroffensive earlier in 1392 had shown. His removal in July 1392 nonetheless attracted comparatively little organized resistance once that counteroffensive had failed. The killing of Jeong Mong-ju earlier that year, discussed in Chapter 24, had eliminated the most capable and respected defender of continued Wang rule, and with him much of the remaining will among Goryeo's officialdom to resist a change of dynasty. When a group of officials, led by Yi Seong-gye's closest allies, formally petitioned Yi Seong-gye to accept the throne, he accepted, in a ceremony designed to echo established East Asian patterns of dynastic transition rather than to announce an obviously unprecedented seizure of power.

This chapter does not repeat the detailed narrative of Gongyang's removal or the mechanics of the 1392 transition, since Chapter 24 has already established that sequence. What matters here is the situation Yi Seong-gye faced the moment he accepted the throne. He possessed military authority built over decades of frontier command and recent domestic political maneuvering. He possessed a coalition of reform-minded officials, most prominently Jeong Do-jeon, who had already spent several years attacking the landholding privileges and political power of Goryeo's entrenched aristocratic families. He possessed, through the 1391 Gwajeon Law, a partially reorganized system of rank-based land-tax rights that had begun to supply the officials now serving the state with a distinct economic foundation. What he did not yet possess was a dynasty with its own name, its own capital, an accepted place in the regional diplomatic order, or a resolved succession. The eight years that followed were devoted to supplying these missing elements, often at considerable political and personal cost.

Section 25.2

Why Found a New Dynasty?

The decision to replace the Wang dynasty outright, rather than simply continuing to dominate the Goryeo court through a compliant king, was not an inevitable outcome of the political crisis of the late 1380s. As late as 1388, when Yi Seong-gye turned his army back from Wihwado, his immediate objective had been to remove a specific set of political rivals and reverse a specific military policy, not necessarily to end the dynasty itself. Many of Yi Seong-gye's own allies initially expected that reform could proceed within the existing dynastic framework, with the Wang house continuing to reign under new political conditions.

The argument for outright dynastic replacement developed gradually and was pressed most forcefully by Jeong Do-jeon and a circle of like-minded officials who concluded that Goryeo's problems were not merely the product of a few corrupt individuals but were embedded in the dynasty's own institutional structure and, in Confucian political terms, in a loss of the moral basis for continued rule. Continuing to install and depose Wang kings as circumstances required, this argument held, would leave the underlying problems of aristocratic land monopolization, weak central administration, and repeated foreign entanglement unaddressed. A new dynasty, built from its founding on Neo-Confucian principles of virtuous, meritocratic government, offered a chance to address these problems structurally rather than through repeated palace interventions. This case for structural, dynasty-level reform, rather than personnel-level adjustment, supplied the intellectual momentum that carried Yi Seong-gye's coalition from domination of the Goryeo court to outright dynastic founding.

Section 25.3

The Problem of Legitimacy

Military force alone could install Yi Seong-gye on the throne, but force alone could not make his rule, or his successors' rule, secure over the long term. Officials, local elites, and ordinary subjects throughout the peninsula had lived under the Wang dynasty for nearly four hundred seventy-five years; a new ruling house needed a persuasive case for why replacing that dynasty was not simply an act of usurpation but a legitimate and, in Confucian terms, morally justified transition.

Confucian political philosophy, as it had developed across East Asia, offered conceptual resources for exactly this problem. Legitimate rule, in this tradition, rested not merely on hereditary succession but on the ruler's capacity to govern virtuously and to secure the welfare of the population. A ruling house that persistently failed in this responsibility, through corruption, military failure, administrative decay, or moral impropriety, could be understood to have forfeited its claim to continued rule, opening the door to a new, more capable house. Jeong Do-jeon and the reform coalition constructed their case for Joseon's founding substantially along these lines, cataloguing the failures of the last Goryeo kings, the abuses of aristocratic landholding, and the dynasty's repeated vulnerability to Mongol, Japanese pirate, and internal military pressure as evidence that continued Wang rule no longer served the welfare of the state or its people.

This argument required care in its presentation. A case built too explicitly around individual criticism of specific Wang kings risked appearing merely personal and vindictive rather than principled. The reform coalition instead emphasized broader institutional failure and framed the transition as a restoration of proper governance rather than a naked transfer of power, drawing on ritual and historical precedent, including patterns of dynastic transition recognized elsewhere in East Asian political history, to present Yi Seong-gye's accession as consistent with established norms rather than as an unprecedented rupture. Historians today generally treat this legitimating narrative as a genuine and historically significant political argument in its own right, while also recognizing that it served the practical interests of the coalition that constructed it, a tension examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Historical Vocabulary
Dynastic Legitimacy and the Mandate Idea

Definition: In the Confucian political tradition that shaped Korean statecraft, dynastic legitimacy rested on a ruler's demonstrated capacity to govern virtuously, maintain social order, and secure the welfare of the population, rather than on hereditary succession alone.

Historical context: This idea, closely related to but historically and institutionally distinct from the Chinese concept of the Mandate of Heaven, allowed political actors to argue that a ruling house which had persistently failed to govern well had lost its claim to continued rule, thereby providing a framework within which dynastic change could be presented as principled restoration rather than mere usurpation.

Why it matters: Jeong Do-jeon and the founders of Joseon relied heavily on this framework to justify replacing the Wang dynasty, and later Joseon rulers and officials would themselves remain subject to the same standard, since the same logic that justified Goryeo's replacement could in principle be invoked against Joseon's own rulers.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that invoking this framework guaranteed automatic acceptance. The argument had to be actively constructed, defended, and reinforced through ritual, diplomacy, and administrative performance, and its persuasiveness to contemporaries cannot simply be read off from its later success.

Section 25.4

Naming the New Dynasty: Joseon

Legitimacy in fourteenth-century Northeast Asia operated not only within the peninsula but also within the broader diplomatic order centered on Ming China, which had itself only recently replaced the Mongol Yuan dynasty in 1368. A new Korean dynasty's standing depended in part on securing acknowledgment from the Ming court, both as a matter of practical diplomacy, since Ming military and political power loomed large over the region, and as a matter of ritual legitimacy within a shared East Asian framework of dynastic recognition.

Rather than unilaterally declaring a new dynastic name, Yi Seong-gye's government submitted two proposed names, "Joseon" and "Hwaryeong," to the Ming court for consideration, a step that simultaneously sought practical diplomatic approval and symbolically acknowledged Ming's position within the regional order. "Joseon" recalled the ancient state examined in Chapter 6, and the founders' own rhetoric emphasized in particular the tradition of Gija Joseon, a semi-legendary account holding that a Zhou-dynasty sage-minister had received investiture over ancient Joseon, since this tradition allowed the founders to frame their new dynasty's name as connecting Korean antiquity to a Confucian narrative already recognized within the Chinese classical tradition. The Hongwu emperor selected "Joseon" in early 1393, and the new dynasty formally adopted the name, securing an important diplomatic success and a further element of legitimating argument alongside the domestic case developed by Jeong Do-jeon and his colleagues.

This diplomatic achievement should not be mistaken for a fully settled tributary relationship. Ming's approval of the dynastic name was a distinct and lesser step than the formal investiture of a Korean king with a Ming patent and seal, an honor Ming did not extend to Taejo. Early Joseon-Ming relations in fact remained difficult throughout the 1390s, complicated by Ming suspicion regarding Joseon's dealings with Jurchen groups along the Manchurian frontier and by recurring disputes over the wording of diplomatic documents sent from Hanyang. Formal investiture of a Joseon king came only in 1401, under Taejong, after the events with which this chapter is concerned. Students should therefore understand 1393 as an important but partial diplomatic achievement, not as the moment when the new dynasty's position within the East Asian order was fully and finally settled.

This diplomatic process nonetheless illustrates a recurring theme in Korean history: legitimacy was rarely established through purely domestic argument alone but was continually negotiated within a wider East Asian framework of recognition, tribute, and ritual acknowledgment. The new dynasty's rulers understood that presenting themselves as properly deferential and cooperative within this framework served their own domestic authority as much as their international standing, even when full recognition remained a work in progress.

Section 25.5

Jeong Do-jeon and the Founding Coalition

If Yi Seong-gye supplied the military authority that made the founding of Joseon possible, Jeong Do-jeon supplied much of its intellectual architecture. A scholar-official trained in Neo-Confucian thought, Jeong Do-jeon had spent years developing a systematic critique of Goryeo's late-dynastic failures and a corresponding vision of what a properly governed Confucian state should look like. His writings addressed land policy, administrative structure, ritual propriety, and the relationship between the monarchy and the broader body of scholar-officials who, in his vision, would bear primary responsibility for the practical work of governance.

Jeong Do-jeon's political vision placed considerable emphasis on a strong, capable bureaucracy of Confucian-trained officials operating through established institutions. In his view the monarch remained the supreme moral and political authority of the state, but routine governance and major administrative powers should be exercised through capable, virtuous ministers acting on the ruler's behalf, rather than through the ruler's unassisted personal discretion. This vision reflected both Confucian political philosophy, which generally distrusted concentrated personal power divorced from properly cultivated advisors, and Jeong Do-jeon's own practical assessment that a durable state required institutions capable of functioning competently regardless of the character of any individual monarch.

This chapter does not attempt to describe the full institutional structure Jeong Do-jeon envisioned, since the State Council, the Six Ministries, the censorial offices, and the examination system that eventually implemented much of his thinking are the subject of Chapter 26. What matters here is that Jeong Do-jeon's vision of a Confucian bureaucratic monarchy, whatever its long-term institutional legacy, generated immediate political friction within the founding coalition itself, because it implied real limits on the personal authority of the royal house, including the authority of Yi Seong-gye's own sons. That friction would prove decisive in the succession crises examined later in this chapter.

Section 25.6

Land Reform and the Reconstruction of the Elite

The 1391 Gwajeon Law, enacted in the final years of Goryeo under the reform coalition that would soon found Joseon, had already begun the work of reorganizing landholding rights before the dynasty formally changed hands. Chapter 24 examined the circumstances of the law's enactment and its role in weakening the entrenched aristocratic families whose accumulated landholdings had drained state revenue and undermined central authority. This chapter is concerned with a different question: how did land reform function, in the years immediately following 1392, as a tool for consolidating the new dynasty's political base?

The Gwajeon system reorganized rights to collect land taxes, known as rank land, distributing these rights according to official rank to both current and former officials, rather than allowing such rights to accumulate as effectively permanent, heritable aristocratic holdings. The formal allocation criteria were therefore rank-based rather than an explicit reward system limited to declared political supporters of the new dynasty. In its broader political effect, however, the reorganization channeled a substantial share of the economic benefits associated with landholding toward the officials who staffed and served the state after 1392, supplying the new official class as a whole with an economic foundation distinct from the landholding privileges the old aristocracy had accumulated under Goryeo. Land reform therefore operated simultaneously as fiscal policy, intended to restore state revenue that had been eroded by aristocratic land monopolization, and, in its broader consequences rather than its formal design, as a support for the new political order's officialdom.

This dual function helps explain why land reform proved so consequential for the founding of Joseon. A new dynasty could not rely indefinitely on military coercion or ideological argument alone; a stable officialdom needed a secure material foundation. By reorganizing who held meaningful economic rights over land, the Gwajeon system helped ensure that the administrative elite serving the new dynasty had a durable economic basis for its status, even though the law's rank-based allocation criteria were not themselves an explicit instrument of political reward.

Historical Vocabulary
The Gwajeon Land Law

Definition: A land law enacted in 1391 that reorganized rights to collect land taxes, distributing such rights primarily according to official rank and service to the state rather than allowing them to function as effectively permanent aristocratic holdings.

Historical context: Enacted in the final years of Goryeo by the reform coalition that would found Joseon the following year, the law responded to decades of aristocratic land accumulation that had eroded state revenue and administrative authority, examined in Chapter 24.

Why it matters: In the years after 1392, the reorganized landholding system helped bind a significant portion of the official class to the new dynasty's fortunes, giving Joseon a material foundation alongside its ideological and diplomatic claims to legitimacy.

Potential misunderstanding: The Gwajeon system did not abolish private landownership or create full state ownership of land; it reorganized specific tax-collection rights associated with office-holding. Students should avoid describing it as a sweeping nationalization of land.

Section 25.7

Continuity and Change among the Elite

It would be a mistake to imagine that the founding of Joseon replaced Goryeo's ruling elite wholesale with an entirely new social class. The historical record instead reveals a more complicated pattern of continuity and change. Many families who had held office and status under Goryeo continued to do so under Joseon, particularly those who had aligned themselves, whether from conviction or pragmatism, with the reform coalition before 1392. Administrative practices, legal conventions, and even significant portions of the pre-existing bureaucratic apparatus carried over into the new dynasty, since building an entirely new administrative system from nothing would have been impractical and destabilizing.

At the same time, genuine changes occurred. Families most closely identified with resistance to the reform program, including the network associated with the executed loyalist Jeong Mong-ju, lost standing, and some officials who had served the Wang dynasty loyally to the end refused to serve the new regime at all, choosing instead withdrawal from public life as an expression of continued loyalty to Goryeo. The reorganization of tax-collection rights under the Gwajeon system further reshaped the economic basis of elite status: officials serving the new dynasty and holding recognized rank gained a secure economic foundation under the new allocation, while families whose standing had depended on older aristocratic landholdings the reorganization curtailed lost economic ground, whatever their political sympathies. The result was neither a complete social revolution nor a simple continuation of the old aristocratic order, but a selective reconstruction of the ruling elite, produced by both explicit political exclusion of prominent opponents and the broader economic consequences of land reform, while preserving substantial institutional and personnel continuity where doing so served practical governance.

Section 25.8

Moving the Capital to Hanyang

Among the most consequential decisions of Joseon's founding years was the choice to abandon Gaegyeong, Goryeo's capital for nearly the entire dynasty's existence, and establish a new capital at Hanyang, the site of the modern city of Seoul. Construction began in 1394, and the royal court relocated shortly thereafter, a remarkably rapid transition for a project of this scale.

Several considerations converged in this decision. Gaegyeong carried nearly four hundred seventy-five years of association with the Wang dynasty; its palaces, temples, and administrative buildings were saturated with the ritual and political memory of the house Joseon had just displaced. A new dynasty seeking to establish its own distinct identity had strong symbolic incentive to govern from a capital built specifically to express its own legitimacy rather than one inherited, physically and symbolically, from its predecessor. Strategic geography also favored Hanyang, situated along the Han River with access to both interior transportation routes and, via the river's course toward the Yellow Sea, maritime and riverine trade, while being reasonably central within the peninsula's populated regions.

Geomantic considerations, reflecting East Asian traditions of siting significant structures according to the perceived flow of auspicious natural energy through mountains, rivers, and terrain, also informed the selection of Hanyang, whose surrounding mountains and river were understood by court geomancers to provide a favorable configuration for a new royal capital. Historians today generally treat these geomantic arguments as historically significant expressions of contemporary cosmological belief, integral to how the founders themselves understood the legitimacy of their choice, rather than as decorative window-dressing over decisions made purely on strategic or political grounds. Strategic, symbolic, and geomantic reasoning worked together rather than as competing, mutually exclusive explanations.

Section 25.9

Building a Dynastic Capital

Construction at Hanyang proceeded with notable speed. Gyeongbokgung, the principal royal palace, was substantially completed by 1395, and the city's initial layout, including administrative offices, royal ancestral shrines, and city walls with gates oriented according to both practical defense and symbolic cardinal significance, followed close behind. This rapid pace of construction should itself be understood historically: a new dynasty anxious to demonstrate its permanence and legitimacy had strong incentive to produce visible, monumental evidence of its staying power as quickly as possible, and Hanyang's construction served this purpose as much as it served practical administrative needs.

The palace complex, royal shrines dedicated to the new dynasty's ancestors, and the city's overall plan were not merely functional infrastructure but deliberate statements of political and cosmological legitimacy. Ancestral shrines in particular carried significant ritual weight within the Confucian political order, since proper veneration of dynastic ancestors was understood as both a filial obligation and a public demonstration of the ruling house's fitness to govern. Building these structures at Hanyang, rather than continuing to rely on facilities associated with the Wang dynasty at Gaegyeong, allowed Joseon's founders to root their own dynastic ritual practice in a capital built specifically for that purpose.

Students should understand the founding of Hanyang as an act of state formation in its own right, not merely as a logistical afterthought to the political events of 1392. A capital city's walls, palaces, and ceremonial spaces functioned as a continuous, highly visible argument for the new dynasty's permanence and legitimacy, communicated to officials, foreign envoys, and the wider population alike.

Section 25.10

What Kind of Monarchy Would Joseon Be?

Even as the new dynasty secured its name, its diplomatic standing, its material base, and its capital, one fundamental question remained unresolved: what balance of power would exist between the throne and the body of Confucian scholar-officials who now staffed the new government? This question was not merely theoretical. It bore directly on how much personal authority individual members of the royal house, including Yi Seong-gye's own sons, would be able to exercise.

Jeong Do-jeon's vision, as discussed above, favored a monarchy in which the king retained supreme moral and political authority but exercised that authority substantially through capable ministers, rather than through extensive direct personal discretion over policy and appointments. This vision reflected genuine Confucian political conviction, but it also had the practical effect of empowering officials like Jeong Do-jeon himself relative to members of the royal family who might otherwise expect to wield direct political power.

Yi Bang-won, Yi Seong-gye's fifth son, held a sharply different view. Yi Bang-won had played an active and often ruthless role in the political struggles that produced the new dynasty, including involvement in the killing of Jeong Mong-ju in 1392, and he believed that princes who had personally contributed to the dynasty's founding deserved substantial political standing and, eventually, a strong claim on succession, rather than being subordinated to a ministerial government dominated by officials like Jeong Do-jeon. This disagreement was sharpened by an unresolved succession question: Yi Seong-gye had designated Yi Bang-seok, his young son by his second wife, Queen Sindeok, as heir apparent, passing over several older sons, including Yi Bang-won, from his first marriage. Jeong Do-jeon supported this arrangement, in part because a young, less politically established heir was more likely to govern within the ministerial framework Jeong Do-jeon favored.

This ideological and succession dispute was sharpened further, and given a concrete and urgent edge, by a more immediate political struggle over military command. As part of his broader program to strengthen central, ministerial control of the state, Jeong Do-jeon pressed to curtail the independent military bases and private armed retainers that several princes, including Yi Bang-won, had accumulated during the founding struggles of the preceding years, and to bring command of these forces more directly under centralized state authority. From Jeong Do-jeon's perspective, princes maintaining independent military power posed an obvious danger to a settled, ministerially guided monarchy. From Yi Bang-won's perspective, this effort threatened to strip him of the very military resources that had made his political influence possible in the first place, at the same moment his succession prospects were already being foreclosed. By 1398, these overlapping tensions, ideological disagreement over where power should reside, personal grievance over a succession arrangement that excluded an accomplished and ambitious prince, and the immediate threat of losing independent military command, had become explosive.

Section 25.11

The First Strife of Princes, 1398

In the eighth lunar month of 1398, with Jeong Do-jeon's campaign to dissolve the princes' private military forces underway and Yi Bang-won's own command increasingly at risk, Yi Bang-won led an armed strike against Jeong Do-jeon and the faction supporting the succession of Yi Bang-seok. Jeong Do-jeon was killed, along with several of his close political allies, and Yi Bang-seok and another of Queen Sindeok's sons, Yi Bang-beon, were also killed in the violence that followed. This episode, known in Korean historiography as the First Strife of Princes, eliminated in a single stroke both the new dynasty's leading institutional architect and the succession arrangement Taejo had established.

The consequences extended well beyond the immediate loss of life. Jeong Do-jeon's death removed the strongest advocate for a monarchy substantially constrained by ministerial government, shifting the balance of the founding coalition's internal debate decisively toward those, like Yi Bang-won, who favored a stronger, more personally assertive monarchy. The killing of Yi Bang-seok and Yi Bang-beon eliminated Taejo's chosen heirs, forcing an entirely new resolution of the succession question. And the violence itself, carried out by one of Taejo's own sons against the government his father had founded, exposed how fragile and personally contested the new dynasty's internal politics remained, even after the diplomatic, material, and architectural achievements chronicled earlier in this chapter.

Section 25.12

Taejo's Abdication and King Jeongjong

The violence of 1398 devastated Taejo personally as well as politically. Having lost two sons and his closest political collaborator in a single episode of court violence carried out by another of his own sons, Taejo abdicated the throne shortly afterward in favor of Yi Bang-gwa, his second son, who took the throne as King Jeongjong. Jeongjong's brief reign, lasting until 1400, should be understood not as a return to stability but as an uneasy interval during which the fundamental question the First Strife of Princes had failed to resolve, namely which of Taejo's remaining sons would ultimately hold power, remained unsettled.

Jeongjong occupied the throne, but effective political and military authority during this period rested substantially with Yi Bang-won, whose role in the events of 1398 had made him the dominant figure among the remaining princes. This arrangement could not last indefinitely, since it left the formal and practical location of power misaligned, a tension that would be resolved only through further conflict.

The instability of this period extended even to the question of where the dynasty would be governed from. In March 1399, only months after the violence of the First Strife of Princes, the court left Hanyang and returned to Gaegyeong, a decision that reflected both the continuing unsettled political atmosphere following Jeong Do-jeon's death and Taejo's abdication, and a persistent attachment among many officials to the old capital. This return should not be read as a repudiation of the reasoning behind Hanyang's founding, examined earlier in this chapter, but it does show that even the new capital, like the succession itself, remained genuinely unsettled during Joseon's founding crisis. Hanyang would not become the dynasty's permanent and uncontested seat until later, once Taejong had consolidated his authority.

Section 25.13

The Second Strife of Princes, 1400

In 1400, tension between Yi Bang-won and Yi Bang-gan, another of Taejo's sons who had his own ambitions and his own base of political and military support, erupted into open conflict, an episode known as the Second Strife of Princes. Yi Bang-won's forces defeated Yi Bang-gan's, and Yi Bang-gan was subsequently exiled rather than executed, a comparatively restrained outcome that nonetheless left no doubt about the outcome of the succession struggle. With Yi Bang-gan removed from contention, no remaining rival could seriously challenge Yi Bang-won's position.

Shortly afterward, Jeongjong abdicated in favor of Yi Bang-won, who ascended the throne as King Taejong later in 1400. The Second Strife of Princes thus completed the process the First Strife had begun: the elimination of Jeong Do-jeon's ministerial vision as the dominant model for the new monarchy, and the establishment of Yi Bang-won as the figure who would determine the practical shape of royal authority in Joseon's earliest institutionalized form.

Section 25.14

From Founding to Consolidation

By the time Yi Bang-won took the throne as King Taejong in 1400, the founding coalition of 1392 had been transformed almost beyond recognition. Taejo, the dynasty's founder, had abdicated and withdrawn from active rule. Jeong Do-jeon, the coalition's principal ideological architect, was dead, along with several of the princes whose claims to succession he had supported. Hanyang stood largely complete as a built expression of the dynasty's ambitions, its palaces and shrines finished in 1395, even though the court itself had returned to Gaegyeong in 1399 amid the instability following the First Strife of Princes and would not permanently resettle at Hanyang until Taejong had consolidated his rule. The dynasty had survived two rounds of internal armed conflict within its first eight years, but neither its succession nor even its seat of government had yet been finally settled.

Taejo had supplied the military power and initial legitimacy that made Joseon's founding possible. Jeong Do-jeon had supplied much of its early ideological program and institutional planning. Yi Bang-won emerged from the succession struggles of 1398 and 1400 with the political authority to determine what the monarchy would actually become in practice, an authority he would use, as later chapters discuss, to strengthen direct royal power relative to the more strongly ministerial vision Jeong Do-jeon had favored. The dynasty's survival through these founding crises did not resolve every question about how Joseon would be governed, but it did establish that the new ruling house could weather severe internal conflict without collapsing, a demonstration of resilience that mattered enormously for the dynasty's subsequent standing. With the succession finally settled and the founding generation's most acute conflicts behind it, Joseon's rulers and officials could turn to the more systematic work of building the institutions of a Confucian bureaucratic state, the subject to which Chapter 26 now turns.

Primary Source Window

The Veritable Records on the Founding of Joseon

Creator: compiled by the Chunchugwan, the royal office of historiography, under King Taejong, drawing on sacho and other contemporaneous records kept by court historians during Taejo's reign · Date: compilation begun in 1410 and completed in 1413 · Type: official court historical record, the founding volume of the Joseon Wangjo Sillok (Veritable Records of the Joseon Dynasty)

Context: Joseon inherited and greatly expanded a tradition of compiling detailed official chronicles for each reign, recording court proceedings, royal decisions, and significant events under strict rules intended to protect the record's independence from a currently reigning monarch's direct interference. Court historians kept contemporaneous notes, known as sacho, and other documentation during Taejo's own reign, but the surviving Taejo Sillok as a finished chronicle was compiled afterward, beginning in 1410 and completed in 1413, under the direction of King Taejong, the same Yi Bang-won whose actions in the 1398 and 1400 succession struggles the chronicle narrates. The early royal annals, including the Taejo Sillok, were subsequently subjected to partial revision under King Sejong, completed in 1442, which corrected certain accounts connected with the princes' conflicts. The surviving text therefore reflects both its original 1413 compilation and a later layer of official historiographical editing. This circumstance is itself historically significant and is addressed further below.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the kind of material such records preserve.

Entries of this kind record the formal petitions urging Yi Seong-gye to accept the throne, the deliberations over possible dynastic names submitted for Ming consideration, court discussions concerning the design and construction of the new capital, and, for 1398, an account of the conflict between princes that resulted in Jeong Do-jeon's death and Taejo's subsequent abdication.

Analysis: Records of this kind provide historians with contemporary or near-contemporary documentation of court decision-making, offering considerably more direct evidence than the later legendary traditions discussed in earlier chapters concerning Gojoseon. They allow historians to reconstruct the sequence of founding decisions with reasonable confidence.

Limitations: The Taejo Sillok was compiled roughly fifteen to twenty years after the events it describes, under the direction of the very ruler, Taejong, whose 1398 armed strike against Jeong Do-jeon and rival princes it recounts. Historians must therefore weigh the possibility that the finished chronicle's treatment of the First and Second Strifes of Princes, and of Jeong Do-jeon's political conduct in particular, was shaped by the political interests of the eventual victor, even though the underlying sacho notes were recorded closer to the events themselves. This does not mean the chronicle is unreliable, but it does mean historians must read it with attention to whose perspective ultimately shaped the finished record.

Perspective: These records reflect the perspective of the court and the dynasty's own historiographical apparatus rather than that of ordinary subjects, officials who opposed the new dynasty, or figures on the losing side of the succession conflicts.

  2. What kinds of bias might affect a record compiled some fifteen to twenty years after the events it describes, under the direction of the political victor of the succession struggle it narrates?
  3. Whose experience of the founding of Joseon would be difficult to reconstruct using court records alone?

Historical Debate

Founding, Usurpation, or Reform?

The question: Should the events of 1392 be understood primarily as a legitimate dynastic founding grounded in genuine reform conviction, as a political usurpation dressed in the language of Confucian legitimacy, or as some combination of both, and how should historians weigh the sincerity of the founders' stated motives against the practical political interests those motives happened to serve?

Interpretation A — A genuine reform founding: Some historians emphasize that Goryeo's late-dynastic problems, aristocratic land monopolization, weak central administration, and repeated external military pressure, were real and severe, and that Jeong Do-jeon's Neo-Confucian critique represented a sincere and historically serious attempt to address structural failures rather than a mere pretext for personal ambition.
Interpretation B — Legitimating language over a political seizure: Other historians emphasize that Yi Seong-gye's coalition benefited directly and substantially from the arguments it advanced, that the killing of Jeong Mong-ju removed the most capable defender of the existing dynasty, and that the elaborate legitimating apparatus, including the search for a dynastic name and geomantic justification for Hanyang, functioned in practice to clothe a military-political takeover in acceptable ideological form.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: the Veritable Records, the surviving writings of Jeong Do-jeon and other reform officials, land registers documenting the practical effects of the Gwajeon system, and the diplomatic record of Joseon's dealings with Ming China.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and emphasis rather than the basic factual sequence of events, specifically how much weight to give sincere ideological conviction relative to self-interested political calculation, categories that were not necessarily mutually exclusive for the historical actors themselves.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these interpretations shapes broader assessments of Joseon's founding ideology and, by extension, of the legitimacy claims later Joseon governments would continue to invoke throughout the dynasty's five-century history.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in this chapter, construct an argument for each interpretation and then explain whether you find one more persuasive, or whether the evidence instead supports treating conviction and self-interest as operating together rather than as competing explanations.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the Dynastic Transition

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a reform-minded scholar-official aligned with Jeong Do-jeon: The replacement of Goryeo is a hard-won opportunity to build genuinely virtuous, meritocratic government, and the reorganization of land rights and construction of a new capital are necessary steps toward realizing a properly ordered Confucian state.
From the perspective of an official who had served the Wang dynasty loyally to the end: The events of 1392 represent an unlawful seizure of a throne that had governed the peninsula for nearly four hundred seventy-five years, and continued service to the new dynasty would betray a personal and moral obligation to the deposed royal house, leading some such officials to withdraw entirely from public life.
From the perspective of a landholding family affected by the Gwajeon reorganization: Depending on whether a family's standing rested on official rank recognized under the new rank-land allocations or on older aristocratic holdings the reorganization curtailed, the new land law represents either a welcome and secure basis for continued status or a direct threat to accumulated wealth built up over generations under the old system.
From the perspective of a prince within the royal house, such as Yi Bang-won: Personal contribution to the dynasty's founding, including dangerous political action taken on its behalf, ought to be rewarded with real political standing, and a succession arrangement or governing philosophy that subordinates such contribution to ministerial authority represents an intolerable injustice.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Ruling Elite from Goryeo to Joseon

What Changed

  • The reigning royal house changed from the Wang family to the Yi family
  • Rights to collect land taxes were substantially reorganized under the Gwajeon system according to official rank, curtailing some old aristocratic holdings while giving officials serving the new dynasty a secure rank-based economic footing
  • The capital moved physically and symbolically from Gaegyeong to the newly built Hanyang
  • Some prominent families, particularly those associated with continued loyalty to the Wang dynasty, lost political standing

What Stayed the Same

  • Many officials who had served Goryeo continued to serve under the new dynasty
  • Much of the pre-existing administrative apparatus and bureaucratic practice carried forward rather than being rebuilt from nothing
  • Confucian scholarly training remained an important basis of elite status and official recruitment across the transition
  • Diplomatic engagement with continental China, now under the Ming dynasty rather than the Yuan, remained a central concern of the state

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents an oversimplified narrative in which 1392 either changed nothing of substance or swept away Goryeo society entirely; the actual pattern was a selective reconstruction that rewarded political alignment while preserving considerable institutional and personnel continuity.

Cause and Effect

The First Strife of Princes

Long-term causes

Ideological disagreement between Jeong Do-jeon's vision of ministerial government and the ambitions of princes, especially Yi Bang-won, who had contributed directly to the dynasty's founding.

Short-term causes

Taejo's designation of Yi Bang-seok, a young son by Queen Sindeok, as heir apparent, passing over older sons from his first marriage, combined with Jeong Do-jeon's support for that arrangement and his contemporaneous campaign to dissolve princes' private military forces and centralize command.

Event

Yi Bang-won's armed strike in 1398, resulting in the deaths of Jeong Do-jeon and the princes favored for succession.

Immediate effects

Taejo's abdication in favor of Jeongjong, and the removal of the strongest advocate for a strongly ministerial monarchy.

Historical complexity: The First Strife of Princes cannot be reduced to a single cause; it combined a genuine ideological dispute over the proper structure of monarchy with an intensely personal succession grievance, and separating these threads cleanly is not always possible.

Everyday Life and Social History

Building and Populating a New Capital

The construction of Hanyang was not merely an architectural or political event; it required the mobilization of substantial labor, materials, and administrative planning, and it reorganized the daily lives of thousands of people who were relocated, employed, or otherwise affected by the new capital's construction. Laborers were drawn from various regions to build the city's walls, palaces, and administrative structures, often under corvée labor obligations owed to the state, a form of service examined more fully in later chapters on Joseon society.

Merchants, artisans, and officials' households relocated to the new capital as the court itself moved, reshaping patterns of commerce and settlement in the surrounding region while leaving Gaegyeong to continue as a significant but no longer primary urban center. Families connected to the old Goryeo aristocracy who chose to remain in or near Gaegyeong, whether from continued loyalty to the Wang dynasty or from more practical considerations, experienced the founding of Joseon very differently than officials and their households who relocated to Hanyang to serve the new government directly. This uneven, geographically differentiated experience of dynastic transition is a reminder that a single political event, the founding of a new capital, touched ordinary lives in widely varying ways depending on region, occupation, and political alignment.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the founding of Joseon not as a single decisive moment in 1392 but as an extended process, lasting until 1400, through which Yi Seong-gye's military and political victory was converted into a functioning, if initially unstable, dynasty. The chapter began where Chapter 24 left off, with Yi Seong-gye's acceptance of the throne following the removal of King Gongyang, and immediately turned to the question of what remained to be accomplished: a name, a diplomatic standing, a material foundation, a capital, and a settled resolution of monarchical authority and succession.

The chapter examined why the reform coalition moved from dominating the Goryeo court to founding an entirely new dynasty, emphasizing the argument, developed most fully by Jeong Do-jeon, that Goryeo's problems were structural rather than merely personal and required dynasty-level replacement rather than continued personnel adjustment within the existing royal house. It then explored the problem of legitimacy directly, showing how the founders drew on Confucian political ideas about virtuous rule to argue that the Wang dynasty had forfeited its claim to continued governance, while recognizing that this argument served the coalition's own practical interests as well as reflecting genuine political conviction, a tension examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Diplomatic legitimacy proved equally important, though only partially achieved within this chapter's timeframe. The chapter traced the negotiation over a new dynastic name, culminating in the Ming court's 1393 selection of "Joseon" from names submitted for its consideration, a choice that connected the new dynasty to the ancient state associated with Gojoseon and, in the founders' own rhetoric, especially with the Gija Joseon tradition, while securing an important but partial diplomatic success; full Ming investiture of a Joseon king would not follow until 1401, after the events this chapter covers. The chapter then turned to the material foundations of the new regime, explaining how the 1391 Gwajeon land law, enacted before the formal change of dynasties, continued after 1392 to supply the officials now serving the state with a distinct, rank-based economic foundation rather than functioning as an explicit reward system for declared political supporters, while also showing that the resulting elite was neither entirely new nor simply a continuation of the old Goryeo aristocracy.

Jeong Do-jeon emerged as the chapter's second major figure alongside Yi Seong-gye, his vision of a Confucian bureaucratic monarchy substantially guided by ministerial deliberation providing much of the new dynasty's early ideological program while also generating friction with princes, especially Yi Bang-won, who favored a stronger, more personally assertive monarchy. The chapter then examined the decision to move the capital to Hanyang in 1394, treating this decision as an act of state formation in its own right, combining strategic, symbolic, and geomantic reasoning, and explained how the rapid construction of Gyeongbokgung and the surrounding capital expressed the new dynasty's claims to permanence and legitimacy in physical form.

The unresolved question of where ultimate authority would reside, in the throne or in a ministerial government, collided with an unresolved succession question in the First Strife of Princes of 1398, in which Yi Bang-won's armed strike killed Jeong Do-jeon and the princes favored for succession, leading to Taejo's abdication in favor of King Jeongjong. The chapter then traced Jeongjong's uneasy interim reign, during which effective authority rested with Yi Bang-won even as formal kingship rested elsewhere, and explained how the Second Strife of Princes in 1400 removed Yi Bang-won's last serious rival, Yi Bang-gan, clearing the way for Yi Bang-won's accession as King Taejong later that year.

By the chapter's conclusion, the founding generation of Joseon had been transformed by two rounds of internal armed conflict: Taejo had abdicated, Jeong Do-jeon was dead, and Yi Bang-won had emerged with the authority to shape the monarchy's future direction. The dynasty's survival through these crises demonstrated a resilience that mattered enormously for its long-term standing, even as it left open exactly how much power the throne would exercise relative to the ministerial institutions Jeong Do-jeon had helped design. That institutional structure, the State Council, the Six Ministries, the censorial offices, and the examination system, is the subject to which the textbook now turns in Chapter 26.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that founding a dynasty is rarely a single triumphant act but an extended, often precarious process of converting military and political victory into durable legitimacy. Yi Seong-gye's accession in 1392 was necessary but not sufficient; it took diplomatic negotiation with Ming China, a reorganization of landholding rights, the construction of an entirely new capital, and, ultimately, two rounds of violent succession conflict before Joseon acquired a settled monarchy capable of the institutional consolidation examined in Chapter 26.

The chapter has also illustrated a pattern that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of Joseon: the tension between ministerial and personal models of monarchy, first fought out between Jeong Do-jeon and Yi Bang-won, would continue to shape Joseon politics for centuries, as later kings, officials, and factions returned repeatedly to the same fundamental question of where ultimate authority should reside within a Confucian state.

Students should carry forward from this chapter an appreciation for the difference between seizing power and legitimating it, an understanding of how material, ideological, diplomatic, and architectural strategies worked together in the founding of Joseon, and a recognition that even a dynasty that would eventually govern the peninsula for over five centuries began its existence amid genuine uncertainty, internal violence, and unresolved questions about its own governing structure. With the founding crises resolved by 1400, the following chapter turns to the institutions, the State Council, the Six Ministries, the censorate, and the examination system, through which Joseon's rulers and officials attempted to give lasting form to the Neo-Confucian state Jeong Do-jeon had first envisioned.
