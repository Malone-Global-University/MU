Treaty Ports and the New International Order

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Within a decade of 1876, Korea gained three treaty ports, a customs service, treaties with the United States and several European states, a newspaper, a hospital and new schools. This chapter asks how Koreans managed that transformation while trying to preserve sovereignty.

Chapter 48 explained the Ganghwa Treaty. Chapter 49 explains what followed: the operation of ports, the widening treaty system, rival Qing and Japanese influence, and new institutions. Evidence includes treaty texts, consular and customs records, mission reports, newspapers and later memoirs, each shaped by its author’s position.

The chapter treats Koreans as actors: officials, merchants, brokers, students and reformers. It leaves the Imo Mutiny and Gapsin Coup to Chapter 50, the Donghak Revolution and war to Chapter 51, and the Gabo reforms to Chapter 52.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the opening of Busan, Wonsan and Incheon.
  2. Compare the ports and their commercial hinterlands.
  3. Explain the 1882 U.S. treaty and the expansion of Western treaties.
  4. Distinguish the Qing–Korean regulations from ordinary treaties.
  5. Analyze tariff sovereignty and the customs service.
  6. Assess new institutions, missions and technologies.
  7. Evaluate education and newspapers as social change.
  8. Interpret competing reform visions without teleology.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a treaty’s text differ from its implementation?
  2. Why did the ports develop differently?
  3. Why did Korea seek treaties beyond Japan, and with what limits?
  4. How did Qing policy differ from earlier tributary practice?
  5. Who gained and lost from the new commerce?
  6. Did reform strengthen the state or deepen dependency?
  7. Whose perspectives are missing from the sources?

Historical Context

Joseon entered 1876 as a Neo-Confucian monarchy with a tributary relationship to the Qing and limited, managed contact with Japan through Tsushima. Fiscal strain, factional conflict and the Daewongun’s legacy (Chapter 47) shaped the court’s choices.

Treaty ports were a nineteenth-century East Asian institution, but Korea’s version reflected its own history, including the old Busan establishment. Japan, the Qing, Russia and Western states all had different aims.

Division I — Treaty Ports and the Changing Commercial Geography

Section 49.1

Joseon after the Ganghwa Treaty

The Ganghwa Treaty and its supplements (Chapter 48) set terms, but implementation was a separate process. King Gojong, whose personal rule had begun in 1873, and the circle around Queen Min had to translate treaty text into ports, regulations and officials while many scholar-officials still regarded Japan as a lesser neighbor to be managed rather than a model. Treaty provisions did not execute themselves; Korean officials negotiated their operation, contested interpretations and sought to limit their effects.

Opening was therefore gradual and contested. This chapter follows commercial, diplomatic and institutional change from 1876 to about 1886, concentrating on how the new environment operated and leaving the Imo Mutiny and Gapsin Coup, which repeatedly interrupted it, to Chapter 50. It treats Korean officials, merchants and communities as actors and distinguishes dates of agreement, port opening and implementation, which often differed.

Several features of the Korean state shaped how opening unfolded. Most government revenue came from the land tax and related levies, much of it collected in kind, so the court had little ready cash for new bureaus, advisers or equipment. Policy was made by a small group around the king, and the influence of the queen’s kin gave court politics a strongly factional character. Korea also continued to send regular tributary missions to Beijing and to maintain the ritual forms of its Qing relationship, even as it signed treaties with Japan and, soon, Western states, so it operated simultaneously in two diplomatic systems.

The treaty itself created obligations that Korean officials had to meet. It required that additional ports be opened, and the delay before Wonsan (1880) and Incheon (1883) shows how carefully the court weighed the risks, especially for a port near the capital. Conservative scholars who objected to contact with foreign states pressed their case through memorials, and the court had to balance their criticism against pressure from Japan and advice from the Qing. Opening was therefore the result of negotiation, delay and partial implementation, rather than the straightforward execution of a text.

Section 49.2

Busan: From the Waegwan to the Modern Treaty Port

Busan was not a new place in 1876. For centuries the Japanese trading house at Choryang, the waegwan, had operated under Joseon–Tsushima arrangements: a walled compound where Tsushima merchants and officials traded under Korean supervision, with residents limited and movement outside tightly restricted. The 1876 settlement converted this inherited institution into a treaty settlement under Japanese consular authority. Japanese residents could now lease land, build warehouses and conduct trade under regulations that, at first, imposed no tariffs, and the settlement grew with merchants, shopkeepers, craftsmen and small shipping agents from Nagasaki, Osaka and elsewhere in western Japan.

Because the settlement was surrounded by Korean villages and markets, daily commerce depended on Korean intermediaries. Brokers arranged purchases of rice, beans, hides and other produce; porters, boatmen and carters moved goods between ships, warehouses and the interior; Korean officials of Dongnae, the nearby county seat, supervised the frontier of the settlement and were drawn into disputes over land, theft, debts and Japanese movement beyond the permitted radius. Japanese consular officials negotiated these questions with Dongnae authorities, which meant that local magistrates, not only the court in Seoul, handled much of the practical business of opening.

The effects spread unevenly. Coastal merchants and brokers able to supply grain to Japanese buyers benefited, while some producers and artisans met imported goods. Currency was a further complication: Japanese coin circulated in the port under the 1876 arrangements, which brought Korean commerce into contact with a foreign monetary system. Busan’s familiarity with Japanese traders made it the least abrupt of the three openings, but its history shows how an older, regulated enclave became a base for expanding Japanese commercial reach and for later steamship services. Evidence includes Japanese consular reports, Korean local administrative records and later gazetteers, all of which privilege officials and merchants over laborers.

The change in Busan’s status began before 1876. In 1872 the Japanese government had taken over the old waegwan from the Tsushima domain, replacing the older domain-based arrangement with direct national control, and the treaty of 1876 and a convention of 1877 on the settlement’s terms then fixed the framework for leasing land and managing the compound. Japanese commercial institutions followed: a bank branch and trading firms opened in the late 1870s, which connected the settlement to credit and to the Japanese monetary system.

Seen from Dongnae and nearby villages, Busan now meant a larger, more permanent and more commercial neighbor. Disputes over land, debts and boundaries recurred, and the local magistrate often had to negotiate with the consul, whose authority was supported by Japanese diplomatic and naval power. Korean brokers who could advance credit, supply goods or find buyers gained from the arrangement, while those who lacked capital or connections could be displaced. Busan thus illustrates how an old regulated enclave evolved into a platform for expanding Japanese commerce, with Korean agency visible in everyday bargaining but constrained by the legal framework.

Section 49.3

Wonsan and the Opening of the Eastern Coast, 1880

Wonsan, known to Japanese as Genzan, was opened in 1880 on the coast of historical Hamgyeong Province, a harbor on the northeastern seaboard far from Seoul and from Busan. The choice reflected Japanese interest in a second market, in northeastern trade routes and in reconnaissance of a coast that lay within reach of Russia’s Pacific frontier. A Japanese settlement was laid out with streets, land parcels and waterfront access, as the 1883 plan held by the Library of Congress shows, and consular and merchant residents arrived to trade in imported cloth and manufactured goods and to purchase hides, beans and other regional products.

Wonsan also had a Korean commercial past. Merchants and brokers in the surrounding region already handled coastal and overland trade, and many adapted quickly. In 1883 local Koreans founded Wonsan Haksa, a school that taught both Korean and foreign subjects, in response to changing commercial conditions; its existence shows that demand for new knowledge did not originate only at the royal court or among missionaries. The port’s opening also tied the northeast to larger markets and to price changes originating in Japan, effects that rural communities in the hinterland experienced without having been consulted.

Wonsan’s trade drew on the products of the northeastern coast and its hinterland, including fish and seaweed, beans and hides, and it reached markets through a mix of coastal shipping and overland routes. Japanese merchants and officials arrived after the opening, although the settlement began smaller than Busan’s and depended on shipping links to Japan for supplies and goods. The port’s remoteness from Seoul meant that regional officials and merchants played a larger role in managing foreign contact than they did at Incheon.

Wonsan’s strategic position shaped diplomatic attention. Russia’s Pacific territory lay close to the northeastern border region, and Korean and Japanese officials alike considered the coast sensitive. The opening also affected the livelihood of fishing and farming households, who were tied to new demand and to prices set elsewhere. Evidence for these local changes is limited, coming mainly from consular and official reports, so historians often infer the effects on rural communities from the broader pattern of trade.

Section 49.4

Incheon and the Maritime Gateway to Seoul, 1883

Incheon, or Jemulpo, opened in 1883 after longer delay than Wonsan, partly because of the Imo Mutiny’s disruption (Chapter 50) and partly because of court hesitation about a foreign port so near the capital. It lay within reach of Seoul by road and, for small craft, by the Han River, which made it the maritime gateway to the capital region’s markets, officials and consumers. The port’s tidal flats and shipping conditions required lighterage and local transport labor, and its early growth depended on landings, warehouses and roads built by foreign and Korean builders.

By the mid-1880s Incheon had separate Japanese, Chinese and general foreign settlement districts. Japanese merchants arrived first in number, with Chinese merchants following under the 1882 Qing–Korean regulations and Western consuls and traders establishing a small but diplomatically significant presence. This mixture made Incheon the place where commercial rivalry among Japan, the Qing and Western powers was most visible, and where Korean customs officials, brokers and carters encountered several foreign merchant communities at once. The port was also a point of entry for new technologies, including steamships and, later, telegraph and railway projects discussed in §49.19.

Incheon’s growth depended on infrastructure. Its tidal range limited the approach of larger ships and made landing by small craft necessary, so lighterage, wharves and porters were central to trade. A road connected the port to Seoul, and Han River boat merchants, known as Gyeonggang merchants, had long carried grain and goods in the capital region and now competed or cooperated with newcomers. Customs offices, consulates and warehouses made the port an administrative as well as commercial center.

Settlement rules were negotiated in stages. The Japanese settlement was arranged in 1883, and in 1884 regulations were agreed for a Chinese settlement and a general foreign settlement, so the port had separate districts for different nationalities. Residents of the districts were governed by their consuls, and Korean authorities supervised the rest of the port town. This layering made Incheon the clearest instance of how treaty law divided a single urban space among several jurisdictions, and a point where Korean, Japanese, Chinese and Western residents encountered one another daily.

Section 49.5

Ports, Shipping, Merchants, and New Commercial Networks

The three ports differed in origin, geography and function. Busan inherited a Japanese commercial presence and a link to southeastern markets; Wonsan opened a northeastern hinterland and a route toward the Russian frontier; Incheon served the capital region. Steamships, mostly Japanese at first, linked them to Nagasaki, Osaka and Shanghai and supplemented coastal sailing craft.

Korean intermediaries, brokers, boatmen and porters carried out much of the labor and organized much of the trade, while Japanese and Chinese merchants brought capital and foreign connections. Because regional experience varied widely, aggregate generalizations about the Korean economy must be treated cautiously, and the evidence, which comes largely from consular, customs and official records, privileges merchants and officials over workers.

Shipping changed the pace of trade. Steamships offered more regular schedules than sailing craft and tied the ports to Japanese and Chinese hubs, but traditional boats continued to carry goods along the coast and rivers, and the two systems coexisted. Control of shipping was a source of influence, since the companies that owned steamers shaped what was shipped and at what price. Korean owners and boatmen sought to retain a share, but they lacked the capital and organization of the foreign lines.

Labor was another dimension. Porters, carters, boatmen and warehouse workers formed a growing port workforce whose wages and conditions are rarely documented. Brokers sat between foreign merchants and producers and set terms of credit and sale, which gave them influence. Historians therefore describe the commercial networks as layered: foreign firms and shipping lines at the top, Korean brokers and merchants as intermediaries, and workers and producers whose experience varied with local conditions.

Division II — Western Treaties, Qing Influence, and International Diplomacy

Section 49.6

Gojong’s Search for Diplomatic Alternatives

Court officials looked beyond Japan for several reasons: to offset Japanese pressure, to follow Qing advice, and to gain information and technology. Li Hongzhang advocated using treaties with Western powers to check Japan and Russia, and Huang Zunxian’s memorandum, brought to Seoul in 1880, argued the same. Russian expansion on the Tumen frontier added to concern about the north.

Opposition came from conservative scholars who objected to contact with Western powers and to Christianity, and the court had to weigh these views. Diversification also had limits: Korea lacked military and financial leverage, partners pursued their own interests, and the Qing sought to keep Korea within its sphere. Gojong’s government therefore pursued a cautious strategy of adding partners while retaining its ties to the Qing.

The sequence of events in 1879–1881 shows how the policy took shape. Li Hongzhang corresponded with the retired Korean official Yi Yu-won, urging that Korea make treaties with Western states in order to check Japan and Russia, and in 1880 Kim Hong-jip, returning from Japan, brought back Huang Zunxian’s memorandum, which Gojong read and discussed with his advisers. The creation of the Tongni Gimu Amun in December 1880 (§49.16) gave the policy an institutional home.

The strategy had real attractions: it promised to dilute Japanese influence, to supply technical knowledge and to give Korea legitimacy as a state recognized by several powers. Its limits were also visible. A treaty with a distant power offered no guarantee of military help, and Qing sponsorship meant that diplomacy was shaped by Beijing’s own interests. Domestically, conservative scholars condemned the policy, as in the large 1881 memorial from Yeongnam, and the memorial leader Yi Man-son was exiled to Sinjido in 1881 after a renewed protest (Academy of Korean Studies), demonstrating both the court’s commitment to its course and the depth of opposition. Diversification was therefore a calculated attempt to gain room for maneuver, not a guarantee of independence.

Section 49.7

The United States–Korea Treaty of 1882

Korean interest in a Western treaty grew from the failure of earlier American approaches. Commodore Robert Shufeldt’s attempt in 1880 to reach Korea through Japanese intermediaries at Busan was rebuffed. The path opened through Li Hongzhang, the Qing statesman who judged that treaties with Western powers could help check Japan and Russia, and who in 1881–1882 negotiated with Shufeldt at Tianjin with the help of Ma Jianzhong. Korean officials, including Kim Hong-jip, had been persuaded by the memorandum of Huang Zunxian (§49.23) that associating with the United States offered a counterweight.

The treaty was signed at Jemulpo on 22 May 1882, for Korea by officials including Shin Heon and Kim Hong-jip. Article I contained a good-offices clause, under which each party would help the other if a third power acted unjustly or oppressively; Article IV placed American citizens in Korea under American jurisdiction; Article V set tariff ceilings of 10 percent on ordinary imports, 30 percent on luxury imports and 5 percent on exports; and Article XIV granted most-favored-nation treatment. The tariff ceilings mattered because, unlike the 1876 regulations with Japan, they envisaged Korean customs duties, while the extraterritoriality and most-favored-nation clauses limited Korean jurisdiction and spread privileges.

Qing negotiators wished to write Korea’s status as a Qing dependency into the first article. Shufeldt refused, and the compromise was a separate letter from Gojong to the American president stating that Korea was a dependency of the Qing while managing its internal and external affairs autonomously. The episode illustrates the double character of Korean diplomacy: Qing sponsorship made the treaty possible but also threatened to redefine Korea’s international position.

Formal relations began in 1883, when Lucius Foote arrived as the first American minister and Korea sent the Bobingsa mission to the United States (§49.17). Korean officials valued the good-offices article but found it difficult to invoke, and American commercial interest and government support proved modest. The most lasting results lay in the channels the treaty opened: American ministers, missionary doctors and teachers entered Korea under its framework (§§49.20–49.21). The treaty thus mattered less for trade volume than for knowledge, personnel and diplomatic contact.

Paraphrase checked against the treaty edition (not a direct quotation):

Article IV places American citizens in Korea under U.S. jurisdiction. Article V sets maximum customs duties on imports, with a lower ceiling for ordinary goods and a higher one for luxury goods, and a lower ceiling on exports, so that the Korean government may collect duties within agreed limits.

Analysis: The tariff article shows Korea gaining a recognized right to levy duties, in contrast with the 1876 Japanese arrangements, while the ceilings and the jurisdiction clause show the limits on that right. Read together the articles show how a treaty could extend sovereignty and restrict it at once.

Limitations: A treaty text records negotiated language, not how it was applied, and it cannot show internal Korean debate, Qing pressure or later enforcement.

Perspective: The text represents state negotiators, American and Korean, with Qing influence behind the scenes. Korean merchants, consumers and villagers are absent.

Section 49.8

The Expansion of Western Treaty Relations, 1883–1886

After the American treaty, Britain, Germany, Italy, Russia and France concluded treaties with Korea in 1883–1886. The texts resembled the American model but differed in detail and in each power’s priorities. The British treaty of 1883 was negotiated by Harry Parkes after London judged an earlier 1882 draft unsatisfactory, and the German agreement of the same year was concluded by the envoy Edward Zappe. Italy and Russia followed in 1884, the Russian treaty bearing particular significance for the Tumen frontier, where Russian territory and Korean border communities were adjacent. France’s treaty of 1886 was delayed in part by disagreement over French missionaries, since the French sought protection for Catholic activity that Korean officials remembered from the persecutions examined in Chapter 45.

Most-favored-nation clauses meant that concessions to one power could spread to others, so privileges multiplied while Korea gained relatively little reciprocal benefit. The treaties brought diplomats, consuls and merchants to Seoul and the ports, expanding the foreign community and the opportunities for rivalry. Each power’s weight differed: Britain supplied many of the textiles that reached Korea through Japanese and Chinese channels, Russia’s interests were strategic, and Germany’s ran partly through advisers such as Möllendorff. Historians therefore stress the variety within the treaty system and caution against treating the agreements as identical or as simple expressions of Western power.

Section 49.9

The Qing–Korean Trade Regulations of 1882

The Regulations for Maritime and Overland Trade between Qing China and Joseon were negotiated at Tianjin after the crisis of 1882 and concluded on 4 October 1882 in the Gregorian calendar, corresponding to the twenty-third day of the eighth lunar month. Korean sources that give 23 August are using the lunar date, not a different Gregorian signing date. The Korean principal envoy Jo Yeong-ha (with Kim Hong-jip and Eo Yun-jung involved in negotiations) dealt with Li Hongzhang and Qing officials including Ma Jianzhong and Zhou Fu. The document was framed as regulations for trade between a Qing superior and its dependent state, rather than a treaty of equals.

The regulations differed from a treaty between formally equal states. Their preamble described Korea as a dependent state of the Qing, and their terms gave Chinese merchants rights unavailable to other foreigners in some respects, including the right to reside and trade in Seoul and at Yanghwajin on the Han River, subject to Chinese jurisdiction under a Qing trade commissioner. Chinese subjects were tried by Chinese officials in disputes with Koreans, an extraterritorial principle, while Koreans in China were subject to Chinese law. Overland trade across the northern border and fishing rights were also regulated.

Qing officials intended the regulations to turn the tributary relationship, which had been primarily ritual and diplomatic, into a framework of commercial and legal oversight. The effects were visible in Seoul’s markets, where Chinese merchants competed with Korean shopkeepers, and in diplomatic practice, since other powers questioned the dependency language. The regulations show that the Qing were not merely preserving tradition but actively redefining it in response to Japanese expansion.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty port, extraterritoriality, and most-favored-nation treatment

Treaty port: a port opened by treaty to foreign residence and trade. Extraterritoriality: foreigners’ exemption from local courts. Most-favored-nation treatment: a clause extending to a signatory any privilege granted to another power.

Misunderstanding: treaty ports were not foreign colonies; Korea retained sovereignty, but its authority within them was limited. Customs tariff, gaehwa and wijeong cheoksa are defined in context.

Section 49.10

Qing Political and Economic Influence in the Early 1880s

Qing influence after 1882 developed in stages. Troops under Wu Changqing arrived in Korea in August 1882, accompanied by the young officer Yuan Shikai, who helped train Korean forces. Li Hongzhang recommended foreign advisers, including Paul Georg von Möllendorff, who served as adviser on foreign affairs and later headed the customs service, and Chinese commercial networks expanded. Yuan’s formal position in this period was as a military officer and adviser; his appointment as the Qing’s senior resident representative in Korea came only in 1885, after the Gapsin Coup (Chapter 50), when Qing policy turned to closer supervision. The distinction matters, because the earlier phase of influence worked partly through advisers and troops, the later through a resident with broader authority.

Qing influence rested on military presence, advice, commerce and ties to court factions, rather than annexation or full administrative control. Korean officials could accept Qing support when they feared Japan while resenting its interference, and historians still debate whether the policy primarily protected Korea or constrained it.

Section 49.11

Diplomatic Diversification and the Limits of Korean Sovereignty

Japan sought commercial and strategic advantage, the Qing sought tighter oversight and a buffer, the United States sought trade and recognition, and Britain, Germany and Russia watched one another. Korean officials played these interests against each other with some skill, for example in seeking tariff changes and in using Western advisers.

Balancing worked only while rivals neutralized one another. Without equal bargaining power, Korea remained vulnerable to pressure from any one state, and the same diplomatic diversity that gave the court options also exposed it to competing demands. Korean sovereignty was thus real but constrained, and its limits depended on the international environment.

Korean officials experimented with several devices. They hired foreign advisers of different nationalities, asked Western governments for teachers and, later, military instructors, and cultivated contacts in several capitals, intending to avoid dependence on one patron. Within the court, factions drew different lessons: officials close to the queen’s family tended to favor Qing guidance, while younger reformers examined Japan as a model, differences that deepened in the crisis examined in Chapter 50.

These devices produced mixed results. Advisers gave Korea expertise but also gave their home states channels of influence, as the case of Möllendorff showed, and the powers frequently suspected one another’s purposes. Korea’s leverage rose when the powers neutralized one another and fell when one acted unilaterally. The central problem was asymmetry: even skillful diplomacy could not substitute for military strength or financial independence, and the limits of sovereignty during these years reflected the structure of the international system as much as Korean choices.

Division III — Customs, Commercial Institutions, and Economic Transformation

Section 49.12

The 1876 Trade Regulations and the Problem of Tariff Sovereignty

The commercial regulations attached to the 1876 treaty gave Japanese merchants tariff-free access and allowed Japanese currency to circulate in the ports, a combination that deprived Korea of customs revenue and allowed foreign coin into local markets. Korean officials were quickly dissatisfied. In September 1878 Korean authorities established a customs post at Dumojin near Busan and began collecting duties from Korean merchants involved in imports and exports, not directly from Japanese traders as is sometimes stated. Japanese protests and pressure forced the closure of the post in December 1878. The episode showed that the question was not only fiscal but one of sovereignty: whether Korea could tax commerce within its own territory. Historians cite it as the link between the tariff-free arrangements of 1876 and the renegotiations of 1883.

The consequences were gradual. With no tariff on Japanese imports and with grain exports growing, Korea’s ability to finance reform depended on other revenue, and officials who sought new institutions (§49.16) found themselves without customs income. The dispute also influenced Korea’s approach to Western treaties, where tariff schedules appeared as a goal.

The tariff question was also one of negotiation. During the 1880 mission to Japan, Kim Hong-jip raised customs questions with Japanese officials, and Korean officials continued to press for duties as the opening of new ports approached. Japan resisted early change because the tariff-free arrangement served its merchants, and only in the 1883 renegotiation did it concede a revised schedule.

The Dumojin episode, the 1880 discussions and the 1883 agreement show a pattern in which Korean officials used diplomacy, rather than force, to recover tariff authority and succeeded only partly. Tariff autonomy, meaning a state’s freedom to set its own duties, remained limited by treaty even after 1883. Historians treat the period from 1876 to 1883 as a lesson for Korean officials about the connection between fiscal capacity, legal sovereignty and the possibilities of reform.

Section 49.13

Renegotiation, the 1883 Commercial Agreement, and the Customs Service

The revised Japan–Korea commercial regulations were signed on 27 July 1883 (the twenty-fourth day of the sixth lunar month), by Min Yeong-mok for Joseon and Takezoe Shinichirō for Japan. Their forty-two articles and accompanying customs schedule ended formal tariff-free trade with Japan but did not restore unrestricted Korean tariff sovereignty. A rate of 8 percent applied to cotton goods and certain major imports, while other import categories were rated at 5, 10, 25, or 30 percent. Article 37 permitted Korean authorities to prohibit grain exports only after a month’s advance notice, creating a recurrent source of dispute; Article 42 required bilateral agreement on revisions, limiting Korea’s ability to change trade terms unilaterally.

Korea also created a maritime customs administration. Offices opened at Incheon, Wonsan and Busan in 1883, with the German adviser Möllendorff, recommended by Li Hongzhang, leading the new service as head of the customs administration. Korean sources distinguish the dates of organization from the start of actual collections. The service gave Korea a revenue institution and a means of recording trade, but dependence on foreign staff and treaty limits on tariff autonomy meant that reform and constraint advanced together.

The customs service was modeled partly on the Chinese Imperial Maritime Customs, which employed foreign staff under Qing authority and which Li Hongzhang and his advisers recommended as a way of securing revenue and checking trade. Möllendorff, who had served in the Qing’s foreign service and the Chinese customs, organized the Korean service and recruited foreign and Korean staff. The new officials recorded trade, assessed duties and enforced regulations in the ports, which gave the state information it had not had before.

The arrangement had political consequences. Revenue from the customs service helped finance new institutions, but the use of foreign officials, many of them Chinese-recommended, linked it to Qing influence, and Japan and the Western powers watched its operation. Möllendorff’s later involvement in diplomatic initiatives, including an attempt in 1885 to bring Russia into Korea’s diplomacy, led to his dismissal, an episode that belongs to the politics of the period after 1884 and shows how entangled customs administration was with great-power rivalry.

Section 49.14

Foreign Settlements, Consular Jurisdiction, and Commercial Privileges

Treaty-port settlements were created through a series of separate arrangements rather than a single template. Busan’s Japanese settlement dated from 1876 and was built on the older Choryang site; Wonsan’s followed its opening in 1880; at Incheon a Japanese settlement was arranged in 1883, a Chinese settlement followed in 1884, and a general foreign settlement was organized in the same period. Land within each district was leased to foreign residents and merchants, who laid out streets, wharves and warehouses and, in some cases, paid ground rent to Korean authorities. The settlements were therefore spaces of foreign residence under Korean sovereignty, but one in which Korean administrative reach was reduced.

Jurisdiction depended on the nationality involved. The 1876 treaty provided that disputes involving Japanese in Korea were handled through Japanese consular authorities and Korean authorities according to the nationality of the accused, a framework that Japanese consuls used to protect residents. The 1882 American treaty (Article IV) gave U.S. officials jurisdiction over American citizens, and later Western treaties followed it. The Qing regulations placed Chinese subjects under a Chinese trade commissioner. Because most-favored-nation clauses spread privileges, a concession to one power could become available to others. Foreign consuls became important figures in port life, mediating or contesting disputes with local Korean magistrates.

In practice, the limits on Korean authority were uneven. Korean officials retained responsibility for Koreans and for matters outside the settlements, but crimes, debts and property quarrels involving foreigners often turned on consular decisions. The geographic freedom of foreigners was also limited at first: Japanese traders were restricted to a short travel radius around the ports, a limit that was widened in 1883 and later, and which became a point of contention. Evidence comes from treaty texts, consular correspondence and Korean administrative records; few sources record how ordinary Koreans who lived next to the settlements viewed these arrangements. Historians generally describe the system as a narrowing of Korean jurisdiction rather than a transfer of territory, while noting that its principles later served as precedents for greater foreign control.

Section 49.15

Imports, Exports, Korean Merchants, and Market Disruption

The principal flow of exports was agricultural. Rice and other grains, beans and hides moved to Japan through the ports, along with gold dust and, in smaller quantities, other products. Imports included cotton cloth, much of it foreign-manufactured and reaching Korea via Japanese and Chinese merchants, cotton yarn, needles and other manufactured goods. The trade connected Korean producers to price movements in Japan and East Asia and gave port merchants, brokers and shippers new opportunities.

Korean commercial intermediaries were central. Brokers and commission agents of the older kind, such as the keakju and yeogak, handled sales and lodging for traveling merchants, while itinerant merchants and boatmen carried goods inland. Many adapted by working with Japanese or Chinese merchants, and some became wealthy. Merchants in Seoul who held privileged positions protested when Chinese traders gained rights to trade in the capital, an example of competition between established guild-like merchants and new entrants under treaty protection.

The effects on producers and consumers differed by region and group. Farmers near ports who sold grain might benefit from higher demand, while exports could raise grain prices for urban consumers and poorer households. Imported cloth competed with domestic cotton weavers, though the extent of the harm is debated and varied locally. The volume of trade grew in the early 1880s, but reliable aggregate statistics are limited, and historians caution against assuming that foreign competition destroyed domestic production uniformly. Currency issues also shaped markets, since foreign coin and the state’s own coinage coexisted. The consequences included dependence on a few export goods and rising tension over grain exports, matters that later fed rural discontent examined in Chapter 51.

Historical Debate

Did Opening Strengthen State Capacity or Deepen Dependency?

The question: Historians disagree about how to weigh the institutional gains of the 1880s against the constraints that came with them.

Capacity: This interpretation points to the customs service, the Tongni Gimu Amun, the missions, the new schools and the press as evidence that Korean officials built tools for governing a changed environment, and it stresses their agency and selective borrowing.
Dependency: This interpretation stresses tariff limits, extraterritoriality, reliance on foreign advisers, Qing oversight and the tie of rice exports to Japanese demand, arguing that the new institutions operated within a framework set by others.
Other readings: Some historians stress internal conflict among Korean elites, which affected how institutions worked regardless of foreign pressure.

Evidence: Treaty texts, customs and consular records, court records and mission reports inform both views, but reliable aggregate trade and revenue statistics are limited.

Points of disagreement: Mainly interpretation and weighting; also what counts as success.

Why it matters: It shapes whether the 1880s appear as a lost opportunity for reform, a first stage of dependency or a mixed experiment, without presupposing later colonization.

Division IV — Government Reform, Foreign Knowledge, and New Technologies

Section 49.16

The Tongni Gimu Amun and the Reorganization of Government, 1880–1882

The Tongni Gimu Amun, created in December 1880, was a response to the need for a body that could handle foreign relations, trade and new technologies, matters that did not fit neatly into the Six Ministries. Its model owed something to the Qing Zongli Yamen and to Japanese examples that Korean observers had studied. It was placed near the king’s authority and staffed by officials drawn from the court and from reform-minded circles, including the queen’s kin and officials such as Kim Hong-jip, who had returned from Japan with new ideas.

The bureau began with twelve departments, among them offices for relations with the Qing, relations with other countries, military affairs, border questions and trade, and was reorganized into seven in 1881. Its remit extended beyond diplomacy into military organization, machinery and the study of foreign techniques, so that it combined functions that Western governments divided among separate ministries. This breadth made it both powerful and vulnerable: it competed with the established Uijeongbu and Six Ministries for authority, and its officials faced criticism from conservatives who objected to the new institution as a break with precedent.

The bureau’s history was affected by the political crisis of 1882 (Chapter 50), after which the structure was reorganized into separate offices for foreign affairs and for other matters, and a Customs administration was added. Historians therefore treat it as a first experiment in modern administrative organization rather than a stable institution. Evidence includes court records and the officials’ own writings. It shows that Joseon officials were creating new offices within a traditional framework, borrowing selectively rather than copying a single foreign model.

Because the bureau was new, its authority depended on practice rather than precedent. It worked beside the Uijeongbu and the Six Ministries, drawing staff from existing offices and using existing revenue sources, and its decisions required royal approval. Officials who feared that the bureau would displace established ministries criticized its creation, and its ambiguous status gave its leaders both influence and exposure.

The bureau’s structure changed repeatedly. After the crisis of 1882 (Chapter 50), reorganization produced separate offices for foreign affairs and for other administrative matters, and further changes followed in the mid-1880s, details of which should be verified in Korean institutional histories. Historians treat the bureau less as a stable ministry than as a laboratory for administrative experiment, one that shows officials trying to add functions to Joseon government without abandoning its Confucian framework.

Cause and Effect

Commercial Opening and Institutional Reform

Long-term causes: fiscal strain, the Joseon–Japan trading relationship and regional diplomacy. Short-term causes: the tariff-free 1876 arrangements, the Dumojin dispute and Qing advice. Immediate trigger: the opening of Wonsan and Incheon. Transformation: a customs service, new bureaus and missions. Immediate effects: new revenue and information, new foreign privileges. Long-term consequences: reform debates and great-power rivalry (Chapters 50–54). Complexity: reform also arose from Qing policy, court politics and merchants’ demands, so no single cause suffices.

Section 49.17

Korean Missions to Japan, Qing China, and the Wider World

Korean missions abroad in these years were a deliberate effort to gather knowledge. The Susinsa of 1876 under Kim Gi-su, sent to Japan after the treaty, visited Tokyo and Yokohama and saw railways, factories and schools; Kim’s account is among the earliest Korean descriptions of modern Japan. A second mission in 1880 under Kim Hong-jip returned with a memorandum by the Qing diplomat Huang Zunxian, which argued for associating with the United States and learning from the West and which circulated widely among Korean officials.

In 1881 the court sent a larger and partly secret inspection mission to Japan, in which officials were assigned to study particular ministries and institutions, including administration, customs, finance, the military and industry. Members such as Pak Jeong-yang, Hong Yeong-sik and others produced reports; two young participants, Yu Gil-jun and Yun Chi-ho, remained in Japan to study. In the same year a technical mission under Kim Yun-sik, the Yeongseonsa, traveled to Tianjin with students and artisans to learn about arms manufacture and machinery, returning in 1882. In 1883 the Bobingsa, led by Min Yeong-ik, traveled to the United States after the treaty and met the American president; Yu Gil-jun remained to study.

The missions show a pattern: Koreans examined Japanese, Qing and American examples and drew different lessons. Some officials emphasized administration, others technology, others diplomatic balance. Their reports shaped bureaus such as the Tongni Gimu Amun but were limited by court politics, finances and conservative opposition. The reports reflect elite perspectives and the observers’ expectations, and do not record how ordinary Koreans viewed the foreign scenes the missions described.

Section 49.18

Military Modernization and the Search for Technical Expertise

Military modernization began in 1881 with the creation of the Byeolgigun, a unit of several dozen to about a hundred young men, many from elite families, trained in Western-style drill by a Japanese officer, Horimoto Reizō, using newly acquired rifles. It was housed and paid separately from the older units of the Five Military Camps, which were consolidated into two camps in the same year. The government hoped the new force would form the core of a modern army, and Korean officials also sent students to Tianjin to learn weapons production (§49.17).

The reforms created inequalities. The new unit received better pay, equipment and attention while the older soldiers, who were drawn largely from poorer urban families, were reorganized and often paid late. Grain stipends for older troops were in arrears, and corruption in the distribution of rations aggravated resentment. Financial constraints limited the program: the state lacked revenue to pay both forces, and, because customs revenue was not yet available (§49.12), reform funds were scarce.

The Byeolgigun’s use of Japanese instructors also raised political questions, since Korean officials had to balance the benefits of expertise against the influence of a foreign power. The tensions among soldiers contributed to the mutiny of July 1882, which Chapter 50 narrates; here the point is that institutional reform created winners and losers within the military. Evidence comes from court records and diplomatic correspondence, which often reflect the views of officials rather than soldiers.

Military reform extended beyond the Byeolgigun. The government set up a machinery bureau for arms and equipment in the early 1880s, sent artisans and students to study arms manufacture at Tianjin (§49.17), and acquired modern rifles from Japan and other sources. Its ability to produce weapons at home remained limited, so Korea depended on imports and on foreign technical help, with the same dependence on Japanese or Qing expertise that shaped other institutions.

After 1882 the military situation changed. Qing forces remained in Seoul, and Chinese officers, including Yuan Shikai, helped to train new Korean units, while the Japanese-trained Byeolgigun did not survive the political crisis of that year. Military reform was therefore repeatedly reorganized under different foreign patrons, which made the army a site of international competition as well as domestic reform. The underlying problems of funding, soldiers’ pay and the rivalry between old and new units persisted, and shaped the politics examined in Chapter 50.

Section 49.19

Steamships, Telegraphs, Postal Services, and New Communications

New transport and communications technologies reached Joseon through the treaty ports, but they did not spread at the same pace or serve every community equally. Japanese steamship lines connected Busan, Wonsan and Incheon with Nagasaki and other Japanese ports, while coastal sailing craft continued to carry much of the trade within Korea. An 1883 shipping agreement negotiated by Möllendorff and the Qing commissioner Chen Shoutang established a monthly Shanghai–Korea service by China Merchants’ Steam Navigation Company; a January 1884 supplement extended its permitted routes to Nagasaki. Competing foreign carriers expanded commercial connections without giving Korean owners comparable control of shipping capacity.

Telegraph construction developed through overlapping Japanese and Qing initiatives. A submarine cable linked Busan with Japan in 1883, while a Qing-financed land line ran from Jemulpo (Incheon) through Seoul toward the northwest. Contemporary U.S. diplomatic reports record the Jemulpo–Seoul section beginning operation on 27 September 1885 and the line connecting at Uiju to China's network on 20 November 1885 (contemporary dispatch). This was not a project completed only in the late 1880s. Japanese officials objected to Qing control of the route, and rival plans for connections to southern Korea became another source of diplomatic competition.

The government also attempted to introduce public postal services. In November 1884 it opened the Uijeongguk under Hong Yeong-sik, but the postal initiative was disrupted during the Gapsin Coup (Chapter 50) and did not become a continuous nationwide service. Traditional courier and relay-station arrangements continued alongside the new experiments. The distinction between a proposed service, a temporarily functioning institution, and a durable communications network is essential to understanding the period.

For officials, consuls, and merchants, steamships and telegraphs could accelerate travel, information, and commercial decisions. For many rural communities, roads, rivers, carts, boats, and older official communications still dominated everyday life. Technology transfer was therefore uneven and politically contested: foreign companies and governments supplied equipment and expertise, while Korean authorities tried to gain the benefits without surrendering control of routes, revenue, or information.

Section 49.20

Western Medicine, Scientific Knowledge, and Practical Innovation

Western medicine entered Korea through several routes before Gwanghyewon. Japanese doctors treated Koreans at a hospital in the Busan settlement from the late 1870s, and the physician Ji Seok-yeong learned smallpox vaccination from Japanese practitioners and began to apply it. These developments occurred alongside long-established Korean medical traditions, in which physicians used herbal and acupuncture practices and the Donguibogam of the early seventeenth century remained authoritative.

Horace Allen, an American Presbyterian physician and missionary, arrived in 1884 attached to the American legation. During the violence of December 1884 (Chapter 50) he treated the seriously wounded Min Yeong-ik, and this success gave him standing at court. The government then agreed to a hospital under royal sponsorship that would employ foreign physicians while remaining a Korean institution. It opened in 1885 as Gwanghyewon and was renamed Jejungwon within weeks.

The hospital offered Western surgical and medical care, vaccination, and a mixture of free and paid treatment, and other American missionary physicians joined the work. On 29 March 1886 a medical training school opened in association with Jejungwon, initially selecting 16 Korean students; 12 proceeded after an initial screening period. The program taught English and scientific subjects alongside medical instruction, but this first attempt did not produce a graduating class of qualified physicians. The institution served both as a medical facility and as an instrument of diplomacy for the court and the missions.

The new medicine did not replace existing practice. Most Koreans continued to consult traditional physicians, shamans and local healers, and access to the hospital was limited by location and cost. The government’s adoption was selective, treating the hospital as an addition rather than a replacement. Historians distinguish proposals from established institutions and note that evidence about patient experience is limited.

Everyday Life and Social History

A porter at Incheon carried goods between lighters and warehouses for wages that depended on ship arrivals; a broker in Busan arranged credit and sales between Korean producers and Japanese buyers; a student at Wonsan Haksa learned subjects absent from village schools; a weaver in a cotton district competed with imported cloth; a Seoul shopkeeper faced new Chinese rivals. These examples illustrate types of experience rather than documented individuals.

Sources for ordinary people are thin and come largely from officials, consuls and later memory, so historians infer much from patterns in trade, prices and settlement.

Division V — Education, Newspapers, Intellectual Change, and Synthesis

Section 49.21

New Schools, Foreign Languages, and Educational Reform

New schools of the 1880s had three kinds of sponsors, and the difference matters. Wonsan Haksa, founded in 1883, was established by local residents of the Wonsan area, with the support of local officials, in response to the commercial and diplomatic changes of the port. Its curriculum is reported to have combined Korean and Chinese learning with practical and foreign subjects, though details should be verified. Its significance lies in showing local initiative: the demand for new education came from communities that dealt directly with foreign merchants.

The state also founded schools. Dongmunhak, established in 1883 under the new foreign-affairs administration, trained interpreters in English and was staffed by a foreign teacher recruited through the customs service. Yukyeong Gongwon, opened in 1886, was a government school for sons of officials that employed American teachers and taught English and other modern subjects. Both aimed to produce administrators and translators for the new bureaucracy rather than to educate the general population.

Missionaries founded the third group. Paejae Hakdang, begun in 1885 by the Methodist missionary Henry Appenzeller, and Ewha Hakdang, begun in 1886 by Mary Scranton, started with very few students; the court later gave both schools names, a sign of official tolerance. Ewha was the first school for girls and began new educational opportunities for women, though in its first years its pupils were few and drawn from restricted circumstances. Missionary schools were also limited by the ban on open proselytizing, which shaped their curricula. Together the schools remained small and urban or port-based, and most Koreans, especially in the countryside, continued to study in village schools or not at all.

Section 49.22

Printing, Newspapers, and the Circulation of New Ideas

Printing and newspapers developed under government auspices. The office Bakmunguk, established in 1883 within the new foreign-affairs administration, handled modern printing and publishing. In October 1883 it began issuing Hanseong Sunbo, a ten-day periodical (sunbo) in classical Chinese, associated with Park Yeong-hyo, then governor of Hanseong prefecture, and with Japanese assistance in the form of equipment and advisers. The paper was circulated to officials and offices rather than sold widely.

Its contents combined government announcements, translated articles on foreign countries drawn from Chinese and Japanese newspapers, and information on trade and technology. This made it a vehicle for what officials considered useful knowledge, and a means of introducing Korean readers to the idea of a periodical press. The Gapsin Coup of 1884 (Chapter 50) interrupted its publication and destroyed its press.

Hanseong Jubo, begun in 1886 as a weekly under the same office, used mixed Korean script and Chinese characters, an early use of Hangeul in printed news, and continued for only a few years. The readership of both papers was small and consisted largely of officials and educated elites; evidence on circulation is thin. Even so, they marked a new form of public communication and helped to form a reading public interested in foreign affairs, commerce and reform. Historians read the papers as state-sponsored, representing official framing rather than public opinion.

Section 49.23

Gaehwa, Wijeong Cheoksa, and Competing Visions of Reform

Responses to opening ranged widely. Gaehwa advocates such as Kim Hong-jip and Kim Yun-sik argued for selective adoption of foreign techniques while preserving Confucian principles and the monarchy, supported by texts such as Huang Zunxian’s memorandum Joseon Chaengnyak, which Kim Hong-jip brought back in 1880. Later scholarship often summarizes this stance as combining Eastern ethics with Western techniques, though the officials themselves used varied language. Park Gyu-su is usually discussed among officials who favored learning from abroad, but his program should be distinguished from that of the younger activists examined in Chapter 50.

Wijeong cheoksa, the defense of orthodoxy against heterodoxy, drew on the teaching of scholars such as Yi Hang-ro, who died in 1868 and was therefore an earlier intellectual influence rather than a participant in the 1880s. Conservative memorials of the early 1880s, including a large collective memorial from Yeongnam scholars in 1881, rejected Western contact and opposed Christianity and the new bureaus. Their arguments concerned culture and sovereignty as well as ritual, since they feared that commerce and foreign technology would erode Confucian social order.

Reformers also disagreed among themselves about pace, models and dependence on Qing or Japan, and conservatives differed over how far to resist. The categories “reformers” and “conservatives” were therefore never unified camps, and many officials held mixed positions.

Section 49.24

The New International Order: Transformation, Continuity, and Uncertainty

By 1886 Korea had three treaty ports, a customs administration, treaties with Japan, the Qing and six Western states, a foreign-affairs bureau, new schools and a newspaper. The commercial opening had created export opportunities and new intermediaries but had also reduced tariff and legal authority. Diplomatic diversification had given the court options but not equal bargaining power.

The period combined institutional experiment and constraint. The Tongni Gimu Amun, the customs service, the missions and the schools show Korean officials and communities acquiring knowledge and building capacity. At the same time, extraterritoriality, Qing oversight and rivalry among powers narrowed the room for independent decisions. The experience varied: port communities and officials felt change most directly, while villagers experienced it mainly through prices and demand.

These developments did not make later colonization inevitable. Contingent decisions, rivalries among the powers and internal conflicts shaped the next decades, which Chapters 50 to 54 examine. What the opening of the 1880s produced was a new environment in which reform and vulnerability developed together, and in which Koreans debated, sometimes violently, how to respond.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Tributary Diplomacy to Treaty Institutions

What changed? Ports, tariffs, treaties with many states, new bureaus, schools, newspapers and missions.

What remained continuous? The monarchy, the yangban bureaucracy, the Qing tributary tie, Neo-Confucian education and the agrarian economy.

When did change occur? Unevenly, with clusters in 1880–1883 and 1885–1886.

Character of change: gradual, contested and uneven, shaped by crises (Chapter 50).

Who experienced it? Port communities, officials and merchants most directly; most villagers experienced greater continuity.

Why it matters: It shows that Korean institutions changed by addition and adaptation rather than replacement.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the opening of Korea as a continuing process of commerce, diplomacy and institution building rather than a single treaty event. The ports, the customs service, the missions and the schools were practical settings in which Koreans negotiated with foreign powers and with one another.

It also shows why the history is difficult to summarize as modernization or as dependency. Officials and merchants gained knowledge and revenue, but the same arrangements limited tariff and legal authority and invited rivalry among Japan, the Qing and Western powers.

The patterns set here shaped what followed. Chapter 50 examines the political and military crises that interrupted these institutions, and later chapters follow the reform debates and the struggle over sovereignty. Understanding the 1880s on their own terms helps students avoid reading the colonization of 1910 back into them.
