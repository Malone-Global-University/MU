Reform, Crisis, and the Fall of Goryeo

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the fifth month of 1388, an army that Goryeo's court had ordered north to invade Ming-held Liaodong stopped at a small island in the Amnok River called Wihwado, and did not cross. Its commander, a seasoned frontier general named Yi Seong-gye, turned his soldiers around and marched them back toward the capital instead. Within four years the dynasty that had sent him north, a dynasty founded in 918 and ruling continuously for nearly four hundred and seventy-five years, no longer existed.

Chapter 23 left Goryeo deeply enmeshed in the Mongol Yuan imperial order: Goryeo kings married Yuan princesses, spent long periods at the Yuan court, governed under close Mongol supervision, and presided over a kingdom whose northeastern territories had been detached and placed under direct Yuan administration. That arrangement, however uncomfortable for Goryeo's rulers, had also imposed a kind of external stability. Yuan power set limits on how far internal Goryeo conflicts could go, backed disputed successions, and provided a fixed reference point around which Goryeo's court politics organized itself for two generations. This chapter begins at the moment that external framework started to come apart, and follows the consequences through to the founding of a new dynasty.

The removal of a dominant outside power rarely produces automatic recovery for the state that had depended on it. When Yuan authority weakened in the fourteenth century, worn down by decades of factional struggle at its own court, fiscal exhaustion, and rebellions across its Chinese territories, Goryeo's king Gongmin saw an opening to reassert royal authority, recover lost territory, and reduce the influence of pro-Yuan aristocratic families who had thrived under the old arrangement. His reform program achieved real successes, most visibly the recovery of the Ssangseong Commandery in the northeast after nearly a century of Yuan control. But removing Yuan oversight did not dissolve the deeper structural problems that had accumulated inside Goryeo during the century of Mongol domination: vast private estates controlled by powerful families, a tax base eroded by land concentration, a state apparatus that struggled to support its own soldiers and officials, and a Buddhist ecclesiastical establishment closely tied to the very aristocratic networks that reform threatened.

At almost the same moment, the collapse of Yuan authority in China convulsed the wider region. Rebel armies known as the Red Turbans, pushed out of Liaodong by Yuan counterattacks, twice invaded Goryeo in 1359 and 1361 and briefly occupied the capital at Gaegyeong. Along the southern and western coasts, Japanese raiders called wokou struck with increasing frequency and violence, disrupting the grain shipments on which the state depended and devastating fishing and farming communities. These crises did not simply damage Goryeo; they also created new kinds of political importance for the men who could organize armies capable of meeting them. Yi Seong-gye's reputation, built over three decades fighting Red Turban remnants, wokou raiders, and northern rivals, grew directly out of this environment of chronic military emergency.

Inside the government, a different kind of change was also under way. A generation of officials trained in Neo-Confucian scholarship, drawn from varied elite backgrounds and often bound together through examination learning and scholarly networks more than through shared lineage, began to argue that Goryeo's problems were not merely military or fiscal but moral and institutional: that a state whose land system enriched a narrow elite and whose religious establishment consumed public resources without producing public benefit could not govern well no matter who occupied the throne. Officials such as Jeong Do-jeon developed this critique into a program for restructuring landholding, curbing Buddhist institutional power, and reorganizing government around Confucian principles of virtuous, accountable rule. On their own, these scholar-officials possessed little military power. Their alliance with Yi Seong-gye's army, forged gradually over the 1380s, would prove decisive.

The immediate spark that brought these currents together was a foreign-policy crisis. As the Ming dynasty consolidated its hold on China and pressed territorial claims into the old Yuan northeastern frontier, Goryeo's court split over how to respond. One faction, led by the powerful general Choe Yeong, pushed for a military campaign to seize Liaodong before Ming forces could secure it. Yi Seong-gye argued that the campaign was militarily reckless and diplomatically catastrophic, but the court overruled him and sent him north as one of the principal commanders of the invasion force. His refusal to cross the Amnok River at Wihwado, and his decision instead to march his army back to Gaegyeong and seize control of the government, transformed a foreign-policy dispute into a domestic political revolution.

This chapter does not yet tell the story of the state that followed. It explains why Goryeo, a dynasty with genuine cultural, religious, and institutional achievements described in earlier chapters, proved unable to reform itself into a durable political order once the external framework of Yuan domination disappeared. It follows the convergence of international upheaval, domestic land and social crisis, military emergency, intellectual change, and elite political conflict that made 1392 possible, without treating that outcome as inevitable from the moment Yuan power began to weaken. Chapter 25 will take up the different question of how Yi Seong-gye and his allies built a new dynasty out of the wreckage this chapter describes.

Understanding this transition matters for more than antiquarian reasons. The pattern this chapter traces, in which the weakening of an overshadowing external power exposes rather than resolves a state's internal contradictions, and in which reform movements initiated to preserve an existing order end by replacing it, recurs at several later points in Korean history. Students who follow carefully how reform, crisis, and dynastic change interacted in fourteenth-century Goryeo will be better equipped to recognize similar dynamics when they reappear in later centuries.

  3. Analyze why removing external Yuan oversight did not by itself resolve Goryeo's internal structural problems.

  8. Trace Yi Seong-gye's rise from frontier and anti-wokou military command to national political prominence.

Historical Context

Chapter 23 explained how Goryeo, after decades of resistance concentrated on Ganghwa Island, submitted to Mongol authority in the thirteenth century and was incorporated into the Yuan imperial system. Goryeo kings took Yuan princesses as principal wives, spent extended periods as hostages and students at the Yuan capital, and ruled formally through the Jeongdong Haengseong, a nominally joint Goryeo-Yuan administrative structure that the Goryeo king himself normally headed, but whose subordinate organs, especially the I-munso, or Judicial Proceedings Office, became powerful channels through which Yuan interests and pro-Yuan officials intervened directly in Goryeo governance. Yuan military demands, most visibly the two failed invasions of Japan launched partly through Goryeo shipyards and manpower, imposed heavy burdens on the kingdom's population and treasury. The northeastern region around present-day Hamhung, detached from Goryeo administration and organized as the Ssangseong Commandery, remained under direct Yuan control for nearly a hundred years, a standing reminder of Goryeo's reduced sovereignty.

This arrangement produced a distinctive late-Goryeo aristocracy. Families whose fortunes depended on close ties to the Yuan court, sometimes through marriage, sometimes through service in Yuan military or administrative posts, accumulated enormous private landholdings and political influence, often at the expense of the royal treasury and of smaller landholders. Buddhist monasteries, long central to Goryeo religious and cultural life as described in Chapter 21, likewise held extensive tax-exempt estates and enjoyed close relationships with the royal court and leading aristocratic houses. By the mid-fourteenth century, the gap between Goryeo's formal governing institutions, modeled on centralized bureaucratic principles described in Chapter 20, and the actual distribution of land and power had grown very wide.

Outside Goryeo, the Yuan dynasty itself was approaching a crisis of its own. Decades of court factionalism, disputed successions, fiscal strain from overextended paper currency, and natural disasters including flooding along the Yellow River combined with widespread rebellion across Yuan China from the 1340s onward. Millenarian religious movements, most importantly the Red Turban movement associated with White Lotus Buddhist teachings, mobilized large rebel armies that the weakened Yuan state struggled to suppress. Out of this general upheaval, a rival power eventually emerged under Zhu Yuanzhang, who would found the Ming dynasty in 1368 and drive Yuan forces out of China proper. Goryeo's fourteenth-century crisis cannot be separated from this larger continental collapse; the same disorder that produced opportunities for Goryeo's kings to reassert independence also produced the armed refugees, raiders, and eventually the new imperial power that would destabilize Goryeo in different ways.

Section 24.1

Goryeo under the Shadow of Yuan

By the middle of the fourteenth century, Goryeo had lived inside the Yuan imperial order for roughly eighty years. The kingdom retained its own royal house, its own bureaucratic apparatus modeled on the institutions described in Chapter 20, and its own territory, but its kings ruled under conditions no earlier Goryeo monarch would have recognized as full sovereignty. Crown princes were raised largely at the Yuan capital, married Mongol princesses as principal consorts, and depended on Yuan backing to secure and hold the throne against domestic rivals. The Jeongdong Haengseong, the nominally joint Goryeo-Yuan administrative structure the Goryeo king himself normally headed, contained subordinate organs, above all the I-munso, that in practice became the principal channel through which Yuan interests and pro-Yuan officials intervened in Goryeo governance, and Goryeo's independent military capacity had been eroded across the Yuan period by a combination of factors: the manpower and shipbuilding demands of the Yuan invasions of Japan discussed in Chapter 23, ongoing fiscal erosion from land concentration, and institutional changes that had shifted military organization and command away from purely Goryeo direction.

This dependency produced a particular kind of domestic politics. Aristocratic families who cultivated close relationships with the Yuan court, whether through marriage connections, service in Yuan posts, or simple proximity to Mongol favor, could translate that access into landholding, office, and influence disproportionate to older measures of Goryeo status. Modern Korean historians often describe this stratum using the term gwonmun sejok, "powerful lineage families," to distinguish them from other late-Goryeo elite and bureaucratic groupings and from the older Goryeo aristocracy of the dynasty's earlier centuries. These families accumulated private estates, sometimes by absorbing state and public land, sometimes by pressuring smaller landholders into dependent status, and by the 1350s controlled a share of Goryeo's arable land that seriously compromised the state's fiscal base.

Buddhist institutions occupied an overlapping position. Monasteries had long held extensive landed property and close court patronage, as Chapter 21 explained, and under Yuan-era conditions many monastic estates grew further, sometimes in close cooperation with powerful lay families. The concentration of both aristocratic and monastic landholding narrowed the tax base available to the crown at precisely the moment military and diplomatic pressures required greater resources. This was the inherited situation Gongmin confronted when he took the throne in 1351: a kingdom formally independent but politically constrained by Yuan oversight, and internally weakened by decades of land concentration in aristocratic and monastic hands.

Section 24.2

A Weakening Empire

The Yuan dynasty that had dominated Goryeo since the thirteenth century began to unravel from within during the 1340s. Succession disputes among Mongol imperial princes produced repeated changes of ruler and unstable court factions. Attempts to finance the state through paper currency issued far beyond its backing produced serious inflation. Flooding and channel shifts along the Yellow River, combined with heavy-handed and poorly managed labor conscription for river-control projects, triggered widespread popular unrest in the 1340s and 1350s. Out of this unrest grew millenarian religious movements, most significantly associated with White Lotus Buddhist teachings, which organized rebel armies known collectively as the Red Turbans for the cloth they wore as a marker of identity.

None of this instability was visible in a single dramatic moment; it accumulated across more than a decade before its consequences reached Goryeo directly. For Goryeo's court, the practical effect was a gradual loosening of Yuan attention and capacity. A Yuan court absorbed by internal rebellion and factional struggle had less ability to intervene in Goryeo succession disputes, less capacity to enforce demands for tribute and manpower, and less interest in supervising a peripheral kingdom's internal affairs closely. This created a genuine opening for a Goryeo king determined to reassert authority. It did not, however, remove the domestic aristocratic and monastic interests that had grown powerful under the old arrangement, nor did it insulate Goryeo from the violent disorder spreading through the collapsing Yuan realm. Weakening Yuan power was therefore a double-edged development: it enlarged the space for reform while also exporting new dangers, in the form of displaced rebel armies and, eventually, raiders and a new rival power, directly toward the peninsula.

Section 24.3

King Gongmin and the Reform Project

Gongmin came to the Goryeo throne in 1351 after years spent at the Yuan court, an upbringing typical of late-Goryeo crown princes but one that gave him direct, close observation of Yuan politics at the very moment that system was beginning to falter. Once enthroned, he moved deliberately and in stages rather than through a single sweeping declaration of independence. In 1356, he moved decisively: purging the powerful Gi Cheol and his faction, whose influence rested substantially on a family connection to a Yuan empress; abolishing the I-munso, the notorious Judicial Proceedings Office attached to the Yuan-supervised Jeongdong Haengseong administration, which had served as one of the most intrusive instruments of Yuan interference in Goryeo governance; discontinuing the use of the Yuan reign-era calendar; and restoring older Goryeo official titles and administrative terminology where these had been altered to conform to Yuan practice. He also worked, with more limited success, to rebuild an independent Goryeo military capacity.

Gongmin's reform ambitions extended beyond removing Yuan political oversight. He sought to restrain the landed and political power of the gwonmun sejok families whose fortunes had grown under Yuan-era conditions, recognizing that they represented an internal obstacle to royal authority as serious as external Yuan supervision had been. This domestic dimension of reform proved considerably harder to carry through than the more visible acts of political independence, because it required confronting entrenched interests with deep roots in landholding, marriage alliances, and court patronage networks that Gongmin's own administration depended on to function. The king's early success in reasserting sovereignty vis-à-vis a weakening Yuan did not translate easily into comparable success against domestic elite power, a tension that would shape the remainder of his reign and outlast it.

Section 24.4

Recovering the Northern Territories

The most concrete and celebrated success of Gongmin's reform program came in 1356, when Goryeo forces recovered the Ssangseong Commandery in the northeast, ending nearly a century of direct Yuan administrative control over the region first described in Chapter 23. The recovery was made possible by the same broader Yuan weakening discussed in Section 24.2: with Yuan military and administrative capacity stretched thin by rebellion elsewhere, resistance to a determined Goryeo campaign in this peripheral northeastern territory proved limited. The recovery carried both practical and symbolic weight. Practically, it restored territory and population to direct Goryeo administration and extended the kingdom's effective northern frontier. Symbolically, it reversed one of the most visible markers of Goryeo's reduced sovereignty under Yuan domination, a point earlier Goryeo court chroniclers and later historians alike have treated as the clearest evidence of Gongmin's determination to restore full independence.

The Ssangseong campaign also had a longer-term significance that would only become apparent decades later. Among the local military and administrative figures who assisted Goryeo's 1356 campaign was Yi Jachun, a frontier commander with long-standing family ties to the region and, until that point, service connections to the Yuan administration there. Yi Jachun's decision to support Goryeo's recovery of Ssangseong rather than resist it gave his family a secure and elevated position within the reintegrated northeastern frontier, and it was this regional military base, inherited and expanded, that his son Yi Seong-gye would draw upon in the following decades. The recovery of Ssangseong should therefore be understood not only as a single successful campaign but as part of the process that produced the military talent later reform politics would depend upon.

Historical Vocabulary
Gwonmun Sejok, Dogam, and the Reforming Vocabulary of Late Goryeo

Definition: Historians use several specialized terms to describe late-Goryeo institutions and social groups that do not translate neatly into single English words. Gwonmun sejok ("powerful lineage families") refers to the narrow stratum of aristocratic houses whose landholding and court influence grew disproportionately during the Yuan-dominated thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. A dogam was a special temporary agency the Goryeo government created to carry out a particular urgent task, such as land investigation or status review, outside ordinary bureaucratic channels; Sin Don's reform commission, discussed in Section 24.5, operated as this kind of body.

Historical context: These terms are drawn from different sources. Dogam is an original administrative term found directly in Goryeo court records. Gwonmun sejok, by contrast, is a modern analytical category, coined by historians beginning in the 1960s to bring together several related expressions found scattered across medieval sources describing powerful late-Goryeo families. It is a useful scholarly shorthand rather than a term contemporaries themselves used as a fixed technical label, and its precise boundaries, including exactly which families belong within it and how sharply it should be distinguished from other elite groups, remain debated among historians.

Why it matters: Understanding these terms helps students see that Gongmin's and Sin Don's reforms operated through specific institutional mechanisms rather than through vague royal decree, and that the aristocratic power they targeted was a real and substantial, if not perfectly uniform, landed social stratum.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should avoid treating the gwonmun sejok as a single, sharply bounded class fully separate from the reforming officials described later in this chapter. Recent scholarship has shown that the officials who became prominent Neo-Confucian reformers came from varied backgrounds, including some established elite lineages, and that Neo-Confucian learning itself was not confined to any single social class. The opposition between an old landed elite and a rising reform movement, while a useful organizing framework for this chapter, should be treated as a simplification of a more socially complicated reality.

Section 24.5

Sin Don and the Limits of Reform

In 1365, a Buddhist monk named Sin Don rose to a position of dominant influence over Gongmin, who granted him extraordinary authority to pursue domestic reform while the king himself turned attention to other matters. Sin Don's background outside the established aristocratic and bureaucratic networks made him, in Gongmin's calculation, less compromised by the very interests reform needed to confront. In 1366, operating through a special reform agency known as the Jeonmin Chujeong Dogam (often grouped within the broader Jeonmin Byeonjeong Dogam tradition of recurring late-Goryeo reform commissions), Sin Don launched an ambitious investigation into landholding, ordering the return of illegally seized land to its original owners or to the state, and reviewing cases in which free commoners had been wrongly reduced to nobi (unfree, bonded) status by powerful families seeking additional labor and dependents.

These measures directly threatened the economic foundations of the gwonmun sejok families and provoked fierce resistance. Sin Don's own position, resting entirely on royal favor rather than independent institutional or landed power, proved fragile once his opponents mobilized against him at court. He was eventually accused of treason and executed in 1371, and Gongmin's broader reform momentum, already strained by the burdens of the Red Turban invasions and wokou raids discussed in the following sections, did not recover. In 1374, Gongmin himself was assassinated by a group that included the courtier Hong Ryun and members of the palace guard known as the Jaje-wi, acting with the involvement of the official Choe Man-saeng; later chronicle accounts offer varying explanations of their motives, which historians treat with some caution, but the assassination brought the most concentrated reform effort of the Yuan-decline period to an abrupt and inconclusive end regardless of its precise causes.

The Sin Don episode illustrates a pattern this chapter returns to repeatedly: identifying the structural sources of Goryeo's crisis, land concentration and status abuse prominent among them, proved considerably easier than resolving them, because the very court and bureaucratic apparatus available to carry out reform was staffed and constrained by the interests reform needed to overcome. Removing Yuan oversight had created the political space for this kind of ambitious domestic reform; it had not created the institutional or military means to see it through against determined elite resistance.

Section 24.6

The Red Turban Invasions

The unrest convulsing Yuan China, described in Section 24.2, did not remain confined within Chinese borders. Red Turban rebel forces, pressed out of Liaodong by Yuan counterattacks, moved into Manchuria and, in two major campaigns in 1359 and 1361, crossed into Goryeo territory. The first invasion struck the northwest, capturing the city of Ŭiju and advancing toward Pyongyang before Goryeo forces, reinforced and reorganized, drove the invaders back across the frontier. The second and more severe invasion in 1361 penetrated much further, and Red Turban forces briefly occupied the Goryeo capital at Gaegyeong itself, forcing King Gongmin to flee south. Goryeo forces eventually recaptured the capital and expelled the invaders, but the occupation, brief as it was, exposed how limited Goryeo's standing military capacity had become after decades of Yuan-era military obligations, fiscal erosion from land concentration, and institutional changes that had shifted military organization and command away from purely Goryeo direction.

The invasions should be understood as a direct consequence of the wider Yuan collapse rather than as an isolated Goryeo problem. The same rebellion that gave Gongmin room to pursue reform against a distracted Yuan court also produced armed, displaced rebel forces with nowhere else productive to go, and Goryeo's territory, adjoining both Manchuria and the sea, absorbed some of the resulting violence. The campaigns against the Red Turbans also had lasting political consequences inside Goryeo: they provided an early stage on which military commanders could distinguish themselves and begin building the reputations that would matter enormously in the following decades. Yi Seong-gye, drawing on the northeastern frontier military base his father Yi Jachun had secured through the 1356 Ssangseong campaign, commanded his own troops in the 1362 counteroffensive that recaptured Gaegyeong from the second Red Turban occupation, an early and important demonstration of the military capability on which his later prominence would rest.

Section 24.7

Wokou Raids and the Coastal Crisis

Alongside the northern threat from Red Turban remnants, Goryeo faced an escalating and, in many years, more chronic danger along its southern and western coasts: raiding by seaborne forces the Goryeo court called wokou, a term meaning "Japanese bandits" though the raiding forces sometimes included non-Japanese participants and operated with varying degrees of organization. Wokou activity had occurred intermittently for generations, but from the 1350s onward, coinciding with political fragmentation within Japan itself during its own period of divided courts and weak central authority, raids grew dramatically in frequency, scale, and destructiveness. Coastal towns, fishing communities, and, most damagingly for the Goryeo state, the grain-transport routes that carried tax rice from southern provinces to the capital and northern garrisons, all became repeated targets.

The fiscal consequences were severe. Goryeo's government depended on tribute grain shipped by sea from its most productive agricultural regions; repeated wokou attacks on transport convoys and coastal warehouses starved the state of revenue at precisely the moment military spending needs, driven by the Red Turban threat and by frontier tensions, were rising. Farming and fishing communities near the coast faced displacement, loss of livestock and stored grain, and in the worst raids, mass killing and captivity. Some coastal regions were abandoned outright as populations moved inland for safety, further shrinking the productive and taxable base available to the state.

Politically, the wokou crisis, like the Red Turban invasions, made military competence an increasingly important source of political influence in Goryeo. Commanders who could organize effective coastal defense and win decisive victories against raiding fleets, most importantly Yi Seong-gye and a smaller number of rival generals such as Choe Yeong, gained standing, resources, and popular gratitude that outstripped what court rank or aristocratic lineage alone could provide. By the 1380s, successful anti-wokou campaigns had become one of the principal routes to national political prominence in Goryeo, a development with direct consequences for the events of 1388 described later in this chapter.

Section 24.8

Land, Labor, and Social Strain

Behind the military crises of the 1350s through 1380s lay a deepening structural problem in Goryeo landholding and labor that both caused and was worsened by those crises. The concentration of land in the hands of gwonmun sejok families and major Buddhist monasteries, described in Section 24.1, had proceeded for decades by the time Gongmin took the throne, through mechanisms including outright seizure of public and smallholder land, manipulation of unclear or contested title records, and the pressuring of vulnerable farming households into dependent tenancy or outright nobi status in exchange for protection or debt relief.

The consequences fell most heavily on ordinary cultivators. Smallholding farmers who lost land to powerful neighbors typically became tenants paying heavy rents in kind, or in the most severe cases were reduced to nobi status, forfeiting their personal legal freedom along with their land. Because the men who administered local government, collected taxes, and staffed the courts that adjudicated land disputes were frequently drawn from or beholden to the same powerful families benefiting from these processes, ordinary households had few effective avenues for redress. The state's own tax base shrank correspondingly, since land held by powerful families and Buddhist institutions was frequently exempt or under-assessed, forcing a heavier relative burden onto the shrinking pool of independent taxpaying smallholders, precisely the households least able to bear it.

Military crisis intensified these pressures rather than relieving them. Wokou raids and Red Turban incursions damaged agricultural production and displaced rural populations, while the state's need for soldiers, supplies, and fortification labor placed additional demands on an already strained peasantry. Sin Don's reform commission, discussed in Section 24.5, targeted precisely these problems, and its failure left them substantially unresolved through the remainder of Gongmin's reign and into the following decades. When Neo-Confucian reformers and Yi Seong-gye's allies later carried out sweeping land reform after 1388, described in Section 24.13, they were addressing a crisis that had been visible and partially diagnosed for a generation without being solved.

Section 24.9

Neo-Confucianism and a New Reforming Elite

Alongside these military and fiscal crises, Goryeo's intellectual and bureaucratic life was undergoing a slower but ultimately consequential transformation. Neo-Confucian scholarship, developed in Song-dynasty China and transmitted to Goryeo through diplomatic and scholarly exchange during the Yuan period, offered a systematic framework for thinking about cosmology, ethics, self-cultivation, and, crucially, statecraft. Goryeo scholars who studied this body of thought, several of whom traveled to the Yuan capital or studied under teachers who had, began to apply its concepts to the specific problems confronting their own kingdom: land concentration, weak royal authority, and what they increasingly criticized as the excessive wealth and social burden of Buddhist religious institutions.

Reform-minded officials came from varied elite backgrounds; some rose from provincial or less-established lineages, while others belonged to families with longer-standing court connections. Examination learning and Neo-Confucian scholarly networks nevertheless helped create new political alignments that did not map neatly onto older lineage divisions, and many of these officials entered government through the civil service examination system, which gave them a shared intellectual formation distinct from, though not necessarily opposed to, hereditary privilege. This shared formation gave them a coherent ideological language for arguing that landholding should serve productive cultivation and state revenue rather than private accumulation, and that religious institutions should be judged by their practical contribution to social and moral order rather than by inherited privilege or royal favor.

Jeong Do-jeon emerged as the most systematic and eventually the most influential voice within this reforming current. Trained in Neo-Confucian scholarship and experienced in provincial administration, Jeong Do-jeon developed increasingly sharp criticisms of Goryeo's land system and of Buddhist institutional power during the 1370s and 1380s, arguing that a state whose economic foundations enriched a narrow elite and whose spiritual life was monopolized by an unaccountable clergy could not achieve genuine good government regardless of who occupied the throne. On their own, Jeong Do-jeon and similarly minded officials possessed extensive intellectual credibility within bureaucratic and scholarly circles but very little independent coercive power. Their eventual alliance with Yi Seong-gye's military strength, discussed in the following sections, would supply what their program otherwise lacked.

Section 24.10

Yi Seong-gye and the Military Reformers

Yi Seong-gye's family held military standing in the northeastern frontier region recovered during the Ssangseong campaign of 1356, discussed in Section 24.4, and his early career developed within the environment of chronic frontier and coastal warfare described in Sections 24.6 and 24.7. Over three decades he built an increasingly formidable reputation, first in campaigns against Red Turban remnants and northern rivals, and later, most consequentially, in a series of major victories against wokou raiding forces along the southern coast during the 1370s and 1380s. His forces developed a reputation for unusually strict discipline, in contrast to some Goryeo troops whose conduct toward civilian populations during campaigns had provoked resentment, and this reputation for order as well as effectiveness contributed to his growing standing both within the military and among the wider population.

By the mid-1380s, Yi Seong-gye stood among a small handful of commanders, alongside the senior general Choe Yeong, whose military reputations gave them substantial independent political weight at court. Unlike the gwonmun sejok families whose power rested on inherited landholding and marriage networks, Yi Seong-gye's influence rested on demonstrated military competence accumulated through direct engagement with the crises of Red Turban invasion and wokou raiding that had done so much to expose the weaknesses of the old aristocratic order. This made him, without his necessarily seeking the role from the outset, a natural point of alliance for Neo-Confucian reform officials such as Jeong Do-jeon, who possessed a program for restructuring Goryeo governance but lacked the military capacity to overcome the entrenched interests that program threatened.

The relationship between Yi Seong-gye and the Neo-Confucian reformers developed gradually over the 1380s rather than emerging suddenly, and should not be read backward as a settled plan to found a new dynasty from an early stage. Through most of this period, the alliance's stated aims concerned reforming Goryeo governance, curbing the power of rival aristocratic and military factions, and addressing the land and status crises described in Section 24.8, not replacing the Wang royal house itself. The foreign-policy crisis of 1388, examined next, would transform this reform coalition's trajectory decisively.

Historical Vocabulary
The "Four Arguments" against the Liaodong Campaign

Definition: Later Korean historical writing, including chronicles compiled under the succeeding Joseon dynasty, attributes to Yi Seong-gye a set of arguments, often summarized as "four difficulties" or "four reasons," against the 1388 campaign to invade Ming-held Liaodong: that a small state should not attack a large one; that campaigning during the summer agricultural season would harm farming; that a mass mobilization northward would leave the southern coast dangerously exposed to wokou raids; and that summer heat and rain would encourage disease and weaken bowstrings and other equipment.

Historical context: These arguments appear in sources compiled after Yi Seong-gye's dynasty had already been founded, which means later chroniclers had an interest in presenting his 1388 decision as prudent, well-reasoned, and nearly inevitable in hindsight.

Why it matters: The arguments plausibly reflect genuine strategic and logistical concerns of the period, since a Goryeo campaign against a large, newly consolidated Ming state during harvest season carried real risks along multiple fronts discussed elsewhere in this chapter, including the wokou threat.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat the "four arguments" as a verbatim transcript of Yi Seong-gye's private reasoning in 1388. They are a later historiographical formulation, useful for understanding the strategic logic later writers wished to emphasize, but not equivalent to a contemporary record of his exact words or motives.

Section 24.11

Between a Falling Yuan and a Rising Ming

The proclamation of the Ming dynasty in 1368 and the subsequent collapse of Yuan authority across China proper, described briefly in Section 24.2, transformed the regional order within which Goryeo had operated for over a century. Goryeo's court initially maintained relations with both the retreating Yuan remnant, which continued to hold power in Mongolia and parts of the northern frontier as the so-called Northern Yuan, and the newly established Ming state, whose claim to imperial legitimacy and territorial reach remained, in the 1370s and 1380s, still being tested and defined.

This dual relationship produced a genuine and consequential division within the Goryeo court over foreign policy. One position, associated most strongly with senior figures including Choe Yeong, favored continued caution toward, or even alignment with, Northern Yuan remnants and resistance to Ming territorial claims into the old Yuan northeastern frontier, territory that included areas Goryeo itself had interests in. A different position, increasingly associated with Neo-Confucian reform officials including Jeong Do-jeon, favored accommodation with the clearly ascendant Ming as the more realistic and, in Neo-Confucian ideological terms, more legitimate imperial successor to the Yuan mandate. This division was not merely a technical disagreement about which distant power to court diplomatically; it connected directly to the domestic contest between the old military and aristocratic order represented by figures such as Choe Yeong and the reform coalition built around Jeong Do-jeon and, increasingly, Yi Seong-gye.

Matters came to a head in early 1388, when Ming notified the Goryeo court of its intention to establish an administrative post, the Cheollyeong Guard (known in Chinese as the Tieling Wei), and to claim jurisdiction over territory north of Cheollyeong that included land Goryeo regarded as rightfully its own following the earlier recovery of Ssangseong. This specific notification, rather than Ming-Goryeo tension in general, was the immediate trigger for the crisis that followed. Rather than negotiate, the Goryeo court under King U and Choe Yeong's direction resolved to preempt Ming consolidation of the region through a direct military campaign against Liaodong, setting the stage for the crisis examined in the next section.

Section 24.12

The Liaodong Campaign and the Wihwado Retreat

In the spring of 1388, the Goryeo court ordered a major military campaign against Ming-held territory in Liaodong, with Choe Yeong as supreme commander and Yi Seong-gye and Jo Min-su leading the right and left armies of the expeditionary force. Yi Seong-gye had opposed the campaign from the outset, advancing arguments along the lines later summarized in the "four difficulties" discussed above: that Goryeo lacked the resources to fight a large, newly consolidated Ming state successfully; that a summer campaign would damage agricultural production; that mobilizing forces northward would leave the wokou-vulnerable southern coast dangerously exposed; and that the season posed serious logistical and health risks to the army. The king and Choe Yeong overruled these objections and ordered the campaign to proceed.

Yi Seong-gye led his portion of the invasion force north to Wihwado, an island in the Amnok River near the Ming frontier, but halted there rather than crossing into Liaodong. After further deliberation among his commanders, he made the decisive choice to turn the army around and march it back toward the Goryeo capital at Gaegyeong instead of continuing the campaign. This decision, remembered in Korean historical tradition as the Wihwado Retreat, was a direct act of insubordination against royal command, and it should be understood clearly as such rather than softened into a merely administrative or logistical adjustment.

Upon reaching Gaegyeong, Yi Seong-gye's forces, joined by Jo Min-su's, overpowered the capital's defenses, arrested Choe Yeong, who was subsequently exiled and executed, and forced the deposition of King U. This sequence of events amounted to a military coup that destroyed Choe Yeong's dominance at court, but it did not by itself settle who would control Goryeo's government afterward; it opened a new and, for a time, genuinely uncertain contest for power among the victors. It is important for students to recognize that the events of 1388 did not yet abolish the Goryeo dynasty; the Wang royal house remained formally on the throne, and no new dynasty had been proclaimed. What had changed was the underlying distribution of real political and military power, a change whose full consequences would unfold, unevenly and not without contest, over the following four years, as Section 24.13 explains.

Section 24.13

The Last Kings and the Purge of the Old Order

The immediate aftermath of the Wihwado Retreat did not deliver uncontested power to Yi Seong-gye. In the weeks following the coup, Jo Min-su, who had commanded the left army alongside Yi Seong-gye during the aborted campaign, emerged as a rival claimant to leadership among the victors. Working together with the senior and widely respected scholar-official Yi Saek, Jo Min-su backed the succession of King U's young son as King Chang, a choice that preserved continuity with the deposed king's line and implicitly resisted the more radical position, associated with Yi Seong-gye and his closest allies, that the succession through King U should be set aside altogether. King Chang's accession in 1388 should therefore be read as evidence that Yi Seong-gye had not yet secured unchallenged control of the government in the immediate aftermath of Wihwado, whatever the balance of military force ultimately favored.

That balance shifted over the following year. Jo Min-su, who had opposed the land reform proposals advanced by Jo Jun and allied officials, was impeached and exiled in 1389, and through that year Yi Seong-gye's faction, together with Jeong Do-jeon and allied Neo-Confucian officials, steadily overcame the remaining resistance associated with Jo Min-su and Yi Saek's camp. King Chang himself was deposed after little more than a year on the throne, in 1389, amid accusations, encouraged by Yi Seong-gye's faction, that the preceding King U's paternity, and therefore the legitimacy of the succession through his line, was in question. He was replaced by King Gongyang, a member of a collateral branch of the royal family whose room for independent action narrowed as competing factions struggled around the throne, even as Yi Seong-gye's coalition grew increasingly dominant, though not yet uncontested, over the following three years.

During Gongyang's reign, Jeong Do-jeon and allied officials, most notably Jo Jun, whose detailed proposals shaped the reform's specific provisions, carried out the sweeping land reform that Sin Don's earlier commission had attempted but failed to complete, most significantly through the Gwajeon Law (Gwajeonbeop) enacted in 1391. Students should understand that gwajeon were principally graded rights to collect land tax (sujo-gwon) from specified plots, distributed to officials and soldiers in place of salary, rather than grants of privately owned farmland; the reform's significance lay in reassigning who held these tax-collection rights and sharply restricting the private, hereditary accumulation of such rights that had characterized the gwonmun sejok families' position. This reduced the scope for private aristocratic estate accumulation and rebuilt the fiscal base available to support the state's own officials and military. This reform directly addressed the structural land and status crisis described in Section 24.8 and represented the reform coalition's most substantial concrete achievement prior to the dynasty's formal end. Alongside land reform, Yi Seong-gye's faction conducted a series of political purges, exiling or executing prominent officials associated with the old aristocratic and pro-Yuan order, including figures who had remained loyal to the Wang royal house and resisted the reform coalition's growing dominance.

The struggle remained genuinely unresolved as late as 1392. Early that year, Yi Seong-gye was seriously injured in a riding accident while returning from a hunting expedition, and Jeong Mong-ju moved decisively to exploit his temporary incapacity. Working through the Gongyang court, Jeong Mong-ju secured the impeachment and exile or removal from office of several of Yi Seong-gye's closest allies, including Jo Jun and, for a time, Jeong Do-jeon himself, in a serious political counteroffensive that briefly put the reform coalition on the defensive. This episode demonstrates that the outcome of the succession struggle was not yet settled even in its final months, and that Gongyang's court retained enough independent capacity to act forcefully against Yi Seong-gye's faction when circumstances allowed.

Among those targeted in Jeong Mong-ju's brief counteroffensive was Jeong Do-jeon, though Jeong Mong-ju himself, a highly respected Neo-Confucian scholar-official who had supported earlier reform efforts alongside Jeong Do-jeon, remained, unlike Jeong Do-jeon, committed to preserving the Wang dynasty rather than replacing it outright. Jeong Mong-ju's assassination in 1392, arranged by Yi Seong-gye's son once Yi Seong-gye himself had recovered enough to respond, reversed this setback, removed the most prominent remaining voice of principled loyalist opposition, and cleared the final significant obstacle to a formal dynastic change. His death should therefore be understood as the resolution of a genuine and, in its final months, renewed political struggle, not merely the elimination of an isolated symbolic holdout. It also illustrates an important point this chapter has emphasized throughout: the reform coalition that gathered around Yi Seong-gye and Jeong Do-jeon was not unanimous in its ultimate aims, and some of its most capable members sought to reform Goryeo rather than end it.

Section 24.14

The Fall of Goryeo, 1392

With Jeong Mong-ju removed and the old aristocratic order substantially weakened and restructured through land reform and political purge, King Gongyang was deposed in the summer of 1392, and Yi Seong-gye ascended the throne, ending the dynasty Wang Geon had founded in 918 and that had governed the Korean peninsula, with the interruptions and transformations examined throughout Part IV, for nearly four hundred and seventy-five years. The formal change of dynasty in 1392 should be understood as the culmination of the process this chapter has traced, rather than as a sudden or freestanding event. The Wihwado Retreat had given Yi Seong-gye decisive military leverage, but full political supremacy was consolidated only gradually, and not without setbacks, through the successive struggles of 1388 to 1392: the contested accession of King Chang against Jo Min-su and Yi Saek, the deposition of Chang and installation of Gongyang, and Jeong Mong-ju's brief but serious 1392 counteroffensive against Yi Seong-gye's closest allies. Only after removing the individuals, most notably Jeong Mong-ju, whose continued loyalty to the Wang line represented the most serious remaining obstacle to formal dynastic change, did that consolidation become complete.

This chapter has resisted treating Goryeo's fall as the inevitable result of any single cause. The weakening of Yuan power created opportunity and danger together, opening space for Gongmin's reforms while also unleashing the Red Turban and wokou crises that exposed Goryeo's military weakness. Structural problems in landholding and social status, present well before Gongmin's reign and unresolved by Sin Don's failed reform commission, continued to erode the state's fiscal and social foundations throughout the period. Neo-Confucian intellectual and bureaucratic change supplied a coherent critique of the existing order and a program for restructuring it, but that program required Yi Seong-gye's military power to become politically effective. And the specific foreign-policy crisis produced by Ming ascendancy in 1388 supplied the immediate occasion on which these long-accumulating pressures converged into decisive political action. No one of these developments by itself would necessarily have produced the dynasty's end; their convergence did.

Chapter 25 takes up the different and equally demanding task facing Yi Seong-gye and his allies after 1392: transforming a successful seizure of power into a durable, legitimate, and institutionally coherent new state. Founding a dynasty and governing one are not the same accomplishment, and the new Joseon order's ideological foundations, capital, administrative structure, and relationship to the social and economic problems this chapter has described remained to be built.

Historical Debate

Why Did Goryeo Fall?

The question: Historians have offered different emphases when explaining why Goryeo, a dynasty with centuries of institutional continuity and real cultural achievement, ended in 1392 rather than reforming itself into a durable state under continued Wang rule. Should the dynasty's fall be explained primarily as the result of external pressures, primarily as the result of internal structural failure, or primarily as the result of the specific political choices made by Yi Seong-gye and his allies in 1388?

Interpretation A — External pressure as the primary driver: Some historians emphasize that the extraordinary sequence of external shocks Goryeo absorbed in a short span, Yuan collapse, Red Turban invasion, escalating wokou raiding, and the rise of Ming power, would have severely tested any premodern state, regardless of its internal condition, and that these pressures created the military emergencies from which Yi Seong-gye's power ultimately grew.
Interpretation B — Internal structural failure as the primary driver: Other historians emphasize that the land concentration, status abuse, and elite capture of government described in Section 24.8 had been building for generations independent of any specific external shock, and that these internal weaknesses explain why Goryeo proved unable to convert external pressure into a successful reform, as opposed to a dynastic replacement, the way some other premodern states managed comparable crises.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same documented sequence of events described throughout this chapter, but differ in how much explanatory weight to assign to forces largely outside Goryeo's control versus forces that had developed within Goryeo's own institutions over a much longer period.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily causation and relative weighting rather than the basic chronology, which is not seriously disputed. Historians broadly agree on what happened; they disagree on which factors should be considered decisive.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these factors shapes how they interpret Yi Seong-gye's rise: as an opportunistic seizure of power made possible primarily by external crisis, as the natural outcome of a domestic reform movement whose logic increasingly pointed beyond reform of the existing dynasty toward its replacement, or as a convergence that resists reduction to either factor alone.

Student task: Using specific evidence from this chapter, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which factors you find most persuasive, while considering what the convergence of both kinds of pressure in the same narrow window of decades might itself suggest about how major dynastic transitions occur.

  • Military reputation, built through campaigns against Red Turban invaders and wokou raiders, became an increasingly important source of political influence by the 1380s, though it did not simply replace lineage and court connection as bases of power
  • Neo-Confucian scholar-officials moved from a marginal intellectual current to a central political force allied with military power
  • The Wang royal house itself, after 1392, ceased to hold the throne for the first time since 918

What Stayed the Same

  • Land concentration among powerful families and Buddhist institutions remained a central structural problem from before Gongmin's reign through Sin Don's failed reform and beyond, until the Gwajeon reform of 1391
  • Coastal and northern frontier vulnerability persisted as a recurring feature of Goryeo's final decades
  • Court politics continued to be shaped by factional competition among elite families and officials, even as the specific factions and their sources of power changed
  • The underlying administrative institutions described in Chapter 20 continued in modified form into the successor Joseon state rather than being invented from nothing

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating 1392 as a total historical rupture. Many of the problems that destabilized Goryeo, and some of the administrative tools used to address them, carried forward into the new dynasty examined in Chapter 25.

Cause and Effect

From Yuan Decline to Dynastic Fall

Long-term causes

Land concentration among gwonmun sejok families and Buddhist monasteries, weakened royal fiscal capacity, and Goryeo's century-long integration into and dependence on the Yuan imperial order.

Short-term causes

Yuan collapse and the Red Turban invasions of 1359–1361, escalating wokou raiding from the 1350s onward, the failure of Sin Don's reform commission, and the rise of Ming power after 1368.

Triggering event

The 1388 order to invade Ming-held Liaodong and Yi Seong-gye's decision to halt and reverse his army at Wihwado rather than obey.

Effects, 1388–1392

Seizure of political control, sweeping land reform under Jeong Do-jeon, purges of loyalist officials including Jeong Mong-ju, and the formal enthronement of Yi Seong-gye in 1392.

Historical complexity: No single cause in this chain was sufficient by itself to end the dynasty. Yuan decline without domestic land crisis might have permitted successful royal reform; domestic crisis without the specific 1388 foreign-policy dispute might have produced continued instability without a clear turning point; and Yi Seong-gye's military reputation without Neo-Confucian ideological and administrative allies might have produced a military strongman without the institutional transformation that followed.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Experience amid Elite Political Crisis

The political drama of court reform, invasion, and dynastic transition examined in this chapter unfolded against a background of considerable hardship for ordinary Goryeo households, particularly along the coasts and in the contested northern frontier. A farming family whose fields lay near a major transport route might see grain shipments seized or destroyed by wokou raiders in one season and face renewed tax demands the next, regardless of whether the harvest had survived. A fishing household near a frequently raided coastal town faced the choice between abandoning ancestral livelihoods for safer inland settlement or continuing to risk periodic violence and captivity. A smallholding farmer whose land bordered a powerful family's growing estate faced a slow, often legally ambiguous process of pressure toward tenancy or bonded status that Sin Don's short-lived reform commission attempted, without full success, to reverse.

Military mobilization touched ordinary life as well. Campaigns against Red Turban invaders, wokou raiders, and eventually the Liaodong expedition of 1388 all required manpower drawn substantially from the general population, whose service, transport labor, and provisioning obligations increased even as agricultural production in affected regions declined. Because surviving written sources from this period were overwhelmingly produced by court officials, Buddhist clergy, and later Joseon-era historians with their own interpretive interests, historians must read these sources carefully alongside archaeological and later comparative evidence to reconstruct how ordinary cultivators, fishers, soldiers, and bonded laborers actually experienced this turbulent final generation of Goryeo rule.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained why Goryeo, a dynasty with nearly four hundred and seventy-five years of institutional continuity, ended in 1392 rather than reforming itself into a durable state under continued Wang rule. The chapter began with the inherited situation from Chapter 23: a kingdom formally independent but closely supervised by Yuan authority, its politics dominated by powerful landholding families whose fortunes had grown under Yuan-era conditions, and its northeastern territory of Ssangseong under direct Yuan administration.

The weakening of Yuan power in the fourteenth century, driven by court factionalism, fiscal exhaustion, and rebellion across Yuan China, created genuine opportunity for King Gongmin's reform program. Gongmin successfully recovered the Ssangseong Commandery in 1356 and reduced formal Yuan oversight of Goryeo governance, but his efforts to address the deeper domestic problem of land concentration among powerful families proved far more difficult, as the failure and eventual execution of his reform agent Sin Don demonstrated.

At the same time, the collapse of Yuan authority exported new dangers directly toward Goryeo. Red Turban rebel forces invaded twice in 1359 and 1361, briefly occupying the capital, while escalating wokou raiding along the southern and western coasts disrupted grain transport, devastated coastal communities, and steadily eroded state finances throughout the following decades. These crises elevated successful military commanders, above all Yi Seong-gye, to a level of political importance that inherited aristocratic standing alone increasingly could not match.

Underlying both the reform efforts and the military crises was a persistent structural problem: land concentration among powerful families and Buddhist monasteries had eroded the state's fiscal base and pushed increasing numbers of ordinary cultivators into tenancy or bonded status. This problem, diagnosed but not solved by Sin Don's reform commission, would only be addressed through the sweeping Gwajeon land reform carried out after 1388.

A parallel intellectual development gave this structural crisis political direction. Neo-Confucian scholar-officials, most importantly Jeong Do-jeon, developed a systematic critique of Goryeo's land system and Buddhist institutional power, arguing for a thoroughgoing restructuring of governance along Confucian principles. This program found in Yi Seong-gye's growing military reputation the coercive power it had previously lacked.

The immediate catalyst for dynastic change came from foreign policy. The rise of the Ming dynasty after 1368 forced Goryeo's court to define its relationship with a new regional power, and the resulting dispute, pitting Choe Yeong's push for a preemptive campaign against Ming-held Liaodong against Yi Seong-gye's strategic objections, produced the crisis of 1388. Yi Seong-gye's decision to halt his invading army at Wihwado and march it back to seize control of the government transformed this foreign-policy dispute into a domestic political revolution.

The four years between the Wihwado Retreat and the formal end of the dynasty in 1392 were spent consolidating this new distribution of power through a genuinely contested political struggle, not a smooth or uncontested transition: the accession of King Chang under the rival sponsorship of Jo Min-su and Yi Saek, the installation of King Gongyang, the enactment of the Gwajeon land reform, the purging of officials associated with the old order, and, in early 1392, Jeong Mong-ju's brief counteroffensive against Yi Seong-gye's closest allies before his own assassination eliminated the last significant voice of principled loyalist opposition. Only after these steps had been taken did Yi Seong-gye ascend the throne, ending the dynasty Wang Geon had founded in 918.

This chapter has emphasized throughout that no single cause explains Goryeo's fall. External pressure from Yuan collapse, Red Turban invasion, and wokou raiding; internal structural crisis in landholding and social status; the rise of a new intellectual and bureaucratic reforming elite; and a specific foreign-policy dispute over Liaodong all converged within a single narrow generation. Removing any one of these factors makes the specific outcome of 1392 considerably less certain, a point students should carry forward into Chapter 25's account of how the new Joseon state was built from the political, institutional, and social material this chapter has described.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced how a centuries-old dynasty ended not through a single catastrophic defeat but through the slow convergence of external upheaval, internal structural crisis, intellectual change, and a specific foreign-policy dispute that gave a rising military commander the occasion to act decisively. The habits of mind this chapter has emphasized, resisting monocausal explanation, distinguishing a seizure of power from the consolidation of a new legitimate order, and recognizing that removing an external constraint exposes rather than automatically resolves internal weakness, are patterns students should carry forward into the study of other major political transitions throughout this textbook.

Goryeo's final decades reveal a dynasty capable of genuine reform ambition, visible in Gongmin's recovery of Ssangseong and Sin Don's land investigation, but unable to overcome the entrenched interests that reform needed to confront using the institutional tools available to it. When military crisis, ideological change, and a favorable foreign-policy occasion converged in the 1380s, the coalition that had originally sought to reform Goryeo found itself positioned, and in the case of Jeong Do-jeon's faction increasingly willing, to replace it instead. Jeong Mong-ju's fate stands as a reminder that this outcome was not shared or desired by every reformer who had worked toward the same general goals.

Students who understand why Goryeo fell, not simply that it fell, are well equipped to read Chapter 25's account of how Yi Seong-gye and his allies transformed a successful political revolution into the foundations of a new Neo-Confucian dynastic order. The land reform, the purges, and the alliance between military power and Confucian scholarship examined in this chapter did not disappear in 1392; they became the raw material out of which Joseon's early institutions, examined beginning in Volume II, were built.

The story of Goryeo's fall closes Volume I of this textbook's continuous historical narrative, extending from the peninsula's earliest prehistoric societies through the ancient kingdoms, Silla and Balhae, and nearly five centuries of Goryeo rule. It is a story of real cultural, religious, and institutional achievement, described throughout Part IV, brought to an end not by any single cause but by the specific, contingent convergence of pressures examined in this chapter. Understanding that convergence prepares students to follow Volume II's account of the very different order that Yi Seong-gye and his successors would attempt to build in Goryeo's place.
