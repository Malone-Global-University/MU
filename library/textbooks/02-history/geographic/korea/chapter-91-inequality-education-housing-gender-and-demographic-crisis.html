Inequality, Education, Housing, Gender, and Demographic Crisis

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Seoul in the 2020s is among the wealthiest, best-educated, and most technologically connected cities in the world. Yet for many of its young adults the central questions of life remain unresolved: whether a stable job will materialize, whether a rental deposit can ever grow into a home, and whether marriage and children can be afforded in money, time, and career. This chapter asks how a society that achieved so much produced so much uncertainty alongside it.

Consider an illustrative composite, not a documented individual: a woman or man in the early thirties, a graduate of a respectable university, living in a small rented apartment in the capital region. The person may have spent years in school and private academies, months preparing for employment examinations, and more months in temporary or contract positions. A large share of savings may be committed to a rental deposit that, under the distinctive Korean jeonse arrangement, is a refundable lump-sum deposit paid to the landlord, economically akin to providing interest-free financing but legally governed by a tenancy agreement and repayment obligation. Parents may still be helping, or may be unable to help at all. Such a life is not universal, and it is not necessarily miserable. But variations of it recur so often in surveys, journalism, and policy debate that they have become a defining social reference point of twenty-first-century South Korea.

This chapter treats that experience historically. Chapters 87 through 90 described South Korea's technological and cultural achievements: semiconductors, broadband, smartphones, and the global reach of Korean popular culture. Those achievements rested on a society that had already been transformed between the 1950s and 1990s, as Chapters 75 through 81 explained, by state-led industrialization, mass schooling, urban migration, and apartment construction. Chapter 91 asks about the social conditions that accompanied that success, and about who gained, who bore costs, and how the distribution of opportunity changed after the democratization of 1987 and the financial crisis of 1997–1998.

The chapter's central question is why extraordinary economic and educational development did not produce equally secure opportunities for all citizens, and how inequalities in employment, housing, education, and gender relations shaped family formation, fertility, aging, and the country's demographic future. Its argument is that these issues are interconnected but not reducible to one explanation. Housing insecurity does not affect owners, renters, and prospective buyers alike. Educational competition produced remarkable achievement and also unequal access to the preparation that leads to prestige. Declines in marriage and fertility reflect economic constraints, institutional arrangements for work and care, and evolving personal preferences, in proportions that historians and social scientists continue to debate.

The chapter also resists a simple story of uninterrupted decline. South Korea's total fertility rate fell to 0.72 in 2023, the lowest figure in the country's recorded history, then rose to 0.75 in 2024 and 0.80 in 2025, according to official statistics, while remaining exceptionally low by any international comparison. About 254,300 births were recorded in 2025. Recovery from a trough is historically significant, yet it does not by itself show that deeper structural pressures have eased. Students should learn to hold both observations together, and to ask what time series, definitions, and denominators a particular claim relies upon.

The evidence for this chapter is unusually statistical. Government censuses and surveys, international comparisons compiled by the OECD, legislative budget analyses, court rulings, newspaper reporting, and the testimony of civic organizations all contribute. Each has strengths and limits. A statistic about private tutoring depends on who was surveyed; a statistic about regional concentration depends on whether the source is a census or a resident-registration count; a statistic about poverty depends on whether the definition is relative or absolute. Six Primary Source Windows in this chapter train students to read such documents critically, and a seventh kind of evidence, public frustration expressed in slang and politics, is treated as something to explain rather than as proof of any particular measurement.

Finally, the chapter is part of this textbook's treatment of South Korea as a distinctive form of capitalist modernity, as set out in the outline for Volume IV, and it prepares the ground for Chapter 92 on North Korea under Kim Jong Un and for the comparative synthesis of Chapter 96. Inequality, schooling, housing, and family are not side issues to the political and diplomatic history of the peninsula. They determine how citizens experience their state and what kind of society confronts the geopolitical questions examined in Chapters 93 through 95. The chapter's endpoint is November 2026, and the final section is explicit about the limits of what can be known about the immediate present.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish absolute improvements in living standards from relative inequality in income, wealth, occupation, and access to opportunity.
  2. Explain labor-market dualism, and how chaebol, public-sector, and small-business employment came to differ in pay, security, and benefits.
  3. Analyze how the 1997–1998 financial crisis and later restructuring contributed to non-regular employment and youth insecurity.
  4. Differentiate income inequality from wealth inequality, and assess the evidence for the role of family background in opportunity.
  5. Trace the development of South Korean social protection from the 1980s to 2026 and distinguish poverty measures from benefit eligibility and receipt.
  6. Evaluate the educational system as both a mechanism of mobility and a mechanism through which advantage may be reproduced.
  7. Explain the Suneung, university admissions tracks, and the private-tutoring economy, using survey data with correct denominators.
  8. Explain how apartments, jeonse, and homeownership turned housing into a principal source of wealth inequality.
  9. Assess the causes and consequences of metropolitan concentration and regional decline.
  10. Analyze gender inequality in work, care, and public life, distinguishing discrimination, unequal caregiving, policy disagreement, and criminal abuse.
  11. Interpret fertility, marriage, and aging statistics, including the 2023–2025 partial recovery, and compare competing explanations of low fertility.
  12. Distinguish policies announced, enacted, implemented, and independently shown to have worked, and recognize the limits of demographic projection.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why can a society become far wealthier while many of its members feel less secure?
  2. How did the division between protected and less-protected workers arise, and what historical decisions sustained it?
  3. What evidence is needed to show that an education system promotes mobility rather than reproducing advantage?
  4. Why did housing become simultaneously a home, an investment, and a marker of status, and who benefited from this?
  5. How did the concentration of jobs, universities, and wealth in the capital region change life in other regions?
  6. Why did greater female education not immediately produce equal employment outcomes or equal division of care?
  7. How should historians distinguish documented discrimination, political conflict, and criminal sexual abuse in discussions of gender?
  8. Is low fertility best understood as an economic, an institutional, or a cultural phenomenon, and why might the question be badly posed?
  9. What does the 2024–2025 rise in births and marriages show, and what can it not show?
  10. What would it mean for prosperity to be broadly accessible, and by what indicators could historians judge it?

Historical Context

South Korea emerged from the Korean War of 1950–1953 as one of the poorest societies in the world, with devastated cities, widespread rural poverty, and limited industry. Over the next four decades, the developmental state examined in Chapters 75 and 76 directed credit toward export industries and large conglomerates, while Chapter 81 traced the growth of factories, apartment complexes, and schools. Literacy became nearly universal, rural populations moved to cities, and living standards, life expectancy, and consumption rose dramatically. The scale of this transformation is the essential background: complaints about inequality in contemporary South Korea occur within a high-income, long-lived, highly educated society, and they cannot be understood as the grievances of a society that failed to develop.

The political transition of 1987, examined in Chapter 82, created conditions for open debate about social rights. Labor unions, women's organizations, and civic groups gained space they had lacked under authoritarian rule. Welfare institutions that had been limited expanded: universal health insurance reached the whole population by 1989, the National Pension launched in 1988 and was extended later, and an employment-insurance system was introduced in 1995. Yet the system was built during a period when most adult men were expected to hold continuous employment with a single firm and when families were expected to support the elderly and the unemployed. Those assumptions began to weaken before the institutions had been fully adapted to the change.

The financial crisis of 1997–1998, treated at the level of crisis management in Chapter 84, was the hinge. Corporate bankruptcies, layoffs, and IMF-supervised restructuring ended the expectation of lifetime employment for many workers, expanded the use of temporary and contract labor, and revealed how little public protection existed for those outside secure jobs. In response, governments enacted new welfare legislation, including the National Basic Livelihood Security system implemented in 2000. This chapter examines the longer social consequences of that period: a labor market with sharply different tiers, a generation that came of age expecting both rising incomes and secure careers and found a narrower path, and rising anxieties about housing and family formation.

Two further structural processes shaped the twenty-first century. The first was the extraordinary growth in tertiary education, which made a university degree a mass expectation and then a baseline rather than a distinction. The second was the rapid decline in fertility, which began long before the crisis: South Korea's fertility fell below the replacement level during the 1980s, and by 2018 the total fertility rate had dropped below 1.0. Together with rising longevity, this meant that the country aged faster than almost any other society for which comparable data exist. The geographic setting for these changes was increasingly the Seoul capital region, which by the 2025 register-based census held just over half of the national population.

Section 91.1

The Paradox of South Korean Prosperity: Growth, Opportunity, and Inequality

The starting point for any account of South Korean society must be the magnitude of improvement. In the early 1950s, the country was among the poorest in the world, a large majority of its people lived by farming, and life expectancy was far below that of industrial societies. By the 2020s, life expectancy had risen to roughly eighty-three years on official estimates, nearly every young person completed secondary school, a large majority entered tertiary education, and the country ranked among the world's high-income economies and leading producers of advanced technology. Households that once measured wealth in food security now commonly possess automobiles, smartphones, and universal health insurance. Any argument about inequality that ignores these absolute gains misrepresents the historical record.

Absolute improvement, however, is not the same as relative equality, and neither is the same as security. Historians and social scientists distinguish at least four dimensions. Income inequality concerns differences in annual earnings. Wealth inequality concerns differences in accumulated assets, above all property. Occupational inequality concerns differences in job security, benefits, and status. Inequality of opportunity concerns whether a child's prospects depend on the family into which that child was born. A society can score well on some dimensions and poorly on others, and South Korea's profile has been uneven. Measured income inequality after taxes and transfers has been moderate by international standards, while relative poverty among older people, wealth concentrated in housing, and wage gaps by gender and employment type have been high.

This chapter's organizing argument is that economic growth delivered large social benefits but did not automatically deliver housing security, predictable employment, family stability, or equal life chances. The mechanism is not mysterious. Rapid growth under the developmental state concentrated rewards in export-oriented firms and in the urban housing market, while social protection lagged behind. When growth slowed after the late 1990s and the number of educated entrants into the workforce rose, the same arrangements that had once spread gains widely began to sort people more sharply. A degree was now necessary but not sufficient; a stable job was prized because it was scarce; a home became both necessary and expensive.

A second point concerns perception. People judge their circumstances against expectations and against their peers, not against their grandparents. A generation that watched its parents move from tenant farming to apartment ownership within one working life could reasonably expect similar progress, and a slowing of that trajectory could be experienced as loss even when absolute conditions were far better than in the past. This does not mean public frustration is merely subjective. It means that historians must use measured indicators, such as wages, asset holdings, and mobility data, to test claims, rather than inferring inequality from sentiment alone, while also treating sentiment as historical evidence about how people understood their situation.

Section 91.2

Chaebols, Small Businesses, and the Dual Labor Market

South Korea's industrial structure, shaped by the policies described in Chapters 75 and 76, left a legacy of two very different employment worlds. At one pole stood large corporations, notably the chaebols such as Samsung, Hyundai, SK, and LG, together with public institutions and the civil service. These offered relatively high wages, bonuses, company benefits, social insurance coverage, strong union presence in some sectors, and long employment tenures. At the other pole stood the great majority of firms: small and medium-sized enterprises, family businesses, and the self-employed. Together these employ most of the workforce, but their wages, benefits, job stability, and capacity to withstand downturns are on average far lower.

Economists call this arrangement a dual labor market. The term does not mean that workers in the lower tier are less capable. It means that the institutions surrounding the two tiers differ. Large firms had access to cheap credit and stable export contracts, which allowed them to offer secure careers. Smaller firms, many of them subcontractors supplying chaebol assembly lines, often faced price pressure from their buyers and could not match large-firm pay. Company-level unions, which are the dominant form of organization in South Korea, strengthened the position of workers inside large firms while leaving workers elsewhere largely unrepresented. The gap in wages, welfare coverage, and tenure between tiers therefore reflected institutions and bargaining power, not simply productivity differences among individuals.

Historical Vocabulary
Dual Labor Market

Definition. A labor market divided into a protected tier with relatively high pay, employment security, and benefits, and a less-protected tier with lower pay, weaker security, and thinner benefits, with limited movement between tiers.

Historical context. In South Korea the division is associated with firm size, regular versus non-regular contracts, and subcontracting chains. It deepened after 1997–1998, when many firms replaced departing regular workers with contract labor.

Why it matters. The term explains why national productivity growth, concentrated in a few export industries, did not raise conditions equally throughout the economy, and why the first job so strongly shapes later career paths.

Potential misunderstanding. Dualism does not mean that all large-firm workers are comfortable or that all small-firm workers are insecure; many small firms pay well and many large-firm employees experience layoffs and long hours.

These institutional differences shaped aspirations. Because large-company and public-sector jobs offered both income and social standing, they became the prize toward which education was directed. Large firms held annual graduate-recruitment cycles, often drawing disproportionately from a small number of universities, and civil-service examinations attracted tens of thousands of candidates for a limited number of posts. Young people who might otherwise have accepted jobs at smaller firms instead extended their preparation, sometimes for years, rather than enter a labor tier from which advancement could be difficult. The dual market thereby transmitted itself backward into education and forward into marriage prospects, since a stable corporate or public post was widely regarded as a precondition for family life.

The persistence of dualism challenges a simple link between productivity and wages. South Korea's most successful sectors, including semiconductors, discussed in Chapter 87, employ relatively few workers compared with their output, while service sectors, retail, and small-scale manufacturing employ many more at lower productivity. When the gains of the first group accrue mainly to its shareholders and employees, as they did, the rest of the economy sees a much smaller benefit. Policy responses, including support for small-firm wages and restrictions on certain subcontracting practices, have had a modest effect on the structure, and the OECD and Korean economists have repeatedly returned to the issue.

Section 91.3

Non-Regular Employment, Youth Insecurity, and the Changing Meaning of Work

Official labor surveys distinguish regular workers, who hold open-ended contracts, from non-regular workers, a category that includes fixed-term contracts, part-time workers, and dispatched, subcontracted, or on-call arrangements. In recent official surveys non-regular workers have represented 38.2 percent of wage employees in August 2025 (about 8.56 million) and 38.5 percent in August 2026 (about 8.67 million), according to the annual supplementary labor-force survey. These percentages apply to wage employees, not to every employed or self-employed person; definitions and survey classification affect comparisons, and labor organizations have argued that a broader classification would yield a higher share. Platform and gig work, including delivery riders and ride-hailing drivers, added further categories whose legal status as employees or independent contractors has been contested.

The expansion of non-regular work is closely associated with the 1997–1998 crisis, though it did not begin there. Legislation in 1998 permitted dispatched labor, and employers facing uncertainty and pressure to cut costs learned that temporary workers could be hired and released at lower cost than regular workers, whose dismissal was legally difficult. A 2007 law restricted the use of fixed-term contracts beyond two years, intending to push employers to convert workers to permanent status; employers in some cases instead released workers before the limit or outsourced the work. The policy example illustrates a recurring theme: reforms that address one margin of the labor market may shift employers to another.

The experience of young adults is central. Many pass through a long sequence of university study, language and certification preparation, internships, examination study, temporary employment, and eventually, for some, a regular post. Statisticians and historians distinguish several conditions often conflated in public discussion. Unemployment counts those actively seeking work. Underemployment includes those working fewer hours than they wish or in jobs below their qualifications. Labor-force inactivity includes people who are preparing for examinations or have stopped searching. In South Korea, a substantial share of young people who are not employed fall into the latter category rather than into measured unemployment, so a low headline unemployment rate can coexist with widespread frustration.

The relationship between work and household formation is direct. Job insecurity limits the capacity to save a deposit, borrow for housing, and commit to long-term obligations. Marriage, which in South Korea remains closely tied to childbearing, is frequently postponed until financial stability is reached. At the same time, historians must avoid portraying all non-regular employment as involuntary or uniformly precarious. Some workers choose part-time or contract work for flexibility, for caring responsibilities, or for specialized professional reasons, and some non-regular professionals have substantial bargaining power. The distinction lies in differences of bargaining power, social insurance coverage, and ability to move to better positions, not in the contract form alone. Evidence shows that workers who begin in non-regular positions are less likely than others to move into regular ones, which is why the category matters.

Section 91.4

Wealth, Family Background, and the Unequal Distribution of Opportunity

As incomes grew and property values rose, accumulated assets, especially real estate, became a more important differentiator of households than annual earnings. The distinction between income and wealth is essential. A household with moderate current income may hold valuable property bought decades earlier, while a young professional may earn a high salary yet have little accumulated wealth and substantial debt. Studies relying on tax and financial records find wealth much more unequally distributed than income in South Korea, as in most countries, and housing makes up a very large share of household assets.

Family background shapes opportunity through several channels: direct financial support for education and housing, access to information and networks, the quality of neighborhood schools, the cost of private tutoring, and inherited property. Parents who bought apartments in the 1980s and 1990s, in areas that later appreciated, were often able to help their children with deposits or down payments. Parents who rented, or who held assets outside the main appreciation zones, could not. Such differences did not require any deliberate exclusion; they arose from the timing and location of earlier investment, compounded by an educational system in which resources purchase preparation.

Historical Vocabulary
Gold Spoon and Dirt Spoon (geumsujeo, heuksujeo)

Definition. Popular slang, widespread in the 2010s, that classifies people according to the wealth of their parents: the "gold spoon" is born into abundance, the "dirt spoon" into scarcity. Variants such as "silver spoon" describe intermediate positions.

Historical context. The terms emerged in online communities and entered mainstream media as young adults described a perceived hardening of class barriers. Related expressions, including "Hell Joseon" and the earlier "give up on three things" (courtship, marriage, childbirth) generation labels, belong to the same vocabulary of frustration.

Why it matters. The slang is evidence of how people perceived class, and it also indicates the political salience of inherited advantage, which fed debates over inheritance taxes, admissions fairness, and hiring practices.

Potential misunderstanding. The slang is a rhetorical simplification. It is not a statistical measurement, and it can obscure the many gradations of family resources and the possibility of mobility that data may or may not show.

Intergenerational mobility measures how closely a person's economic position resembles that of his or her parents. Researchers use tax records, longitudinal surveys, and educational data to estimate it, and findings depend on the cohort studied and the measure used. Several studies indicate that mobility in South Korea was high for cohorts that entered the workforce during rapid growth and has weakened for later cohorts, though the evidence base is incomplete and researchers disagree about the magnitude. Historians should therefore treat claims of a fully closed class structure with caution, while recognizing that survey evidence on parental background and educational outcomes, discussed in Section 91.9, points to meaningful reproduction of advantage.

The relationship to meritocratic ideals is politically sensitive. South Korea's institutions, from the civil-service examination heritage described in earlier volumes to the modern university admissions system, are built on the principle that rewards should follow effort and measured ability. Perceived unfairness, therefore, strikes at the legitimacy of institutions rather than at a single policy. Scandals in the late 2010s and early 2020s involving privileged admissions practices for the children of public figures intensified criticism and led to reforms in disclosure and admissions procedures. These episodes show how the language of fairness became central to political debate, without in themselves proving a general pattern.

Section 91.5

Poverty, Social Protection, and the Limits of Economic Security

The South Korean welfare state was built late and quickly. Universal health insurance was achieved in 1989 and administratively unified in the early 2000s. The National Pension began in 1988 and expanded to rural farmers and fishermen in July 1995 and to much of the urban population in April 1999, establishing broad compulsory eligibility without guaranteeing that every worker had paid sufficient contributions to draw a pension. Employment insurance began in 1995 and was extended after the financial crisis. The National Basic Livelihood Security system, enacted in 1999 and in force from 2000, replaced earlier limited relief with a statutory right to minimum livelihood support for those below a threshold of income and assets, and its eligibility rules have been revised repeatedly. A Basic Old-Age Pension introduced in 2008 was reorganized as the Basic Pension in 2014. Disability support, child allowances, and other programs followed.

Despite this expansion, important gaps remained. Social insurance depends on contribution histories, which favor workers with continuous regular employment. Workers in non-regular jobs, the self-employed, and women with interrupted careers often accumulate smaller pension entitlements. The pension system matured only recently, so many people now over sixty-five receive little or no earnings-related pension, and for them the means-tested Basic Pension is a major source of income. As a result, older adults have faced a high rate of relative poverty; by OECD measures in the early 2020s close to two-fifths of those aged sixty-six and over lived below the relative-poverty line, the highest figure among member countries.

Students must distinguish four things that popular discussion often merges. Relative poverty means income below a fraction of the national median, usually fifty percent; it measures inequality at the bottom as much as deprivation. Absolute material deprivation means lacking the means to meet defined basic needs. Eligibility means meeting the legal criteria for a benefit, which in the case of the National Basic Livelihood Security system depends on both income and the presence of family members legally obliged to provide support. Receipt means actually receiving the benefit; some eligible households do not apply, and some who need help fall outside eligibility. Studies of "blind spots" in the system have documented cases in which households in severe difficulty were not receiving support, and the family-support obligation rule was gradually relaxed.

Underlying these arrangements was a historical assumption that families, above all adult children and their spouses, would care for aging parents and other vulnerable relatives. As households became smaller, as young adults moved to cities, and as the number of one-person households increased, that assumption became less reliable. The chapter returns to this change in Sections 91.18 and 91.19. The point here is that the welfare system was constructed alongside, and in part as a substitute for, family obligations that were weakening, and that coverage and adequacy remained uneven by age, employment history, and sex. Social spending as a share of national income rose substantially over several decades but remained below the OECD average for most of the period.

PART II — Education, Meritocracy, and the Competition for Social Mobility. Why a celebrated educational achievement coexisted with expensive private tutoring, examination pressure, and unequal access to prestige.
Section 91.6

From Educational Expansion to an Intensely Competitive Society

Mass education was one of the most successful projects of the postwar state and of ordinary families. After 1945 and especially after the war, primary schooling became nearly universal within a generation; middle-school and then high-school attendance followed in the 1970s and 1980s. A high-school equalization policy introduced in the mid-1970s assigned students to schools by lottery in many cities, intended to reduce the competition for elite secondary schools. Vocational training expanded to supply industry, and the 1980s saw rapid growth in university places. Families, including those with little wealth, invested heavily in their children's schooling, in part because land reform and war had eliminated many older inherited hierarchies and education seemed the surest ladder.

Education therefore functioned both as a national development strategy and as a family strategy. The state needed literate and technically trained workers for export industry; families needed a route to better employment. Where schooling had once separated a small elite from the majority, it now became a mass credential. As the share of each cohort completing a degree rose, the credential's distinguishing power declined, and competition moved to the quality and prestige of the institution. This process, in which widespread access raises the stakes for rank rather than eliminating competition, is a recurrent theme in the history of educational expansion elsewhere, and in South Korea it was unusually intense.

The historical argument requires care. The educational system's achievements, including high literacy, strong international test scores, and one of the highest tertiary attainment rates in the world, are real and were partly founded on broad access. Access to schooling and equality of opportunity, however, are not identical. Children of poorer households entered the system in vast numbers but met it at a disadvantage in preparation, in neighborhood school quality, and in the resources families could devote to supplemental instruction. The expansion of education raised the average level of attainment for everyone and left questions of relative position, and of the economic return to different credentials, open for the following decades.

The geography of this expansion also mattered. Industrial towns needed technical high schools, expanding cities needed new secondary classrooms, and families sometimes moved specifically to gain access to schools reputed to offer better examination preparation. National policies created common credentials, but schools varied in instructional resources, peer composition, and connections to higher education. As higher education expanded, inequalities increasingly appeared not simply between students who could and could not attend university, but between institutions of different prestige and between graduates who secured stable employment and those who did not. Thus a policy success—mass access—shifted the arena in which social advantage was contested.

Section 91.7

The Suneung, University Admissions, and the Examination Society

The College Scholastic Ability Test, known as the Suneung, is a national examination taken each November by high-school seniors and others. It replaced earlier national entrance examinations in the early 1990s, with the first test used for admissions to the 1994 academic year, and has been revised many times in format, subjects, and scoring. It is administered nationwide on a single day, an event of such national significance that businesses adjust opening hours and traffic is managed to help students reach test sites.

Admissions in practice are more complex than the image of a single examination suggests. Universities use several tracks: regular admission, in which Suneung scores are decisive; early or "susi" admission, which weighs school records, interviews, essays, and activities; and special tracks for categories such as rural students or students from low-income households. The balance among tracks has shifted over time, and governments have repeatedly altered rules in response to criticism, with some favoring record-based assessment to reduce exam pressure and others favoring examinations as more objective. The Suneung is therefore one significant determinant among several, not the sole one, and its weight varies by university and year.

Prestige is concentrated. Seoul National University, Korea University, and Yonsei University, grouped as SKY, together enroll a small share of undergraduates but supply a disproportionate share of senior positions in government, law, large corporations, and media. Graduates of these universities benefit from alumni networks and from recruiters' expectations. The competition for places in them shapes the experience of millions who will never enroll, because the entire hierarchy of universities below them is ranked by distance from the top.

The examination's ideal is meritocratic: a standardized test applied to all is meant to be fair, and many South Koreans see it as less vulnerable to nepotism than interviews or recommendations, a view with deep roots in the earlier state-examination tradition described in the volumes on Goryeo and Joseon. Critics respond that preparation for the test is not equally available. Families with more money, time, information, and proximity to prestigious academies prepare their children more effectively, so a test that is formally equal can reproduce social advantage. Both claims have evidence, and the debate at the end of Part II examines how historians might adjudicate between them.

Admissions policy also changed over time in response to criticism of excessive reliance on a single examination. University-specific assessments, school-performance records, interviews, and admissions tracks emphasizing student activities were intended in different periods to recognize more than test scores. Yet each innovation generated its own fairness debate: examinations could reward costly coaching, while assessments of extracurricular portfolios could reward parental knowledge and money. The resulting controversy was not a choice between one objective test and wholly subjective admission. It concerned how any institution could measure achievement while minimizing dependence on circumstances that applicants did not control.

Section 91.8

Hagwons, Private Tutoring, and the Cost of Educational Competition

Private supplementary education has a long history in South Korea. In the 1970s, it was already widespread enough to draw government alarm, and in 1980 the military government of Chun Doo-hwan banned most private tutoring as part of broad educational measures. The ban was widely evaded and in 2000 the Constitutional Court ruled it unconstitutional. After that, private education expanded in forms that included for-profit academies (hagwons) offering subject instruction, test preparation, art, and sports; individual tutors; correspondence and online lectures; and after-school programs. Language instruction, particularly English, was a large segment, and in some districts academies became a central institution of neighborhood life.

Families allocated money, time, and labor to this effort. Mothers in many households managed schedules, transportation, and information about academies, a form of unpaid work that is often invisible in statistics and that constrained some women's employment. Fathers, in the stereotyped image, provided income and, in some cases, lived separately from the family so that children could attend school abroad or in a preferred district. This image applies to a minority and should not be generalized, but it shows how far household organization could be adapted to educational goals.

The advantages of wealthier households are well documented in government surveys. Spending per student rises sharply with household income, and participation rates are higher in the capital region and in districts with concentrated academies. Because instruction tailored to the examination and to admissions requirements can be purchased, differences in household resources can translate into differences in preparation. Governments responded in various ways: limiting evening hours of academies, expanding public after-school programs, broadcasting examination-preparation lectures through public channels, and limiting questions on the Suneung to those covered in public curricula, with uneven results.

The annual private-education expenditure survey, carried out by the statistics agency and the Ministry of Education, is the principal source. Its 2026 qualification is instructive. Total private-education expenditure fell from ₩29.2 trillion in 2024 to ₩27.5 trillion in 2025, but average spending among participating students increased. Students must therefore distinguish total spending, which depends on the number of students; the student population, which is shrinking because of declining births; participation rate, the share of students who use private education at all; and household-level cost, the average spent by participants. A fall in the total can coexist with higher cost per participating family, and the aggregate decline may reflect demographic shrinkage as much as reduced competitiveness. In the 2025 survey, monthly spending averaged ₩458,000 across all pupils but ₩604,000 among those who actually received private education; the latter rose about 2 percent from the prior year. The distinction matters when interpreting the falling aggregate.

Section 91.9

Universities, Credentials, Graduate Employment, and Unequal Returns to Education

University enrollment grew from a small elite minority before the 1970s to a majority of each cohort by the 2000s. Many private universities were founded or expanded in response to demand and often depended heavily on tuition. The OECD's 2025 education review reported tertiary qualifications among approximately 71 percent of South Koreans aged 25–34, among the highest figures in the organization, while documenting substantial differences in educational attainment by parental background. A figure for the 25–34 age group describes a cohort, not the whole population, and older cohorts, especially women born before the 1960s, have much lower attainment, a gap that illustrates how fast the change occurred.

Comparing editions requires care. The OECD’s 2025 Korea country note reported 71 percent tertiary attainment among people aged 25–34, compared with 48 percent across the OECD. Its 2026 country note gives 58 percent among Korean adults aged 25–64, a wider age group including older cohorts educated before university expanded. These are not competing estimates of the same population. In the 2025 note, the share was 85 percent among young adults with at least one tertiary-educated parent but 45 percent among those whose parents had not completed upper secondary school; this 40-percentage-point difference provides a more direct illustration of intergenerational inequality than national attainment alone.

The tertiary system is hierarchical. At the top are the SKY universities and a few other prestigious institutions; below them are other Seoul-based universities, regional national universities, private regional institutions, and two- and three-year vocational colleges. Differences in graduates' employment, wages, and prestige are substantial. Regional universities face shrinking applicant pools as the school-age population declines, and some have closed or reduced enrollment, an effect of demographic change that is already visible in the education sector. Tuition at private universities rose for many years; scholarships and student loans expanded, though student borrowing has been less prominent than in some other countries because many families financed tuition from savings.

Why does high attainment not guarantee matching jobs? When a large majority of young people hold degrees, the supply of graduates exceeds the supply of positions that traditionally rewarded them, especially in the protected tier of the labor market. The result is competition for a limited number of desirable posts and, for many graduates, work below their qualification level. Credentialism, the demand for ever more or higher credentials as a screening device, can increase investment in education without increasing the number of good jobs. Studies also find that returns to a degree differ by institution and field, and that the labor-market advantage of top-ranked universities persists.

Education therefore stands in tension as a mechanism of mobility and of reproduction. For individuals from poorer families, a degree has been a real route to better employment, and many entered middle-class life through it. At the same time, the strong association between parental background and both attainment and institutional prestige means that existing inequalities are in part reproduced. The two statements are not contradictory: a system can offer substantial upward movement to many while leaving relative advantage persistent, and the weight of each depends on the cohort and the measure.

Section 91.10

Educational Pressure, Youth Expectations, and the Limits of Meritocracy

For many South Korean students the school day is followed by an evening at an academy and then by self-study, and surveys of adolescents regularly report short sleeping hours, high levels of stress about grades, and low satisfaction with life relative to peers in other wealthy countries. Parental aspirations are strong across social classes: surveys indicate that very large majorities of parents hope their children will complete university. Students who do not reach their goals may experience a sense of failure, and the transition from education to work is itself a source of anxiety, particularly for those aiming at the protected tier of employment.

Experience varies. Students in the capital region and in districts with concentrated academies have access to more instruction and more information than those in rural areas, where there may be few academies and where the population of school-age children is falling. Children from low-income households may rely on public after-school programs, volunteers, and online resources. Students with disabilities encounter barriers in mainstream settings and in the examination itself. Children of migrant background, including those from multicultural families, may face language difficulties and discrimination in schools, a subject taken up in Section 91.19.

Vocational and non-university pathways deserve treatment as meaningful alternatives, not as failures. Specialized high schools, polytechnic programs, apprenticeship-style initiatives, and skilled trades provide routes to employment, and some have been revitalized by government policy. Their status, however, is constrained by a wider culture in which university completion is the dominant measure of success, and by wage and security differences that make the university route financially rational for many even when it is costly. Policies promoting vocational education have had limited effect on this perception.

Historians must avoid overreaching. It is tempting to explain youth mental health, low fertility, or social discontent as consequences of educational competition alone. The evidence supports a more limited statement: educational pressure is one significant element in young people's lives and in families' budgets and time, and it interacts with housing, employment, and gender expectations. Students should not treat any single factor as the master explanation of demographic behavior, as the second debate in this chapter emphasizes.

School experiences also differed by institutional pathways. Students entering vocational high schools, apprenticeships, or technical colleges might gain direct routes to skilled work, but families sometimes judged these options against the prestige of a four-year university. Public vocational-policy initiatives sought closer links to employers, yet those links were uneven across industries and regions. A serious social history therefore asks not only whether an individual student gained admission to an elite university, but also whether alternative routes offered respected, adequately paid, and adaptable careers. This criterion connects educational reform to the dual labor market described in Part I.

Historical Debate

Did South Korea's Education System Promote Social Mobility or Reproduce Class Inequality?

The question: South Korea's educational expansion transformed a poor society into one of the most highly credentialed in the world. Did that expansion offer genuine opportunity for children of modest origins, or did it mainly convert family resources into educational advantage?

Interpretation A — Education as a mobility engine: Emphasizes the achievements of educational expansion: children of farmers and factory workers became professionals and managers within a generation, a large share of today's elite had humble origins, and exam-based selection offered a less corrupt route than patronage. Many of those who experienced this rise saw schooling as the central source of opportunity.
Interpretation B — Education as reproduction of advantage: Emphasizes the persistence of unequal family resources. Spending on private tutoring rises with income, parental education predicts attainment, elite universities enroll students disproportionately from advantaged districts, and property wealth passed from parents shapes life chances independently of schooling.
Interpretation C — Changing returns to credentials: Argues that the answer depends on the cohort. The credential's value was high when degrees were scarce, and fell as attainment became universal, so a degree no longer ensures mobility even where access is equal.

Evidence: Intergenerational income and education data, admissions records by school type and district, surveys of tutoring spending, longitudinal studies of cohorts, and evidence on graduate employment.

Points of disagreement: Mostly interpretation and measurement: which cohort and indicator count, whether rank or absolute attainment matters, and how to isolate the effect of schooling from that of family wealth.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects judgments about the legitimacy of meritocratic institutions and the design of admissions and education policy.

Student task: Identify what kinds of evidence would demonstrate genuine intergenerational mobility, as opposed to high enrollment, and explain why high university enrollment cannot by itself prove equal opportunity.

Section 91.11

Apartments, Urban Development, and the Transformation of Korean Housing

The large apartment complex is the most characteristic residential form of modern South Korea. Early experiments in the 1960s were followed by large-scale construction in the 1970s and 1980s, and a national drive to build two million housing units in the late 1980s, accompanied by the planning of new towns around Seoul, was a response to shortages and rising prices. Chapter 81 described the early phase; the present section concentrates on consequences. Today apartments make up about half of all dwellings nationwide and a much higher share in metropolitan areas and new towns, a proportion unusual among wealthy countries, many of which rely more on detached houses and low-rise housing.

Apartment growth was driven by several forces: migration from the countryside to the cities, which required rapid construction; a state that favored large builders, standardized design, and pre-sale financing; and rising household incomes. Apartment living was also attractive for reasons beyond cost: modern heating and plumbing, security, shared amenities, and the standardization that made units easy to buy and sell. Unlike older low-rise neighborhoods of detached or semi-detached houses, rental housing in multi-family buildings, public rental housing, and rural homes, apartments came to carry a distinctive social meaning.

Housing became simultaneously a place to live, a store of household wealth, and a symbol of status. A standardized product could be compared across neighborhoods, so prices were readily seen and discussed. Neighborhood reputation, access to subway lines, and above all assignment to particular school districts determined price differences. Redevelopment, in which older complexes were demolished and rebuilt at higher densities, allowed owners to realize gains and in some cases displaced tenants and poorer residents. As a result, those who bought early in appreciating districts accumulated substantial wealth, while the same housing system raised barriers for those entering later.

This dual role, as consumption and investment, helps explain why housing policy is politically explosive. Measures that lower prices harm existing owners, who are numerous and politically active; measures that protect prices raise entry costs for non-owners. Every government since the 1980s has intervened with lending limits, tax measures, supply programs, or regulation of resale, with mixed and contested results.

Apartment development reshaped neighborhood organization as well as household finance. Large complexes could bring schools, clinics, parks, shopping facilities, and rail connections into coordinated residential environments, while redevelopment could displace tenants and older residents whose rights differed from owners'. The physical uniformity of high-rise housing should not be confused with social uniformity: complexes varied in age, maintenance, ownership tenure, price, and surrounding services. Comparison of a new condominium district with an older neighborhood must therefore include who gained access to amenities, who bore relocation costs, and who could benefit from the increase in land value.

Section 91.12

Seoul, Gangnam, and the Concentration of Economic Opportunity

South Korea's economy and population are concentrated to an unusual degree in one metropolitan region. The 2025 register-based census found that the capital region, comprising Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province, contained 50.9 percent of the national population. The region also holds most headquarters of major companies, many leading universities, the principal medical centers, the main cultural institutions, and a disproportionate share of national wealth. A family seeking the best combination of jobs, schools, and hospitals faces a strong incentive to live there, and the incentive reinforces itself as employers locate where workers are.

Within the capital region, Gangnam, the district south of the Han River, occupies a special place. Planned and developed beginning in the 1970s with new roads, bridges, and apartments, and aided by the relocation of well-regarded high schools from the north of the river, it became associated with academies, corporate offices, and high property values. School assignment tied to neighborhoods meant that families paid for access through housing prices, a mechanism that economists call capitalization of school quality into property values. The district's name entered global awareness through popular culture, discussed in Chapter 90, but the social reality it names is of an exceptionally expensive residential and educational zone.

Seoul is not uniformly prosperous. The city contains older neighborhoods, small apartments, semi-basement units, and the single-room rental dwellings known as goshiwon, where low-income residents, often young people and older single adults, live in very small spaces. Commuting patterns also matter: many who work in Seoul live in Gyeonggi or Incheon and travel long distances, so that housing cost is paid in time and in transportation expense. Redevelopment and transport investment have changed the relative position of districts over time, producing residential segregation by income and housing type. To equate the capital with wealth would ignore this internal diversity.

Provincial cities and rural counties face the opposite condition. They lose young residents who leave for university and employment and often do not return, which lowers school enrollment, reduces the tax base, and weakens local services. Government attempts to decentralize, including the creation of Sejong as an administrative city, the relocation of public institutions to innovation cities, and support for regional universities, have shifted some functions but have not reversed the overall concentration. The structural relationship between regional decline and low fertility, since fertility is particularly low in Seoul, is one of the interactions discussed in the OECD's 2026 survey.

Section 91.13

Jeonse, Wolse, Renters, and the Insecurity of Housing Deposits

Historical Vocabulary
Jeonse and Wolse

Definition. Under jeonse, a tenant pays the landlord a large lump-sum deposit, typically a substantial fraction of the property's value, and pays no monthly rent; the deposit is returned when the lease ends, normally after two years. Under wolse, the tenant pays a smaller deposit plus monthly rent.

Historical context. Jeonse spread during the twentieth century when credit was scarce and landlords valued lump sums that they could invest, including in further property. Falling interest rates in recent decades reduced landlords' incentive and shifted many contracts toward wolse.

Why it matters. The tenant is effectively lending money to the landlord and bears the risk that it will not be returned.

Potential misunderstanding. Jeonse is not a form of rent-free housing; the tenant forgoes interest on the deposit, and the arrangement is not equivalent to a mortgage.

Jeonse arrangements offered real opportunities. A tenant able to assemble or borrow a deposit could live without monthly rent, and a landlord could use the deposit to finance property purchases. For landlords who owned multiple units, a rising market allowed ever more purchases funded by tenants' deposits, a practice often called "gap investment" because the buyer paid only the gap between the purchase price and the deposits. For tenants it provided forced saving and the possibility of eventual purchase. When property prices rose and interest rates stayed low, the arrangement worked for many participants.

It also concentrated risk. Deposits were large relative to household savings and were frequently financed by loans. If the property fell in value, if the landlord was insolvent, or if the landlord could not obtain a new tenant to supply funds for repayment, the tenant might not recover the deposit. After 2022, rising interest rates and falling prices in some segments exposed these vulnerabilities. A wave of cases emerged in which tenants, particularly in villas and multi-household buildings in Seoul and Incheon, could not recover their deposits.

The jeonse fraud crisis of the early 2020s involved several different phenomena that students must distinguish. Some losses were ordinary deposit-repayment disputes in which landlords delayed repayment. Some stemmed from falling property values that left the sale price below the total of deposits and mortgages. Some arose from landlord insolvency. And some resulted from deliberate criminal fraud, in which operators acquired many properties using tenants' deposits, inflated valuations, or brokers acting in concert, and then defaulted or disappeared. Treating all losses as one kind of event obscures differences in responsibility and in the appropriate policy response.

The scale of harm and several widely reported tragedies led to government action. In 2023, the National Assembly passed special legislation to assist victims, providing measures such as support for tenants who meet defined criteria, assistance with alternative housing, and procedures for purchasing or managing affected properties; the framework was later amended after criticism that its eligibility rules excluded many victims. Evaluation of the outcome requires distinguishing legislation enacted, funds allocated, and relief actually delivered, and the record shows continuing disagreement about adequacy. This section is therefore a case study in how a distinctive housing institution transferred risk to tenants.

The legal and financial distinction at the center of jeonse deserves emphasis. A tenant pays a refundable deposit to the landlord and ordinarily expects its return when the tenancy ends; the arrangement can be understood economically as financing that the landlord may use, but the tenant is not simply a bank lender. Protection depends on tenancy registration, priority rules, guarantees, property value, and the status of competing claims. When home prices or landlord liquidity declined, tenants could struggle to recover funds even without deliberate fraud. Fraud cases added fabricated leases, concealed obligations, or intentional misrepresentations. Remedies for insolvency and prosecution of fraud therefore address related but different problems.

Section 91.14

Homeownership, Household Debt, and Intergenerational Housing Inequality

Entering homeownership became progressively harder for young households in the Seoul region during the 2010s and early 2020s. Apartment prices in the capital rose sharply during several periods, notably between 2017 and 2021, even as wages grew far more slowly, so that the ratio of house price to income reached levels that put purchase out of reach without family help or very high borrowing. A phrase popular among young buyers, "pulling in every last resource" (yeongkkeul), describes buying with all available savings and loans, and many did so under the fear that prices would continue to rise.

Household debt rose to among the highest levels relative to national income in the world, with mortgages and jeonse loans as major components. Lending regulation, including limits on loan-to-value and debt-to-income ratios, was repeatedly tightened and loosened by successive governments, and interest-rate changes after 2022 raised repayment burdens. Government interventions on the supply side, including new housing programs and redevelopment rules, and on the demand side, including taxes on holding and transferring property, were adopted with results that remain debated; analysts disagree on whether price rises stemmed mainly from monetary conditions, constrained supply in desirable locations, speculative expectations, or policy reversals.

The result is a widening difference between households that acquired property earlier and those trying to enter during high-price periods. Owners benefited from appreciation without additional labor, and could transfer assets to their children; non-owners faced larger deposits and higher borrowing. Asset inequality can therefore persist, and even grow, when wage inequality changes relatively little, because wealth responds to asset prices and inheritance rather than to wages. Younger professionals might have high salaries but little net worth, and older owners might have modest incomes but large property wealth, a pattern that complicates the use of income alone to describe inequality.

The central question is whether housing has increasingly served as a mechanism of wealth accumulation for existing owners while raising barriers to independence for others. The evidence supports the claim for specific periods and locations, particularly the Seoul region in the late 2010s and early 2020s, but not for all places or years: prices fell in some regions and in 2022–2023 in many segments, and homeownership rates for the population as a whole have been fairly stable. Students should therefore specify the period, the region, and the group when making claims.

Property taxation, lending standards, and housing supply policies affected groups unevenly. Measures limiting speculative purchases could restrain highly leveraged buyers but also reduce access to credit for first-time purchasers; increased supply could ease shortages over time yet have little immediate effect in an especially desired school district. Regional differences further complicate the picture: a falling price in a shrinking provincial city does not necessarily help someone employed in Seoul. A balanced account therefore compares prices relative to earnings and available credit, not only national house-price indexes, and distinguishes owner-occupiers from investors and renters.

Section 91.15

Women's Education, Employment, Unpaid Care, and Economic Independence

Women's opportunities changed profoundly during the period. Female literacy and schooling, once far lower than men's, converged; by around 2010 young women's tertiary enrollment equaled or exceeded that of young men. Women entered professions including law, medicine, teaching, and public administration in growing numbers, and expectations about marriage and family changed. Legislation including the Equal Employment Act of 1987 and later revisions prohibited discrimination in hiring and promotion, and the abolition of the legal household-head (hoju) system, found unconstitutional by the Constitutional Court in 2005 and abolished in 2008, removed a legal foundation of patriarchal family law.

Equality of education did not translate into equality of employment. Women's labor-force participation has been lower than men's, and many women have interrupted careers at marriage or childbirth, producing a pattern in which participation falls in the late twenties and thirties and rises again later, though this "M-curve" has flattened among younger cohorts. The gender wage gap, at around thirty percent in recent OECD comparisons, has been the largest among member countries. Part of the gap reflects differences in occupations, hours, and tenure, which are themselves consequences of unequal caregiving; part reflects promotion barriers and discrimination. Women who return to work after a break often take non-regular positions at lower pay, which links gender to the dual labor market described in Section 91.2.

Unpaid care is central. Time-use surveys show that women perform several times more unpaid housework and childcare than men, a gap larger than in most OECD countries. Long working hours for men, expectations that mothers manage education, and limited availability of flexible work reinforce the division. Parental leave was introduced in the late 1980s and has been extended and made more generous, with measures aimed at encouraging fathers to take leave, but take-up has long been lower for men, and in smaller firms and non-regular positions leave is less accessible. Public childcare expanded substantially, especially after 2000, and free childcare for young children was introduced in stages, yet availability, quality, and hours do not always match working schedules.

Women are not a uniform group. A highly paid professional, a part-time retail worker, a married woman in her fifties returning to work, an unmarried woman in her thirties, and a migrant care worker face different constraints. The OECD's analyses identify career penalties associated with motherhood and gender differences in employment as significant components of South Korea's social and demographic challenges, and Section 91.17 explores why these penalties affect decisions about marriage and children.

Section 91.16

Feminism, the #MeToo Movement, Gender Conflict, and Digital Society

Women's organizations existed before democratization, but after 1987 they gained new freedom and influence. Campaigns in the 1990s led to laws on sexual violence, domestic violence, and sexual harassment in the workplace; a ministry responsible for gender equality was created in 2001 and later combined with family policy. Debates about military service, which is compulsory for men, about family law, and about abortion, which the Constitutional Court in 2019 ruled could not be criminalized under the existing statute, though legislation to replace it was not completed, kept gender issues in public view.

The murder of a woman in a public restroom near Gangnam Station in May 2016 by a man who said he hated women galvanized many young women, who saw it as evidence of everyday danger, while others disputed that interpretation. In January 2018, prosecutor Seo Ji-hyun gave a televised account of having been sexually harassed by a senior colleague; her allegations were followed by many others in law, politics, entertainment, religion, and academia. A prominent provincial governor, Ahn Hee-jung, who had been regarded as a presidential prospect, was accused by a former aide and was later convicted of sexual crimes. The movement exposed the extent of workplace harassment and the difficulty of seeking redress, and produced both legal changes and a backlash.

Digital technology, whose growth was examined in Chapter 87, created new forms of abuse. The Nth Room case of 2019–2020 involved the production and distribution through encrypted messaging channels of sexually exploitative material, including of minors, and produced a large public outcry; its principal organizers received long prison sentences, and the legislature strengthened criminal provisions on digital sexual crimes. In August 2024, a wave of reports about sexually explicit synthetic images ("deepfakes") of women and girls, including students, circulated in messaging groups, and public alarm led to legislation criminalizing not only production and distribution but also possession and viewing. These episodes involve criminal abuse and are not matters of political opinion.

The political interpretation of gender inequality became sharply contested. Some young women emphasized discrimination, violence, and unequal care; some young men, facing military service, competitive labor markets, and a perception that they were blamed collectively, expressed resentment of feminism and, in some cases, of gender-equality policy; political parties and campaigns used these divisions. Surveys suggest the gap between young men and women on gender-related attitudes is large in several age groups, though findings vary with question wording and the analyst must distinguish attitudes from behavior. Historians should separate legitimate disagreement over public policy, such as the design of quotas, military service, or the role of a gender ministry, from harassment, discrimination, and criminal sexual abuse, which are different categories and are not made equivalent by being discussed in the same debate.

Section 91.17

Marriage, Family Formation, and South Korea's Low Fertility

Historical Vocabulary
Total Fertility Rate (TFR)

Definition. The average number of children a woman would bear in her lifetime if she experienced the age-specific birth rates observed in a given year. It is a synthetic, period measure.

Historical context. South Korea's TFR was around six in 1960, fell below the replacement level of about 2.1 in the 1980s, reached 1.18 in 2002, dropped below 1.0 in 2018, and was 0.72 in 2023, 0.75 in 2024, and 0.80 in 2025.

Why it matters. It summarizes fertility independent of the size of the population, unlike the raw number of births.

Potential misunderstanding. The TFR does not describe the number of children women actually end up having, which can be measured only for completed cohorts; and a year's TFR can shift because women delay or accelerate births, not only because they change their final family size.

Marriage in South Korea is strongly linked to childbearing: births outside marriage have historically accounted for only a very small share of the total. Changes in marriage therefore translate directly into changes in births. The average age at first marriage has risen steadily, reaching the early thirties for both sexes, and the share of adults who never marry has grown. Households have shrunk, and one-person households became the most common type, accounting for more than a third of all households in the 2020s. Couples who do have children have, on average, fewer than before, and most have only one.

Explanations of these changes include employment insecurity, housing costs, educational expenses, career considerations, and unequal caregiving expectations, each of which is documented in the preceding sections. A woman who anticipates a career penalty for motherhood, a couple unable to afford a home in a desirable school district, or a young man without a regular job may postpone marriage or childbirth. At the same time, personal preferences have changed. Surveys show that fewer young people regard marriage or children as necessary, and living single or childfree has gained social legitimacy. The relative weight of constraint and preference is the subject of the second historical debate below, and it is important not to treat low fertility as a failure of individuals.

The record after 2023 complicates the story of uninterrupted decline. Births rose in 2024 and again in 2025, to approximately 254,300, and the TFR to 0.80. Official statistics reported approximately 240,000 marriages in 2025, an increase of 8.1 percent over 2024. Analysts have suggested several contributing factors, including the delayed marriages after the pandemic, a larger cohort of women born in the early 1990s now in their early thirties, and policy measures; but the relative contributions are not established, and independent evidence of causation is limited.

An increase in annual births or marriages does not by itself establish that the long-term demographic problem has been resolved. A TFR of 0.80 remains far below the replacement level, so each generation is smaller than the last by a wide margin. The cohort of women of childbearing age will shrink in the coming decades, which would reduce births even if the TFR stayed constant. And a rise could reflect timing effects that later reverse. The 2024–2025 rebound is thus historically significant as evidence that fertility is not on a fixed downward path, and it is insufficient as evidence of a durable recovery.

Historical Debate

Is Low Fertility Principally an Economic Problem, an Institutional Problem, or a Change in Personal and Social Values?

The question: South Korea's fertility is among the lowest ever recorded. What best explains it?

Economic explanations: Emphasize housing prices, unstable employment, educational costs, and the difficulty of financing family life, supported by correlations between housing prices and marriage timing in the capital region and by the association of secure employment with marriage.
Institutional and gender explanations: Emphasize long working hours, career penalties for mothers, unequal division of unpaid care, and the limits of childcare and leave institutions, supported by the observation that countries with more equal division of care and more flexible work often show higher fertility among wealthy societies.
Value-change explanations: Emphasize shifting preferences: greater valuation of personal independence, reduced belief that marriage and parenthood are required, and widespread concern about the intensity of child-rearing competition, supported by attitude surveys.

Evidence: Time series of births and marriages, regional comparisons, surveys of intentions and attitudes, comparisons with other East Asian and European societies, and evaluations of policy measures.

Points of disagreement: Largely causation and weighting. The explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the same person may be affected by all of them. Disagreement also concerns whether stated reasons in surveys are reliable guides to behavior.

Why it matters: The preferred explanation shapes policy, since housing, leave and childcare, and cultural measures are different instruments, and one-time cash payments address few of the mechanisms identified.

Student task: Evaluate the evidence for each interpretation and explain why individual decisions cannot be reduced to one nationally uniform cause.

Section 91.18

An Aging Society: Elderly Poverty, Pensions, Healthcare, and Caregiving

Rising life expectancy and low fertility together changed South Korea's age structure faster than in almost any other country. The country crossed the threshold of an "aged society," with people sixty-five and older at 14 percent of the population, in 2017, and the "super-aged" threshold of 20 percent in 2024. Official 2026 statistics estimated that about 21.6 percent of the population was aged 65 or older. The median age rose steeply, and the working-age population began to shrink, which raises concerns about labor supply, the tax base, and the financing of pensions and health care.

Older adults are economically diverse. Some own housing and have stable pensions or continue working, and employment rates among those over sixty-five are high by international standards, partly because many have no sufficient pension. Others, especially women, who live longer than men, those with interrupted employment, and people in rural areas, are poor. Living arrangements have shifted from coresidence with adult children to couples alone or one-person households, and some older people live alone with little contact, an issue that has led to local programs of monitoring and support. To equate older age with poverty, dependency, or incapacity misrepresents the evidence.

Public services partly replaced family care. The long-term care insurance system, launched in 2008, finances home and institutional care for older people with defined needs, and universal health insurance covers most medical costs, though copayments and uncovered services remain a burden. Adult children still provide much informal care, and migrant care workers fill a growing role, a subject in Section 91.19. The balance among public, market, and family care remains contested, particularly regarding the quality of institutions and the burden on women, who provide much of the unpaid care.

Pension reform is the clearest policy response. The National Pension, whose contribution rate had been 9 percent of income since the late 1990s, was projected to deplete its reserves under existing rules in the coming decades. In March 2025 the National Assembly passed an amendment providing for phased increases in the contribution rate beginning in 2026 and setting a nominal replacement rate of 43 percent. The reform addressed financing and adequacy together and was the result of long negotiation; but it did not resolve questions of intergenerational fairness, since younger workers pay higher contributions, or of the coverage of workers outside the system.

Population aging also changed the geography of care. In communities losing working-age residents, older people could face longer journeys for specialist medical attention, fewer nearby relatives, and a shrinking pool of paid caregivers. In urban areas, one-person elderly households presented different challenges, including isolation even when hospitals and public transport were close. Long-term care insurance expanded services, but eligibility and the availability of caregivers did not erase differences between regions and household incomes. The distribution of health and care services matters as much as the national share of older people when assessing what an aging society means in daily life.

Section 91.19

Rural Decline, Migration, Multicultural Families, and Population Redistribution

Rural South Korea has been losing people since the 1960s. Villages are dominated by older residents, schools have closed for lack of pupils, and many counties have been classified by national researchers as at risk of eventual depopulation. Small cities and agricultural regions face labor shortages, a thinning of services such as clinics and public transport, and a loss of tax revenue. Internal migration continues to flow toward the capital region, especially among young adults seeking education and jobs, though in some years movement to and from the capital region has been roughly balanced in other age groups.

International migration has partly filled the gaps. South Korea was long a country of emigration, and many Koreans settled in Japan, the United States, China, and elsewhere, as earlier chapters described. From the 1990s, it became a destination: the industrial trainee system of the early 1990s was replaced in 2004 by the Employment Permit System for temporary migrant workers, who work in manufacturing, agriculture, fishing, and construction, and ethnic Koreans from China and elsewhere received special visas. Foreign residents, including students, workers, marriage migrants, and others, now number well over two million, roughly five percent of the population by official estimates. Marriage migrants, mostly women from Asian countries who married rural Korean men, contributed to the creation of "multicultural families," recognized in the 2008 Multicultural Families Support Act.

Migrant labor is significant in sectors where Korean workers are scarce. Farms and small factories depend on it, as does elder care, where migrant workers, often ethnic Koreans from China, have a large share of caregiving jobs. Working conditions have been criticized: tied employment, in which a worker may change employers only under limited conditions, weak enforcement of housing standards, and exposure to workplace accidents have been documented by civic groups, labor organizations, and the government's own inspections. Reforms have loosened some restrictions without eliminating the concerns.

Students must distinguish the demographic effect of migration, which concerns the number and age of residents, from questions of citizenship, cultural belonging, and labor rights. Naturalization is possible but limited, and migrants on temporary visas cannot expect long-term settlement; children of multicultural families, who are citizens, may still encounter prejudice in schools. Debates on national identity, in which older ideas of a single ethnic nation (minjok), discussed in Chapter 1, coexist with growing diversity, are active. South Korea's demographic future thus depends not only on births but also on the movement, distribution, and inclusion of people.

Migration statistics must be used cautiously. Counts of foreign nationals staying in Korea may include short-term visitors as well as long-term workers, students, and family migrants; they should not all be described as permanent immigrants. At the end of 2025, the Ministry of Justice's broad count was approximately 2.78 million foreign nationals staying in the country, including roughly 620,000 short-term stays. The often-cited comparison with a population of around 52 million is therefore an indication of presence under a specified administrative definition, not a census of settled immigrant households. Local schools, employers, and municipalities experience different consequences depending on the composition and duration of those stays.

Section 91.20

South Korea's Social and Demographic Future in 2026: Policy, Reform, and the Limits of Prediction

As of November 2026, the interactions described in this chapter can be summarized in a chain that is neither deterministic nor a single cycle. A dual labor market makes secure employment scarce and valuable; competition for it raises educational expenditure and credential inflation; concentration of good jobs and schools in the capital region raises housing prices there; high housing costs and unequal care shape marriage and childbearing; low fertility, together with longevity, ages the population and reduces the number of future workers; and regional decline and the demand for labor increase reliance on migrants. Each link has evidence behind it, and each has exceptions.

Policy has addressed many of these links. Governments since the mid-2000s, beginning with the framework law of 2005 and the committees created under it, have spent very large sums, reported to exceed several hundred trillion won across the period, on childcare, leave, housing support for newlyweds, and cash payments. Recent measures have included extended parental leave and benefits, wider access to flexible hours, housing supply and loan programs, regional development plans, the pension reform of 2025, and discussion of immigration policy. The reader should distinguish programs announced by politicians, legislation enacted by the Assembly, measures implemented by agencies, and outcomes independently demonstrated by evaluation. Several announced measures of 2025–2026 had not yet been evaluated at the time of writing, and this chapter makes no claims about their effect.

Competing explanations of low fertility imply different policy priorities. If the main obstacle is the cost of housing and unstable employment, reform of the labor market and housing supply would matter most. If it is the division of care and the working-hours culture, leave, childcare, and workplace norms matter. If it is a change in values, then policy may have limited power, and the goal may be to support those who want children rather than to raise a numerical target. One-time cash incentives, including large private-sector bonuses such as that announced by a construction company in 2024 for each employee's baby, can ease a decision for some families, but cannot be assumed to solve structural problems, since the costs of raising a child extend over two decades and are shaped by housing, education, and career.

Demographic projections deserve caution. Official long-range projections depend on assumptions about fertility, mortality, migration, and policy, and they have been revised repeatedly; a projection that assumes a continued rise in fertility yields a different population from one assuming stability. The rebound of 2024–2025 shows that fertility can change direction, but not how long a change will last. Projections are scenarios, not forecasts, and historians should label them as such.

The chapter's conclusion is that South Korea's future depends not simply on producing more economic output or achieving a numerical fertility target, but on the distribution of opportunity and the ability of individuals and households to construct secure and fulfilling lives. The country's achievements in education, technology, and income are real, and so are the tensions that accompany them. How these tensions are managed will affect not only the economy but the democratic politics examined in Chapter 95, and will form part of the comparative picture drawn in Chapter 96.

The choice of evidence is itself consequential for policymaking. An annual fertility rate may rise because births previously postponed occur in one year, because more couples marry, because cohort sizes change, or because several developments coincide; a single rise does not isolate the contribution of a subsidy or leave policy. Similarly, an increase in the number of insured workers does not prove that benefit levels are adequate, and a fall in national tutoring expenditure may partly reflect fewer school-age children. The most responsible evaluation of the 2026 policy agenda therefore follows implementation over several years and checks distributional outcomes across age, income, region, and gender rather than only one headline national target.

Cause and Effect

Concentration, Competition, Housing Costs, Gender Relations, and Demographic Change

Long-term causes

State-directed growth that concentrated rewards in large firms and in Seoul; rapid educational expansion; late-built social protection; patriarchal family expectations; early fertility decline and rising longevity.

Short-term causes

The 1997–1998 crisis and expansion of non-regular work; housing-price surges in the capital region in the late 2010s; high interest rates after 2022; growing recognition of workplace and digital gender abuse.

Immediate context

A cohort of young adults educated at mass scale, facing a scarce protected tier of employment and expensive housing, in a society where marriage and childbearing remain closely linked.

Transformation

Delay or avoidance of marriage and births, shrinking households, record-low fertility in 2023, and a rapidly aging population by 2024.

Immediate effects

Declining school enrollment, university and regional strain, labor shortages filled partly by migrants, and rising pressure on pensions and care systems.

Long-term consequences

A smaller future workforce, financing challenges, regional depopulation, and continuing debate on how to distribute opportunity. A partial rebound in 2024–2025 shows that trends are not fixed.

Historical complexity: No one factor accounts for the outcome. Housing prices affect owners, renters, and prospective buyers differently; education interacts with employment; and values changed alongside constraints.

Everyday Life and Social History

Students, Working Adults, Young Renters, Parents, and Elderly Households

Everyday life is documented through time-use surveys, household budget surveys, and national censuses, together with oral histories and journalism. A student in a metropolitan area may spend much of the day in school and at an academy; a rural student may have fewer options for tutoring but a longer commute. A working adult may work long hours, which official data have shown to be among the longest among OECD members, though they have declined, leaving less time for family. A young renter in a small apartment or a single-room unit may spend a large share of income on housing and set aside savings for a deposit.

Parents, and particularly mothers, coordinate schooling, childcare, and tutoring, and many rely on grandparents, who have provided considerable informal childcare. Elderly households may include couples who farm into their seventies or one-person households in apartment complexes or rural villages. Because the evidence comes mostly from surveys and administrative records, individual voices are less visible than those of institutions, and historians use oral histories and memoirs to supplement it. The experiences of these groups should not be treated as typical of the whole society: class, region, gender, and age shape them differently.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined why South Korea's extraordinary economic and educational development did not produce equally secure opportunities for all citizens, and how inequalities in employment, housing, education, and gender relations influenced family formation, fertility, aging, and demographic prospects. Its central argument is that growth delivered large and real improvements in living standards while generating new forms of competition and insecurity, and that these were interconnected but not reducible to a single explanation.

Part I established the paradox of prosperity. Absolute gains in longevity, schooling, and consumption coexisted with relative inequality in wealth, occupation, and opportunity. A dual labor market, rooted in the industrial structure shaped by the developmental state, divided workers between a protected tier at large firms and in the public sector and a less-protected tier elsewhere. The crisis of 1997–1998 expanded non-regular work, and young people responded by prolonging educational and examination preparation in pursuit of scarce secure posts.

Wealth, more than income, became the sharper divide. Housing assets, family support, and networks shaped life chances, and popular slang such as "gold spoon" and "dirt spoon" recorded how people perceived class. The chapter urged caution: slang is evidence of perception, not a measurement, and mobility findings depend on cohort and method. Social protection expanded rapidly after 1987 and again after 1997, but contribution-based systems left gaps for workers with irregular careers, and older adults experienced high relative poverty.

Part II traced educational expansion from near-universal primary schooling to a tertiary attainment of about 71 percent among those aged 25–34. Education served as a national and family strategy, and its mass spread shifted competition to institutional rank, with the Suneung, SKY universities, and the private-tutoring economy at the center. The private-education survey showed why denominators matter: total spending fell between 2024 and 2025 while the average among participants rose.

The debate on mobility and reproduction showed that both claims have evidence. Educational expansion gave many children of modest origins a route upward, and parental background still predicts attainment and institutional prestige. Credentials lost distinguishing power as they became common. The chapter cautioned against attributing youth distress or demographic behavior to educational competition alone.

Part III turned to housing and gender. Apartments became home, investment, and status marker, and the capital region came to contain 50.9 percent of the population by the 2025 census, with Gangnam as the emblem of capitalized school quality. Jeonse offered opportunities and concentrated risk, and the fraud crisis of the early 2020s required distinguishing disputes, falling prices, insolvency, and criminal fraud. Homeownership, debt, and asset inequality showed how wealth can diverge even when wages do not.

Women's education and employment changed profoundly, yet a wage gap of around thirty percent, career penalties for mothers, and unequal unpaid care persisted. Women are not a uniform group, and the experiences of professionals, part-time workers, and returning mothers differ. The chapter set the legal change of the household-head system and the expansion of childcare beside the limits of implementation.

Part IV examined gender conflict and demography. The #MeToo movement, the Nth Room case, and the 2024 deepfake abuse controversy involved harassment and crime, distinct from policy disagreements about gender-related measures, which became politically polarized. Fertility fell to 0.72 in 2023 and rose to 0.80 in 2025, with births of about 254,300 and marriages up 8.1 percent, a rebound that is historically significant but insufficient to establish a durable recovery.

Aging followed. South Korea became super-aged in 2024, and the 2025 pension reform phased in higher contributions from 2026 and set a 43 percent nominal replacement rate. Older adults are economically diverse, public care partly replaced family care, and migrants increasingly fill roles in agriculture, manufacturing, and care, raising questions of citizenship, rights, and identity distinct from the demographic effect of migration.

The chapter concluded that the interactions among these developments are real but not mechanical, that policy claims must distinguish announcement, enactment, implementation, and demonstrated outcome, and that projections are scenarios. South Korea's central social challenge in 2026 is not only maintaining growth or reaching a fertility target but ensuring that prosperity translates into broadly accessible opportunities for secure and fulfilling lives. The chapter prepares the comparison with North Korea in Chapter 92 and the political analysis of Chapter 95.

Key Terms

Dual labor market
A labor market with a protected tier of high pay and security and a less-protected tier of lower pay and weaker benefits, with limited movement between them.
Regular employment
An open-ended employment contract with standard social-insurance coverage and legal protection against dismissal.
Non-regular employment
Fixed-term, part-time, dispatched, subcontracted, or on-call work; the definition is contested.
Chaebol
A family-controlled conglomerate such as Samsung, Hyundai, SK, or LG, central to export industry and to upper-tier employment.
Underemployment
Working fewer hours than desired or in a job below one's qualifications, distinct from unemployment.
Labor-force inactivity
Not working and not seeking work, including people preparing for examinations.
Intergenerational mobility
The degree to which a person's economic position differs from that of his or her parents.
Gold spoon / dirt spoon
Slang classifying people by parental wealth; evidence of perceived class, not a statistical category.
Income inequality
Differences in annual earnings among individuals or households.
Wealth inequality
Differences in accumulated assets, particularly housing, generally greater than income inequality.
Relative poverty
Income below a stated fraction (often half) of the national median; measures inequality at the bottom.
Absolute deprivation
Inability to afford a defined set of basic needs.
National Basic Livelihood Security system
The statutory minimum-livelihood program enacted in 1999 and in force from 2000, based on income and asset tests.
Social insurance
Contribution-based protection, such as pensions, health, and employment insurance, whose benefits depend on coverage and contribution history.
Suneung
The national College Scholastic Ability Test, introduced in the early 1990s, one of several factors in university admission.
SKY
Seoul National, Korea, and Yonsei Universities, the most prestigious institutions.
Hagwon
A private for-profit academy offering supplementary instruction.
Meritocracy
A principle that rewards should follow measured ability and effort; its realization depends on equal access to preparation.
Credentialism
Increasing reliance on educational credentials as a screening device, which can raise educational investment without increasing good jobs.
Tertiary attainment
Completion of post-secondary education, expressed as a share of an age group (for example, those aged 25–34).
Denominator
The population against which a statistic is calculated; essential for interpreting percentages and averages.
Apartment complex
A large planned multi-building residential development, the dominant housing form in cities.
Redevelopment
Demolition and reconstruction of older housing at higher density, which can create gains for owners and displace tenants.
Capital region
Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province; 50.9 percent of the population in the 2025 register-based census.
Jeonse
A lease in which a tenant pays a large refundable deposit instead of monthly rent.
Wolse
A lease with a smaller deposit and monthly rent.
Jeonse fraud
Deliberate schemes in which tenants' deposits are not returned; distinct from ordinary disputes, falling prices, and insolvency.
Household debt
Borrowing by households, chiefly mortgages and deposit loans, high relative to national income.
Gender wage gap
The difference between men's and women's typical earnings, reflecting occupation, hours, tenure, and discrimination.
Unpaid care work
Housework and caregiving without pay, performed disproportionately by women.
Total fertility rate
The average births per woman implied by a year's age-specific rates; 0.72 in 2023, 0.75 in 2024, 0.80 in 2025.
Super-aged society
A society in which people aged 65 and older are at least 20 percent of the population; reached by South Korea in 2024.
Long-term care insurance
A social insurance system, begun in 2008, financing care for older people with defined needs.
Multicultural family
An official category for households with at least one member who is a marriage migrant or naturalized foreigner.
Projection
A conditional estimate of future population based on stated assumptions, not a prediction.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it connects the achievements described in Chapters 87 through 90 to the lives of the people who produced and lived among them. It shows that the transformation of a poor, war-ravaged society into a wealthy one was neither automatically equal nor automatically secure, and that the benefits and burdens of success were distributed through institutions: firms, schools, housing markets, families, and laws.

The chapter reveals processes that extend beyond Korea: educational expansion that shifts competition to rank, housing as a store of wealth, aging with low fertility, and the difficulty of reconciling work and care. It also reveals features specific to the peninsula's history, notably the developmental state's legacy, the jeonse institution, and the speed of demographic change.

Later chapters build on these themes. Chapter 92 examines a very different society under Kim Jong Un, inviting comparison of two Korean modernities, and Chapter 95 shows how economic insecurity, generational conflict, and demands for accountability entered democratic politics. Chapter 96 synthesizes the demographic and social pressures facing the peninsula in 2026.

Continuities survive: education as a family strategy, the pull of the capital, and the expectation that families care for their members. What remains open is whether institutions will adapt so that prosperity widens opportunity. Historians cannot predict the answer, but they can clarify what evidence would be needed to judge it, and that is the skill this chapter has aimed to develop.
