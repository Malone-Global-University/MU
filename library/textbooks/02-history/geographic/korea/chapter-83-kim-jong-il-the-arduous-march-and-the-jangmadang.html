Kim Jong Il, the Arduous March, and the Jangmadang

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In principle, a North Korean household did not buy its staple grain; it received it. Rations were issued through the workplace and the local distribution center, as one visible expression of a state that claimed responsibility for feeding, employing, housing, and educating its people. In the 1990s that arrangement gave way across much of the country, and families who had been told to wait for the state increasingly secured food by walking, gathering, bartering, and trading. The state that had promised provision did not collapse. The society it governed was nevertheless transformed.

The Public Distribution System was more than a mechanism for moving grain. It tied a person's food to a place of work, a place of residence, and a position in a hierarchy of political trust, and it gave the party-state a daily instrument for rewarding attendance and discipline. When the system failed to deliver, as it did unevenly but severely in the mid-1990s, the failure was therefore at once a nutritional emergency, an economic breakdown, and a challenge to the logic by which the state justified its authority. This chapter studies how that failure came about, how it was endured, and how the North Korean state nonetheless retained political control.

The scale of the human catastrophe is certain even though its precise measure is not. North Korea's population was roughly 21 million at the time of its 1993 census. In the years that followed, hunger, malnutrition, and the diseases that prey on weakened bodies killed a large number of people, with the worst conditions concentrated between about 1995 and 1998 in the northeastern provinces. Estimates of excess deaths range from a figure of about two hundred thousand associated with North Korean officials, through the several hundred thousand to roughly one million that most demographic studies suggest, to two or three million in some activist and advocacy estimates. Section 83.9 explains why the range is so wide and how historians evaluate it. The chapter treats that uncertainty as a feature of the evidence, not a reason for vagueness about the reality of the famine.

The central historical question is a political one. Observers in 1991 watched the Soviet Union dissolve and the governments of Eastern Europe fall, and many expected the North Korean system to follow. Instead, Kim Il Sung died in July 1994 and was succeeded by his son Kim Jong Il, who consolidated power through the worst years of the crisis. This chapter asks how the state retained control when its economic institutions could no longer reliably supply food, employment, and essential goods. The answer developed here is neither inevitable nor simple. It involves coercion and information control, a hierarchy of access that protected some groups more than others, the political loyalty of a military given privileged standing under the doctrine of Songun, Chinese material support, international humanitarian aid, and the state's partial and reluctant toleration of the markets on which households had come to depend.

The chapter develops four connected transformations: the breakdown of the state-directed economy; the humanitarian catastrophe that official North Korean language calls the Arduous March; Kim Jong Il's consolidation of authority; and the emergence of an increasingly market-dependent society organized around the jangmadang, the informal and then semi-tolerated markets that became central to household survival. These processes unfolded together, and each shaped the others. Hunger drove people into trade, trade eroded the state's monopoly over livelihood, and the state's response to both defined the political settlement that emerged by the early 2000s.

Evidence for this subject is unusually difficult, and students should read the chapter with that in mind. North Korea permitted no independent press, restricted the movement of foreign observers, and published little reliable data about the crisis. Historians rely on official North Korean statements, which are valuable as evidence of how the state represented events but not as neutral accounts; on assessments by United Nations agencies and nongovernmental organizations, whose access was limited and negotiated; on the 1993 and 2008 censuses, which allow indirect estimates of loss; on nutrition surveys; on interviews with refugees and defectors, which are invaluable but not a random sample; on South Korean, Chinese, and international records; and on later scholarship that compares these imperfect sources. The Primary Source Windows in this chapter model how to read each kind of evidence critically.

The chapter observes the division of labor in the textbook's outline. The economic stagnation and succession preparations of the 1970s and 1980s belong to Chapter 80, and the foundations of the party-state and Juche to Chapter 77. South Korea's contemporaneous democratic consolidation is treated in Chapter 82, and its 1997 financial crisis in Chapter 84. Inter-Korean engagement and the Sunshine Policy are reserved for Chapter 85, the nuclear question for Chapter 86, and North Korea's later management of a market-dependent society under Kim Jong Un for Chapter 92. This chapter establishes the origins of that society and ends in 2002, while treating North Korea, in keeping with the textbook's commitments, as a parallel Korean history with its own internal dynamics and not merely as a foreign-policy problem for the South.

The chapter's defining insight is that North Korea survived the 1990s, but the society that emerged was not the one that entered the crisis. The party-state remained, the leadership remained hereditary, and political liberalization did not follow. Yet the relationship between citizens and the state, between households and officials, and between work and income had changed in ways that no subsequent leader could simply reverse.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the structure of North Korea's planned economy, and its dependence on external trade, made it vulnerable to the end of the Cold War.
  2. Trace the mechanisms linking Soviet collapse, energy shortage, industrial decline, agricultural failure, and the breakdown of food distribution.
  3. Describe how the Public Distribution System operated, whom it served, and how it connected food to employment, residence, and political classification.
  4. Distinguish the roles of natural disaster, economic vulnerability, state policy, and distribution failure in producing famine, and evaluate competing scholarly interpretations.
  5. Assess the evidence behind famine mortality estimates and explain why historians present ranges rather than a single figure.
  6. Compare how hunger and survival strategies differed among Pyongyang, industrial cities, agricultural districts, and the northern border region.
  7. Analyze how Kim Jong Il consolidated authority after 1994, distinguishing succession in practice from the formal acquisition of offices, and evaluate the role of Songun.
  8. Evaluate the achievements and limitations of international humanitarian aid and the political disputes surrounding it.
  9. Explain the rise of the jangmadang, including the prominence of women traders, and distinguish marketization from below from state accommodation of markets.
  10. Interpret official terminology such as Arduous March, and read official documents, humanitarian assessments, and refugee testimony as different kinds of evidence.
  11. Evaluate why expanded commercial activity did not amount to political democratization, and how the 1990s continued to shape North Korean society after 2002.

Historical Context

Students arrive at this chapter with the political and economic system established in earlier chapters. The Democratic People's Republic of Korea was founded in 1948 and reconstructed after the Korean War as a party-state organized around the Workers' Party of Korea, state ownership of industry, collectivized agriculture, and the ideology of Juche, examined in Chapter 77. Its economy emphasized heavy industry and military production, and its political life centered on the authority of Kim Il Sung. Chapter 80 traced the stagnation of the 1970s and 1980s, the accumulation of foreign debt, and the preparations for hereditary succession. Those matters are summarized here only as far as the crisis of the 1990s requires.

Two features of the inherited system matter most. First, although state ideology emphasized self-reliance, the economy depended in practice on imports of petroleum, coking coal, machinery, spare parts, and fertilizer inputs, and on markets and partners within the socialist bloc, above all the Soviet Union and China. Second, the state claimed to provide the basic necessities of life through institutions rather than markets: employment assigned to every adult, housing and services linked to the workplace, and food distributed through a rationing system. Peasants on cooperative farms were in a somewhat different position, since they received their food largely from their own farm's distribution, but they, too, were bound within a system of state procurement and plan targets. A system built this way functioned only so long as the flow of goods through it continued, because it held few buffers and possessed few alternative channels.

The peninsula's geography also shaped vulnerability. Only about a fifth of North Korea's territory is arable, and much of that land lies in the western and southwestern plains, particularly in the Hwanghae provinces, while the northeastern provinces of North and South Hamgyong and Ryanggang, with their mountains, mines, and industrial cities, produce less grain than their populations consume. The northern frontier along the Amnok (Yalu) and Duman (Tumen) rivers adjoins northeastern China, where ethnic Korean communities in the Yanbian region maintained kinship ties across the border. These regional contrasts, together with a long history of political division into northern and southern states, should not be read as destiny. They did, however, determine where the crisis fell hardest and where survival strategies could take hold.

Section 83.1

North Korea at the End of the Cold War

By 1990 the North Korean economy had been organized for more than three decades around state ownership, central planning, and a priority for heavy industry and defense. Ministries and planning bodies set production targets, state enterprises received allocations of materials and labor, and the party exercised oversight within factories through management arrangements associated with the Taean work system. Foreign trade was a state monopoly, and consumer goods occupied a subordinate place. Employment was assigned, nominally universal, and attached to benefits: rations, housing, health care, and often supplementary goods flowed through the workplace. For an urban household, therefore, a job was less a source of wages than the point of entry into a system of provision.

That system had already shown strain. As Chapter 80 explained, growth slowed in the 1970s and 1980s, foreign debt accumulated after the borrowing of the 1970s, and the state struggled to modernize aging industrial plant. Ration levels had been trimmed more than once in the late 1980s, and the authorities encouraged citizens in the early 1990s, according to reports from the period, to economize on food by eating fewer meals. Small-scale unofficial exchange and household production of consumer goods existed in the interstices of the planned system. None of this amounted to crisis in 1990, but it meant that the economy entered the decade with limited reserves and a declining capacity to absorb shocks.

The central paradox was that an ideology of self-reliance coexisted with a deep practical dependence on external supply. North Korea's industry used imported petroleum and coking coal; much of its machinery and many of its spare parts came from, or were designed for, Soviet and Chinese sources; and its export earnings depended on a narrow range of goods sold largely to socialist-bloc partners, often on favorable or barter terms. The state's capacity to feed its population depended in turn on energy, fertilizer, and transport that rested on those flows. Understanding the crisis of the 1990s therefore requires attention to the structure of the economy and not merely to the weather of particular years.

North Korea’s industrial pattern had once been an apparent advantage. Postwar investment concentrated on power generation, mining, chemicals, metallurgy, machine building, and military production, sectors capable of producing rapid growth when equipment and external inputs were available. But an industrial structure organized around large complexes was difficult to operate piecemeal. A fertilizer plant could not compensate for the loss of power by simply producing at household scale; a mine that lost electrical power could not keep its output moving on a railway that also lacked power. When shipments from abroad faltered, bottlenecks spread from one enterprise to another. The vulnerability was systemic, not a collection of unrelated factory closures.

A shortage of consumer goods had also encouraged relationships that formal planning documents rarely captured. Households cultivated garden plots, exchanged favors, repaired and resold objects, and used personal contacts to obtain scarce goods. Informal exchange remained constrained and was not equivalent to an open market economy, but it meant that economic life was more complicated than a diagram of ministries and quotas. Those existing practices later supplied skills and contacts for survival during famine. There was consequently no sharp division between a completely market-free 1980s and a suddenly commercial 1990s.

Section 83.2

The Soviet Collapse and the Loss of Economic Support

The economic effect of the Soviet collapse was transmitted through specific mechanisms. During the 1980s Soviet trade with North Korea had been conducted largely through clearing arrangements and on terms that, in practice, subsidized North Korean imports. Beginning in 1991, Moscow moved to settle trade in hard currency at world prices. North Korea had little hard currency, and the barter and credit arrangements on which its supplies of crude oil, coking coal, machinery, and spare parts rested ceased to function in their old form. After the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, Russia, preoccupied with its own economic upheaval, was neither willing nor able to maintain the old relationship. Trade between the two countries fell to a small fraction of its former level within a few years, and the loss extended to the other socialist-bloc economies of Eastern Europe, which had been partners for machinery, consumer goods, and exports.

China remained the most important remaining partner, and its role needs to be stated carefully. Beijing also moved toward more commercial terms of trade in the early 1990s, and its establishment of diplomatic relations with South Korea in 1992 followed Moscow's recognition of Seoul in 1990. Yet China continued to supply fuel and food at levels and prices that were not strictly commercial, and this support, though it fluctuated and was never publicly detailed, was one of the conditions that allowed the North Korean economy to continue functioning at a reduced level. China's own calculations, which favored stability on its border and avoided a collapse that could send refugees across it, are part of the explanation.

It is useful to distinguish the direct economic mechanisms from the broader diplomatic isolation that accompanied them. The recognition of South Korea by North Korea's two principal patrons altered the diplomatic environment and heightened the leadership's sense of insecurity, a theme taken up in Chapter 86. But the damage to the economy came through price, currency, and supply chains: the loss of cheap energy, the inability to obtain parts for Soviet-designed plants, the disappearance of export markets, and the absence of credit. The state attempted limited responses. It announced the Rajin-Sonbong free economic and trade zone in 1991 in the hope of attracting foreign investment, and a party plenum in December 1993 acknowledged that the plan then ending had not met its goals and called for a period of adjustment emphasizing agriculture, light industry, and trade. These measures were modest relative to the scale of the shock.

Preferential trade had hidden an important mismatch between domestic purchasing capacity and the international cost of energy. In a clearing arrangement, deliveries could be recorded against planned exports or bilateral accounts rather than settled in dollars at each transaction. A requirement to pay hard currency forced North Korea to earn internationally usable money before buying fuel or grain. Because its export base was narrow and many of its products had weak demand in competitive markets, the change could reduce imports even when a supplier remained willing to sell. The collapse of these arrangements was thus a change in how trade was financed as well as in the identity of trading partners.

Relations with China show why the crisis should not be narrated as complete abandonment by the outside world. China's trade increasingly reflected commercial calculations, while Beijing also had strategic reasons to prevent disorder along its frontier. Supplies could consequently fluctuate and remain politically negotiated rather than disappearing altogether. This uneven support helped sustain the North Korean state without restoring the productive capacity lost after the collapse of Soviet-bloc trade. Diplomatic recognition of Seoul by Moscow and Beijing intensified Pyongyang’s insecurity, but students should keep that political consequence distinct from the immediate economic effects of import shortages.

Section 83.3

Industrial Decline, Energy Shortages, and Economic Breakdown

North Korean industry was an interdependent system, so a shortage in one input propagated through the whole. Electricity came largely from hydroelectric and coal-fired plants, and coal mining, in turn, depended on electricity and equipment. Fuel imports declined, and the flooding that struck coal mines and power facilities in the mid-1990s compounded the shortage. As electricity supply fell, factories ran below capacity or stopped, and those that continued found that spare parts and raw materials were not available. Outside estimates of capacity utilization in the late 1990s suggest that much of the industrial base operated at a small fraction of what it had been designed to produce, although the figures are uncertain because the state published little data.

Transport was affected in two ways. A heavily electrified railway network suffered when power was unreliable, and trucks and tractors lacked fuel. The movement of grain, coal, fertilizer, and finished goods across a mountainous country slowed or ceased. Because the planned economy relied on the central direction of goods across long distances, the breakdown of transport meant that even available supplies could not reach the places that needed them.

Industrial failure reduced the state's capacity to supply households and to sustain agriculture in several ways. Fertilizer plants depended on energy and feedstock, so reduced industrial output meant less fertilizer for farms. Pumps for irrigation depended on electricity. The production of consumer goods, already limited, shrank further. Workers remained attached to their enterprises, but many received little or no wages in kind or in cash, and attendance, which the state still required, often gave way to foraging and informal work. Reports suggest that managers and workers in some places sold equipment or scrap metal, often across the Chinese border, to obtain food, a practice that further degraded industrial capacity. Estimates by outside institutions, notably South Korean ones, indicate that North Korean output contracted in most years from 1990 to 1998, by some measures cumulatively by roughly a third, though such figures carry wide margins of error. Output began to recover from a very low base around 1999.

Energy shortages also linked the crisis to the changing rhythm of ordinary life. An unreliable railway could leave supplies stranded even where grain existed; a hospital without consistent electricity faced difficulty storing medicines and providing routine treatment; a residential building without sufficient fuel or electricity could not provide adequate heat during winter. Industrial losses therefore moved outward into nutrition, public health, education, and household labor. Formal employment often remained assigned even as factories stood idle. The visible continuity of workplace institutions concealed the disappearance of much of their material function.

Economic measurement requires particular caution here. International estimates of North Korean output often reconstruct production from trade, agriculture, industrial observations, and later South Korean statistical models rather than from a transparent series of North Korean national accounts. Comparisons across decades can illuminate the direction and approximate size of contraction, but they are less precise than statistics for states with independently accessible economic data. Similarly, an idle factory did not necessarily mean that everyone attached to it stopped working: employees might be sent to agricultural campaigns, maintenance tasks, or informal activity, some authorized and some not. The analytical point is the breakdown of the planned production network, not an invented nationwide unemployment rate.

Section 83.4

Agricultural Vulnerability and the Limits of Central Planning

North Korean agriculture was fragile before the floods of 1995. With only about a fifth of its land arable and a short growing season in the north, the country had long worked to raise yields through intensive methods: heavy applications of chemical fertilizer, irrigation and pumping, mechanization, and the expansion of cultivation onto hillsides and terraces. This approach worked as long as energy, fertilizer, and spare parts were available. It also had costs. Steep slopes cleared of trees were prone to erosion, and soil nutrients were depleted when fertilizer applications declined. The system had been pushed toward a level of production that could be sustained only by continuing imports and industrial supply.

Farming was organized in cooperative farms, in which work teams carried out tasks under state plans and delivered a share of output to the state. Cooperative farmers received their own food largely from the farm's distribution, while small household plots and animals supplemented it. The arrangement gave farm families few incentives to maximize output beyond plan requirements and few means to respond to local conditions, and central directives on crops, planting schedules, and methods, sometimes poorly adapted to local soils and weather, limited flexibility. When shortages appeared, the state's usual response was to demand more from farms and to mobilize labor through campaigns, instruments that worked poorly when the underlying inputs were missing.

Outside assessments in the late 1990s estimated annual grain shortfalls, relative to minimal needs, on the order of one to two million tons, and North Korea lacked the hard currency to import such quantities commercially. Reserves were limited, and the state had not built buffers against a run of bad years. These weaknesses meant that food security was precarious before the most severe natural disasters struck, a point central to the historical debate presented later in this section. The disasters of 1995 to 1997 did not create the vulnerability; they exposed and amplified it.

Farm production depended on a chain of industrial services. Chemical fertilizers required inputs and energy; machinery required fuel and parts; irrigation pumps required electricity; and transport was needed to move harvests to stores and distribution centers. Disrupting any link lowered the effectiveness of the others. Expansion onto steep or marginal land created additional risks when heavy rain washed soil away, while repeated cultivation could damage long-term fertility. Agricultural vulnerability was therefore partly environmental and partly the consequence of decisions about production targets, land use, and import dependence.

There was also a distinction between producing a national harvest and ensuring that every family could eat. Cooperative farms supplied state procurement networks and usually retained part of their output for members. People outside agriculture relied more on what public agencies could transport, store, and deliver. An apparently adequate district harvest did not guarantee that nearby industrial workers would receive grain, and a poor harvest did not establish which social groups would bear the greatest shortage. Distribution and entitlement—the actual ability to obtain food—must be considered alongside yields. This distinction becomes the analytical foundation of the famine chapter rather than an argument that agricultural production was unimportant.

Section 83.5

Kim Il Sung's Death and Kim Jong Il's Succession, 1994

Kim Il Sung died of a heart attack on July 8, 1994, at the age of eighty-two, and his death was announced the following day. It came at a charged moment. Former U.S. President Jimmy Carter had visited Pyongyang in June in connection with the nuclear dispute examined in Chapter 86, and a first summit between the leaders of North and South Korea had been planned for later that month of July; it did not take place. A state funeral followed, and Kim Il Sung's body was preserved and placed in the Kumsusan memorial palace, which became a central site of the leader's posthumous cult. Many observers outside North Korea expected the death to produce instability.

It did not, and the reasons lie in the preparations made over the preceding two decades, discussed in Chapter 80. Kim Jong Il had been elevated within the party in the 1970s and given prominent positions at the Sixth Party Congress in 1980. In December 1991 he became Supreme Commander of the Korean People's Army, in April 1992 he was given the rank of marshal, and in 1993 he became chairman of the National Defence Commission. Through the party's organizational and propaganda apparatus, and through the security services and the Guard Command responsible for the leader's protection, he had extended his control over the instruments of coercion and personnel. The succession was therefore an institutional transfer that had been rehearsed in practice, and the hereditary principle, an unusual feature among communist states, had been normalized in political language.

It is important to distinguish succession in practice from the formal acquisition of senior offices. Kim Jong Il governed from 1994, but he observed a period of mourning of about three years and did not immediately assume his father's formal titles. He became General Secretary of the Workers' Party of Korea in October 1997, and in September 1998 a revised constitution designated Kim Il Sung as Eternal President, abolished the presidency as an office to be filled, and made the chairmanship of the National Defence Commission the highest post of the state, to which Kim Jong Il was re-elected. The gap between 1994 and 1998 is analytically significant, because it shows that the most severe years of famine coincided with a leadership that exercised authority informally, and that formal consolidation followed rather than preceded the worst of the crisis. Kim Jong Il's succession also unfolded alongside the October 1994 U.S.–DPRK Agreed Framework, an arrangement concerning North Korea's nuclear program that included deliveries of heavy fuel oil, the diplomacy of which belongs to Chapter 86.

The transfer was unusual not simply because it was hereditary but because it took place in a political system that publicly claimed revolutionary rather than dynastic legitimacy. Official propaganda presented the son as the guardian and continuation of his father's revolutionary achievements. Memorial institutions, the state funeral, and the continuing symbolic presidency of Kim Il Sung helped sustain that claim. The arrangement did not remove the importance of the Workers’ Party, military commanders, or internal security organizations: succession depended on the cooperation of those bodies. It should not be explained as a personal inheritance operating independently of institutions.

The absence of an overt succession struggle does not demonstrate that all elites shared the same interests or that every decision was predetermined. Foreign observers lacked direct access to leadership deliberations, while official media were designed to present unity. What is demonstrable is the continuity of key coercive and administrative institutions, Kim Jong Il's already substantial command roles, and the later reorganization of the constitutional hierarchy. These facts establish why the state could survive a leadership transition even as economic provision deteriorated; they do not establish private motives that cannot be documented.

Section 83.6

The Public Distribution System and the Promise of State Provision

The Public Distribution System took shape in the late 1940s and 1950s as the state nationalized trade and collectivized agriculture, a process examined in Chapters 73 and 77. Under it, households outside the cooperative farm sector, which by common estimates meant roughly 60 to 70 percent of the population, obtained grain, principally rice and maize, through state distribution rather than purchase on an open market. Ration amounts reportedly varied with age and occupation, from a few hundred grams of grain per day for the youngest children and oldest adults to 700 grams or more for ordinary workers and higher amounts for heavy laborers and soldiers. Households collected rations, in principle at regular intervals, from distribution centers tied to their workplace or neighborhood, paying low state prices. The system also supplied other goods such as cooking oil, soy sauce, and vegetables, and vouchers for clothing and daily necessities.

The system connected employment, residence, and social classification. A person's ration depended on his or her job and registered place of residence, so that leaving a workplace or relocating without permission could mean losing access. The state also maintained distinctions in the quality and reliability of supply. Residents of Pyongyang and key workers in priority sectors, party and military elites, and the armed forces received better and steadier provision, and special supply channels served the leadership. The classification system known as songbun, which ranked families by political background, shaped access to many opportunities, although its precise effect on food allocation is debated and is taken up in Section 83.10.

The system also served as an instrument of political authority. By making food contingent on attendance and good standing, it gave workplaces, party cells, and officials a daily form of leverage, and it reinforced the notion that survival depended on the state. Students must understand this institution before studying its breakdown, because the famine was not simply a matter of too little grain. It was the failure of a particular set of arrangements through which grain was supposed to reach households, and because the system had few reserves and no alternative channels for most urban residents, an interruption in the flow left people with nowhere else to turn but their own devices.

An entitlement to a stated quantity of grain was different from a measured delivery. Official ration scales described what people were supposed to receive, but administrative allocations depended on harvests, imports, reserves, the priority of the recipient's institution, and the ability of local distributors to obtain stocks. In some places rations were delayed; elsewhere they were reduced below nominal levels or stopped altogether. The distinction between entitlement, actual receipt, and total household consumption matters because families increasingly supplemented missing rations with purchases and private production. A government announcement of a ration did not prove that the grain reached a particular neighborhood.

The cooperative-farm exception was consequential. Farm members had a different claim on the harvest, and some had access to household plots or gleaned fields, although these opportunities did not make them immune to hunger. Industrial towns lacked an equivalent agricultural base. This difference helps explain why the location and occupational composition of a population affected survival chances. It also complicates any simple explanation that famine followed the grain harvest alone: a family in a food-producing region and a family dependent on a distribution depot could experience the same national shortage differently. Reports from migrants and the assessments of FAO and WFP are strongest when interpreted with this institutional distinction in mind.

Historical Vocabulary
Public Distribution System (PDS)

Definition: The North Korean state's system of rationing, through which most nonfarm households received staple grain and other necessities at low state prices according to occupation, age, and place of residence.

Historical context: Developed in the early decades of the DPRK alongside nationalization and collectivization, it became the main channel of food for the urban and industrial population and a basic element of the social contract between the party-state and its citizens.

Why it matters: Because food reached households through this system, its breakdown, rather than the absence of food everywhere, explains why some groups starved while others did not.

Potential misunderstanding: The PDS was not a welfare program in the modern sense of a safety net triggered by need. It was a mechanism of state allocation tied to employment, residence, and political standing, and it could be withheld or reduced for reasons of discipline or priority.

Section 83.7

Floods, Drought, and the Escalation of Food Shortages

A series of natural disasters struck an economy already in difficulty. Heavy flooding in July and August 1995 damaged farmland, destroyed stored grain, and disrupted mines, power plants, and transport, and in August the North Korean government made an unusual appeal to the United Nations and other governments for help, an acknowledgment of need that departed from its usual insistence on self-reliance. Floods recurred in 1996, and in 1997 a drought and damage from a storm surge on the west coast hit harvests again. North Korean authorities reported large losses of grain and large numbers of people affected, figures that international assessments treated as indicating serious damage while debating their precise scale.

The effect of these events depended on conditions that human decisions had shaped. Hillsides cleared for cultivation and fuel, with reduced forest cover and eroded soils, increased runoff and flood damage. Depleted reserves left little margin, and the shortage of hard currency limited the capacity to import. Fertilizer and fuel shortages meant that farms could not replant or recover fully. International assessments from the Food and Agriculture Organization and the World Food Programme in these years documented shortfalls in cereals far larger than weather alone could explain, and they described a structural food deficit.

The pattern illustrates why environmental shocks intensified a crisis already rooted in economic and institutional weaknesses without being its sole cause. Comparable floods or droughts in a society with reserves, diversified import capacity, and flexible distribution might have caused hardship without mass death. In North Korea in the mid-1990s the same events fell on a system that could neither absorb the loss nor redistribute what remained effectively.

The sequence of disasters also raised difficult questions about evidence. North Korean reports emphasized damaged farmland, homeless residents, and lost grain, whereas humanitarian field missions had to evaluate crop conditions, estimated needs, and observed food consumption while operating under negotiated access. Different estimates of losses need not indicate that one party fabricated the entire emergency: they could reflect different territories, crops, seasons, or methods of calculation. The best historical explanation cross-checks official damage claims against independent crop assessments, trade and import data, and testimony from affected regions.

The floods had consequences beyond the area submerged. A damaged railway bridge could delay delivery of fertilizer or grain to districts that had not flooded, and a flooded mine could reduce fuel available for the next agricultural season. Drought then struck an already weakened production system. Because shortages of electricity, chemicals, and transport were mutually reinforcing, each harvest began with fewer resources than the previous one. Repeated shocks mattered through cumulative institutional exhaustion as well as their direct impact on agricultural output.

Weather also became part of the regime's public explanation for suffering. Natural disasters were real, but assigning primary responsibility to rain and drought directed attention away from policy choices, restrictions on movement and commerce, and the allocation of scarce stocks. An official causal claim must be distinguished from a historical conclusion supported by several independent types of evidence. The central issue is not whether the floods occurred; it is why their consequences became catastrophic within the North Korean system.

Section 83.8

The Breakdown of Public Food Distribution

The breakdown was gradual and uneven. Ration reductions and interruptions had appeared in some regions, particularly the northeast, in the early 1990s, and by 1994 and 1995 supplies in many areas were irregular and smaller than the nominal standard. In the worst-affected areas, rations stopped for months at a time in 1996 and 1997. Pyongyang and some favored institutions continued to receive supplies longer and more reliably, though at reduced levels, while other districts received little or nothing. Partial and irregular distribution continued in many places, and the system began to recover to a limited degree after 1998 with international aid and better harvests, though it never returned to its earlier role.

The breakdown altered relationships among households, workplaces, local officials, and the center. Local officials who controlled limited stocks faced choices about whom to supply, and personal connections, institutional position, and the ability to pay or to offer favors increasingly determined access. The state intensified its demands for grain procurement from cooperative farms at the same time as farm families, facing their own shortages, had incentives to hold back part of the harvest, to under-report, or to divert grain to private use, creating tension between farmers and the authorities. Workplaces, which had been conduits of provision, ceased to be reliable; workers who were not paid or fed had to find food elsewhere, but the state still expected attendance and political participation.

The result was a reversal of the system's premise. Citizens had been told that the state would feed them in exchange for work and loyalty. Increasingly, they fed themselves, while the state continued to demand loyalty and, in principle, labor. This asymmetry, a state that still claimed the prerogatives of provision without delivering its substance, was the condition from which both the political settlement of the Kim Jong Il years and the market society of the following decade emerged.

Evidence collected later from North Korean migrants helps illuminate the change in the sources of food. In a study of migrants interviewed in China, National Research Council researchers found that the share describing government rations as their household's principal food source fell dramatically between 1995 and 1997, while buying, barter, foraging, and cultivation became more important. This was a study of migrants, not a representative sample of all North Koreans; its value is in showing a mechanism of change that is consistent with other evidence, not in providing a national percentage. It demonstrates why a formal system can continue to exist in law while losing its central place in people's lives.

The failure of distribution also changed local authority. A factory director who previously allocated tasks and benefits might now be asked to authorize absence, identify surplus equipment for exchange, or tolerate an employee's trading. Distribution employees, police officers, and local administrators could interpret regulations strictly, overlook violations, or demand unofficial payment. These possibilities varied across places and officials; it would be misleading to assume that every transaction involved a bribe or that all local authorities behaved alike. Yet the multiplication of such negotiations is an important measure of the declining reach of centralized allocation.

Section 83.9

Famine, Starvation, Disease, and Demographic Loss

The famine killed in several ways. Some people died of starvation, but many more died of illnesses to which malnourished bodies were vulnerable: diarrheal diseases, respiratory infections, and tuberculosis. Shortages of medicines and fuel, the breakdown of water and heating systems, and the severity of the northern winters compounded the effects. Children and older people were disproportionately vulnerable; young children suffered long-term harm through stunting, which reflects chronic undernutrition, and wasting, which reflects acute undernutrition, and pregnant and nursing women faced elevated risks. The worst period for mortality, by most accounts, ran from about 1995 or 1996 to 1998, with the most severe conditions in the northeastern provinces, although scholars differ on exact starting and ending dates and some date the onset of food stress to 1993 or earlier.

Estimates of the number who died differ widely, and the differences reflect the methods and evidence behind them. Several kinds of evidence have been used. One approach uses population projections and demographic data available at the time; later analyses can also compare North Korea's 1993 and 2008 censuses, supported by international organizations, to estimate the shortfall in population relative to what would have been expected; this method depends on assumptions about emigration, fertility, and the reliability of the censuses themselves, and a lower birth rate during the famine years also reduced the population independently of deaths. A second approach uses interviews and surveys of refugees in China and South Korea, asking about deaths in their households and communities; these provide direct testimony, but refugees were disproportionately from the northeast, from households that had managed to leave, and from the survivors of the crisis, which skews the sample. A third uses nutrition surveys, which measure the condition of children but do not directly measure deaths. A fourth is official statements, including figures cited by North Korean officials in the late 1990s that were far below most outside estimates.

Taken together, the estimates fall into a range. A figure of about two hundred thousand has been associated with North Korean officials. Demographic studies using census and survey evidence, including Daniel Goodkind and Loraine West’s 2001 estimate of approximately 600,000 to one million excess deaths during 1995–2000, support estimates in the hundreds of thousands, and the share of the pre-famine population at a few percent. Some estimates by activist organizations and by writers such as Andrew Natsios reach two to three million, though these rest on weaker evidence and are treated with caution by most demographers. Historians cannot give one authoritative number. The responsible approach is to present the range, explain the methods behind it, and recognize that all the evidence points to a catastrophe of very large human cost.

Excess mortality means deaths above the number expected under plausible conditions without the catastrophe; it is not simply the number of deaths recorded during the famine years. A calculation requires estimates of how many people would have lived and died in ordinary circumstances, as well as adjustments for births, migration, and the accuracy of population counts. This explains why two careful demographic studies can disagree while both recognize mass death. A decline in fertility during famine reduces the later population but cannot legitimately be counted as a death. Likewise, evidence from a malnutrition survey helps measure survivors' condition but does not itself establish total deaths.

The medical consequences also outlasted the most acute years. Stunting and underweight among surviving children show cumulative nutritional injury, while the restoration of calories alone could not instantly reverse every developmental effect. Hospitals faced shortages of medicine, clean water, and reliable energy at precisely the time their patients were most vulnerable. The 1998 nutrition survey and subsequent assessments allow historians to describe this wider human damage without using it as a substitute for a mortality count. The ethical obligation is to convey both the scale of suffering and the limits of what available records can measure.

Historical Debate

What Caused the North Korean Famine?

The question: Was the famine primarily the result of natural disasters, of the collapse of external economic support, of the structural failures of the state-directed economy and its agricultural policy, or of the way the state allocated and withheld food? Few historians choose a single answer, and the debate concerns the weight and interaction of these factors.

Official North Korean interpretation: The government attributed the crisis chiefly to floods and drought and to hostile external pressure, and used the language of the Arduous March to frame it as a trial endured by a loyal population.
Economic and structural interpretations: Many scholars emphasize the loss of Soviet and Chinese support, energy and fertilizer shortage, and the vulnerability of a heavily input-dependent agriculture, arguing that disasters struck a system without resilience.
Entitlement and distribution interpretations: Drawing on the argument associated with Amartya Sen that famines often result from the loss of people's means of obtaining food and not only from shortage of supply, scholars such as Stephan Haggard and Marcus Noland have stressed that the central failure lay in access: the breakdown of the PDS, the collapse of urban purchasing power, and the political allocation of what food remained, together with the state's reluctance to seek help early and to permit open access.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on agricultural and trade assessments by FAO and WFP, census and survey evidence, refugee testimony, and analysis of official policy. The evidence supports the importance of all the factors, and the state's own information controls limit the ability to test any single account.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns causation and weight, not whether disasters occurred or whether the economy had failed. It also concerns the question of intent: most scholars find evidence of prioritization of the capital, the military, and favored institutions, but the claim that the state deliberately starved particular regions or classes is much less well established.

Why it matters: If the famine is explained mainly by weather, it appears as a natural calamity; if it is explained by economic structure and the allocation of food, it appears as a failure of institutions and policy with implications for the legitimacy and accountability of the state.

Student task: Construct an argument for the relative importance of two of these factors, and identify a piece of evidence that could weaken your own argument.

Section 83.10

The Unequal Geography of Hunger

The famine did not fall evenly. Pyongyang, whose residents held priority within the PDS and whose officials controlled access to stocks, experienced reductions and shortages, but conditions there were on the whole better than elsewhere. The industrial and mining cities of the northeast, whose populations were large, nonagricultural, and dependent on the PDS, suffered most: North and South Hamgyong and Ryanggang had little surplus grain, were far from the western grain districts, and depended on transport that was failing. Agricultural districts in the western plains, notably the Hwanghae provinces, produced grain, but even there farm families faced procurement demands and reduced inputs, and conditions varied by farm and county. Northern border regions combined severe hunger with proximity to China, which offered both opportunities for trade and the risks of illicit crossing.

Access to food depended on geography, institutional position, employment, and social connection. Members of the military and personnel of favored enterprises generally received better supplies, though soldiers, too, suffered malnutrition in many units. People with relatives in the countryside, in China, or among the pro-Pyongyang Korean community in Japan could draw on help, and some who had remittances were better placed. Those who lacked such connections, the elderly living alone, the unemployed, and families in remote or mining districts, were most exposed.

Whether the pattern reflects deliberate discrimination is a matter of evidence. The prioritization of Pyongyang, the military, and key institutions is well documented. Claims that the songbun system was used to withhold food from families of low political standing rest more on testimony than on documentary proof, and the concentration of deaths in the northeast is also explained by its structural position as a food-deficit industrial region. Historians therefore describe a hierarchy of access that favored the politically central and institutionally protected, while remaining cautious about asserting a deliberate policy of regional or class punishment.

The geography can be understood through a contrast between grain production and grain dependence. The western plains included important areas of rice and maize cultivation; the industrial northeast combined factories, mines, and dense nonfarm populations. Workers in that northeast depended on deliveries over routes increasingly disrupted by energy and transport shortages. In a 1999 crop-and-food assessment, FAO and WFP observed that areas with a larger agricultural share of the population often had better direct coping opportunities than heavily urban or industrial districts, notably North Hamgyong. That finding identifies a structural mechanism rather than proving that every western household was secure or every northeastern one hungry.

Historians should also separate geographic patterns that can be established from the motives attributed to policymakers. Prioritization of the capital and selected institutions is supported by multiple types of evidence; a national order explicitly commanding the starvation of a particular province is a much more specific claim and requires different proof. The population of the northeast included families assigned there for political reasons as well as workers with otherwise different standing. A regional death concentration might therefore reflect both institutional favoritism and the vulnerability of an industrial food-deficit economy. Neither factor should erase the other.

Section 83.11

Families, Communities, and Strategies for Survival

As rations and wages failed, households improvised. People gathered wild plants, grasses, roots, and tree bark, and prepared what were called substitute foods, such as ground corn stalks and cobs, to stretch limited grain. Families cleared and cultivated small plots on hillsides and unused land, which supplemented diets but, by encouraging further clearing of slopes, contributed to erosion and flood risk. They sold or bartered household possessions, clothing, tools, and furniture for food, and some fished or hunted where possible. Farm theft, both from collective fields and from storage, became common enough that the authorities treated it as a serious offense.

Movement was another strategy. Some people traveled to relatives in the countryside, where food was more likely to be available; others moved toward markets, railway stations, or the border in search of food or work. Informal assistance within kinship and neighborhood networks mattered greatly: families shared what they had, and those with relatives abroad or in better-supplied areas could call on them. Yet the crisis also strained the bonds of community. Theft, abandonment of elderly or sick family members, and breakdowns of trust are documented in testimony, and they belong in any honest account alongside evidence of mutual aid.

These strategies mattered historically because they were learned. People who had depended on institutions for decades discovered that they could, and had to, obtain food through their own initiative, through exchange, and through personal networks. This knowledge, and the habits of calculation, trade, and bargaining that went with it, persisted after the acute emergency passed and formed the basis of the market economy that followed.

Survival was not a single household decision made once. A family might begin by eating less, then exchange clothing for grain, seek help from a rural relative, and eventually send one member to trade or search for work. Each step changed the family's future options. Selling a blanket could buy food in the present while making the winter more dangerous; exchanging tools could remove the means of producing income later; leaving children in the care of neighbors could preserve a household's trading capacity while increasing the children's dependence on others. Such trade-offs help explain why severe hunger continued even among families exercising considerable initiative.

Distance and access to networks shaped the usefulness of these strategies. A person living near a Chinese border crossing or a town market might find buyers and suppliers unavailable to someone in an isolated industrial settlement. Rural relatives could help, but cooperative farmers were also vulnerable to poor harvests and procurement demands. Some migrants interviewed by researchers described buying, bartering, growing, or gathering food after ration deliveries failed. Their evidence should not be universalized: those with the resources or contacts to travel were more likely to survive and later be interviewed than people who never escaped a remote district.

People's adaptations were not necessarily expressions of opposition to the regime. Trading grain, traveling without a permit, or keeping some produce for family consumption could be understood as practical necessities rather than deliberate political protest. The same household might attend compulsory political meetings, display loyalty to the leadership, and break economic regulations to feed children. This coexistence of outward institutional continuity and private improvisation is central to understanding why new market activity did not automatically produce organized democratic movements.

Section 83.12

Women, Children, and the Transformation of Household Life

The crisis reorganized domestic labor and responsibility. Women, who were primarily responsible for feeding and caring for the household, bore much of the burden of finding food. Men were generally assigned to workplaces whose attendance the state continued to enforce even when they offered neither wages nor rations, while many married women, who were less often tied to full-time assigned work, had greater freedom of movement and time. This gendered division of obligations meant that women were often the household members who traveled, gathered, bartered, and traded, and it established the social conditions for their later prominence in informal commerce, examined in Section 83.18.

Children were affected in several ways. Malnutrition harmed their growth and health; schools and kindergartens, which had been part of the state's provision, were disrupted by shortages, closures, and absence, as pupils and teachers alike struggled to obtain food. Families separated as members left in search of food, and some children were orphaned or abandoned. The authorities maintained orphanages and institutions for young children, but these were often themselves short of food. Children living on their own in markets, stations, and streets became known by the term kkotjebi, and their visibility, including to foreign observers and in refugee testimony, became one of the crisis's emblematic features, though the number cannot be established with confidence.

Older people, particularly those without family support, faced severe risk, and mortality among them was high. Birth rates fell sharply, marriages were postponed, and the structure of families changed. The experience of these years shaped a generation: children who grew up during the famine entered adulthood in a society where reliance on the state for provision had been shaken and where commercial activity was familiar.

The state's pre-crisis gender arrangements help explain the pattern without treating all women as economically free. Women remained subject to political campaigns, workplace obligations, household registration, and surveillance. Married women were often positioned differently from men in assigned employment, while unmarried women, mothers in different work categories, and women in rural cooperatives faced different constraints. The growing importance of market activity therefore reflected a specific distribution of official and domestic obligations, not an inherent entrepreneurial disposition. It also layered new commercial responsibilities on top of existing unpaid care work.

Loss of food altered schools in ways that enrollment figures alone might not register. Buildings and teachers could remain formally assigned while classes became intermittent, children searched for food, or families used children to help collect and sell small goods. Malnutrition could affect concentration and attendance even where a school was open. The category kkotjebi requires particular care: children living and moving outside ordinary family support were not always without parents, and the label could obscure the distinct circumstances of homelessness, separation, temporary migration, and institutional failure. Human Rights Watch's early accounts of children on the Chinese side of the border provide evidence of visible vulnerability, not a comprehensive count of North Korean children.

Gender and generation intersected in the longer aftermath. Some adolescent girls and boys learned to trade or transport goods while schooling was disrupted; women who established customer and supply networks could later become central providers for extended families; and grandparents often depended on younger relatives whose own livelihoods were insecure. Researchers reconstruct these changes largely from later interviews, so the pace and distribution of transformation remain uncertain. What is clear is that the household became a crucial site of economic organization at a time when public institutions could no longer deliver their promised material security.

Everyday Life and Social History

Eating, Working, and Surviving in the 1990s

Daily life changed in concrete ways. Before the crisis, an urban household could expect its grain to arrive through the distribution center, tied to the workplace, and the rhythm of the day centered on assigned work, political study sessions, and neighborhood organization through units known as inminban. As distribution faltered, the household's day came to center on securing food: a morning of gathering or travel, an afternoon of bartering or tending a hillside plot, and attendance at work or meetings where the state still required it. Meals shrank and changed in content, with more reliance on maize, potatoes, and gathered plants. Heating and lighting were unreliable, and water and sanitation systems deteriorated in many places.

Experiences differed sharply by place and position. A cadre family in Pyongyang, a mining family in North Hamgyong, a farm household in Hwanghae, and a family with relatives across the border each faced different constraints and opportunities. Historians reconstruct these lives from sources that are mostly retrospective and partial, including interviews with refugees and defectors, who were disproportionately from the northeast, and from nutrition and demographic data. They must read such sources carefully and avoid treating the experience of one region or group as typical of the whole country.

Section 83.13

The State's Response: Control, Mobilization, and Political Authority

The authorities responded to the crisis through explanation, mobilization, and control, while declining to describe it as mass starvation. Official media attributed difficulties to natural disasters and to hostile pressure from outside, and used the language of the Arduous March to present hardship as a test of the revolutionary spirit of the people and as an occasion for loyalty to the leader. Campaigns urged greater production, the reclamation of land, and the cultivation of potatoes and other crops, and mass mobilization of labor, drawing on long-established methods, continued. The state did not publish mortality figures and, for most of the period, discouraged open acknowledgment of the scale of hunger.

Control of information and movement was central to political survival. The government restricted contact with foreigners, limited access to outside media, and required travel permits for movement between regions, which constrained the ability of hungry people to leave the worst-affected areas and limited the spread of information. The neighborhood and workplace organizations and the security services maintained surveillance and punished offenses ranging from theft to unauthorized border crossing. Reports, based largely on defector testimony and impossible to verify fully, describe the punishment of officials blamed for failures in agriculture and distribution; such accounts are plausible but should be treated with caution as evidence of specific events.

The implementation of control was uneven. Local officials, themselves hungry, sometimes tolerated trading, travel, or small-scale production that the formal rules prohibited, whether from sympathy, necessity, or in exchange for payments. The state also made limited adjustments, including tolerating household plots and informal markets in practice, and in 1998 and 1999 it launched campaigns to reorganize land and promote potato farming that contributed to modest improvement. Distinguishing official claims from documented material conditions, and assessing how rules were enforced differently across districts, is among the main tasks of historians of this period.

Official language mattered because it offered an account of crisis compatible with the leadership's legitimacy. By invoking revolutionary endurance, the Arduous March framed deprivation as a collective test and minimized public discussion of administrative responsibility. That language must be read alongside the state's appeals for outside assistance and its limited changes in agricultural practice: Pyongyang simultaneously sought to maintain the appearance of self-reliance and accepted resources it could not produce domestically. This was not necessarily a single coordinated strategy adopted at one moment. Different agencies and local officials faced immediate problems that national slogans could not solve.

Control was especially consequential where hunger made mobility necessary. A permit system that linked residence and employment to entitlement could inhibit searching for food, while police and border guards were themselves operating in a society of scarcity. Some rules continued to be enforced; others were ignored, bargained over, or applied selectively. Later refugee testimony describes this variation, but it is least reliable when used to infer a uniform national practice. The historical question is how central institutions retained coercive capacity while their ability to administer everyday production and distribution weakened.

There is no contradiction in observing political repression and some economic accommodation at the same time. A local official could tolerate trading because a town depended on it without conceding a right to independent political organization. The state could accept food aid while restricting monitors, and it could allow household plots while continuing to claim comprehensive authority over land and production. These mixed practices help explain survival of the regime better than either an image of perfectly enforced total control or a story of simple state withdrawal.

Section 83.14

Songun and the Consolidation of Kim Jong Il's Rule

Songun, usually translated military-first politics, was the doctrine and practice by which the Korean People's Army was given a leading place in the state's ideology, resource allocation, and public symbolism. North Korean official accounts associate its mid-1990s prominence with Kim Jong Il's inspection of a military post on January 1, 1995. That event should not be conflated with the separate, retrospectively emphasized visit to the 105th Tank Division in August 1960. Military inspections remained prominent in the official narrative, and Songun was elaborated into an explicit political principle in the late 1990s as the 1998 constitutional revision elevated the National Defence Commission. Songun drew on the narrative of Kim Il Sung's guerrilla origins and presented the army as the vanguard of the revolution, a role previously ascribed to the party and the working class.

The doctrine served several functions in the politics of succession. Kim Jong Il's authority rested heavily on his command of the armed forces and security services, and honoring the military reinforced the loyalty of the institution most able to threaten the leadership. The army also took on economic roles, in construction, in agricultural labor, and in the management of enterprises and trade, and it received priority access to food and resources even as its own conscripts, who served for many years, suffered malnutrition in many units. Defense industry and weapons programs continued to be funded throughout the crisis, a point that provoked sharp criticism abroad.

Historians evaluate Songun carefully. It is clear that the military occupied a privileged place and that resources were directed to it, but it would be an error to treat Songun as the explanation for every decision, or to assume that it reflected a settled, coherent plan. Interpretations differ. Some see it as a strategy of securing the loyalty of the military and ensuring regime survival, some as an ideological construction that gave a name to arrangements already in place, and others as a genuine reorientation of the political system away from the party. The question of how party and army balanced one another under Kim Jong Il remains an area of active research.

The military's position was partly institutional and partly representational. The regime used inspections, commemorations, and the imagery of guerrilla sacrifice to link Kim Jong Il's authority with national defense. But symbolic prominence did not automatically translate into unlimited material provision for every soldier or unit. The armed forces included privileged commanders and organizations controlling resources as well as conscripts vulnerable to deprivation. These differences separate the military leadership's political importance, preferential claims on some stocks, and the mixed experiences of ordinary soldiers.

Military-related enterprises and work units also participated in the wider economy. When central allocations failed, institutions with vehicles, access to transport, authority over stocks, or international contacts had advantages in securing goods. Yet the exact scale of such activity is difficult to reconstruct because internal accounts are scarce and outside testimony is selective. The significance is that the state institutions did not simply stand apart from the emerging markets: some adapted to exchange while still enforcing rules against independent private activity. Songun thus helps explain the hierarchy of power but cannot substitute for an economic account of how resources moved.

Section 83.15

International Humanitarian Aid and the Politics of Relief

North Korea's request for assistance in August 1995 opened the country to a degree of international humanitarian involvement unusual in its history. The World Food Programme began operations in 1995 and 1996, joined by UNICEF, the Food and Agriculture Organization, and several nongovernmental organizations, including Médecins Sans Frontières, Save the Children, CARE, and Caritas. Donor governments provided food directly and through the UN. In 1995 South Korea supplied 150,000 metric tons of rice as a grant. Japan agreed in June to provide 300,000 tons, comprising 150,000 tons as a grant and 150,000 tons on deferred-payment terms, and in October agreed to an additional 200,000 tons on deferred-payment terms. These Japanese arrangements must not all be described as outright gifts. In subsequent years China, the United States, South Korea, Japan, and European donors were among the main providers of food or related support. Total deliveries from all sources reached on the order of a million tons or more of food in some years of the late 1990s and early 2000s, though figures vary by source.

Relief was contested. Agencies sought access to distribution points, the ability to monitor where food went, and the right to conduct assessments independent of official supervision, while North Korean authorities restricted travel, limited the number of counties open to monitors, and constrained the use of Korean-speaking staff. Questions of diversion, that is, whether food intended for vulnerable groups was redirected to the military or elites, arose repeatedly, and evidence on its extent is contested. In September 1998 Médecins Sans Frontières withdrew from the country, stating that conditions prevented it from ensuring that aid reached those most in need, while other agencies judged that continued engagement, despite the limits, saved lives and stayed.

Assessing the effect of aid requires balance. Aid agencies and many scholars concluded that deliveries, especially those targeted at children, pregnant women, and the elderly through nurseries, kindergartens, schools, and hospitals, contributed to the improvement in nutrition recorded after 1998. Others stressed the limits: assistance never covered the full deficit, access was partial, and the state's slowness to seek help and its restrictions reduced effectiveness. The politics of aid also became entangled with diplomacy, including the nuclear issues of Chapter 86 and the engagement efforts of Chapter 85. What the episode reveals about the state's relationship with international institutions is a combination of dependence on outside help and determination to limit scrutiny.

Delivering food and proving where it went were distinct challenges. Aid could enter through a port or warehouse and still require transportation, local accounting, and access to nurseries, hospitals, or distribution centers. International agencies used site visits, shipment records, nutritional measurements, and negotiations with the government to assess whether assistance reached target groups. Restrictions on unscheduled travel and independent interviews limited these checks. The presence of a monitor at an approved facility could not reveal all distribution elsewhere, while suspicion of diversion alone could not establish that every delivery was misappropriated. Both propositions should guide interpretation of the aid record.

Humanitarian agencies sometimes reached different judgments about what compromise was acceptable. Médecins Sans Frontières withdrew in 1998, arguing that monitoring and access did not permit its preferred standard of assistance; other organizations continued because withdrawal could also deprive vulnerable residents of food or medical support. Neither choice can be evaluated simply by counting shipments. The ethical question includes the needs of people who could be reached, the people excluded from access, and the risk that resources or publicity could serve the government as well as the intended recipients.

The nutrition data provide one way to assess change over time but not to assign credit exclusively to aid. Better harvests, cross-border commerce, domestic trading, public-health intervention, and improved external support may all have affected children's nutritional status. Moreover, surveys did not cover every locality or measure the dead. The available evidence supports the conclusion that humanitarian aid was significant and often lifesaving without establishing that it alone ended the famine or that every delivery can be reconstructed.

Section 83.16

From Informal Exchange to Survival Markets

Markets in North Korea did not begin in the 1990s. Farmers' markets, held periodically, had been permitted since the 1950s as outlets for surplus agricultural produce and household goods, and household plots and kitchen gardens provided a small private sector alongside the collective farms. In the 1980s the state encouraged local production of consumer goods from leftover materials, and favors, barter, and informal dealings formed what scholars call a second economy within the planned order. Scholars debate how large and how autonomous these pre-1990s activities were, with some stressing their limited scale and others arguing that they established practices and networks that the crisis would expand.

The breakdown of the PDS and of wages turned this limited exchange into a necessity. Households bartered possessions for food, farm families sold part of their produce, workers and managers sold materials and goods, and people produced and traded items without authorization. The boundaries between what the state permitted and what it prohibited shifted and blurred. Regulations allowed certain agricultural produce and household goods to be sold in designated markets while restricting trade in grain and in state-supplied goods, but enforcement varied with local conditions, and many transactions took place outside official channels.

The result was a rapid expansion of exchange, driven by survival and by the collapse of alternative channels. What began as supplementary activity became, for many households, the principal means of obtaining food and essential goods.

The pre-famine history matters because market relations were not invented in a single disaster year. Farmers' markets permitted limited exchange of products outside ordinary state provision; household gardens allowed modest production; and informal contacts supplied goods that official shops could not provide. The August 3 consumer-goods initiatives of the 1980s created additional, though regulated, opportunities for enterprises and households to obtain income from locally available materials. Later arrangements sometimes used that institutional language to accommodate work outside state enterprises. These precedents did not create a competitive national economy, but they formed legal and practical gray zones that people expanded when conditions became desperate.

The shift from occasional surplus sales to trade for survival transformed the meaning of exchange. When a factory no longer produced wages or rations, selling a repaired utensil or a portion of a household plot's harvest could become the means of buying grain. The trader then needed more than the item itself: information on prices, transport, a trusted buyer, and an understanding of which officials might tolerate the transaction. Those social networks grew through repeated exchanges. A market could thus develop even without an announced national reform because countless households were responding independently to the same shortage.

This transition was unequal. A household with spare goods, access to grain, or a relative in China could begin trading from a stronger position than someone who possessed only labor or a ration card that no longer yielded food. An enterprise manager with transport or spare materials had options not available to an exhausted pensioner. The existence of a market did not mean that everyone could buy food; purchasing power became a new determinant of survival when the public entitlement system failed. This is a crucial distinction between the emergence of markets and the achievement of food security.

In historical terms, the process is best described as overlapping informalization and partial accommodation, rather than instantaneous liberalization. Some activities remained explicitly prohibited, some took place in recognized farmers' markets, and others were tolerated irregularly by local officials. The state later attempted to tax and regulate activities that had grown largely without prior approval. The chronology helps resolve the debate over markets from below and markets from above: both mattered, but at different stages and to different degrees.

Section 83.17

The Rise of the Jangmadang

The jangmadang, literally market ground, developed from the earlier farmers' markets into larger and more varied spaces of trade. In towns and cities, open-air markets grew up with stalls and traders selling maize, rice, noodles, vegetables, and household goods, followed increasingly by Chinese manufactured goods such as clothing, shoes, and household items. Wider commercial networks linked markets: traders moved goods by train, truck, and bicycle between regions, wholesalers and intermediaries connected suppliers in the countryside and border towns with urban buyers, and prices responded to supply and demand and differed across regions.

Prices in the markets rose far above state prices during the crisis, reflecting scarcity, and the gap between the market and state price of grain was a central feature of the period. Local authorities and market managers collected fees from stallholders, and informal payments to officials became part of the cost of trading. Currency was mixed: the North Korean won was used alongside Chinese yuan and U.S. dollars in some transactions, and the decline in the value of the won relative to goods contributed to the shift toward hard currency and barter. State stores, whose shelves emptied, lost their function.

The markets stood in tension with the planned economy. They operated on principles of exchange and price that the official ideology rejected, and they offered individuals an income independent of workplace and state. The authorities responded inconsistently, restricting markets at times, taxing and managing them at others, and tolerating them in practice because suppressing them would have deprived households of food. The jangmadang thus became a site of negotiation between households, traders, officials, and the state.

A market stall depended on movement beyond the stall itself. Rural suppliers needed to move produce to towns; border traders needed to secure goods from Chinese merchants; urban traders needed storage, transport, and regular customers. The breakdown of planned rail services made these tasks more uncertain, and traders worked with whatever movement was possible by train, truck, bicycle, or on foot. A price difference between two districts could offer a livelihood to someone able to carry food, but the cost of travel, inspection, losses, and informal payments could eliminate the profit. The jangmadang therefore connected local household survival to wider, often fragile commercial networks.

Market exchange also created new information. Buyers learned which towns had grain, which routes were open, and which goods could be resold. Traders compared prices in ways that official plan prices had not required ordinary consumers to do, while the supply of imported clothes and household items exposed residents to products outside domestic distribution. These changes were incremental and geographically uneven. In an isolated town, trade might remain small and precarious; in a border district, contacts could support a more varied market. It would be anachronistic to project the better-developed market networks of later decades back onto the first famine years.

Local government could both constrain and facilitate commerce. Some administrators charged stall fees or enforced rules concerning location and permissible goods, and police could confiscate merchandise or seek unofficial payments. Other officials relied on trading to secure food for their own households or offices. Such interactions meant that markets were never wholly outside the state: they developed inside a network of discretionary enforcement, official permissions, and administrative needs. The emerging economic order was consequently neither a complete command economy nor an unregulated free market.

Three categories must be distinguished: official ration prices, official retail prices, and prices actually paid in markets. A nominal low state price could coexist with a high street price if state outlets had little to sell. Under those conditions the practical question for a household was not what rice officially cost but whether it had an entitlement, received a delivery, or possessed sufficient goods or cash to buy elsewhere. This distinction prepares students to understand why the government adjusted administered prices in July 2002 without thereby creating the markets it sought to manage.

Historical Vocabulary
Jangmadang

Definition: A Korean term meaning market ground, used in North Korea for open markets where goods are traded outside the formal system of state distribution.

Historical context: The term was applied to the farmers' markets and, in the 1990s, to the expanded markets that grew during the breakdown of the PDS. Over the following years the state moved from tolerating them to formally recognizing modified market institutions.

Why it matters: The jangmadang were the principal institution through which households obtained food and goods outside the state system, and the foundation of the market-dependent society that emerged.

Potential misunderstanding: The jangmadang did not mean that North Korea had adopted a market economy in the sense of private ownership and legal protection of property. They operated within a party-state that retained control of land, major enterprises, and political life.

Historical Debate

Marketization From Below or Accommodated by the State?

The question: Did North Korean markets expand because ordinary people created them in response to the collapse of state provision, or because the state, for its own reasons, permitted, managed, and sometimes encouraged them? A related question concerns how much marketization began before the 1990s.

Marketization from below: Many scholars emphasize that households, traders, and officials improvised exchange out of necessity, often against formal rules, and that the state's later acceptance followed rather than directed this expansion. On this view, markets were a society's response to state failure.
State accommodation and management: Others stress that the state tolerated and later regulated markets, collected revenue from them, and adjusted its policies, notably in 2002 and 2003, to harness and control rather than suppress them, and that pre-1990s market institutions gave the state a framework to build on.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on refugee and defector testimony, regulations and official measures, price data collected by observers, and the earlier history of farmers' markets and the second economy. Much of this evidence is indirect and comes from outside the country.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and the relative weight of social initiative and state decision, and chronology, including how much market activity predated the famine. Many historians accept elements of both: markets originated in limited prewar and 1980s practices, expanded explosively from below during the famine, and were then partly accommodated and regulated by the state.

Why it matters: If markets are mainly a product of social initiative, they represent a limit on state power; if they are mainly accommodated by the state, they appear as an instrument of survival of the regime. The answer shapes interpretations of the regime's durability.

Student task: Identify evidence that would help distinguish a market created by households from one created by state decision, and explain what each could and could not show.

Section 83.18

Women Traders, Household Income, and New Social Hierarchies

Women became prominent in the markets for reasons already noted. Men were bound to workplaces whose attendance the state demanded, while many women had greater scope to trade, and households needed income. Women carried out the bulk of retail and much of the wholesale trade, produced and sold food and goods at home, and traveled to buy and sell. As commercial income became more important than wages, which had lost most of their value, it altered household economics: in many families the woman's earnings were the main source of income, a change that could increase her influence in household decisions and, in some accounts, shift relations between husbands and wives.

The gains were uneven and carried risk. Traders paid fees and bribes to market managers and officials, faced harassment and confiscation of goods, and risked punishment for violating regulations. Gender norms did not disappear: women remained largely excluded from senior political positions, and the political order stayed patriarchal. Traders who had capital, connections to officials, or kin across the border prospered more than those with little, and the poor, widows, and those without networks remained vulnerable.

New hierarchies emerged. People who could convert position, access, or connections into trading capital, including officials, managers, and members of favored institutions, gained advantage, and some began to accumulate substantial wealth, a development that would become more marked in the following decade and is examined in Chapter 92. Families with relatives in Japan or China could draw on remittances. The pre-crisis order, in which status was determined largely by political classification and position within the state, was joined by a second axis of differentiation based on commercial income and connections.

The connection between women's market work and the organization of male employment was especially important. Men employed in industrial or administrative institutions were often expected to report to work even when the institution provided little income. Married women were more likely to have room, though not an unlimited right, to move between domestic production and trading. Some used skills developed through cooking, sewing, gardening, or repairing goods; others became intermediaries who bought supplies in one location and resold them in another. The scale and profitability of these activities varied by region and household resources. The most important historical change was that income could flow through networks that formal workplaces neither directed nor reliably compensated.

Earning money did not automatically mean personal freedom. A woman who spent the day in a market often still carried responsibility for food preparation, children, and older relatives. Her mobility could expose her to inspection, confiscation, coercion, or demands for payment by male-dominated security agencies. Women with a trusted supplier or relative abroad could build more stable trading operations; those who began by selling their last possessions might never accumulate working capital. This combination of expanded economic responsibility and continued social vulnerability is essential to an accurate account of gender change.

Market income also complicated the existing hierarchy of political background, position, and access. A family's social classification continued to matter for education, residence, political prospects, and treatment by authorities, but cash and personal connections could sometimes purchase services or ease restrictions. Officials with authority over transportation or licenses could benefit from the new commerce. Historians should avoid imposing the later label of a fully developed wealthy trading class on every 1990s vendor. What emerged first was a widened range of material circumstances, from subsistence-level sellers to households and institutions able to convert connections into capital.

The interpretation of this transformation rests heavily on refugee interviews and later research, whose respondents often recall family arrangements that were invisible in state records. Such testimony can illuminate who sold goods and who brought home income; it cannot establish a national percentage of women traders for the famine years without an appropriate sample. The stronger historical interpretation compares individual experiences with the institutional facts that made those experiences possible, rather than treating a vivid interview as a statistical portrait of an entire society.

Section 83.19

China, Border Trade, Migration, and Defection

The border with China became a channel of trade, migration, and information. Crossing points and informal routes along the Amnok and Duman rivers connected North Korean towns such as Sinuiju, Hyesan, and Hoeryong with Chinese border cities, and goods such as food, clothing, and consumer items moved in, while minerals, seafood, and scrap metal moved out. Chinese traders and officials, and the ethnic Korean communities of Yanbian, who shared language and often kinship with North Koreans, played important roles as intermediaries. Border trade provided supplies, income, and contacts that were scarce elsewhere, and its scale and importance rose through the late 1990s.

Migration across the border took several forms and should not be treated as a single phenomenon. Some people crossed temporarily to find food, to trade, or to visit relatives, and returned; others stayed longer in China, working or hiding, often with the help of ethnic Korean families; others traveled onward to third countries and eventually to South Korea. Some women entered marriages in China, voluntarily or under coercion, and trafficking was a documented risk. Estimates of the number of North Koreans in China in the late 1990s vary widely, from tens of thousands to figures several times larger, and cannot be established with confidence. China did not treat these migrants as refugees and returned many under bilateral arrangements, and those repatriated faced punishment whose severity varied with period and with the reason for crossing; access by the UN refugee agency was limited.

Defection to South Korea remained small in the 1990s, with arrivals numbering in the dozens or low hundreds per year before rising past a thousand per year in the early 2000s, and the departure of Hwang Jang-yop, a senior party official, in 1997 drew particular attention. The border also became an avenue for the flow of information, through travelers, traders, and goods, which introduced outside perceptions and contacts that the state could not fully control. Students should distinguish the experiences of those who crossed for a short trip to trade from those who fled hunger, those who sought permanent departure, and those who were trafficked.

Border geography made movement possible but never easy. The Amnok/Yalu and Duman/Tumen rivers separated North Korea from Chinese provinces containing commercial centers and Korean-speaking communities. Crossing points differed in transport links, seasonal conditions, and the intensity of policing. An individual seeking help from relatives faced a different decision from a trader transporting goods or a person intending permanent resettlement. Travel within North Korea could require permissions, and getting to the river might be as consequential as crossing it. The best evidence shows a range of improvised routes and motives, not one uniform refugee journey.

The Chinese side offered both support and danger. Some ethnic Korean residents supplied shelter, food, work, and information, while others feared punishment for assistance or exploited the vulnerability of people without lawful status. North Korean women faced particular risks of coercive marriage, sexual exploitation, and trafficking; laborers might be underpaid or threatened with denunciation. China generally treated North Koreans crossing without authorization as unauthorized economic migrants rather than granting them regular refugee protection, even though some returnees faced persecution. The legal and humanitarian debate over that policy should be presented with attention to actual risks rather than assuming all migrants fit a single motive or legal category.

A published example helps students understand the limits and value of testimony. Human Rights Watch's 2002 report describes a young man who spent months in Musan seeking contact with relatives across the frontier and feared harsh consequences if captured. The report is identifiable and publicly accessible, but the interview is retrospective, edited, and drawn from a small, nonrandom group of people who reached South Korea. It can reveal an individual's understanding of danger and the practical importance of personal networks; it cannot demonstrate how every migrant was treated. The source window later in this section makes that evidentiary distinction explicit.

Border exchange also changed information flows. People who returned from China could compare local markets, prices, and material circumstances across the river. Goods conveyed information even without political literature: packaging, manufactured objects, and new trading relationships could suggest possibilities beyond state distribution. This did not mean that North Korean information controls disappeared, and evidence for the breadth of such contacts is difficult to quantify. It means that food-seeking migration and trading helped create channels the authorities could regulate and punish but could not entirely prevent.

Multiple Perspectives

One Crisis, Many Positions: How the 1990s Were Experienced

Industrial workers in the northeast: Tied to factories that had stopped, workers lost wages and rations and had little access to land or kin in the countryside. Many were among the most vulnerable, and some turned to foraging, to selling equipment, or to movement in search of food.
Cooperative farmers: Farm families had some proximity to food, but faced procurement demands from the state and shortages of inputs, and some withheld produce or sold it in markets. Their position gave some leverage but exposed them to pressure from officials.
Women traders: Responsible for household survival, women found in trade a livelihood and, in many cases, new influence within the family, while bearing the risks of fees, confiscation, and harassment.
Local officials: Caught between orders from above and the needs of a hungry community, officials enforced some rules, tolerated others, and in many cases sought payments, as their own living standards also declined. Their discretion shaped who ate.
International aid workers: Agency staff sought to reach the most vulnerable under restrictions on access and monitoring, and disagreed among themselves about whether continued engagement or withdrawal served those in need.
Migrants and refugees: Those who crossed into China faced risks of arrest, repatriation, and exploitation, but also found food, work, and, for some, a path abroad. Their reasons and outcomes varied widely.

These groups did not hold equal power or freedom of action. Officials and the leadership could allocate food and impose penalties; workers, farmers, and migrants could not. The aim is to show the range of experience, not to equate responsibility.

Cause and Effect

From Economic Disruption to Market Society

Long-term causes

A command economy dependent on imported energy and inputs, intensive and fragile agriculture, limited reserves, a rationing system with no alternative channels, and stagnation in the 1970s and 1980s.

Short-term causes

The end of preferential Soviet and bloc trade after 1991, falling fuel and fertilizer supplies, and the shift of Chinese trade toward more commercial terms.

Immediate trigger or context

The floods of 1995 and 1996 and the drought and storm damage of 1997, a political transition after Kim Il Sung's death, and the delay in appealing for help.

Event or transformation

Industrial and agricultural decline led to the breakdown of public food distribution and a famine concentrated in the northeast; households responded with gathering, barter, and trade.

Immediate effects

Large excess mortality, stunting of children, displacement, border migration, international aid, and the spread of markets.

Long-term consequences

A population accustomed to obtaining income and food outside the state, a state that tolerated and then regulated markets, and new commercial hierarchies alongside political ones.

Historical complexity: No one factor explains the sequence. Weather, economic structure, policy, and the distribution of food interacted, and the political survival of the state was itself a consequence of choices and contingencies, not a foregone outcome.

Section 83.20

After the Arduous March: A State-Directed Economy Transformed

By the end of the 1990s the worst of the crisis had passed, but recovery was incomplete. Estimates indicate that output began to rise from a very low base around 1999, helped by better harvests, aid, and Chinese support, though it remained well below the levels of the late 1980s. The PDS was partially restored but never regained its earlier role, and many households continued to depend on markets for a substantial part of their food and income. International food aid continued through the early 2000s, and the improvement in child nutrition recorded in surveys after 1998 was real but left chronic malnutrition widespread. The relationship between citizens and officials had also changed, with payments and personal connections playing a larger part in daily dealings.

The state responded to the new situation with limited measures, of which the best known were the economic management improvement measures of July 1, 2002. They raised official prices and nominal wages dramatically, devalued the currency sharply, allowed some additional enterprise discretion, and adjusted farm procurement. In one widely cited contemporary price series, the state retail selling price of rice rose from 0.08 to 44 won per kilogram, a 550-fold increase; the corresponding state procurement price rose by a different multiple. Nominal wages increased substantially but generally by far less than the rice selling-price multiplier. The measures aimed to narrow the gap between administered and market prices and to restore a role for official transactions. Price increases, shortages, and monetary conditions contributed to inflationary pressures, while the mismatch between wages and staple prices reduced the purchasing power of some wage-dependent households; these were related but analytically distinct outcomes. The following year the state moved to recognize market institutions in modified, regulated form, developments that belong to Chapter 92.

The expansion of commercial activity did not amount to political democratization, and it is important to explain why. The party-state retained control over land, major enterprises, the media, and the security services; markets operated within limits the authorities could tighten; and the apparatus of surveillance and punishment remained. Kim Jong Il's authority, formalized between 1997 and 1998 and supported by the military, did not depend on the state's ability to feed the population, and many people's livelihoods now depended on the tolerance of local officials. Commercial activity may even have reduced pressure on the state by providing households with a way to survive. The experiences of the 1990s nonetheless continued to shape North Korean society: a generation had learned to rely on markets and networks, officials had learned to profit from them, and the state could no longer govern as if provision were its monopoly. These changes form the foundation for the diplomacy of Chapters 85 and 86 and for the market-dependent society examined in Chapter 92.

The post-famine economy was not simply a smaller version of the 1980s command economy. State enterprises and collective farms remained, and the government maintained formal control of major assets, employment, and political organizations. Yet the means by which many households obtained food had shifted toward trade, garden production, and personal networks. Formal and informal economic arrangements operated simultaneously: a worker could retain an assigned workplace while depending on a family member's sales; a local official could enforce political rules while collecting fees from merchants. Such arrangements altered what state power meant in practice without abolishing it.

The July 2002 changes should be read in that light. Increasing nominal wages and official prices, modifying some enterprise incentives, and narrowing the gap between official and actual transactions acknowledged conditions that had developed during the 1990s. These measures did not begin private exchange, nor did they grant traders independent political rights. The difference between a state procurement price, an official consumer price, a market price, and wages determines whether a reported price multiplier describes a real change in purchasing power. Students should evaluate the 550-fold published increase in the official rice selling price within that broader set of prices and incomes, not in isolation.

A second distinction concerns recovery. An increase in production from an exceptionally low point can coexist with persistent food insecurity and damage to health. Nutrition surveys recorded improvements after 1998, but high rates of chronic malnutrition continued, and market access depended on purchasing power. International aid, better harvests, institutional changes, and private activity all contributed in ways difficult to separate statistically. Political continuity should not be mistaken for social restoration, and recovery should not be mistaken for a return to the earlier system of guaranteed distribution.

In the longer history of the two Koreas, the crucial result was divergence in the relationship between livelihood and political rights. South Korea's democratic institutions were developing amid a market capitalist economy, while North Korea's new commercial practices remained embedded in hereditary authoritarian rule. This comparison identifies different contemporary directions without duplicating Chapter 84's distinct account of South Korea's financial crisis. By 2002 an unresolved tension remained: households depended on exchange, and the state continued to claim the power to permit, constrain, or redirect it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Command Economy Before the Famine and the Order of 2002

What Changed

  • The PDS shifted from the main source of food for most nonfarm households to a partial and unreliable one, with markets supplying much of the rest
  • Wages lost much of their value and commercial income became essential to many households
  • Women's economic role expanded, and a second axis of status based on commercial income and connections emerged alongside political classification
  • Officials' dealings with citizens came to involve fees and payments, and the state moved from prohibiting to tolerating and regulating markets
  • The population acquired new connections to China and, through the border, to outside information

What Stayed the Same

  • Single-party rule, hereditary leadership, and the leader's cult
  • State ownership of land and major enterprises, and the formal framework of central planning
  • Control of information, movement, and political expression, and the apparatus of surveillance and punishment
  • The privileged position of the military and the leadership's priority on security

Pattern of change: Change was gradual, uneven, and contested, accelerating in the mid-1990s and consolidating after 1998. Urban industrial workers and women traders experienced it most directly; the leadership experienced greater continuity. The pattern matters because it shows how a regime can survive economic collapse by tolerating changes beneath its political surface.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how North Korea experienced economic collapse, catastrophic famine, hereditary succession, and the growth of informal markets without the collapse of its political regime. Its central claim is that North Korea survived the 1990s but emerged as a different society: the party-state endured, while the relationships among households, workplaces, officials, and the state were reshaped.

The economic foundations of the crisis lay in the structure of a command economy that proclaimed self-reliance yet depended on imported energy, machinery, and inputs, and on trading partners within the socialist bloc. The shift of Soviet trade to hard currency in 1991 and the Soviet dissolution that December, followed by China's move toward more commercial terms, removed the preferential supply on which industry relied. Electricity and fuel shortages, interdependent production networks, and failing transport produced industrial decline, which in turn reduced the supply of fertilizer, pumping power, and consumer goods to farms and households.

Agriculture was vulnerable before the great disasters. Limited arable land, a reliance on chemical fertilizer and irrigation, cooperative farms with weak incentives, depleted reserves, and a lack of hard currency for imports meant that the floods of 1995 and 1996 and the drought and storm damage of 1997 fell on a system with little resilience. These natural events intensified but did not alone cause the famine.

The Public Distribution System, through which most nonfarm households obtained grain, tied food to employment, residence, and political standing. Its breakdown, uneven across regions and social groups and most severe in the northeastern provinces, reversed the premise of the social contract: citizens increasingly fed themselves while the state continued to demand loyalty and, in principle, labor. Famine killed through starvation and, above all, through the diseases of malnourished populations, with children and older people most vulnerable. Estimates of excess deaths range from about two hundred thousand, associated with North Korean officials, through several hundred thousand to roughly one million in most demographic studies, to two or three million in some advocacy estimates, and the range reflects the methods and limits of censuses, surveys, and refugee testimony.

Politically, Kim Il Sung's death in July 1994 was followed by an institutional succession prepared over two decades, in which Kim Jong Il governed in practice before assuming the offices of General Secretary in 1997 and, under the revised constitution of 1998, the chairmanship of the National Defence Commission. The doctrine of Songun elevated the Korean People's Army and reinforced the loyalty of the institution most able to threaten the leadership, though historians caution against treating it as the explanation for every decision. The state explained the crisis through the language of the Arduous March, restricted information and movement, and tolerated, unevenly, the survival strategies of its citizens.

International humanitarian aid, beginning with the unusual appeal of August 1995, contributed to improvements in nutrition recorded after 1998, but access, monitoring, and diversion were persistent disputes, and the withdrawal of Médecins Sans Frontières in 1998 showed the limits of relief under restriction. Aid, trade with China, and household initiative together sustained survival.

The jangmadang grew from the limited farmers' markets and second economy of earlier decades into the principal institution through which households obtained food and goods. Women, freed by the gendered organization of work to travel and trade, became prominent in commerce, gaining income and, in many cases, influence, while facing risks of extortion and confiscation. New hierarchies based on commercial income and connections appeared beside political classification. The border with China became a conduit of trade, migration, and information, with experiences of crossing ranging from short trading trips to flight, trafficking, and defection.

The historical debates presented here concern the weights of weather, structure, policy, and distribution in causing the famine, and the extent to which markets arose from below or were accommodated by the state. The evidence, official documents, humanitarian assessments, censuses, and retrospective testimony, is partial in each case, and historians must read each kind critically.

The July 2002 measures, which raised prices and wages and devalued the currency, showed the state adapting to a market-influenced economy while retaining central control. Commercial activity did not produce political democratization, since the party-state retained control of land, enterprises, information, and security. The society that emerged was market-dependent, commercially stratified, and tied to China, and it forms the foundation for the diplomacy of Chapters 85 and 86 and for the society examined in Chapter 92.

Command economy

Public Distribution System (PDS)

Deaths above the number expected without the crisis, estimated by comparing observed and projected populations.

Low weight for height, a measure of acute undernutrition.

Foods such as ground corn stalks, wild plants, and bark used to stretch limited grain during the famine.

A Korean term for homeless or vagrant children living in markets, stations, and streets.

Neighborhood units through which the state organized residents and maintained surveillance.
Songbun
The classification of families by political background that shaped many opportunities; its effect on food allocation is debated.

Open markets where goods were traded outside state distribution, which expanded greatly during the 1990s crisis.

Informal exchange, household production, and favors operating alongside the planned economy.

The growth of exchange governed by supply and demand and prices, in North Korea without private ownership rights or political liberalization.
Humanitarian monitoring
Agencies' verification of where aid goes, contested in North Korea because of access restrictions.
Diversion
The redirection of aid from intended recipients to others; alleged in North Korea, with evidence disputed.
Repatriation
The return of border crossers to North Korea by Chinese authorities, which carried risk of punishment.
Defector
A person who leaves North Korea, often via China, and settles in South Korea or elsewhere.
Yanbian
The ethnic Korean region of northeastern China across the Duman River from North Korea.

A free economic and trade zone announced in 1991 in the far northeast.

Economic management improvement measures that raised prices and wages, devalued the won, and adjusted enterprise and farm management.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state can fail at the tasks on which it bases its legitimacy and yet endure. The North Korean experience of the 1990s separates two things often assumed to go together: the capacity of a government to provide for its people and its capacity to retain power. Understanding how they came apart requires attention to institutions, information, coercion, and the adaptations of ordinary people, and not only to the decisions of leaders.

The chapter also reveals a Korean history that unfolded in parallel with, and largely apart from, the democratization and growth examined in Chapters 82 and 84. In the same decade that South Korea deepened its democracy and entered a financial crisis of its own, North Korea endured a catastrophe whose human cost is still being measured. Treating the two as parallel histories, as this textbook does, makes the divergence of the two Koreas intelligible without reducing either to the other.

The developments traced here shaped what followed. The market habits and cross-border networks formed in these years became part of everyday life, the doctrine of Songun framed the politics of the following decade, and the experience of aid and its limits conditioned North Korea's diplomacy, topics taken up in Chapters 85 and 86. The commercial society that emerged is the subject of Chapter 92.

Finally, the chapter models a way of working with difficult evidence. Official statements, humanitarian surveys, censuses, and testimony each tell part of the story, and none is sufficient alone. Students who learn to hold these sources in tension, and to state clearly what remains uncertain, are prepared to study the rest of this textbook, and the contemporary peninsula, with the care the subject requires.
