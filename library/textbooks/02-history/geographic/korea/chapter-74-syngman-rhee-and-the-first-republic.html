Syngman Rhee and the First Republic

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On 15 March 1960, voters in the southern port city of Masan took to the streets to protest an election they believed had been decided before the ballots were counted. Six weeks later, after police gunfire in Seoul and other cities, the president who had led the Republic of Korea since its founding announced that he would step down. Between those two dates lay the most dramatic assertion of popular political agency in South Korea’s history to that point.

The April Revolution of 1960 was not a sudden eruption. Its immediate trigger was an election in which officials of the governing Liberal Party and the administration manipulated ballots, intimidated opponents, and arranged the vice-presidential victory of Yi Ki-pung. But the confrontation that followed drew on grievances accumulated across twelve years: the use of the National Security Law against political critics, a constitutional amendment passed under martial law in 1952, a second amendment declared adopted in 1954 through a disputed rounding of the vote count, a dependent and unevenly performing economy, accusations of corruption and patronage, the execution of the opposition leader Cho Bong-am in 1959, and the suspension of a major critical newspaper. Students, journalists, opposition legislators, professors, and ordinary urban residents each brought different experiences and demands into the crisis of 1960.

Chapter 74 studies the First Republic, the political order that existed from the proclamation of the Republic of Korea in August 1948 until Syngman Rhee’s resignation in April 1960. It concentrates on the postwar years of 1953 to 1960, because the Korean War, examined in Chapters 67 through 72, and the physical reconstruction described in Chapter 73 had already shaped the conditions in which the government operated. The chapter’s task is to explain how a constitutional republic with elections, a National Assembly, courts, and competing political parties developed into an increasingly authoritarian presidential system, why opposition survived despite repression, and why the government’s authority collapsed within days once the confrontation of April 1960 reached its peak.

The chapter does not treat Rhee’s presidency as a simple dictatorship, nor does it excuse the abuses documented by contemporaries and later investigators. Rhee governed a state that had been invaded in 1950, that faced a heavily armed rival government in the north, and that depended on American economic and military assistance for its survival. Many of his supporters sincerely regarded anti-communism and strong executive leadership as necessary for national survival. At the same time, the government used the language of security to delegitimize lawful opponents, altered constitutional rules to preserve its president’s tenure, and relied on police and administrative influence to shape elections. Understanding the First Republic requires holding both facts in view.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. Constitutional texts and National Assembly proceedings record the formal rules and how they were changed. Newspapers, including the government-aligned press and critical papers such as the Kyunghyang Shinmun, reveal competing public narratives, though censorship and ownership shaped what they could print. American diplomatic records, published in the Foreign Relations of the United States series, and the reports of the United Nations Commission for the Unification and Rehabilitation of Korea (UNCURK) provide external assessments. Trial records and investigations conducted after Rhee’s fall disclosed how the 1960 election was organized, while later truth-commission and judicial reviews reassessed cases such as that of Cho Bong-am. Student statements, photographs, and oral recollections preserve the experience of the protests. Each category has limits, and the chapter asks students to evaluate them.

The chapter is organized into twenty numbered sections in five divisions. Division I examines the constitutional foundations of the Republic and the origins of authoritarian practice, culminating in the Busan political crisis of 1952. Division II follows the consolidation of presidential power between 1953 and 1956. Division III turns to economic dependence, corruption, civil society, and the American alliance. Division IV examines organized opposition and the crisis of the late 1950s. Division V gives full treatment to the events of 1960, from the Daegu demonstrations of 28 February to Rhee’s resignation in April.

Two cautions guide the narrative. First, the revolution was not inevitable; it resulted from specific decisions by officials, opposition leaders, students, and citizens, and the outcome could have been different had the government responded to the Masan protests differently or had the security forces behaved otherwise on 19 April. Second, the fall of the First Republic did not by itself establish a stable democracy. The Second Republic that followed lasted barely a year, and its fate belongs to the beginning of Chapter 75. Chapter 74 ends where the First Republic ends, with an uncertain democratic opening whose meaning remained to be worked out.

Finally, the chapter belongs to a book that treats North and South Korea as parallel histories. While the First Republic developed in the south, Kim Il Sung was consolidating the one-party state in the north, a process examined in Chapter 77. The two governments each justified repression by reference to security and national legitimacy, but their institutions, outcomes, and the capacity of their societies to challenge their rulers differed sharply. The events of April 1960 have no equivalent in the north’s history, and understanding why is part of the larger project of this volume.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the constitutional order established in 1948, including the presidency, National Assembly, judiciary, and the method of presidential selection.
  2. Distinguish a constitutional republic with real but restricted institutions from a fully functioning liberal democracy.
  3. Analyze how anti-communism functioned both as a response to genuine security threats and as an instrument for delegitimizing domestic opponents.
  4. Explain the Busan political crisis of 1952 and the constitutional amendment that followed, and assess what they revealed about the vulnerability of representative institutions in wartime.
  5. Trace the 1954 amendment controversy and explain why it represents an institutional turning point rather than merely an episode of electoral trickery.
  6. Evaluate how elections, local administration, police, and patronage operated as a machinery of political control, and why elections nevertheless continued to matter.
  7. Assess the political consequences of dependence on American economic and military assistance for government decision-making and legitimacy.
  8. Compare the Democratic Party and Cho Bong-am’s Progressive Party as distinct forms of opposition rather than a single movement.
  9. Interpret contemporary state accusations against Cho Bong-am in light of later judicial and historical reassessment.
  10. Explain the sequence from the Daegu demonstrations through Masan, 18–19 April, and Rhee’s resignation, identifying the roles of students, citizens, security forces, and political institutions.
  11. Evaluate competing scholarly explanations for the growth and persistence of authoritarianism under the First Republic and the reasons for its collapse.
  12. Distinguish Rhee’s announcement of resignation on 26 April 1960 from his formal resignation on 27 April, and use primary sources responsibly to analyze the April Revolution.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the constitutional design of 1948 leave room for an increasingly powerful presidency?
  2. In what ways did the Korean War and continuing confrontation with North Korea make authoritarian practices easier to justify, and where did justification shade into pretext?
  3. Why did the government alter constitutional rules in 1952 and 1954 rather than simply rely on existing institutions, and what do these choices reveal about the value placed on legal form?
  4. Why did elections continue to matter to the government and its opponents even when competition was unequal?
  5. How did dependence on American aid shape the government’s economic decisions, its patronage networks, and its relationship with Washington?
  6. What different strategies did opposition politicians, journalists, and political alternatives such as the Progressive Party adopt, and why did the state treat them differently?
  7. Why did students and secondary-school pupils become prominent political actors in 1960, and why is it misleading to treat the revolution as inevitable?
  8. What evidence do historians use to reconstruct events such as the 1952 crisis and the March 1960 election, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  9. How should historians weigh personal leadership, institutions, wartime conditions, and international context when explaining the First Republic’s authoritarianism?
  10. What did the April Revolution demonstrate about constitutional accountability and popular political agency, and what remained unresolved?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter should bring a basic framework drawn from earlier chapters, which are not retold here. Chapters 64 through 66 explain how liberation in 1945, the Soviet and American occupations, and the Cold War produced separate governments in 1948, and how political violence on Jeju and in the Yeosu-Suncheon region shaped the early Republic’s security thinking. Chapters 67 through 72 trace the road to the Korean War, the invasion of June 1950, the intervention of United Nations and Chinese forces, and the armistice of 27 July 1953. Chapter 71 addresses the massacres and civilian suffering that marked the war. Chapter 73 describes the physical reconstruction, land reform, and aid-dependent recovery of the early postwar years.

Several conditions formed the immediate setting of the First Republic. The country had been divided at the 38th parallel without a peace treaty, so both governments claimed to represent all Korea, and the Republic of Korea regarded the existence of a rival state with a large army as an immediate threat. A very large proportion of the population had been displaced or impoverished by the war. Institutions inherited from the Japanese colonial period, particularly the police and sections of the administrative bureaucracy, continued in altered form, and many of their personnel served in the new government. Political parties were new, weakly organized, and often built around prominent individuals. Education had expanded rapidly since liberation, creating a growing population of literate young people, and the press, though constrained, was an important arena of political debate.

The international setting also mattered. The United States provided most of the military assistance and a large share of the economic assistance that sustained the state, and American officials had both leverage over, and dependence on, a South Korean government they regarded as a Cold War ally. The United Nations had endorsed the Republic of Korea as a lawful government and maintained a commission, UNCURK, that observed and reported on its political life. Neighboring Japan, only recently the colonial power, was a source of resentment in Rhee’s politics and a country with which formal relations did not exist until 1965.

Historians therefore approach the First Republic with a particular analytical caution. It is tempting to read the period backward from 1960, as a story that necessarily ended in revolution, or forward from the military coup of 1961, as a prelude to Park Chung-hee’s government. This chapter avoids both tendencies. The First Republic had its own political logic, its own constituencies, and its own contested legitimacy, and the people who lived in it, whether supporters, opponents, or the many who were simply trying to rebuild their lives, did not know how it would end.

Section 74.1

The Founding of the First Republic and the Constitutional Order of 1948

The Republic of Korea was proclaimed on 15 August 1948, three years to the day after Japan’s surrender, but its constitutional foundations had been laid during the preceding months. The elections of 10 May 1948, organized under American military government with United Nations observation and boycotted by many leftists and by prominent moderate nationalists, produced a National Assembly of 200 members, with 100 further seats reserved and left vacant for representatives of the north. The Assembly served as a constituent body, and a constitution was promulgated on 17 July. The origins of the 1948 states and the political violence that accompanied them are examined in Chapter 66; this section is concerned with the constitutional framework itself.

The constitution established a republic with a unicameral National Assembly, an executive consisting of a president, a vice president, a prime minister, and a State Council, and an independent judiciary headed by a Supreme Court. It also created a Constitution Committee to review the constitutionality of laws and an Impeachment Court, though neither became an effective check on executive power. A draft prepared under the legal scholar Yu Jin-o had envisioned a cabinet system responsible to the legislature, but Rhee, the most prominent figure in the Assembly’s majority, insisted on a presidential model. The resulting compromise placed the president at the center of government but made him dependent on the Assembly in two respects: the Assembly elected the president and vice president, and it had to approve the prime minister. The constitution limited the president to two four-year terms. It also guaranteed rights and liberties, including freedom of speech and assembly, but several of these were stated as subject to limitation by law, a drafting choice that would matter once the National Security Law and other statutes were enacted.

On 20 July 1948 the Assembly elected Rhee president by a large majority, with the rival nationalist leader Kim Gu receiving a small number of votes, and elected Yi Si-yeong vice president. Rhee, born in 1875, was one of the most recognized Korean political figures. He had been a reformist journalist and prisoner in the 1890s, had earned a doctorate in the United States, served as the first president of the Korean Provisional Government in exile until his impeachment in 1925, and had lobbied the American public and government for Korean independence for decades. He returned to Korea in October 1945 and quickly became a leading voice of anti-communism and of immediate separate elections in the south. His claim to leadership rested on this long record of nationalist activity, and it was widely accepted by conservatives, though not by all nationalists. Kim Gu, a leader of the Provisional Government, opposed separate elections and was assassinated in June 1949, a killing that removed the most prestigious rival to Rhee’s authority.

The new state also sought international recognition. In Resolution 195 (III) of 12 December 1948, the United Nations General Assembly described the Republic of Korea as a lawful government exercising effective control in the part of Korea where the United Nations Temporary Commission had been able to observe elections, and as the only such government in Korea. The geographical qualification was explicit: this was not a finding that Seoul exercised authority over the northern half of the peninsula. Rhee and his successors nevertheless invoked the resolution in support of their claim to represent the whole Korean nation; the DPRK made its own competing nationwide claim, examined in Chapter 77. The distinction between international recognition, effective territorial jurisdiction, and an asserted right to rule all Korea is essential to understanding the First Republic’s legitimacy arguments.

The central tension of the constitutional order was unresolved from the beginning. The presidency was powerful and the president personally dominant, yet the Assembly chose him and could, in principle, constrain him. As long as Rhee commanded a reliable majority, the tension remained latent. When he did not, as occurred after the Assembly elections of May 1950, it became a political crisis. Historians therefore treat the First Republic’s history as in significant part a history of how that tension was resolved, and in whose favor.

Historical Vocabulary
First Republic, Popular Sovereignty, Constitutional Amendment, and Term Limit

Definition. The First Republic is the conventional name for the constitutional order from August 1948 to April 1960, under the constitution of 1948 and its amendments. Popular sovereignty is the principle, stated in the constitution, that sovereignty resides in the people and that governmental authority derives from them. A constitutional amendment is a formal change to the constitution, requiring a legislative supermajority; the 1948 constitution required a two-thirds vote of the Assembly. A term limit restricts how many terms an officeholder may serve; the 1948 constitution allowed a president only one reelection.

Historical context. The numbering of republics is a scholarly and later political convention: South Koreans count the First Republic (1948–1960), Second Republic (1960–1961), and so on. Each change of republic marks a major constitutional change, though the transitions were not equally abrupt.

Why it matters. Rhee’s governments revised the constitution repeatedly, and each revision altered who held power. Understanding the formal rules is necessary for understanding how they were bent.

Potential misunderstanding. The term “republic” in this usage refers to a constitutional order, not to a single government. The First Republic is not synonymous with Rhee’s presidency, though Rhee dominated it; nor does the existence of constitutional forms by itself show that popular sovereignty operated in practice.

Section 74.2

Syngman Rhee, the Governing Coalition, and the Politics of Anti-Communism

Rhee’s political base in 1948 was a coalition rather than a unified party. It combined his personal followers, conservative landed and professional elites associated with the Korean Democratic Party, youth organizations, northern refugees, former officials of the American military government, and members of the police and military. Within a year the coalition had fractured: the Korean Democratic Party leaders, including Kim Seong-su, moved into opposition over the distribution of cabinet posts and the role of the presidency. Rhee relied increasingly on his own supporters, on youth organizations such as those led by Yi Beom-seok, and on the administrative apparatus. His government also promoted a state ideology known as the One-People Principle (Ilminjuui), associated with the education minister An Ho-sang, which emphasized national unity and opposition to class division.

An especially contentious feature of the early Republic was the retention of personnel who had served the Japanese colonial state. The police, whose upper ranks included many former colonial officers, were considered by Rhee and American advisers to be essential to maintaining order against leftist activity. A special committee created by the Assembly in 1948 to investigate and prosecute colonial-era collaborators, the Special Committee for Investigation of Anti-National Activities, was obstructed and its work effectively ended by a police raid on its offices in June 1949. The episode illustrated both the political strength of the security apparatus and the unfinished reckoning with the colonial past, a theme addressed in Chapter 60 and again in later chapters on historical memory.

Anti-communism became the defining principle of state legitimacy. Its roots were real: the political violence of 1946 to 1948, the armed insurgencies in Jeju and the southern mountains, the infiltration of the military by leftist officers, the confrontation with a northern state that claimed the whole peninsula, and above all the invasion of June 1950, which killed and displaced millions. For a large segment of the population, particularly northern refugees and families who had suffered in the war, anti-communism was a matter of lived experience, not merely of official rhetoric. Rhee’s government accordingly argued that unity behind the state was a condition of survival.

Historians nonetheless distinguish this security rationale from the practical uses of anti-communist language. The label “communist” or “fellow traveler” could be attached to critics of the government who had no connection with the north, including moderate nationalists, trade unionists, and opposition politicians. Documented cases include prosecutions under the National Security Law, discussed in the next section, and the 1952 accusations against opposition legislators, discussed in 74.4. The distinction is important analytically: a state facing a genuine external threat may legitimately strengthen its security institutions, but a democratic order requires that opposition be distinguishable from treason. The First Republic’s history is in part the history of the erosion of that distinction.

The north offers a parallel but different case. The Democratic People’s Republic of Korea also justified repression by reference to external enemies, including “American imperialists” and South Korean “puppets,” and removed domestic rivals, including leaders of the former Southern Korean Workers’ Party, during and after the war. The two systems emerged from the same conflict and shared practices of mass mobilization and security policing, but they differed in a fundamental respect: the south retained competitive elections, an independent press, and a parliament in which opposition could speak, however constrained, while the north did not. That difference helps explain why opposition in the south could mobilize publicly in 1960, a point developed in Chapter 77 and the Chapter 74 conclusion.

Section 74.3

The National Security Law, Police Power, and Political Repression

The National Security Law was enacted on 1 December 1948, in the aftermath of the Yeosu-Suncheon rebellion, which Chapter 66 examines. It criminalized the formation or membership of organizations that sought to overthrow the state or disturb its constitutional order, with severe penalties, and it was amended with harsher provisions in December 1949. In its early form it was directed principally at the Korean Workers’ Party in the south, other leftist organizations, and suspected infiltrators. In practice, however, its definitions were broad enough to be applied to a wide range of activities, and it became the principal legal instrument of political control.

Enforcement depended on institutions. The police, the military counterintelligence organization, and public prosecutors investigated suspected offenses. The police could detain suspects for lengthy periods, and complaints of coercive interrogation, including torture, were recorded in the press and by later investigations. A voluntary registration and re-education organization, the National Guidance Alliance, was created in 1949 to enroll former leftists; its membership lists, as Chapter 71 notes, later facilitated wartime killings. Censorship operated through a combination of statutes, including a press registration ordinance inherited from the American military government, administrative pressure on publishers, and occasional prosecution of journalists. Estimates of the number of arrests under the National Security Law in its first year run to the tens of thousands or more, though the figures vary by source and definition, and students should treat any single total with caution.

The judiciary’s role was real but limited. Courts did acquit defendants and sometimes criticized police methods, and judges occasionally resisted government pressure. But judicial independence was constrained by executive control of appointments and budgets, by the authority of prosecutors, and by the political atmosphere of a state at war. Cases involving security charges were typically resolved in the government’s favor. The Constitution Committee and the Impeachment Court, which might have restrained abuses, were rarely used and had little influence. As a result, constitutional guarantees of speech and association were frequently subordinated in practice to statutory restrictions and administrative discretion.

The relationship between repression and earlier violence requires care. The state’s concern with insurgency in 1948 and 1949 was based on real armed conflict, and the political killings of the war years were committed by several parties, including the northern occupation forces and South Korean police and military units, as Chapters 66 and 71 describe. This chapter does not repeat that history. It notes instead that the security institutions created or strengthened in those years, including a police force accustomed to political surveillance and a legal framework for punishing political organization, remained in place after the armistice and were available for use against peacetime opponents.

The central tension is therefore between constitutional guarantees and actual treatment of dissent. The constitution promised rights; statutes and administrative practice limited them; and the judiciary offered uneven protection. For ordinary citizens, the consequence was a political environment in which open criticism of the government carried risk, and where accusations of communist sympathy could be used to silence opponents. That environment shaped the choices of journalists, politicians, and students throughout the 1950s.

Historical Vocabulary
National Security Law and Martial Law

Definition. The National Security Law (Gukga Boan-beop) is the statute of December 1948 that punishes anti-state organizations and, as amended, activities deemed to benefit them. Martial law is the temporary replacement of ordinary civil authority by military authority in a defined area, typically during war or emergency; the 1948 constitution authorized the president to declare it under stated conditions, subject to the Assembly’s right to demand its lifting.

Historical context. Both instruments were used by Rhee’s government: martial law in Busan in 1952 and again on 19 April 1960, and the National Security Law repeatedly. Their invocation reflected genuine emergencies in some cases and political calculation in others.

Why it matters. These instruments define the legal terrain on which political conflict occurred. Understanding them clarifies why opposition was both permitted and risky.

Potential misunderstanding. Neither instrument is inherently illegitimate; many democratic states have security statutes and emergency powers. The historical question is how they were used and whether adequate checks existed.

Section 74.4

The Busan Political Crisis and the Constitutional Amendment of 1952

The first major test of the constitutional order came during the war. The Assembly elected on 30 May 1950, only weeks before the invasion, contained a large number of independents and opponents of Rhee, and by early 1952 it was clear that it would not reelect him when his term expired that summer. The government and Assembly were then operating from Busan, the wartime capital. In late 1951 Rhee proposed an amendment to provide for direct popular election of the president and a bicameral legislature, calculating that he could win a popular vote through rural support and the administrative machinery even if he could not win a vote of the Assembly. The Assembly rejected the proposal decisively in January 1952. In April, opposition legislators countered with a draft amendment that would have shifted to a cabinet system responsible to the Assembly, which would have reduced the president’s role.

Rhee responded by mobilizing outside pressure. Organizations that claimed to represent the people, including groups with names such as the White Skeleton Corps, held demonstrations demanding the Assembly’s dissolution or the recall of members who opposed the president, and legislators reported threats. Contemporary pro-amendment agitation involved the extraparliamentary Liberal Party, the Korean National Association and the Korean Youth Corps, alongside other regime-aligned demonstrators; youth groups and the police participated in, facilitated, or protected particular actions, though their roles were not identical and individual organizers’ relationships with the government varied. The National Guidance Alliance, which had operated in 1949–1950 and was destroyed amid wartime violence, was not an organizer of the 1952 campaign. On 25 May the government declared martial law in Busan and surrounding areas, nominally to suppress guerrilla activity in the southern mountains, and placed the army chief of staff and the provost marshal in positions of control. The next day military police towed a bus carrying legislators to military headquarters, and a number of Assembly members were detained. Several were accused of participating in a conspiracy involving funds from the communists; most historians regard the charges as a pretext for removing opposition from the chamber, though the details of the case remain debated and some defendants were later released. Vice President Kim Seong-su resigned in protest in May.

The crisis alarmed American and United Nations officials, who were fighting a war in the name of defending Korean sovereignty and were embarrassed by the arrest of elected legislators. Ambassador John Muccio, the American military command, and Washington conveyed strong disapproval, and American military planners considered contingency measures in the event that Rhee’s actions endangered the war effort, plans later associated with the name Everready. Some Korean officers were reportedly reluctant to involve the army in a conflict with the Assembly, an early instance of the issue of military political neutrality that would matter later. The documentary record, particularly American diplomatic reporting published in the Foreign Relations of the United States series, makes this crisis one of the best-documented episodes of the Republic’s first decade.

The crisis was resolved through a compromise that heavily favored Rhee. On the night of 4 July 1952, with the Assembly surrounded and many members under pressure, legislators approved by an overwhelming margin, 163 votes in favor, none opposed, and three abstentions among 166 legislators present, an amendment combining elements of the government and opposition drafts. It provided for direct popular election of the president and vice president, a bicameral legislature (the upper house was never constituted), and the Assembly’s ability to vote no confidence in the State Council. Martial law was lifted in late July. On 5 August 1952 Rhee was reelected in the first direct presidential election, with roughly three-quarters of the votes cast, ahead of Cho Bong-am and Yi Si-yeong, and the independent Ham Tae-yeong was elected vice president.

The consequences were lasting. Rhee had shown that the presidency could prevail over the Assembly by combining popular legitimacy, outside mobilization, and coercion, and that wartime conditions limited the capacity of domestic and international critics to resist. The direct election itself had a democratic dimension, for Rhee did enjoy genuine popularity, particularly among rural voters and refugees who credited him with national leadership. But the manner of its adoption established a pattern: constitutional change would be achieved through pressure on the legislature when ordinary procedures failed. The crisis also revealed the vulnerability of representative institutions during wartime, when the claims of national survival could be invoked against legislative independence.

Section 74.5

The Postwar Presidency and the Organization of the Liberal Party

Rhee had not originally wanted a political party, preferring to present himself as a leader above faction, but the Assembly crisis demonstrated the need for an organization that could mobilize votes and control the legislature. The Liberal Party (Jayu-dang) was established at the end of 1951, with Rhee as president. It drew on several groups: legislators loyal to the president, an “outside” faction led by figures such as Yi Beom-seok that drew on youth organizations and local networks, and members of the bureaucracy. Early rivalries between the legislative and outside factions were resolved in the president’s favor, and the party’s character was shaped by its dependence on him.

After the armistice the Liberal Party became the organizational instrument of the presidency. Rhee dominated its leadership, selecting candidates and removing figures who displeased him, and the party’s coherence depended less on a platform than on loyalty to the president and access to state resources. Yi Ki-pung, who had served as Rhee’s secretary and as mayor of Seoul and who became Speaker of the National Assembly in 1954, emerged as the central organizer, managing legislative strategy and relationships with provincial administrators. The party was integrated with the state administration: provincial governors, county heads, and police officials often cooperated with local Liberal Party networks, and government employees were expected to support the ruling party.

The party’s strength rested on several bases. Rhee’s personal prestige remained real, particularly among older voters, refugees from the north, and rural residents who associated him with national independence and the defense against communism. The party also provided benefits: access to employment, fertilizer and relief grain distribution, business licenses, and local favors. Where such incentives were insufficient, administrative pressure, intimidation by police or party-affiliated youth groups, and manipulation of electoral procedures supplemented them. The party’s support was therefore partly genuine and partly produced.

Historians stress that the Liberal Party was not a modern mass party in the sense of having an elaborate ideology or disciplined membership. It was a patronage coalition built around a very old and very dominant leader, and its weaknesses became visible as Rhee aged and the question of succession emerged. The vulnerability was structural: because authority flowed from the president rather than from party institutions, any crisis affecting him threatened the entire system. Section 74.6

The 1954 Constitutional Crisis and the Rounding Amendment

Rhee’s second term was due to end in 1956, and under the constitution he could not run again. In the National Assembly elections of May 1954, held with extensive government involvement, the Liberal Party won roughly 114 of 203 seats, supplemented by independents who joined it afterward, giving it a large majority but not a clear two-thirds. The party immediately introduced an amendment to exempt the first president from the term limit, together with other changes. Because the constitution required approval by two-thirds of the Assembly, the proposal needed 136 votes of 203 members, since two-thirds of 203 is approximately 135.33.

On 27 November 1954 the vote produced 135 in favor, one short of the required number as ordinarily calculated, and the Assembly leadership announced that the amendment had failed. Two days later, after consultations within the Liberal Party, the leadership reversed the declaration. It argued, with the support of a mathematical rationale reportedly provided by a university mathematician, that 135.33 should be rounded to the nearest whole number, 135, and that the amendment therefore had passed. The opposition legislators, protesting the reversal, left the chamber, and the amendment was promulgated. The device became known as the “rounding amendment” (sasa-ogip gaeheon), and it remains one of the best-known episodes of constitutional manipulation in Korean history.

The amendment’s content is as significant as the procedure. It exempted the first president from the two-term limit, allowing Rhee to run again in 1956 and thereafter, and it abolished the post of prime minister, concentrating executive authority in the president. It provided that the vice president would succeed the president if the office became vacant, which made the vice presidency strategically crucial. It also removed the Assembly’s power to pass no-confidence votes against the State Council and introduced a mechanism for referenda on major national questions. Some provisions had economic content, loosening earlier statements favoring state control of key industries and facilitating privatization of state-held enterprises. The overall effect was to weaken constitutional restraints on the executive and to turn a temporary wartime concentration of power into a durable feature.

Historians analyze the episode as an institutional turning point, not merely as an example of colorful manipulation. The point is not that the rounding argument was absurd, though most mathematicians and legal scholars regarded it as unpersuasive, but that the government was willing to override the plain meaning of the constitution rather than abandon the objective. The decision signaled that legal procedures were subordinate to the preservation of presidential tenure, and it established a precedent that later governments would follow, including Park Chung-hee’s revision to permit a third term, which belongs to Chapter 75. The episode also generated lasting opposition, since many politicians who had cooperated with the government earlier now concluded that they could not accept the new rules, a development that contributed to the formation of the Democratic Party described in Section 74.13.

Section 74.7

Elections, Local Government, and the Machinery of Political Control

Elections remained central to the First Republic’s legitimacy. National Assembly elections occurred in 1948, 1950, 1954, and 1958; presidential elections in 1948 (indirect), 1952, 1956, and 1960; and local elections, beginning in 1952, selected provincial and local councils and, for a time, local executives. The government regarded high turnout and large majorities as evidence of popular support, and it invested considerable effort in securing them.

The machinery of control rested on the administrative system. The Home Ministry supervised the police and provincial governors, who in turn directed county and township officials. Police and local officials compiled voter lists, determined polling arrangements, and appointed election supervisors. Government employees, including teachers, were expected to support the ruling party, and refusal could affect their careers. Pro-government organizations, among them youth corps, farmers’ and labor federations, and women’s associations, assisted in campaigns. In rural areas, village headmen and local notables, who controlled access to benefits such as fertilizer, relief grain, and loans, could influence votes through patronage and social pressure. Reports from observers, including UNCURK and foreign journalists, noted instances of intimidation of opposition candidates, obstruction of opposition campaigners, and irregularities in counting, though the extent varied by year and region.

Yet the system was never wholly effective, and the reasons illuminate the period. First, the secret ballot, the multi-candidate contests, and the presence of opposition observers and newspapers constrained outright fabrication in urban areas, where administrative control was weaker and voters were more educated and anonymous. Second, the pattern of voting reflected differences between town and country: rural voters tended to support the government and urban voters the opposition, a contrast captured in the phrase yeochon yado, “the ruling party in the countryside, the opposition in the city.” As the urban population grew through migration, this pattern worked against the government. Third, both sides continued to treat elections as consequential, which sustained the possibility of political change.

Elections therefore continued to matter even as competition became unequal. For the government, they conferred legitimacy at home and with the United States. For the opposition, they offered the only available legal route to power and a stage on which to express criticism. For voters, they were occasions for expressing dissatisfaction, as the 1956 and 1958 results show. This is the context for understanding why electoral fraud in 1960 provoked such a strong reaction: because the election had mattered, its falsification was experienced as a theft of something real.

Section 74.8

The Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections of 1956

The elections of 15 May 1956 were the first held after the rounding amendment and the first in which the opposition fielded a unified conservative candidate against Rhee. The Democratic Party nominated Shin Ik-hui for president and Chang Myon for vice president. Cho Bong-am, running on the ticket of the Progressive Party Promotion Committee, campaigned for the presidency on a platform that emphasized peaceful unification and reform. The Liberal Party renominated Rhee, aged eighty-one, and chose Yi Ki-pung as its vice-presidential candidate. The Democratic Party’s campaign was captured by a slogan that can be paraphrased as “We cannot go on living this way; let us change it,” which resonated in cities, and large crowds attended Shin’s rallies in Seoul.

On 5 May, ten days before the vote, Shin Ik-hui died of a stroke while traveling by train to a campaign event. His death transformed the contest. Voters who had intended to vote for him faced a dilemma, and the opposition’s presidential vote was reduced. Many voters nonetheless cast ballots for the deceased candidate, and these were counted as invalid, producing an unusually large number of invalid ballots. Official presidential returns recorded 5,046,437 votes for Rhee and 2,163,808 for Cho Bong-am, with 1,856,818 invalid or blank votes among 9,067,063 ballots cast. Rhee therefore received 69.99 percent of the 7,210,245 valid presidential votes but about 55.65 percent of all presidential ballots cast. Contemporary observers judged that many invalid ballots probably reflected votes cast for the deceased Shin, but the numerical total cannot establish the intention behind every invalid ballot. Cho’s showing, as a candidate with no established party organization and a history that his opponents regularly invoked, was a substantial vote for an alternative.

The vice-presidential contest produced the most surprising result. Chang Myon, the Democratic Party candidate, defeated Yi Ki-pung by a narrow margin, with roughly four million votes against approximately three million eight hundred thousand. The result was politically significant because the vice president was the constitutional successor to a president who was eighty years old, and because it showed that a government-organized campaign could be defeated in a contest in which the opposition had a living candidate and a clear message. The outcome was also a rebuke to the Liberal Party leadership. It encouraged the opposition and alarmed the government, and its consequences extended to the party’s determination to prevent a repetition in 1960. Chang Myon was later the target of an assassination attempt in which he was wounded, an incident that illustrated the tension of the period.

The results complicated the government’s claims to uncontested popular legitimacy. Rhee was reelected, and he retained substantial support, especially in the countryside. But the election demonstrated that a large minority, concentrated in the cities, rejected the Liberal Party, and that the opposition could win when it was able to concentrate its vote. UNCURK and other observers noted the vigor of the campaign and the continued vitality of opposition, as well as complaints of interference. For historians, the 1956 election is evidence that the First Republic was not a closed system, and that its later hardening reflected choices by the government in response to a real electoral threat.

Section 74.9

American Aid, Economic Dependence, and the Politics of Reconstruction

The physical reconstruction of the south, described in Chapter 73, depended heavily on foreign assistance. American economic aid during the postwar years totaled on the order of two billion dollars, with estimates varying by definition, and military assistance was larger still. Aid financed a large share of imports and a substantial part of the government budget, and much of it arrived as commodities, including raw materials, fertilizer, and, after 1955, surplus agricultural goods under Public Law 480. This section is concerned not with the reconstruction process but with the political consequences of dependence.

Aid shaped government decisions in several ways. Counterpart funds, the local currency generated by the sale of aid goods, supplied a significant portion of the state’s revenue and gave American agencies a voice in budgetary decisions. Negotiations between the Korean government and American economic officials, conducted through bodies such as the Combined Economic Board and later the aid mission, covered the allocation of aid, fiscal and monetary stabilization, and the exchange rate. American advisers pressed for budget discipline and currency stabilization to control inflation, while Rhee’s government favored rapid industrial rebuilding and resisted measures it regarded as damaging to its political base. Disputes over the official exchange rate of the new currency, the hwan, which had replaced the won in 1953, were persistent: an overvalued rate made imported aid goods cheap for favored importers but discouraged exports.

Economic performance was mixed. Growth was respectable in the mid-1950s as the economy recovered from the war, with processing industries based on imported inputs, particularly sugar, flour, and cotton textiles, expanding. But the economy remained dependent on imports and aid, exports were small, and agriculture, still the main occupation, was burdened by small holdings and seasonal hunger. When aid levels began to decline in the late 1950s as American policymakers reconsidered their commitments, growth slowed, and urban unemployment and underemployment, already high because of migration from the countryside and demobilization, worsened. The weakening economy contributed to public dissatisfaction by 1960, though economic hardship by itself did not cause the revolution.

The political implications were twofold. Dependence gave the United States leverage over Rhee, but it also gave Rhee leverage over the United States, since American officials wanted to avoid the collapse of an ally in a Cold War border state. Aid also strengthened the government’s control over economic resources, since the allocation of foreign exchange, import licenses, and counterpart funds was a source of political patronage, a subject taken up in the next section. Finally, dependence affected perceptions of legitimacy: critics, including intellectuals and students, increasingly questioned whether a government sustained by foreign aid and a handful of favored firms was serving the broader population. The later developmental state described in Chapter 75 would make economic performance a central claim to legitimacy, a claim the First Republic could not sustain.

Section 74.10

Corruption, Patronage, Business Interests, and Public Dissatisfaction

Accusations of corruption accompanied the Republic from its early years. In the winter of 1950–51, the National Defense Corps, a militia of conscripted men, suffered thousands of deaths from cold and hunger after its officers were found to have diverted funds and supplies, a scandal that led to the execution of its commander and the resignation of Vice President Yi Si-yeong. In the following years, allegations touched many sectors: the sale of former Japanese-owned properties, known as vested property, at below-market prices to buyers with political connections; the allocation of scarce foreign exchange and import licenses to favored firms; the award of government contracts and bank loans; and the diversion of aid goods and counterpart funds.

The mechanisms were largely administrative. In an economy with controlled exchange rates and rationed foreign currency, access to dollars at the official rate or to import licenses conferred a substantial profit, and officials who controlled such access could trade it for political contributions or personal gain. Firms that cultivated relations with the ruling party and administration gained opportunities, and some firms that later became major conglomerates built their early capital in these conditions, though the mature conglomerate system described in Chapter 76 did not yet exist. Political funds raised from businesses financed elections, and the administration’s control over taxation and licensing gave it means of coercion as well as reward.

Historians distinguish several kinds of evidence. Individual corruption by officials is documented in prosecutions, press investigations, and, after 1960, trial records. Systemic incentives arising from controlled markets and aid allocation are visible in economic records and in the reports of American and international advisers. Partisan allegations, made by the opposition in Assembly debates and campaigns, were sometimes accurate and sometimes exaggerated, and they must be assessed against independent evidence. Rhee himself was not generally accused of personal enrichment, but many of those around him were, and critics argued that he tolerated or depended on the practices of his associates.

Patronage could sustain a governing coalition, since it distributed benefits to supporters and bound local elites to the ruling party. But it also weakened public confidence, particularly among urban, educated people who saw its effects in the cost of living, in unequal opportunity, and in the contrast between the wealth of connected insiders and the poverty of many ordinary citizens. By the late 1950s, corruption had become a standard theme of opposition politics, of newspaper commentary, and of student discussion. It was one strand among several in the accumulation of grievances that preceded 1960.

Historical Vocabulary
Patronage and Electoral Fraud

Definition. Patronage is the distribution of jobs, licenses, contracts, or favors by officials in exchange for political loyalty or support. Electoral fraud is the deliberate manipulation of an election’s process or results, through methods such as ballot stuffing, falsified counts, voter intimidation, or the exclusion of observers.

Historical context. Both practices were documented in the First Republic. Patronage worked through the administration’s control of scarce goods and licenses; electoral fraud became systematic in 1960, when officials planned in advance how to inflate the government’s vote.

Why it matters. The distinction helps explain why 1960 differed from earlier elections. Patronage, though ethically questionable, operated within an electoral system that voters could still influence. Systematic fraud removed the voters’ capacity to decide.

Potential misunderstanding. These terms should not be used interchangeably, nor should the existence of patronage be taken to show that all government support was manufactured. Many voters supported the government for reasons of conviction or interest.

Section 74.11

Universities, Students, Journalists, Religious Organizations, and Civil Society

The social base of political criticism expanded rapidly in the 1950s. Primary and secondary education grew quickly after liberation, compulsory primary schooling was introduced, and university enrollments rose well beyond what the economy could employ, creating a large population of educated young people, many of them in Seoul and other cities, with limited prospects. Students often came from families that had sacrificed to educate them, and they absorbed a political culture that emphasized national duty, anti-colonial memory, and the ideals of democracy taught in the new curriculum and promoted by the American presence. The result was a generation that took the language of constitutionalism seriously and measured the government against it.

The state attempted to channel and control student life. The Student National Defense Corps, created in 1949, organized students into military-style units under school authorities, and the government regularly mobilized students for demonstrations, including anti-armistice and anti-Japan rallies. School administrations, appointed or supervised by the Education Ministry, monitored teachers and students, and police informants were present on campuses. These controls were real, but they also gave students experience in organization and public demonstration, and the government’s reliance on student mobilization created precedents that students later redirected.

Journalists, lawyers, and intellectuals formed other centers of independent discussion. Newspapers, including the Dong-A Ilbo, the Chosun Ilbo, and the Kyunghyang Shinmun, varied in their relationship to the government, and some were openly critical; the Kyunghyang, associated with the Catholic Church, became prominent in this role. Monthly journals such as Sasanggye, founded in 1953, published essays on democracy, development, and national division. Churches also played varied roles. Rhee himself was Protestant and many Protestant leaders supported his government, while the Catholic Church, whose leaders included Chang Myon’s circle, provided institutional support for opposition and for critical journalism. Buddhist communities were drawn into politics by the government’s intervention in a dispute over monastic reform in 1954. Bar associations and professional groups also asserted limited independence.

These forms of civil society were constrained but real. Monitoring, censorship, and the threat of prosecution limited what could be said, and the government could close newspapers or arrest critics. Yet public debate continued, and by the late 1950s the combination of educated urban populations, independent publications, and organized opposition provided the infrastructure for protest. This section prepares for 1960 without implying that revolution was inevitable. The student protests were not planned in advance by organizations but arose from specific events, and the response of the authorities, discussed in Division V, shaped their trajectory.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under the First Republic

For most South Koreans in the 1950s, politics was one part of a life dominated by reconstruction and scarcity. Refugees from the north and the war-damaged countryside crowded into Busan, Seoul, and other cities, living in temporary housing on hillsides and along riverbanks, and many depended on aid goods, relief grain, and informal work. In the countryside, where most people still lived, farmers worked small plots, many as tenants or smallholders after the land reform described in Chapter 73, and endured a seasonal shortage in spring when stored grain ran out, known in Korean as the “barley hump.” Families paid school fees at significant sacrifice, and children, especially boys, were pushed toward education as a route to advancement.

Political life reached ordinary people through local channels. Village heads and local officials distributed relief and fertilizer and organized voters; police were a visible presence; and neighbors learned which opinions were safe to voice. Women, though enfranchised in 1948, were less visible in politics, though women’s organizations supported both the government and, increasingly, civic causes. Historians reconstruct this experience from memoirs, oral histories, newspapers, statistical surveys, and literature, but surviving records favor urban, literate, and male perspectives, and the experiences of rural women, laborers, and the poor are harder to recover.

Section 74.12

The United States, the Security Alliance, and Rhee’s Political Independence

Rhee’s relationship with the United States was a dependent alliance marked by repeated friction. He had spent decades in the United States and knew American politics, and he used that knowledge to resist as well as to cooperate. In 1953 he opposed the armistice negotiations, which would leave the country divided, called for continuing the war to unify Korea, and on 18 June released roughly twenty-five thousand North Korean prisoners who had refused repatriation, a step that disrupted the negotiations and enraged American officials, as Chapter 72 describes. American planners considered a range of contingencies, and Washington ultimately secured Rhee’s acquiescence in the armistice by offering a mutual defense treaty, signed on 1 October 1953, along with economic assistance and support for the expansion of the South Korean army.

The alliance thereafter combined deep dependence with sustained bargaining. Rhee valued the American security guarantee and the presence of American forces, which deterred another invasion. But he resisted American efforts to reduce the size of the South Korean army, to stabilize the currency in ways he disliked, and to normalize relations with Japan, toward which he maintained a hostile posture, including the proclamation in 1952 of a maritime boundary that led to the seizure of Japanese fishing vessels. He also periodically called for a march north to unify the peninsula by force, a posture that American officials sought to restrain. Eisenhower administration officials found him difficult, but they judged that no alternative leader could maintain stability.

Historians stress the distinction between American support for South Korea’s security and approval of every action by Rhee’s government. American diplomats criticized the abuses of 1952, the rounding amendment, and electoral practices, and the American press increasingly described the government as authoritarian. But the United States rarely used its leverage decisively, partly because it feared instability and partly because its priority was containing communism and maintaining the alliance. The tension between American rhetoric supporting democracy and its tolerance of authoritarian practice was visible to Korean observers, including students.

During the Republic’s final years these tensions became increasingly apparent. As American aid declined and policymakers examined whether the Rhee government could be reformed, the embassy reported on growing public discontent. In 1960, American officials, including Ambassador Walter McConaughy, were prepared to convey strong disapproval of the use of force against demonstrators and to counsel concessions, as Section 74.20 notes. The alliance had made Rhee’s government possible, but it did not guarantee the government’s survival once its legitimacy collapsed.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Explain the Growth and Persistence of Authoritarianism under the First Republic?

The question: The First Republic began with democratic institutions and aspirations yet developed increasingly authoritarian practices that lasted for twelve years. Scholars disagree about which factors best explain this development and why it persisted despite opposition.

Emphasis on leadership and political culture: Some accounts stress Rhee’s personality, his conviction that he embodied the nation, his long experience as an exile leader unaccustomed to institutional constraint, and the influence of ideas that prized unity and hierarchy.
Emphasis on war, division, and the security state: Others stress the conditions of civil conflict, the invasion of 1950, the armed confrontation with the north, and the legacies of colonial and occupation institutions, particularly the police, as producing a state that equated opposition with subversion.
Emphasis on institutional design and political economy: A third approach points to the constitutional design that gave the presidency broad powers, the absence of strong parties and courts, and an aid-dependent economy in which control of scarce resources gave the executive means of patronage and coercion.
Emphasis on international context and pluralism: Still others stress the role of the American alliance in tolerating and supporting the regime, and some political scientists describe the Republic as a hybrid system in which elections and opposition genuinely existed alongside systematic abuse, an interpretation that helps explain why opposition remained effective.

Evidence: These interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: constitutional texts, Assembly debates, police and prosecution records, American and UN reports, economic data, memoirs, and trial records from 1960. They differ in the weight assigned to each factor, not necessarily in their account of events.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern causation and interpretation more than chronology. They also concern the relative importance of Rhee personally, and whether the abuses were primarily his design or the product of a coalition of officials and party figures acting partly on their own initiative.

Why it matters: The explanation affects how the First Republic’s end is understood. If authoritarianism was mainly a matter of one man’s will, his removal might have been expected to restore democratic order; if it was rooted in institutions and security conditions, the problems would outlast him, as the subsequent crisis of the Second Republic suggests.

Section 74.13

The Democratic Party and the Struggle for Parliamentary Opposition

The rounding amendment and the pressure of the Liberal Party convinced many opposition legislators that they needed a unified organization. In September 1955 the Democratic Party was founded, bringing together the heirs of the Korean Democratic Party and the Democratic Nationalist Party, independents who had left the Liberal Party, and other groups. It was a coalition, and it contained two principal tendencies. An older faction, associated with Shin Ik-hui and including figures such as Cho Byeong-ok, drew on veterans of earlier conservative politics; a newer faction centered on Chang Myon brought in former officials, professionals, and Catholic networks. Their rivalry persisted throughout the party’s existence.

The party’s program was conservative in economic terms, favoring private enterprise, and firmly anti-communist, but it criticized authoritarianism, electoral abuse, and corruption and advocated constitutional government, including, at times, a return to a cabinet system responsible to the legislature. It drew its strength from urban voters, professionals, journalists, and sectors of the educated middle class. In the 1956 election it showed that it could win, and in the Assembly it provided a platform for criticism, though its legislative power was limited by the Liberal Party majority and the powers of the presidency.

Internal divisions affected its performance. Disagreements over strategy, whether to cooperate with the government on some issues or to confront it, over nominations, and over relations with other opposition groups produced tensions, particularly after Shin’s death in 1956 left Chang Myon the party’s principal figure. In 1959 and early 1960 the factions competed over the presidential nomination, with Cho Byeong-ok receiving it and Chang Myon accepting the vice-presidential candidacy. The Democratic Party also hesitated at crucial moments, as in the case of Cho Bong-am, whose prosecution the party did not strongly oppose, partly from caution about being associated with accusations of communist sympathy.

At the constituency level, the party’s work was less abstract than the speeches of its nationally known leaders. Candidates needed local committees, sympathetic newspapers, meeting places, and supporters willing to appear publicly despite the risks of administrative retaliation. Electoral contests could turn on family and regional ties, occupational associations, and the credibility of a candidate as much as on formal party doctrine. The prominence of urban Democratic victories reflected the concentration of educated voters and press networks in cities, but it did not imply that every city resident supported the opposition or that every village was uniformly loyal to the president. This geographical variation gives the Assembly’s debates significance: they linked local complaints about officials and elections to national arguments about constitutional power.

Even constrained, parliamentary opposition mattered. It offered voters an alternative, forced the government to defend its actions publicly, and maintained the practice of competitive politics that would permit a transition in 1960. The Democratic Party’s eventual government in the Second Republic was shaped by the same internal divisions, a point taken up at the beginning of Chapter 75.

Section 74.14

Cho Bong-am, the Progressive Party, and the Suppression of Political Alternatives

Cho Bong-am was one of the most unusual figures in the First Republic. Born in 1898 on Ganghwa Island, he had been a founder of the early Korean Communist movement in the 1920s and had served time in prison under Japanese rule. After liberation he broke with the Communist leadership, and by 1946 he had publicly distanced himself from it. He served as the Republic’s first minister of agriculture, associated with land reform measures described in Chapter 73, and later as a vice speaker of the National Assembly. In 1952 he ran for president and finished second, and in 1956, as the candidate of a reform-minded progressive movement, he won roughly one-third of the votes cast for the two main candidates, a result that surprised observers.

Following the 1956 campaign, Cho’s supporters formed the Progressive Party in November 1956. Its program differed from that of the Democratic Party in several respects. It emphasized a mixed economy with a greater role for state planning and welfare, political reform to expand participation, and, most controversially, a policy of peaceful unification as an alternative to the government’s call for unification by force. Its supporters included former leftists, intellectuals, and some workers and peasants, and its position placed it to the left of the conservative opposition while remaining, by its own account, anti-communist and committed to constitutional government.

The government regarded the party as a threat both ideologically and electorally, since it competed for the same disaffected voters who might otherwise support the Democrats and since its unification policy contradicted official doctrine. In January 1958, security authorities arrested Cho and other party leaders, charging them with violations of the National Security Law and, in Cho’s case, with espionage involving contacts with the north. The Interior Ministry revoked the party’s registration in February. Lower courts imposed relatively light sentences on Cho, but appellate proceedings increased them, and the Supreme Court upheld a death sentence in 1959. Cho was executed on 31 July 1959.

Students must distinguish contemporary accusations from later findings. The government’s case rested on charges that Cho had accepted funds from northern agents and that his party’s program served communist aims, evidence largely derived from testimony of co-defendants and others obtained under conditions later questioned. Many contemporaries, including opposition journalists, regarded the prosecution as politically motivated, while others accepted the charges. In 2007 South Korea’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission concluded that the case had been unjustly decided and recommended a retrial, and in January 2011 the Supreme Court acquitted Cho of the principal charges. Historians now generally treat the case as a political suppression of an alternative program, while recognizing that the details of Cho’s past and contacts remain subjects of research. The episode demonstrated the limits of permissible dissent: a conservative opposition could be tolerated, but a program that questioned the government’s unification doctrine could be destroyed.

Section 74.15

The National Security Law, Press Restrictions, and Political Intimidation, 1958–1959

After the elections of May 1958, the government moved to tighten its legal controls. In December the Liberal Party introduced a revised National Security Law with broader definitions of offenses, including provisions on the dissemination of information alleged to benefit the enemy, and amendments to the local autonomy law that replaced the direct election of mayors and local executives with appointment. Opposition legislators, who had staged a sit-in in the chamber for several days, were forcibly removed on 24 December by several hundred police officers, and the Liberal Party members passed the bills within minutes in a session without opposition. The episode, known as the December 24 security-law incident, was widely condemned as a breach of parliamentary procedure and as a sign that the government intended to restrict criticism ahead of 1960.

The press was a principal target. The Kyunghyang Shinmun, a Seoul daily associated with the Catholic Church, had published critical editorials and reports on the government, the National Security Law revisions, and the abuses of the period. On 30 April 1959, the Office of Public Information revoked its publication license, invoking an ordinance of the American military government on newspaper registration that remained in force. The measure effectively suspended the paper. The newspaper sought judicial review on 5 May. On 26 June 1959 the Seoul High Court stayed enforcement of the license cancellation, but the government responded within hours by withdrawing the cancellation and substituting a publication-suspension order. The paper challenged this new measure; on 29 August its application to suspend enforcement was rejected. On 26 April 1960, after the political collapse of the Rhee government was under way, the Supreme Court stayed the suspension and the newspaper could resume. The sequence distinguishes outright license cancellation from the replacement suspension and demonstrates how administrative authorities could evade an initial judicial remedy.

The case illustrated both the government’s strategy and its limits. Licensing, administrative regulation, prosecution, and informal pressure could silence individual publications and encourage self-censorship. But suppression could also intensify criticism: the Kyunghyang became a symbol of resistance, other newspapers and journals continued to report on the dispute, and the opposition made the closure a political issue. The measure also increased the perception among urban, educated readers that the government feared the truth.

Political intimidation extended beyond the press. Opposition activists were harassed, rallies were obstructed, and the police and affiliated organizations increasingly intervened in the preparation for the 1960 election. The accumulation of these measures reflected the government’s growing concern about the opposition’s strength and the succession problem. It also meant that, by 1960, many citizens regarded the government as having abandoned constitutional restraints.

Section 74.16

The 1958 Elections, the Liberal Party Leadership, and the Succession Problem

The National Assembly elections of 2 May 1958 were held amid allegations of coercion. In the constituency returns, the Liberal Party won 126 of the 233 seats and the Democratic Party won 79, with the rest distributed among independents and small parties. Some contemporary U.S. diplomatic analyses counted 137 legislators as belonging to the Liberal Party after the election, a party-strength count that should not be mistaken for its 126 constituencies won. The result gave the government a majority but fell short of the two-thirds needed for constitutional amendment, so the Liberal Party could not continue to amend the constitution without opposition support or irregular means. The Democrats won most of the seats in Seoul and other major cities, confirming the urban-rural divide and the government’s weakness among urban voters.

The result also sharpened the succession problem. Rhee was eighty-three at the May 1958 Assembly election and eighty-four on election day, 15 March 1960; he turned eighty-five eleven days later. Under the amended constitution, the vice president would succeed him if the presidency fell vacant, and Chang Myon’s election as vice president in 1956 meant that the opposition stood to inherit power. The Liberal Party leadership, led by Yi Ki-pung, regarded control of the vice presidency as essential to the survival of the party and its beneficiaries. Yi’s position within the party grew as Rhee aged, and his family relationship with the president was strengthened when Rhee adopted Yi’s eldest son in 1957. The government also moved to reduce the opposition’s opportunities by appointing loyal officials to key positions, including the Home Ministry, which supervised the police and elections.

The 1958 returns showed that electoral competition could both strengthen and endanger the governing coalition. The Liberal Party retained a clear governing majority, yet the Democrats’ victories in Seoul and other urban districts supplied parliamentary seats, publicity, and a measure of organizational continuity. The difference between seats won on election day and subsequent partisan alignments also reminds students that a legislature is not simply an election-night scoreboard: affiliations could shift as politicians responded to presidential power and the incentives of joining the ruling side. Opponents could expose irregularities in the Assembly even when they could not defeat bills; officials could claim a popular mandate while acknowledging privately that urban opinion was turning away from the party. This was an unevenly competitive electoral system, not a system in which votes ceased to matter.

Succession added a personal and constitutional dimension to these institutional tensions. Because Rhee could continue to seek reelection under the 1954 amendment, the vice presidency was not simply an honorific office. If he died or became unable to serve, the office could transfer executive authority to a politician selected in a separate popular vote. Chang Myon’s victory in 1956 therefore posed a concrete danger to the Liberal Party’s continuity: a future presidency might change hands without the party losing an Assembly majority. Yi Ki-pung’s allies treated control of the vice presidency as a question of political survival, but the particular forms of election manipulation that followed were choices by identifiable officials, not a logically unavoidable result of Rhee’s age or the constitution.

These considerations shaped the strategy for 1960. The ruling party could not be confident of winning a fair vice-presidential contest, given the 1956 result and the 1958 urban losses. It therefore began, well in advance, to plan measures to guarantee the outcome, an approach that, as Section 74.17 shows, extended from administrative pressure to systematic fraud. The structure of the system, in which authority depended on one aging individual and the party’s survival depended on controlling a specific office, helps explain the intensity of the government’s actions and the severity of the crisis that followed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Constitutional Institutions and Political Participation, 1948–1960

What Changed

  • The president went from being chosen by the Assembly (1948) to being elected directly (from 1952), changing the sources of presidential authority.
  • The term limit and the prime ministership were removed or weakened by the 1954 amendment, concentrating executive power.
  • Opposition evolved from a fragmented group of independents to organized parties, including the Democratic and Progressive Parties.
  • The educated urban population and the press expanded, increasing the audience for political criticism.
  • Security legislation was expanded in 1958, and local executives went from elected to appointed.

What Stayed the Same

  • Regular elections, a National Assembly, courts, and a constitution continued to exist throughout.
  • Anti-communism remained the state’s defining ideology and the justification for security measures.
  • The police and administration remained central instruments of political control.
  • Rhee continued as president, and dependence on American support persisted.

Timing and experience: Change occurred unevenly, with sharp breaks in 1952 and 1954 and gradual hardening after 1958. Rural residents experienced greater continuity of local patronage, while urban residents, journalists, and opposition politicians experienced the greatest change, including both new opportunities for organization and new forms of repression.

Why the pattern matters: The persistence of forms alongside the erosion of substance explains both the government’s ability to claim constitutional legitimacy and its opponents’ ability to appeal to the same constitution against it.

Section 74.17

The Election Crisis of 1960: From Daegu to the March 15 Vote

The 1960 election campaign began with a blow to the opposition. The presidential and vice-presidential elections, originally expected in May, were moved forward to 15 March, a change widely interpreted as intended to shorten the opposition’s preparation and to reduce the time available for organizing observers. On 15 February, the Democratic Party’s presidential candidate, Cho Byeong-ok, died while undergoing medical treatment in the United States. With Cho Bong-am executed and the Progressive Party dissolved, Rhee had no serious opponent for the presidency. The contest therefore centered on the vice presidency, where Chang Myon, the Democratic candidate, faced Yi Ki-pung, and where the government’s determination to win was most intense.

On 28 February, a Sunday, a Democratic Party campaign speech by Chang Myon was scheduled in the southeastern city of Daegu. The authorities ordered several secondary schools to hold classes or special events that day, widely understood as an attempt to keep students from attending. Students from several schools, estimated at roughly a thousand though accounts vary, protested in the streets, demanding an end to political interference in education and fair elections. Police arrested many. The Daegu demonstration was a small and local event, but its significance was symbolic: it was a student protest against the use of schools for electoral purposes, and it was reported nationally, setting a precedent for the protests that followed.

Meanwhile the Liberal Party and the Home Ministry, headed by Choe In-gyu, implemented a plan to secure the result. Evidence from investigations and trials after Rhee’s fall indicates that officials directed local authorities to cast a large share of ballots, reportedly around forty percent, in advance for the ruling party’s candidates, to organize voting in small groups in which ballots were shown to party monitors, to exclude or harass opposition observers, and to adjust the totals to produce target margins. Police and local officials were assigned responsibilities, and orders passed through the administrative chain. On election day, the Democratic Party, reporting violations, declared the election invalid and withdrew its observers before the polls closed. The official results gave Rhee roughly nine and a half million votes, an overwhelming share, and Yi Ki-pung roughly eight million two hundred thousand against about one million eight hundred thousand for Chang Myon, figures implausibly lopsided in light of the 1956 result.

Why did this election provoke a revolutionary crisis when earlier abuses had not? Several factors converged. The fraud was planned centrally and executed systematically, not simply the result of local pressure. It aimed to deprive voters of a meaningful choice on the question that most concerned them, the succession. The opposition had no legal remedy, since courts and administration were controlled by the government. The accumulation of earlier grievances, including the 1954 amendment, Cho Bong-am’s execution, the closure of the Kyunghyang, and the 1958 security law, meant that many citizens regarded the election as the culmination of a pattern. Finally, young people who had absorbed the language of constitutional democracy in schools were prepared to treat the fraud as a violation of principle. These conditions made the election a catalyst, but they did not make the revolution inevitable; the government’s next steps would shape the outcome.

Section 74.18

The Masan Protests and the Death of Kim Ju-yeol

The first major confrontation occurred in Masan, a port city on the southern coast with a significant opposition tradition and a population of workers, merchants, and students. On election day, local Democratic Party members and residents protested what they saw as flagrant ballot manipulation, and by evening crowds gathered near the city’s government buildings and police stations. Police dispersed the crowds with tear gas and, according to reports, opened fire, killing several people and wounding many more; the official count of dead on the night was about eight, though sources differ. Large numbers of protesters, including many young people, were arrested, and some disappeared.

The government responded by treating the protests as subversive. Officials declared that communists had instigated the demonstrations, repeating the pattern of earlier decades, and the police conducted further arrests. Many Masan residents, including families of those arrested or missing, knew that their relatives were ordinary citizens, and the claim was widely disbelieved. Local newspapers, opposition networks, and families kept attention on the city, and secondary-school students, who were among the participants, remained active.

On 11 April, public recognition and reporting of the recovered body of Kim Ju-yeol, a first-year student at Masan Commercial High School missing since 15 March, electrified the city; a tear-gas projectile was embedded in the region of his eye. The conventional public chronology dates the discovery to 11 April, but a military intelligence report brought to wider attention in 2025 recorded an apparent discovery and notification on 10 April. That document does not by itself resolve every step in the body’s recovery, identification, and public disclosure. Photographs of the body, published in newspapers, circulated nationally and provoked public outrage. The evidence suggested that police had fired tear gas directly at a protester and then disposed of the body, an interpretation that officials denied or minimized. The discovery prompted a second wave of demonstrations in Masan, in which tens of thousands reportedly participated, and police again used force. Authorities again blamed communist agitators.

Local circumstances made the protests especially persistent. Masan was a commercial and maritime city with students, wage earners, smaller businesses, and organized opponents of the governing party, not merely an anonymous stage on which Seoul’s national conflict appeared. Election officials and police had direct power over residents’ immediate encounters with the state, and families could identify demonstrators who disappeared after the first marches. The protests were therefore rooted in municipal networks of kinship, school affiliation, commerce, and party organization. Accounts emphasizing spontaneous public outrage and accounts documenting opposition-party activity need not be mutually exclusive: a protest could involve organizers while still attracting many participants who acted from their own grievance and alarm. Nothing in that observation makes the subsequent police violence unavoidable or legitimate.

The chronology of Kim’s death also illustrates why the historian must distinguish an event from the survival and circulation of evidence about it. The 2025 disclosure of an April 10 military intelligence report changes what can responsibly be said about the earliest official notice of a body believed to be Kim’s; it does not erase the demonstrable importance of April 11, when news and public recognition provoked a new wave of anger. Photographs and newspaper reports helped people outside Masan see the circumstances of the death, but those materials must be checked for their original date, publication, and context rather than treated as self-explanatory images. A local official’s report, a military intelligence memorandum, a family account, and a photograph may each capture different stages in the same episode. Their discrepancies are a reason for closer reconstruction, not for dismissing the event’s established political significance.

The Masan events changed the character of the crisis. A protest that might have been contained as a local grievance became a national political issue, because the death of a secondary-school pupil carried unusual moral weight and because the government’s response, denial and repression, confirmed critics’ views of its illegitimacy. Students in other cities, including Seoul, Busan, and Daegu, began organizing demonstrations, newspapers questioned the official account, and the opposition used the incident in the Assembly. The roles of students, families, local communities, newspapers, and opposition networks were each important; none alone produced the national crisis.

Section 74.19

April 18–19: Student Demonstrations, Police Gunfire, and Nationwide Revolt

On 18 April, several thousand students from Korea University in Seoul marched to the National Assembly building and presented demands including fair elections and an end to police interference, before returning toward their campus. On their way back in the evening, they were attacked by organized groups of men associated with pro-government organizations, who beat many students; dozens were injured. The attack, which occurred while police were reported to have taken little action, outraged students and the public and was widely reported in the next morning’s newspapers. It was the immediate cause of the escalation.

On 19 April, students from Seoul National University, Dongguk, Yonsei, and other institutions, as well as secondary-school students, marched toward the center of the city, and they were joined by large numbers of ordinary citizens, including workers, young men, vendors, and the unemployed. Crowds moved toward the government buildings and the presidential residence at Gyeongmudae. Their demands included fair elections, punishment of those responsible for fraud and violence, and, increasingly, the president’s accountability. Police blocked the approaches, and near Gyeongmudae they fired on the crowd, killing and wounding many. Protesters attacked symbols of the government, including police stations, the offices of a government newspaper, and the headquarters of pro-government organizations, and some of these were burned. Estimates of casualties vary, but at least a hundred people were killed on 19 April across the country, with one national heritage account recording 186 dead and 6,026 injured across the wider April Revolution, not solely the demonstrations on 19 April. Published totals depend on the period and events counted.

The protests were not confined to the capital. In Busan, Daegu, Gwangju, Daejeon, and other cities, students and citizens also demonstrated, clashed with police, and attacked government targets, and the geography of protest, examined in the Map Study below, reflected both the reach of student networks and the broad resentment of the election. The government declared martial law on the afternoon of 19 April in Seoul and several other cities and appointed an army general as commander. Troops were deployed, but in many places soldiers avoided firing on demonstrators and were received by the crowds with relative tolerance, a sign that the army was not willing to defend the regime by massacre.

The use of lethal force accelerated the government’s loss of legitimacy. Shooting unarmed students and citizens converted anger into outrage, and the refusal of the army to repeat the violence deprived the regime of its last reliable instrument. On 21 April the cabinet resigned, and on 22 April the government began to replace key officials, but these concessions came too late to restore confidence. The events of 19 April demonstrated how rapidly a government that had relied on police and administrative control could lose authority once those instruments failed and public opinion turned decisively against it.

Section 74.20

The Fall of Rhee, the End of the First Republic, and the Meaning of April 1960

In the days after 19 April, the government sought to restore order while offering limited concessions. On 21 April the cabinet resigned en masse, and Rhee dismissed senior officials, including the home minister. On 23 April Yi Ki-pung announced that he would give up the vice presidency to which he had been declared elected, and on 24 April Rhee resigned as president of the Liberal Party, expressing regret for the deaths and promising to consider reforms. These measures did not satisfy the protesters, who had come to regard the elections as void and the president himself as responsible. Martial law remained in force, but the army maintained a posture of restraint.

On 25 April, 258 professors from universities in Seoul, according to the National Institute of Korean History’s account, assembled, unanimously adopted and signed a fourteen-point declaration, then marched through central Seoul toward the National Assembly, carrying banners that honored the students who had died. Their action carried particular weight because of the professors’ status. After the declaration, large crowds of students and citizens again filled the streets, and soldiers did not disperse them. That night and the next morning the Assembly adopted a resolution calling for the president’s resignation and for new elections. On the morning of 26 April, American officials, including Ambassador Walter McConaughy and the commander of United Nations forces, met with Rhee and conveyed the view that he should respond to public opinion. Later that day Rhee announced publicly that he would resign if the people wished it, that new elections should be held, and that he would withdraw from party politics. Students should note carefully that this announcement on 26 April was a statement of intention. Rhee’s formal resignation was submitted to the Assembly on 27 April, and the Assembly accepted it.

The collapse of Liberal Party rule followed quickly. After Rhee’s resignation, a caretaker government led by Heo Jeong, a veteran conservative politician, administered the transition, investigations of the election fraud and the shootings began, and senior figures of the old regime, including Choe In-gyu, were arrested and later tried. Rhee left for Hawaii in late May and lived there until his death in 1965. On 15 June the National Assembly amended the constitution to establish a parliamentary cabinet system with a largely ceremonial president, and elections on 29 July produced an overwhelming victory for the Democratic Party. The Second Republic took office in August, with Yun Bo-seon as president and Chang Myon as prime minister.

The meaning of April 1960 has been interpreted in several ways. It was an assertion of constitutional accountability: protesters demanded that the government obey the rules it had claimed to uphold. It was an expression of popular political agency, especially by students and urban citizens, in a society that had been ruled by security institutions. It was also a revolution of limited scope, which removed a president and party but left much of the bureaucracy, police, and economic order intact. The stability of the new democratic order remained uncertain: it inherited factional divisions, an economy in difficulty, a security establishment whose loyalties were untested, and a mobilized population with rising expectations. The military’s seizure of power in May 1961 belongs to Chapter 75, and its explanation should not be read back as the inevitable consequence of 1960.

Students should also see April 1960 in comparative and long-term terms. The movement established a precedent that later generations would invoke, in the struggles of the 1970s and 1980s examined in Chapters 79 and 82, in the identification of South Korean democracy with popular mobilization against unaccountable authority, and in the memory of the dead students. It showed that, in contrast with the north, where no comparable public challenge to the leadership was possible, the south’s combination of elections, press, education, and civic organization could, under conditions of crisis, bring down a government. Understanding why Rhee lost power requires attention to all these elements: the institutions that limited him, the opposition that survived, the grievances that accumulated, and the people who took the risk of acting.

Cause and Effect

How Cumulative Repression and the Fraudulent 1960 Election Produced a Revolutionary Crisis

Long-term causes

A strong presidency, the National Security Law, constitutional amendments of 1952 and 1954, a politicized police, and an aid-dependent economy that fed patronage and public dissatisfaction.

Short-term causes

The 1958 security law and local autonomy changes, the execution of Cho Bong-am, the closure of the Kyunghyang, the succession problem, and the death of Cho Byeong-ok.

Immediate trigger

The systematic fraud of 15 March 1960, aimed at securing Yi Ki-pung’s election, followed by police violence in Masan and the discovery of Kim Ju-yeol’s body.

Event or transformation

The nationwide protests of 18–19 April, police gunfire, martial law, and the army’s restraint, culminating in the professors’ march and Rhee’s resignation.

Immediate effects

Collapse of the Liberal Party, a caretaker government, a shift to a cabinet system, and the election of a Democratic Party government.

Long-term consequences

A durable memory of popular accountability, but an unstable Second Republic and a new political context for later events examined in Chapter 75.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the revolution. The fraud was necessary as a trigger but would not have produced revolt without accumulated grievances, an organized urban population, a free enough press to spread news, and an army unwilling to massacre protesters. Government choices at each stage affected the outcome.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the First Republic of South Korea, from the constitution of 1948 to the resignation of Syngman Rhee in April 1960. Its central argument is that the Republic combined constitutional institutions and competitive aspirations with an increasingly authoritarian presidency, and that the persistence of opposition, together with the fraudulent election of March 1960, produced a confrontation that ended Rhee’s rule.

The constitution of 1948 created a republic with an Assembly, courts, and a presidency, but it left unresolved the relationship between a powerful president and the legislature that elected him. Rhee, whose long nationalist career gave him genuine authority, relied on a coalition that included the police, youth groups, and the administrative apparatus, and he defined anti-communism as the basis of state legitimacy. The Korean War gave that claim real force, but the government also used it to marginalize lawful opponents, and the National Security Law became the principal instrument of political control.

The Busan political crisis of 1952 showed how wartime conditions allowed the presidency to prevail over the Assembly through martial law and coercion, producing an amendment for direct election that Rhee could win. After the war, the Liberal Party became the organizational vehicle of the presidency, and in 1954 the rounding amendment removed the term limit for Rhee and weakened other constraints. These episodes represented institutional turning points, not simply manipulation, because they altered the rules governing power.

Elections nonetheless remained meaningful. The administration shaped them through police, officials, and patronage, but urban voters, the press, and opposition parties could and did challenge the government, as the results of 1956 and 1958 show. The Democratic Party offered a conservative parliamentary opposition, while Cho Bong-am’s Progressive Party proposed alternatives on economic reform and peaceful unification and was suppressed, with its leader executed in 1959 and later acquitted. The opposition was not uniform, and the state treated its components differently.

The economy and society formed the conditions of this politics. Dependence on American aid gave Washington leverage but also gave Rhee bargaining power, while the control of foreign exchange and licenses fed patronage and corruption. Education expanded and produced an urban, literate population of students and journalists whose standards the government could not meet. Rhee’s relationship with the United States was marked by dependence and resistance, and American support for South Korea’s security did not mean approval of every action of its government.

By 1958 and 1959 the government had hardened its controls through a revised security law, appointment of local executives, and the closure of the Kyunghyang Shinmun. The succession problem, created by Rhee’s age and the vice presidency’s constitutional significance, made the 1960 election a matter of survival for the ruling party, and officials planned systematic fraud. The death of the opposition’s candidate, Cho Byeong-ok, left the vice-presidential contest as the focus.

The fraudulent election of 15 March 1960 triggered protests in Masan, where police violence and the later discovery of Kim Ju-yeol’s body turned a local protest into a national crisis. After the attack on Korea University students on 18 April, mass demonstrations on 19 April led to police gunfire, hundreds of casualties across the country, and martial law. The army’s restraint, the resignation of the cabinet, and the professors’ demonstration of 25 April deprived the regime of its remaining supports.

Rhee announced on 26 April that he would resign and formally resigned on 27 April. A caretaker government and a constitutional amendment introduced a cabinet system and the Second Republic. The April Revolution demonstrated constitutional accountability and popular agency, but it did not guarantee a stable democracy, and its limits, as well as its legacy, shaped what followed.

Review Materials

Key Terms

First Republic
The constitutional order in South Korea from August 1948 to April 1960, dominated by President Syngman Rhee.
Popular sovereignty
The principle that governmental authority derives from the people, stated in the constitution but contested in practice.
Constitutional amendment
A formal change to the constitution; under the 1948 text it required a two-thirds Assembly vote.
Term limit
A rule restricting the number of terms a president may serve; the 1948 constitution allowed one reelection, and the 1954 amendment exempted Rhee.
National Security Law
The December 1948 statute punishing anti-state organization, used against both subversion and lawful dissent.
Martial law
The temporary substitution of military for civil authority; declared in Busan in 1952 and in several cities in April 1960.
Anti-communism
The state’s defining ideology, rooted in security threats but also used to delegitimize domestic opponents.
Busan political crisis
The 1952 confrontation between Rhee and the Assembly, involving martial law and arrests, resolved by the amendment of 4 July.
Rounding amendment
The November 1954 amendment declared adopted after the leadership rounded 135.33 votes to 135.
Liberal Party (Jayu-dang)
The governing party established in 1951, built around the presidency and administrative networks.
Democratic Party (Minju-dang)
The main parliamentary opposition party, founded in 1955.
Progressive Party (Jinbo-dang)
The reformist party around Cho Bong-am, formed in 1956 and dissolved by the government in 1958.
Yeochon yado
A phrase describing the pattern of rural support for the ruling party and urban support for the opposition.
Patronage
The distribution of jobs, licenses, and favors in exchange for political loyalty.
Electoral fraud
Deliberate manipulation of an election, as in the systematic fraud of 15 March 1960.
Vested property
Former Japanese-owned assets taken over by the state and sold, sometimes to politically connected buyers.
Aid dependence
Reliance on American economic and military assistance to sustain the economy and budget.
Hwan
The currency introduced in 1953 to replace the won, whose official rate was a recurring subject of aid negotiations.
UNCURK
The United Nations Commission for the Unification and Rehabilitation of Korea, which observed and reported on southern politics.
Student National Defense Corps
A state-supervised student organization created in 1949 to organize students in military-style units.
Civil society
Independent organizations such as newspapers, churches, professional groups, and student bodies that operate outside the state.
Kyunghyang Shinmun
A critical daily whose license was revoked on 30 April 1959.
April Revolution
The popular uprising of March–April 1960 that ended Rhee’s presidency.
Caretaker government
The interim administration under Heo Jeong that managed the transition after Rhee’s resignation.
Cabinet system
A parliamentary form of government in which the prime minister and cabinet are responsible to the legislature, adopted in June 1960.
Legitimacy
The acceptance of a government’s authority as rightful, challenged in 1960 by evidence of fraud and violence.
Second Republic
The parliamentary order established in 1960 under Yun Bo-seon and Chang Myon, examined at the start of Chapter 75.
Historiography
The study of how historians have interpreted a subject, as in debates over the First Republic’s authoritarianism.

Chapter Reflection

The First Republic matters because it shows how a state can possess the forms of constitutional government while the substance is eroded, and how that erosion can be contested from within the same constitutional language. Rhee’s presidency was neither a simple tyranny nor a functioning democracy. Its elections, Assembly, press, and parties gave opponents tools that they used, and its security institutions, patronage networks, and constitutional manipulations gave the government tools it also used. The tension between these two sets of resources defines the period.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that extend beyond 1960. The Korean War’s legacy of security institutions, the dependence on a patron state, the entanglement of political and economic power, and the emergence of an educated urban public were conditions that would continue to shape South Korean politics under later governments. The patterns of constitutional amendment to preserve power, and of mass mobilization to resist it, recur in Chapters 75, 79, and 82, and students who understand 1960 will recognize both the similarities and the differences in later episodes.

The April Revolution also contributes to the larger design of this textbook, which treats the two Korean states as parallel histories. The same war and the same inheritance produced in the south a state whose citizens could, at a decisive moment, remove a president through public action, and in the north a system developed in Chapter 77 in which comparable action was not available. That contrast is not a verdict on the societies but an explanation of how institutions, evidence, and choices shaped two different trajectories.

The chapter ends, as the First Republic ended, at a threshold. The movement of April 1960 achieved an extraordinary assertion of accountability, but it did not settle what kind of democratic order South Korea would build, or whether that order could withstand its own divisions and the pressures of the Cold War. Those questions, and the answer the military gave to them in 1961, belong to the next chapter.
