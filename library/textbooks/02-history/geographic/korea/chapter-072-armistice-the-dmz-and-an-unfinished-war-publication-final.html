Armistice, the DMZ, and an Unfinished War

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the morning of July 27, 1953, two senior officers sat at separate tables in a hastily prepared hall at Panmunjom and signed a stack of documents that stopped a war without ending it. Twelve hours later the guns went quiet along the front. The agreement they signed settled where armies would stand. It did not settle who would govern Korea.

The signers were Lieutenant General William K. Harrison Jr. for the United Nations Command and General Nam Il for the Korean People's Army and the Chinese People's Volunteers. By most accounts the ceremony was brief and conducted without speeches or a handshake. Their signatures were followed later that day by those of the three commanders in whose names the agreement was made: General Mark W. Clark for the United Nations Command, Kim Il Sung for the Korean People's Army, and Peng Dehuai for the Chinese People's Volunteers. The president of the Republic of Korea, Syngman Rhee, signed nothing. He had spent months denouncing an armistice that would leave his country divided, and in June 1953 he had ordered the release of thousands of prisoners to demonstrate that he could still obstruct the settlement. Whether the Republic of Korea stood inside or outside the agreement is a question this chapter will answer carefully, because the answer is neither simple nor merely legal.

The war these men were ending had begun on June 25, 1950, in the midst of an unresolved civil and ideological struggle examined in Chapters 65 through 67. It had carried armies down to the Pusan Perimeter and up to the Yalu River, drawn in the People's Republic of China, and destroyed cities, villages, and families on a scale that Chapter 71 described. By the summer of 1951 the front had settled near the 38th parallel, and for the following two years it moved only by short, bloody increments around ridgelines with names that American and Commonwealth soldiers gave them. Both sides were still fighting. Yet at the same time, in tents and then in a rebuilt hall at Panmunjom, their delegates were arguing about lines on maps, about the nationality of inspectors, and, above all, about whether prisoners of war could be sent home against their will.

This chapter therefore studies a paradox. By mid-1951, neither coalition could achieve its original aims at a cost it was willing to pay. Yet it took roughly two more years, and several hundred thousand further casualties across all armies and populations, to negotiate a halt. Historians have offered many explanations, and this chapter treats them as complementary rather than competing: the political stakes of the prisoner question, the belief on both sides that battlefield pressure could improve bargaining positions, the calculations of Washington, Moscow, Beijing, and Pyongyang, and the intransigent opposition of the Republic of Korea's government to any settlement that preserved division. Section 72.1 begins with the diplomatic opening in the summer of 1951, and the chapter then follows the negotiations through their deadlock in 1952 and their resolution in 1953.

The chapter also examines what the armistice actually said. It is common to hear that the Korean War "never ended" or that the two Koreas "are still at war." These statements contain a truth, since no peace treaty has been signed, but they obscure the detailed legal and institutional structure the armistice created. The agreement fixed a Military Demarcation Line along the final battle line, withdrew each side two kilometers to form a four-kilometer-wide Demilitarized Zone, created a Military Armistice Commission and a Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission, regulated reinforcement and rotation of forces, addressed coastal islands and the Han River estuary, and recommended a political conference to settle the larger questions. Students should come to understand this structure as a real instrument of military order, not as a mere pause. They should also understand the limits of what it could do.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and unusually partisan. The armistice text itself survives in authenticated English, Korean, and Chinese versions. Negotiating records, diplomatic cables, and military histories on the United Nations side have been published for decades, notably in the series Foreign Relations of the United States and in official histories of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the U.S. Army. Since the 1990s, selected Soviet and Chinese documents have become available through archives and translation projects, which has changed the scholarly picture of Communist decision-making, though access remains incomplete. North Korean and South Korean official narratives interpret the same events in sharply different terms. The testimony of prisoners, soldiers, and civilians adds further layers. This chapter attends throughout to whose evidence is being used and what it can and cannot establish.

Finally, the chapter concludes Volume III and prepares the way for Volume IV. The armistice ended continuous large-scale fighting, but it did not end the competition between two Korean governments, each claiming to be the legitimate government of the whole peninsula. The line drawn in 1953 became the border of two societies that would now rebuild, rearm, and reimagine themselves within rival political and security systems. Chapter 73 begins that story. Chapter 72 asks how the war ended, what the ending left unresolved, and why a military boundary intended as a temporary arrangement became one of the defining conditions of modern Korean history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Korean War ended in an armistice rather than a decisive military victory or a peace treaty, distinguishing military stalemate, diplomatic deadlock, and political disagreement.
  2. Trace the course of the armistice negotiations from the opening of talks in July 1951 through the signing of July 27, 1953, identifying the major turning points.
  3. Analyze why the question of prisoner repatriation became the principal unresolved issue and how the compromise embodied in the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission made agreement possible.
  4. Assess the relative importance of battlefield conditions, American policy under Eisenhower, Soviet and Chinese reassessments after Stalin's death, and Syngman Rhee's actions in producing the final agreement of 1953.
  5. Describe the principal provisions of the Korean Armistice Agreement, including the Military Demarcation Line, the Demilitarized Zone, and the commissions established to supervise the ceasefire.
  6. Distinguish the Military Demarcation Line from the 38th parallel, and the four-nation Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission from the five-nation Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission.
  7. Explain why the Republic of Korea did not sign the armistice, and evaluate what its non-signature did and did not mean in practice.
  8. Evaluate competing accounts of prisoner exchange and non-repatriation, using statistics, testimony, and official reports while preserving the agency of individual prisoners.
  9. Analyze the U.S.–ROK Mutual Defense Treaty as a product of the armistice bargain and compare it with the support North Korea received from China and the Soviet Union.
  10. Explain the failure of the 1954 Geneva Conference on Korea by comparing the competing proposals for elections, reunification, and the withdrawal of foreign forces.
  11. Interpret the unresolved maritime boundary and the later Northern Limit Line controversy without treating that line as part of the 1953 agreement.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of the armistice's historical meaning and assess how it shaped the transition from the wartime period to the Two Koreas of Volume IV.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a war that neither coalition could win decisively nevertheless last roughly two years beyond the opening of negotiations?
  2. How do historians distinguish a military stalemate, a diplomatic deadlock, and a political dispute, and why do the distinctions matter for the Korean case?
  3. Why did the fate of a relatively small proportion of the world's prisoners of war become the central obstacle to ending a war involving millions of people?
  4. How should historians weigh Stalin's death, Eisenhower's policies, battlefield pressure, and Syngman Rhee's defiance against one another as explanations for the settlement of 1953?
  5. What did the armistice agreement establish legally and institutionally, and what did it deliberately leave to a later political settlement?
  6. Why was the Republic of Korea's president able to resist the settlement so forcefully, and what did the United States concede to secure his acquiescence?
  7. What does the evidence about prisoner "choice" reveal about the limits of treating a prisoner's declared preference as a simple expression of free will?
  8. Why did the 1954 Geneva Conference fail, and how does that failure explain the armistice's long life as the governing instrument of the peninsula's security?
  9. How have different governments and communities remembered July 27, 1953, and what do those memories reveal about the political uses of a war's ending?
  10. In what sense was the armistice both an achievement and a postponement, and how should that dual character shape an account of Korea after 1953?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter should already know the outline of the war. The Korean People's Army crossed the 38th parallel in June 1950 and nearly overran the peninsula. The United Nations Command, a multinational coalition led by the United States and acting under Security Council resolutions, held the Pusan Perimeter, landed at Incheon, and advanced to the Amnok (Yalu) River in the autumn. Chinese intervention in late 1950 drove the coalition back below Seoul, and a seesaw campaign in the first half of 1951 returned the front to the vicinity of the 38th parallel. These campaigns, examined in Chapters 68 through 70, ended any realistic prospect that either coalition could reunify Korea by force without a dramatically wider war.

The political background was equally important. The peninsula had been divided in 1945 as a temporary arrangement for accepting the Japanese surrender, and the two zones of occupation had hardened into the Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea in 1948, as Chapters 64 through 66 described. Each government claimed sovereignty over the whole of Korea. That is the essential fact for understanding the armistice. A ceasefire could freeze armies in place, but it could not reconcile two governments that each denied the other's legitimacy, and the leaders of the Republic of Korea in particular regarded any arrangement that accepted division as a betrayal of the national cause.

The war also involved forces far larger than the Korean governments. The United States, fifteen other states contributing combat forces under the United Nations Command, the People's Republic of China, and the Soviet Union, which supplied equipment, air support, and diplomatic direction without admitting direct participation in the ground war, all had their own interests. For Washington, the war was part of a global containment policy, and the principal fear was escalation into a general war with China or the Soviet Union. For Beijing, the war had begun as a defensive intervention but had become a test of the new state's military credibility and domestic mobilization. For Moscow, it was a costly commitment whose benefits were increasingly doubtful. For the two Korean governments, it was the central national struggle. These differing stakes meant that agreement among the belligerent commands was never simply a matter of two sides weighing a common cost.

Students should also keep in mind the scale of devastation. Chapter 71 examined the civilian toll, massacres, displacement, and prisoner experience. By 1951 much of the peninsula had been fought over, bombed, or occupied, and a very large population was displaced. Estimates of total war deaths vary widely and run into the millions when civilians are included, and this textbook does not rely on any single figure. This context explains the urgency felt by many Koreans for an end to fighting, and it explains why the opposition of some Korean leaders to an armistice that left the country divided was also bound up with grief, anger, and the fear that a ceasefire would make division permanent.

The Road to Armistice: Negotiations and Military Stalemate

How a war that had become difficult to win nevertheless took approximately two years to negotiate to a halt.

Section 72.1

Why Negotiate? The Military Stalemate and the Opening of Armistice Talks

By the early summer of 1951, the strategic logic of the war had changed. In the first twelve months the front had moved hundreds of kilometers in both directions: south to the Pusan Perimeter, north to the Amnok River, south again below Seoul after Chinese intervention, and north once more as the United Nations Command recaptured the capital in March 1951. Each of these movements had been driven by the expectation that one more offensive might end the war. In April and May 1951 the Chinese People's Volunteers and the Korean People's Army launched large offensives intended to push the coalition off the peninsula. They inflicted heavy losses and recovered ground but failed to break the coalition's line, and the United Nations counteroffensive that followed carried the front back to the vicinity of the 38th parallel. By June the two armies faced one another across a belt of hills and valleys stretching across the peninsula, and neither had a plausible plan for a decisive breakthrough that did not require unacceptable risks.

Military exhaustion was real on both sides, though it took different forms. The Chinese forces had advanced far from their supply bases under United Nations air attack and had suffered severe casualties and logistical strain in the spring offensives. North Korea's cities, rail lines, and industry had been devastated by bombing, and its population had endured occupation, evacuation, and displacement. For the United Nations Command, the problem was less material than political. The dismissal of General Douglas MacArthur in April 1951 had followed a public dispute about whether to widen the war against China, and the Truman administration was determined to avoid a general war in Asia while Soviet power loomed in Europe. American military leaders told Congress that extending the war risked a larger conflict on unfavorable terms. The coalition's other contributing governments, several of them wary of escalation, shared the concern. A limited war that could not be won cheaply was increasingly a war to be ended.

Diplomacy had begun before the public announcement. In late May and early June 1951, the American diplomat George F. Kennan, then on leave from the State Department's Policy Planning Staff, met privately with the Soviet representative to the United Nations, Yakov Malik, in an exchange recorded in American diplomatic documents. Soviet and Chinese archival materials that have become available since the 1990s suggest that Moscow, Beijing, and Pyongyang were simultaneously considering whether to pursue talks. Kim Il Sung and Mao Zedong's representatives consulted Stalin in June, and the available record indicates that the three agreed that negotiations offered a way to halt the military reversals of the spring while preserving their core positions. Historians still debate how far Communist leaders regarded the talks as a genuine search for settlement and how far as a tactical pause that might allow them to rebuild, but the documentary evidence supports the view that negotiating was a considered decision rather than a sudden collapse of will.

On June 23, 1951, Malik, speaking in a radio series entitled "The Price of Peace," publicly suggested that the belligerents begin discussions for a ceasefire and armistice with mutual withdrawal from the 38th parallel. The statement was a carefully worded signal rather than a formal proposal, and it could be read as an invitation or, if necessary, disavowed. The Truman administration responded cautiously, seeking confirmation that the Soviet statement reflected Chinese and North Korean intentions. General Matthew Ridgway, who had succeeded MacArthur as commander of the United Nations Command, was authorized to broadcast a message to the Communist commanders on June 30 proposing a meeting. On July 1 Kim Il Sung and Peng Dehuai replied agreeing to meet and proposing Kaesong as the site.

Context: Malik was the Soviet ambassador to the United Nations and a seasoned diplomat. The broadcast came a few weeks after private contacts with an American diplomat and during consultations among Soviet, Chinese, and North Korean leaders. It was delivered as part of a general discussion of international peace, not as a Korea-specific diplomatic note, which gave Moscow room to deny a formal commitment.

Paraphrase: The following is an editorial paraphrase, not a direct quotation. Malik stated that the Soviet peoples believed the most acute international problem, the conflict in Korea, could be settled. He suggested that, as a first step, the belligerents begin discussions for a ceasefire and an armistice providing for the mutual withdrawal of forces from the 38th parallel. He presented the proposal as consistent with Soviet support for peaceful settlement and as a matter for the parties directly involved to take up.

Analysis: The statement is a diplomatic signal. It names the 38th parallel as the basis for withdrawal, anticipating the dispute over the line that would occupy the negotiators for months. It positions the discussion as one among belligerents, avoiding a role for the United Nations as an institution, which the Soviet bloc regarded as a party to the war. It describes a ceasefire as a step, not an end, and does not mention prisoners at all, a notable absence given the issue's later importance.

Limitations: The broadcast cannot by itself establish what Soviet leaders intended, whether Chinese and North Korean leaders had approved its wording, or whether Moscow regarded it as a genuine opening. Those questions depend on other evidence, including Soviet archival documents and American assessments. Because Malik spoke in his own name as a diplomat, the text also reveals little about the preferences of the two Korean governments.

Perspective: The source represents the Soviet leadership's public diplomacy. It does not represent the Chinese, North Korean, South Korean, or United Nations Command perspectives, and it does not represent those who bore the cost of continued fighting.

The first formal meeting of the delegations took place at Kaesong on July 10, 1951. The United Nations Command delegation was headed by Vice Admiral C. Turner Joy, commander of U.S. Naval Forces, Far East. It also included Major General Laurence Craigie of the Air Force, Major General Henry Hodes of the Army, Rear Admiral Arleigh Burke, and Major General Paik Sun-yup of the Republic of Korea Army. General Nam Il, a Soviet-born Korean who held senior positions in the North Korean government, headed the Communist delegation, which included Chinese representatives. The inclusion of a South Korean general on the United Nations side reflected the ROK Army's role in the coalition, but it also masked a persistent tension: the Republic of Korea's president had not wanted talks at all.

A crucial feature of these negotiations, and one that shaped everything that followed, was that fighting continued while the delegates talked. The United States had decided, and the Joint Chiefs of Staff had instructed General Ridgway, that no ceasefire would be agreed until the terms of an armistice were settled. The reasoning was that an early ceasefire would allow the Communist forces to rest, reinforce, and rebuild while talks dragged on, depriving the coalition of its military leverage. The Communist side had initially asked for a cessation of hostilities before negotiating details, but accepted the sequence. The consequence was that every delay at the table had human costs on the front. Soldiers and civilians died in actions whose purpose was to influence a negotiation that might have ended the war months earlier. This fact, more than any other, defines the tragedy of the armistice talks, and the chapter returns to it throughout.

Historians distinguish three related but separate conditions in this period. A military stalemate existed because neither side could achieve its war aims by force at acceptable cost. A diplomatic deadlock emerged later, in specific disputes over lines, inspection, and prisoners. A political disagreement over the future of Korea underlay both: the two Korean governments, and their allies, held irreconcilable views about who should govern the peninsula. The first condition made negotiations possible. The second delayed them. The third meant that even a successful armistice could only suspend, not resolve, the underlying conflict. Keeping these conditions distinct helps students avoid the common error of treating the armistice either as a complete peace or as a meaningless truce.

Section 72.2

Kaesong, Panmunjom, and the Dispute over the Military Demarcation Line

Kaesong had been the capital of the Goryeo dynasty, a city of palaces, merchants, and monasteries that Chapter 19 and Chapter 20 described. In 1951 it was a battered city lying below the 38th parallel but, by the summer, behind Communist lines. Its selection as the site of talks was the first of many disputes that blended procedure with symbolism. The United Nations delegation arrived by vehicle under white flags and was met by Communist guards and escorts, a sequence that the Communist press presented as the coalition's delegates coming to sue for peace. The United Nations Command complained that the arrangements gave the other side propaganda advantages and a measure of control over access to the conference site. The first weeks were consumed by the question of the agenda.

On July 26, 1951, the delegates agreed to a five-item agenda that structured every later discussion: adoption of the agenda; fixing a military demarcation line to establish a demilitarized zone as a basic condition for a cessation of hostilities; concrete arrangements for a ceasefire and armistice, including a supervisory organization; arrangements concerning prisoners of war; and recommendations to the governments concerned. The agenda's second item opened the dispute over territory. The Communist delegation insisted that the 38th parallel, the prewar dividing line, must be the basis for the demarcation line and that foreign forces must withdraw from Korea. The United Nations Command rejected both demands. It argued that the parallel had no military significance and that the line should reflect the actual positions of the armies, and it considered the question of foreign troops a political matter for a later conference rather than a precondition for ending the fighting.

The initial United Nations proposal on this point was strikingly aggressive. Arguing that the coalition's air and naval power had not produced a corresponding territorial advantage on the ground, the American negotiators proposed a demilitarized zone on a line substantially north of the front, in effect asking the Communist side to give up territory as compensation for the coalition's air and naval superiority. The Communist delegates denounced the proposal and the argument behind it, and for roughly two weeks the talks nearly ceased. The episode illustrates a recurring pattern: each side tried to convert military advantages that were not visible on the ground into negotiating leverage, and each viewed the other's claims as an attempt to obtain at the table what it could not win in battle. The American side eventually abandoned the proposal and accepted the existing line of contact as the basis, while the Communist side moved away from the demand for the parallel.

Historical Vocabulary
Armistice, Ceasefire, Peace Treaty, and Military Demarcation

Definition. A ceasefire is a halt in fighting, which may be brief, local, or part of a larger agreement. An armistice is a more formal military agreement, concluded by commanders, that suspends hostilities, defines lines, and typically establishes mechanisms for supervision, while leaving final political questions to later negotiation. A peace treaty is a formal agreement between states that ends a war and settles its political consequences, including territory and sovereignty. Military demarcation is the fixing of a line separating the armed forces of opposing sides; it is a military arrangement, not necessarily a political or legal boundary.

Historical context. The Korean Armistice Agreement was concluded by military commanders. It says in its own text that it remains in effect until expressly superseded by mutually acceptable amendments or by an appropriate agreement for peaceful settlement at a political level. It establishes a Military Demarcation Line, but its own wording indicates that the line is not a political frontier.

Why it matters. The legal distinction explains why the Korean War is often described as unfinished, and also why that description requires care.

Potential misunderstanding. The absence of a peace treaty does not mean that the armistice is meaningless or that unrestricted warfare is legally permitted. The armistice remains a substantive military agreement with binding obligations on its parties, even though the political dispute continues.

The negotiations were interrupted in August 1951 after a series of incidents in and around the Kaesong neutral zone. The Communist delegation alleged that United Nations aircraft had bombed the area, and the United Nations Command denied responsibility and countercharged that Communist troops had violated the zone's neutrality. The reliability of the competing claims is difficult to establish, but the political effect was clear: the talks were suspended, and each side regarded the other as responsible. During the pause the fighting intensified in the eastern mountains in the engagements that soldiers remembered as Bloody Ridge and Heartbreak Ridge, and the offensive pressure by both armies was understood by both as an attempt to shape the eventual terms.

When the negotiations resumed on October 25, 1951, they did so at a new site, the hamlet of Panmunjom, about eight kilometers east of Kaesong, in an area where the lines were closer. The hamlet, whose name derives from a Chinese-character rendering of a Korean place name, had no historical importance before 1951. Its selection reflected a practical compromise over the security of the site, and it would become, over decades, the best-known location in divided Korea. The talks at Panmunjom made rapid progress on the demarcation question. On November 27, 1951, the delegates reached a provisional agreement that the line of contact on that day would be the demarcation line if an armistice were signed within thirty days. The arrangement was clever in design, since it gave each side an incentive to settle the other agenda items quickly or accept that the line would be adjusted to reflect any changes in the front. When the deadline passed without an armistice, the delegates agreed that the line would instead be determined by the actual line of contact at the time of signing, which had two important consequences. First, battlefield outcomes continued to matter, which encouraged continued fighting for terrain. Second, the Military Demarcation Line that was eventually drawn would be a product of the war's final months, not of the November 1951 map.

The ceasefire arrangements, the third agenda item, produced their own disputes, which were largely resolved by mid-1952. The Communist side sought to restrict the rehabilitation of airfields in North Korea after an armistice, and the United Nations Command resisted, concerned that restrictions would hinder repair of facilities while leaving Communist air capacity unconstrained. A more serious difficulty concerned the nationality of the neutral nations to supervise the armistice. The Communist side proposed the Soviet Union as a neutral, and the United Nations Command rejected the proposal on the ground that the Soviet Union had supplied the war and could not be considered neutral. The eventual compromise, discussed in §72.11, nominated Sweden and Switzerland by the United Nations Command and Poland and Czechoslovakia by the Communist side. By the spring of 1952, the delegates had resolved most of the substantive questions on the first three items and the fifth, the recommendation for a political conference. What remained was the fourth item: prisoners.

Section 72.3

Prisoner Repatriation and the Breakdown of Negotiations

The prisoner question occupied the negotiators for far longer than any other item, and it came to carry meanings that went well beyond the number of people involved. Chapter 71 described the experiences of prisoners and the violence within the camps; this section addresses the diplomatic argument. The Third Geneva Convention of 1949, which governs the treatment of prisoners of war, states in Article 118 that prisoners shall be released and repatriated without delay after the cessation of active hostilities. The Communist delegation relied on this provision to argue for an "all for all" exchange. The United States, which had signed but not yet ratified the convention, and the United Nations Command argued that the convention's drafters had not intended to require the forcible return of prisoners to governments they feared, and that the humanitarian purpose of the convention supported a policy of voluntary repatriation. President Harry Truman had committed the administration by early 1952 to the position that the United States would not compel prisoners to return, a position that many in the administration also regarded as a matter of principle in the emerging Cold War, since the return of prisoners to Communist states was widely understood to mean punishment or reeducation.

On December 18, 1951, the two sides exchanged lists of prisoners. The Communist side reported that it held about 11,500 prisoners from the United Nations side, of whom roughly 7,100 were South Koreans and the remainder Americans and other nationalities. The United Nations Command reported that it held approximately 132,000 prisoners from the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers. The disparity of numbers led immediately to a second dispute. The United Nations Command had earlier reported the number of missing South Korean and other personnel as much larger than the Communist list suggested, and it concluded that many captured South Korean soldiers had been incorporated into the Korean People's Army or compelled to serve in other capacities rather than held as prisoners. The Communist side denied misrepresentation. The question of what happened to South Korean captives, many of whom were conscripted into northern units or died in captivity, would shadow the repatriation story long after 1953, as §72.13 explains.

In the spring of 1952 the United Nations Command screened the prisoners in its custody to determine who would return to Communist control. The screening was conducted in compounds on Geoje Island and elsewhere, where the camp population was already divided between prisoners who supported the Communist side and prisoners who opposed it, and where leaders of both factions exercised control through intimidation and violence. An early, preliminary estimate communicated during the spring of 1952 placed those willing to return at about seventy thousand out of approximately one hundred seventy thousand prisoners and civilian internees; it was not the completed screening result. The later United Nations Command tally recorded 169,944 people screened, including civilian internees, of whom 83,722 were classified as willing to return and 86,222 as unwilling. These categories did not all consist of captured foreign combatants, and the numbers also reflected a process disputed for coercion and interference inside the camps. The sharp scale of non-repatriation shocked the Communist delegation. The Communist delegation reacted with fury and denied the legitimacy of the screening, charging that prisoners had been coerced, tortured, and manipulated by agents of the Republic of Korea and the Chinese Nationalist government on Taiwan. The United Nations Command responded that the screening had been conducted under supervision, though later review and the testimony of prisoners and guards cast doubt on the extent to which the compounds' internal politics had shaped outcomes.

The Geoje Island crisis of May 1952 illustrates the intertwining of camp politics and diplomacy. In early May, prisoners in one compound seized the camp commandant, Brigadier General Francis T. Dodd, and held him for several days while they presented demands that included an end to forcible screening and recognition of the prisoners' organization. His successor, Brigadier General Charles F. Colson, negotiated his release and signed a statement acknowledging that prisoners had been mistreated, a statement that the United Nations Command later repudiated but that the Communist side used extensively in propaganda. Troops then reasserted control of the compounds, and the commanders responsible were reorganized. The incident, which Chapter 71 described in greater detail, mattered for the negotiations because it gave the Communist side evidence that the United Nations Command did not fully control the camps and gave the United Nations side an illustration of the Communist side's willingness to manipulate prisoners. It also changed command at the top: General Mark W. Clark replaced Ridgway as United Nations commander in May 1952.

Why did the prisoner question matter so much? Historians have identified several reasons that operated together. For the Truman administration, the principle of voluntary repatriation had become a moral and political commitment that could not be abandoned without damaging the administration's standing at home and the coalition's cohesion abroad. For the Chinese and North Korean governments, the refusal of tens of thousands of their soldiers to return was a political embarrassment that questioned the legitimacy of their regimes and risked having a propaganda victory handed to their adversaries. For the Republic of Korea, the return of captured North Korean soldiers was unacceptable, since many South Koreans regarded them as enemies who would simply renew the war. For prisoners themselves, the stakes were personal and in some cases life-threatening. Each had to decide whether to return to a society and government that might treat them as traitors, to remain in a society whose language or politics might be unfamiliar, or to attempt a new life in a third place. The scholar Monica Kim has argued in her study of the interrogation rooms of the war that the questioning of prisoners about their allegiance was itself a political act that tried to produce citizens for competing visions of postwar order, which complicates any assumption that a prisoner's stated preference represented a simple expression of settled conviction.

This chapter preserves prisoners' agency while recognizing the pressures upon them. Some refused repatriation because they had opposed communism, some because they feared punishment, some because they had been pressured by fellow prisoners or camp administrators, some because they had family or opportunities elsewhere, and some because they had changed their minds under conditions of captivity. Others chose to return despite pressure not to. Evidence for individual motives is limited, and generalizations are dangerous. What the historical record shows clearly is that the decision of how many prisoners returned and how they were asked became a matter of high politics. By the middle of 1952, the delegates had agreed on every substantive item of the armistice except the one on which each side's government regarded concession as impossible. The talks could continue, but they could not conclude.

Section 72.4

Fighting While Negotiating: The Politics of Delay, 1951–1952

The front line during the long negotiations did not become static. It hardened into a pattern of trench systems, bunkers, and outposts on hills that soldiers contested with artillery, infantry assaults, and counterattacks. The names that the soldiers gave these places now appear in military histories: Bloody Ridge and Heartbreak Ridge in 1951; the contested hills called Old Baldy and Pork Chop Hill, the Hook, and White Horse Mountain in 1952 and 1953. The battles for such places were often costly and small in strategic effect. A hill might change hands several times in a few days, with losses far out of proportion to its value, and the historians who have studied the period have asked what the commanders hoped to achieve.

One answer is that both sides believed battlefield pressure could improve their negotiating positions. The United Nations Command, particularly during the period of Ridgway's and later Clark's command, held to the view that the Communist negotiators were responsive to military pain and that relaxing pressure would harden their positions. The Communist side, for its part, pursued an "active defense" under Peng Dehuai after the failed offensives of 1951, strengthening its position with extensive tunnels and fortifications that the Chinese later called an underground wall. The battle for the position the Chinese called Shangganling and the Americans Triangle Hill in October and November 1952 became a central episode in Chinese memory of the war. These dynamics reflected a deeper pattern in which each side's use of force to demonstrate resolve prolonged the very conflict that negotiations were intended to end.

Air power occupied a particular place in this logic. The United Nations Command held effective control of the skies over most of Korea, and it used that advantage to attack North Korean cities, industry, transport, and power systems. In June 1952, American aircraft struck the hydroelectric complexes along the Yalu, including the large Suiho facility, in an operation designed to disrupt power supplies in North Korea and parts of Manchuria. In July and August 1952, large raids struck Pyongyang. In the spring of 1953, aircraft attacked irrigation dams and their dikes, producing flooding that threatened rice crops at a critical season. Supporters of this "air pressure" strategy argued that it forced Communist concessions, though historians have continued to debate its effectiveness. Critics, both then and now, have noted that the campaign increased civilian suffering, that the Communist side adapted by moving production underground and mobilizing labor for repair, and that the evidence that the bombing produced specific diplomatic concessions is limited. The destruction it caused is part of the context for the reconstruction challenges examined in Chapter 73.

The Republic of Korea's army grew substantially during the war, and the expansion of its divisions was both a military and a political development. The United States and the United Nations Command increasingly relied on South Korean units to hold sections of the front, a process that reduced the burden on American and other contributing forces and strengthened Rhee's leverage. A larger ROK army meant that Rhee could threaten to continue the fight alone, a threat that grew louder in 1953. Meanwhile, domestic opinion in the contributing countries shifted. In the United States, the prolonged war became a political liability for the Truman administration, and the question of Korea played a significant role in the 1952 presidential election. The Republican candidate, Dwight Eisenhower, pledged in October to go to Korea himself, a promise he kept after his election in a visit in December 1952.

Cause and Effect

Why the Armistice Negotiations Lasted Two Years

Long-term causes

The unresolved political division of Korea since 1945; the claims of two governments to sovereignty over the whole peninsula; the Cold War framework that made each great power reluctant to appear to concede; and the legacy of the Geneva conventions, which gave both sides legal arguments about repatriation.

Short-term causes

The decision not to declare a ceasefire until all terms were settled; the belief that battlefield pressure improved bargaining positions; disputes over the demarcation line, airfield rehabilitation, and neutral supervision; and the controversies over prisoners that followed the 1952 screening.

Immediate trigger

The adoption in January 1952 of the voluntary-repatriation position by the United States, the results of the spring 1952 screening, and the Geoje-do crisis; these made the prisoner question appear a matter of principle, not negotiating detail.

Effects

Continued heavy casualties during the talks; recurring recesses; the American recess of October 1952; increased political pressure in the United States; and a hardening of the ROK government's belief that the armistice would not serve Korean interests.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the delay. Scholars disagree about how much Communist leaders believed they could outlast the United Nations Command, how much the Truman administration's commitment to voluntary repatriation prolonged the talks, and whether earlier compromises on the prisoner question were available. The evidence suggests that several mutually reinforcing factors operated at the same time.

In the autumn of 1952, the diplomatic situation reached a new low. On October 8, the United Nations Command's chief delegate, Lieutenant General William K. Harrison Jr., who had succeeded Joy in May, announced an indefinite recess of the plenary meetings after the Communist side rejected a final proposal on prisoner repatriation. The recess was not a diplomatic break, since liaison channels remained open, but it signaled that the United Nations Command would not make further concessions without a change in the Communist position. In the same weeks, the fighting around Triangle Hill and White Horse reached a peak. At the United Nations General Assembly, which took up the prisoner question in the autumn, a compromise resolution proposed by India and supported by many delegations urged a mechanism for handling prisoners who did not wish to return. The General Assembly adopted it in December 1952 over the opposition of the Soviet bloc, which rejected it. The resolution did not itself break the deadlock, but it prepared an Indian role that would prove important in 1953.

Why did both coalitions continue to fight when a comprehensive victory appeared increasingly unlikely? Part of the answer is that a limited military objective, improving the line of contact before the armistice fixed it, remained meaningful. Part is institutional: commanders in the field and officials at home hesitated to appear to reward the opponent by relaxing pressure. Part is political: the United States feared that withdrawing from the principle of voluntary repatriation would signal weakness, and the Communist governments feared that conceding the principle would signal the failure of their ideological project. And part is simply the momentum of a war that had become an organized system, with supply lines, replacement systems, and domestic expectations that did not disappear because negotiators were meeting. At the end of 1952, the war had lasted two and a half years, the negotiations eighteen months, and the armistice was no closer, though the conditions that would produce it in 1953 were beginning to form.

The Final Diplomatic Breakthrough of 1953

Why the final agreement became possible in 1953, and why South Korea's own president remained strongly opposed to its central political outcome.

Section 72.5

Eisenhower, Stalin's Death, and Changing Conditions for an Armistice

Dwight D. Eisenhower took office on January 20, 1953, having campaigned on the promise to bring the Korean War to an honorable end. His administration did not abandon military pressure. Its early public statements were tough, and in his State of the Union address of February 1953, the president announced that the Seventh Fleet would no longer be used to shield the Chinese mainland from possible Nationalist attack, a change that was widely read as a signal of readiness to widen the pressure. Within the National Security Council, officials examined courses of action for the Korean war that ranged from continued stalemate to major escalation, including the use of atomic weapons and expanded operations against China. A paper prepared in the spring analyzed these options and their risks. The record shows serious discussion of escalation, but it also shows doubts, particularly among military leaders and allies, about whether escalation would produce a better outcome than negotiation, and the administration's priority remained a settlement that preserved the principle of voluntary repatriation.

The first diplomatic move of the new year came from the United Nations side. On February 22, 1953, General Clark wrote to the Communist commanders proposing an exchange of sick and wounded prisoners, a humanitarian step permitted by Article 109 of the Geneva Convention and one that would test the Communist side's willingness to resume serious business. For weeks there was no answer. Then, on March 5, Joseph Stalin died. The Soviet leader had presided over the Soviet role in the war since 1950, and his death opened a period of uncertainty and reassessment among his successors, among them Georgy Malenkov, Lavrenty Beria, and Vyacheslav Molotov. Soviet documents that have been published since the end of the Cold War show that the new leadership moved within days and weeks to reconsider the war, and they record a decision in mid-March to seek an end to the fighting and to inform Beijing and Pyongyang of its advice. Chinese leaders, including Zhou Enlai, who traveled to Moscow for the funeral, were consulted. The documents indicate that the Soviet leadership regarded the war as a drain and a source of dangerous tension with the United States, though how far this represented a break with Stalin's policy and how far an acceleration of tendencies already evident remains debated.

On March 28, 1953, Kim Il Sung and Peng Dehuai replied to Clark's February letter, agreeing to the exchange of sick and wounded prisoners and proposing that the delegates resume their discussions. Two days later, Zhou Enlai issued a statement proposing that prisoners who were unwilling to return be transferred to a neutral state to ensure a just settlement of the question. The proposal, endorsed by Molotov, was precisely the kind of compromise the United Nations side had been seeking: it dropped the Communist demand that all prisoners return, and it avoided an outright American commitment to release the unwilling into the custody of the Republic of Korea or to Taiwan. It did not settle every point, but it ended the deadlock of 1952. Whether the Chinese and North Korean leaders had already been moving toward concession, or whether Stalin's death accelerated the change, is difficult to establish from the available documents, which are incomplete. Chinese sources suggest that Mao Zedong and his colleagues had been weighing the costs of continuing the war, including the casualties of the outpost battles, the strain of supplying the front, and the priorities of China's first Five-Year Plan, which began in 1953. North Korea's own leadership faced the devastation of a country that had been subjected to prolonged bombardment, and it needed the resources of its allies for reconstruction that a continued war would delay.

Historians therefore disagree, and the disagreement is instructive. One long-standing interpretation, advanced by Eisenhower himself in his memoirs, attributes the breakthrough to American signals that the United States might use atomic weapons or expand the war if talks failed, including a message that Secretary of State John Foster Dulles conveyed through Indian channels in May. Other historians have found little evidence in Chinese and Soviet sources that these signals were decisive, and they emphasize instead the effects of Stalin's death, the weariness of the Communist belligerents, and the accumulating effects of conventional military and air pressure. A third group of scholars stresses the effect of the compromise on prisoners, arguing that the Communist concession on voluntary repatriation, in the form of neutral custody, was the real turning point and that it could have come earlier if the Communist side had believed it could obtain terms it could present as acceptable. The most defensible conclusion is that no single event caused the ceasefire. Conditions changed on several fronts, and the interaction of those changes made agreement possible.

The battlefield also mattered in these months. The front remained active, and the Communist forces launched major attacks in the spring and summer, including heavy fighting around Pork Chop Hill in March and April and the largest Chinese offensive of the year in June and July. The battles had a dual purpose. They improved the Communist side's tactical position, and they signaled to the United Nations Command, and to Rhee, that the Communist forces remained capable. In the United States, the Eisenhower administration faced criticism from sections of its own party for prolonging a war it had promised to end and from others who feared that a compromise would be a betrayal. In the Communist capitals, there were disagreements, though their exact shape is difficult to reconstruct. The framework in which agreement became possible was therefore a convergence: weariness, reassessment, and new leadership in Moscow, a new American administration willing to accept a settlement that did not require complete Communist surrender, and a proposal on prisoners that gave both sides a formula they could present as consistent with their principles.

Section 72.6

Operation Little Switch: The Exchange of Sick and Wounded Prisoners

The agreement on the limited exchange of sick and wounded prisoners was reached in liaison talks that began in early April 1953 and concluded on April 11. These talks addressed the procedures, categories, and logistics of the exchange. The sick and wounded who would be returned included those whose medical condition made them unlikely to recover without proper care, those who had been maimed, and those who, in the judgment of the holding side, were unable to continue military service. The negotiators agreed that each side would return prisoners in numbers to be determined by its own definitions, subject to later discussion, and that the exchange would occur at Panmunjom. The agreement itself was narrow, but it was important as a test of the machinery of exchange, and it was understood by both sides as an indication that more could follow.

Operation Little Switch began on April 20, 1953, and continued until May 3. In that period, the United Nations Command delivered to the Communist side about 6,670 prisoners, of whom roughly 5,200 were North Koreans, about 1,000 were Chinese, and a smaller number were civilians. The Communist side delivered 684 prisoners to the United Nations Command, including 471 South Koreans, 149 Americans, and 64 prisoners of other nationalities. The disparity in numbers anticipated the larger disparity that would appear in Operation Big Switch and reflected the different size of the prisoner populations each side held. The exchanges were conducted by ambulance and truck over the road to Panmunjom, with medical personnel from both sides receiving and examining the prisoners and with representatives of the international press observing from designated areas. The logistics were complicated, and the movement of large numbers of weakened prisoners over a few days required coordination between military authorities that had no other regular contact.

The exchange had immediate political consequences. Returned United Nations prisoners described conditions of captivity, including malnutrition, disease, forced marches, indoctrination sessions, and, in many accounts, deaths among those who did not survive the first winters of captivity, accounts that Chapter 71 examined. The Communist side presented its own picture of the exchange, emphasizing the humane treatment of prisoners under its custody and the political awareness of those it returned, and it showed returning Communist prisoners to its press as evidence of mistreatment in United Nations camps. The two narratives were not wholly inconsistent. Each captured something of the experience of prisoners who had endured hardship and violence, though each also served propaganda purposes. Individual prisoners experienced the exchange in very different ways: for many sick and wounded men it was a deliverance, for some it was a confusing and frightening transition, and for others it meant returning to a government that would question their loyalty and conduct.

For the diplomats, the significance of Little Switch lay in what it showed about momentum. A limited exchange, carried out without major incident, built a measure of confidence that the larger machinery of repatriation could operate. It also demonstrated that the Communist side was willing to cooperate in a prisoner exchange after months of refusing to do so, and that the United Nations Command was willing to proceed without first having resolved the question of non-repatriates. The success of the first exchange was immediately followed by the resumption of plenary talks at Panmunjom on April 26. At the table, the Communist delegation proposed on May 7 a framework that would transfer prisoners who declined repatriation to a neutral state for a period of explanation, and the negotiators began to work on its details. For the Republic of Korea, however, the resumption of talks and the first exchange signaled that an armistice was approaching, and Syngman Rhee responded with a campaign of public and diplomatic opposition. Demonstrations against the armistice swept South Korean cities in April and May, and Rhee threatened that the Republic of Korea might withdraw its forces from the United Nations Command and fight on alone. The threat was never carried out, but it forced the Eisenhower administration to take South Korean opposition seriously.

Section 72.7

The Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission and the June 8 Agreement

The central diplomatic task of May 1953 was to convert the principle of neutral custody into a workable system. The United Nations Command's final proposal of May 25 and the Communist counterproposal that followed over the next days came close, and on June 4 the delegates reached agreement on the terms. They were formally concluded on June 8, 1953, as the Terms of Reference for the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission. The compromise had three essential features. First, all prisoners who wished to be repatriated would be returned to their side within sixty days of the armistice becoming effective, without any further process. Second, prisoners who did not choose repatriation would be transferred to the custody of a neutral commission. Third, representatives of the home countries of those prisoners would be allowed to explain to them, within a specified period, their rights and the conditions they might face on returning, and after that period the fate of those who remained unwilling to return would be referred to a political conference.

The commission created for this purpose, the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission, consisted of five states: Sweden, Switzerland, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and India. Sweden and Switzerland were selected by the United Nations side; Poland and Czechoslovakia by the Communist side; India was agreed as the fifth member and as the chair and umpire, with the power to break deadlocks. The Indian role was central. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's government had been a mediating voice throughout the war, and India's non-aligned status made it acceptable, in the circumstances, to both sides. An Indian general, K. S. Thimayya, chaired the commission, and India supplied a custodial force of several thousand troops, under the command of Major General S. P. P. Thorat, to guard the prisoners in neutral custody. Indian military and medical personnel would therefore be responsible for the actual care and security of prisoners.

Paraphrase: The following is an editorial paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the main provisions. All prisoners who wished to be repatriated were to be handed over to their own side within sixty days of the armistice taking effect. The remaining prisoners were to be transferred to the custody of the five-nation commission, with Indian forces responsible for physical custody and administration. Within a specified period representatives of the prisoners' own countries could meet them to explain their rights and the conditions awaiting them, with commission members present and with no coercion permitted. Analysis: The terms embody a compromise. The Communist side secured the principle that every prisoner would be given an opportunity to hear explanations from his own government's representatives, which it believed would bring back many who had refused. The United Nations side secured the principle that no prisoner would be forcibly returned. Neither side fully prevailed. The formula bought time, shifting the final decision to a later stage and to a body of neutrals, and it tied the prisoners' fate to the political conference that the armistice recommended.

It says nothing about conditions inside the camps before neutral custody, about the extent of political pressure on prisoners, or about the choices individual prisoners made. The concern about coercion embodied in the terms is evidence that both sides suspected the other of manipulating prisoners.

Perspective: The document represents the negotiating positions of two military delegations and reflects the diplomatic compromise they reached. It does not represent the voices of the prisoners it governs, of the Republic of Korea, whose government had not agreed to it, or of the Indian custodial force charged with implementing it.

The compromise resolved the prisoner question diplomatically without resolving it ideologically. The Communist governments accepted that prisoners might refuse repatriation and would not be forced back, which was a concession to the voluntary principle that they had resisted for a year. The United States and the United Nations Command accepted that explanation by the prisoners' home governments would be part of the process, and that India, rather than the United Nations Command, would hold the prisoners during that process, which gave up the position that the camps under United Nations control would handle the final disposition. Each side could therefore tell its own public that its principle had been honored. The Communist side could point to the explanation procedures, and the United Nations side could point to the guarantee against forcible return. The settlement thus shows how compromise can be built on ambiguity, and it also shows why that ambiguity would produce further controversy in the autumn, as §72.14 explains.

Concerns about coercion, political influence, and safety ran through the terms. The Communist governments feared that prisoners in United Nations camps had been pressured by anti-Communist leaders and by representatives of the Republic of Korea and Taiwan, and the explanation procedure was designed to counteract that pressure. The United Nations Command feared that prisoners who had refused repatriation would be intimidated by Communist representatives during the explanations or that they would be exposed to violence from fellow prisoners or from the Indian guards' inability to protect them. The terms accordingly attempted to regulate the circumstances in which explanations were given. How well they succeeded is evaluated in §72.14. The terms also did not apply to the Republic of Korea's own prisoners and civilians, whose views Rhee's government had not accepted, a gap that Rhee exploited ten days later.

Section 72.8

Syngman Rhee's Opposition, the June Prisoner Release, and the Final Bargain

Syngman Rhee's objection to an armistice was fundamental and consistent. He had led the First Republic since 1948, regarded the Republic of Korea as the only legitimate government of Korea, and believed that an armistice that left Communist forces in North Korea and Chinese troops on Korean soil would make permanent the division that the war had been fought to end. He wanted the war to continue until the Chinese were driven out and the North was reunified, or at the least he wanted Chinese forces withdrawn from Korea and the Korean People's Army disarmed as conditions for any ceasefire. He was also genuinely concerned about South Korea's security: the army was growing but remained dependent on American equipment and support, and without a firm American commitment he feared the armistice would be followed by another invasion. These positions were not mere obstinacy. They reflected a worldview shaped by decades of anti-colonial struggle and by the experience of the war. They also created grave difficulties for the United States, which commanded the Republic of Korea's army under the United Nations Command and could not afford open conflict with its ally.

Rhee's opposition escalated as the talks advanced. In April and May 1953, he organized or tolerated mass rallies against the armistice, wrote to Eisenhower and to Clark in strong terms, and boycotted the armistice delegation after late May. He demanded that any armistice be accompanied by a mutual defense treaty with the United States, expansion of the Republic of Korea's army to twenty divisions, American air and naval support, and economic aid. The Eisenhower administration, preoccupied with concluding the agreement, treated these demands as bargaining positions that might be partially met but not as veto rights. General Clark also considered contingency plans, including approaches to replace Rhee or place the Republic of Korea under direct United Nations control in the event of an outright break, though these plans were never implemented and the available records suggest officials hoped never to need them.

On the night of June 17–18, 1953, Rhee acted. He ordered the Republic of Korea's military police, who guarded the camps holding North Korean prisoners who had refused repatriation, to open the gates and allow the prisoners to leave. Over the following days, more than twenty-five thousand prisoners, a figure often given as around twenty-seven thousand, were released or escaped into the South Korean population, where many were sheltered by civilians and later absorbed into South Korean society. The United Nations Command was caught by surprise and unable to prevent the release. General Clark and the American government were alarmed, since the release violated the terms that had just been agreed and handed the Communist side an argument that the United Nations Command could not control its own ally. The Communist delegation protested vigorously, suspended the talks for several days, and demanded explanations and guarantees. The Chinese military, which had already launched a major offensive on June 10, intensified attacks on the sectors held by South Korean troops, and Peng Dehuai reportedly intended to demonstrate to Rhee, and to the United States, the cost of defiance.

The release was a calculated act of political leverage. Rhee knew that the Communist side would demand an assurance that the Republic of Korea would abide by the armistice, and he wished to ensure that the United States would pay a price for his cooperation. The United States understood the message. President Eisenhower sent Walter S. Robertson, the Assistant Secretary of State for Far Eastern Affairs, to Seoul. Robertson arrived in late June and remained until mid-July in prolonged negotiations with Rhee that became tense and exhausting for both. The American offer, which Robertson communicated and refined, included a mutual defense treaty, to be negotiated after the armistice; long-term economic assistance, with an initial figure of around two hundred million dollars; support for the expansion of the Republic of Korea's army; continued American naval and air support; and consultations with the Republic of Korea before and during the political conference that the armistice would recommend. Rhee also sought a commitment that if the conference did not produce a settlement within ninety days, the United States and the Republic of Korea would withdraw and consider further steps, and a joint statement issued in early August expressed a version of this understanding.

At the same time, the Chinese offensive reached its climax in July with heavy fighting in the Kumsong sector in the central front, where Chinese forces broke through lines held by South Korean divisions and pushed back the line. The attack, which lasted from about July 13 to 20, caused serious South Korean casualties and forced the United Nations Command to commit reserves. It also conveyed a message to the Republic of Korea's leadership that continued war would be costly and that its army, whatever its growth, could not defeat the Chinese without substantial American support. By July 9, Rhee had communicated that he would not sign the armistice but would not obstruct its implementation. This formulation, which preserved his objections while accepting the practical consequences, became the basis of South Korea's position. It explains the apparent paradox of the Republic of Korea's non-signature: the government declined to endorse the armistice, but its army remained under United Nations Command control, and its forces complied with the cease-fire. The non-signature should therefore not be read as proof that South Korea was wholly outside the settlement.

Multiple Perspectives

The Final Bargain of 1953: Six Vantage Points

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom of action.

From the perspective of Syngman Rhee and the ROK government: An armistice that left Korea divided and Chinese forces on Korean soil abandoned the national goal for which Koreans had fought and suffered. Without an American security guarantee, the South would be exposed. Opposition, including the release of prisoners, was the only means of securing commitments from a larger and more powerful ally.
From the perspective of the Eisenhower administration: The priority was to end a costly war without abandoning the principle of voluntary repatriation or provoking a wider conflict. Rhee's defiance was a dangerous complication, to be managed through assurances that cost less than continued war and that would bind the Republic of Korea to a settlement it did not endorse.
From the perspective of Chinese negotiators and commanders: The war had achieved the essential aim of preventing the destruction of the North Korean state and establishing China's military credibility, but its continuation was burdensome. Compromise on prisoners was acceptable if it could be presented as consistent with principle, and military pressure, including on South Korean sectors, was a means of demonstrating strength while talks concluded.
From the perspective of North Korean representatives: The country had survived an invasion by far stronger forces and could present the armistice as a victory, but it was devastated and needed an end to the bombing and the opportunity to rebuild. The prisoner question was also a question of the regime's legitimacy, since refusals to return embarrassed its claims.
From the perspective of the prisoners: Their fate was decided by commanders and diplomats, yet their choices mattered. Some sought to return home, others feared punishment or had come to oppose the government that sent them to war, and still others were subject to intense pressure from fellow prisoners and camp authorities. The release of June 18 gave thousands a freedom they had not been offered by any formal procedure, though it also placed them in uncertain circumstances.
From the perspective of Korean civilians: Many in both Koreas wished above all for the end of fighting, for reconstruction, and for the return of missing relatives. Others in the South shared Rhee's fear that the armistice would entrench division, and many families that had been separated would find that the new line made reunion impossible.

The relationship between the Republic of Korea's refusal to endorse the armistice and its decision not to obstruct implementation is the key to understanding the Rhee episode. He used every instrument available to him to obtain commitments from the United States, and he succeeded in securing a long-term security treaty that no American administration would otherwise have offered at that moment. At the same time, he lacked the capacity to wage war alone and understood that the American commitment was conditional on his cooperation. The final bargain of July 1953 was therefore a settlement between allies as much as between enemies. It left the Republic of Korea's president opposed to the settlement's central political outcome, the continued division of the peninsula, while locking his government into a security relationship that would shape South Korea for decades. On July 19, the delegates at Panmunjom announced that all questions had been settled, and the way to signing was clear.

Division III · Sections 72.9–72.12 · July–August 1953

July 27, 1953: The Armistice and the Creation of the DMZ

What the armistice established, how it operated, and what it left unresolved.

Section 72.9

The Signing at Panmunjom and the Cessation of Hostilities

The final text of the Korean Armistice Agreement was settled on July 19, 1953, and in the following week the negotiators prepared the documents for signature. Multiple copies in English, Korean, and Chinese had to be produced, bound, and arranged, and a hall was prepared at Panmunjom to hold the signing. The hall was a simple structure erected quickly for the purpose, and the arrangements were designed so that the two senior delegates would sign at separate tables without ceremony that might imply recognition or reconciliation. On the morning of July 27, 1953, at about ten o'clock, Lieutenant General William K. Harrison Jr., the senior delegate of the United Nations Command, and General Nam Il, the senior delegate of the Korean People's Army and the Chinese People's Volunteers, entered the hall from opposite doors, sat, and signed the documents. By most accounts the proceedings lasted only a few minutes and were conducted without speeches or a handshake. The two men then left by their separate doors.

The signatures of the delegates did not complete the agreement. The armistice was concluded in the names of the commanders of the opposing forces, and the commanders signed later that day at different locations. Kim Il Sung, Supreme Commander of the Korean People's Army, signed in Pyongyang, and Peng Dehuai, Commander of the Chinese People's Volunteers, signed at Kaesong. General Mark W. Clark, Commander-in-Chief of the United Nations Command, signed at the United Nations Command's forward headquarters at Munsan, south of the line. Clark later wrote that he took no satisfaction in the occasion, noting that he had signed an armistice without victory, a sentiment shared by many American officers who had expected the war to end differently. The geography of the signing underscores a central fact about the agreement: it was a military instrument concluded between commands, not a political treaty concluded between governments, and the commanders who gave it effect did not meet.

The twelve-hour interval between signature and the effective moment of the ceasefire was built into the agreement. Paragraph 12 of the armistice provides for a complete cessation of hostilities by all armed forces under the commanders' control, effective twelve hours after signature, and Paragraph 63 provides that all other provisions become effective at 2200 hours on July 27. The interval allowed time for orders to reach units across a front hundreds of kilometers long and for commanders to prepare for the movements the agreement required. It also meant that the fighting did not stop with the signatures. Artillery fire and some local actions continued through the day, and the final hours saw heavy fire along some sectors. Participants on both sides later described a sudden and disorienting quiet when the ceasefire took effect, though students should treat such recollections with the caution due to retrospective accounts. At ten o'clock in the evening, organized large-scale fighting stopped.

Analysis: The selected passages show the structure of the agreement. Paragraph 1 creates a military boundary and buffer. Paragraph 12 sets the moment hostilities end. Paragraphs 36 and 37 create a supervisory body composed of states from both camps' preferred lists. Paragraph 60 does not settle the political questions but recommends a conference to address them, and Paragraph 62 stipulates that the armistice remains in force until replaced by a political-level agreement. Taken together, the provisions define a military order intended to prevent a return to war while a political settlement was pursued.

Limitations: The text states obligations and recommendations, not their performance. Paragraph 60 is a recommendation from the commanders to governments, not a binding obligation, and the agreement says nothing about the Republic of Korea's government, which did not sign. The text cannot show how the commissions worked, how the line was maintained, or how disputes arose. Its definition of boundaries refers to military control and not to sovereignty; the agreement contains no recognition of either Korean government's claims.

Perspective: The document represents the perspective of military commanders negotiating on behalf of two coalitions. It does not represent the Republic of Korea's government, the political goals of either Korean state, or the experiences of civilians whose lives the line would alter.

The absence of a Republic of Korea signatory was conspicuous. The agreement was signed on the United Nations side by the Commander-in-Chief of the United Nations Command, who acted on behalf of the sixteen states contributing forces and who commanded the Republic of Korea's army. The armistice imposed obligations upon the military commands and the forces under their control, rather than making the ROK government a separately named signatory. The Commander-in-Chief of the United Nations Command exercised operational control over ROK forces, whose ceasefire compliance was secured by command arrangements and Seoul’s assurances. This operational application should not be confused with a separate ROK governmental signature or with a comprehensive settlement of its legal position. On the day of signing, the United States and fifteen other contributing states also issued a joint declaration affirming their intention to resist renewed aggression and warning that any renewed attack could not be confined to the Korean peninsula. The South Korean government declined to sign and issued statements stressing its objection to the settlement while promising not to obstruct it. In practice, the ROK forces ceased fire when ordered.

It is important to emphasize what the signing did and did not accomplish. It ended organized hostilities and created the machinery for managing the ceasefire. It did not end the political conflict, did not settle the status of either Korean government, and did not constitute recognition of either by the other. The commanders themselves recognized this in the agreement's preamble, which describes its purpose as ensuring a complete cessation of hostilities and of all acts of armed force in Korea until a final peaceful settlement is achieved. The word "until" carries the weight of the agreement: its authors regarded it as a bridge to a political settlement, not as the settlement itself. That the bridge would last for far longer than anyone in the hall expected is the subject of the rest of this chapter.

Section 72.10

The Military Demarcation Line and the Demilitarized Zone

The line that the armistice established was neither the 38th parallel nor a line drawn from first principles. It was a military line determined by the positions of the armies at the moment of agreement, adjusted by the negotiators to provide for a coherent and defensible line. From the Han River estuary on the Yellow Sea in the west, it ran northeast and then east across the peninsula's waist, passing close to Panmunjom, through the Cheorwon plain and the mountains of the central region, before descending to the East Sea near the town of Goseong. Its length is commonly given as about 248 kilometers. The line was marked on the ground with 1,292 markers, set at intervals along its course and numbered consecutively, with responsibility for maintenance divided between the two sides.

Around this line the armistice established the Demilitarized Zone. Under Paragraph 1, each side withdrew two kilometers from the line, creating a buffer four kilometers wide that runs the length of the line. Within seventy-two hours of the armistice taking effect, all military forces, supplies, and equipment were to be withdrawn from the zone. The zone covers roughly nine hundred square kilometers. Its purpose, as the text states, is to act as a buffer to prevent incidents that might lead to a resumption of hostilities. It was never intended to be a boundary of sovereignty; the agreement fixes the line as the limit of military control and is silent on its political status. The responsibility for civil administration and relief within the zone was assigned to the commanders on each side of the line, and the number of civilians permitted to enter was limited by the Military Armistice Commission.

The difference between the new line and the 38th parallel was significant and has often been misunderstood. The parallel runs roughly straight across the peninsula's width. The Military Demarcation Line, by contrast, follows the shifting positions of the war's end, and as a result it crosses the parallel at a diagonal. In the west, the line lies south of the parallel: the ancient city of Kaesong, which had belonged to the south in 1945, fell to the North, as did the Ongjin peninsula and the surrounding territory except for the islands discussed in §72.12. In the east, the line lies north of the parallel: the Republic of Korea gained territory in the mountainous interior, including areas around Cheorwon, Yanggu, Inje, and Goseong, that had been part of the North under the 1945 division. The net territorial change was modest, but the human consequences were large, since the new line cut across counties, roads, rail lines, rivers, and farmland. Roads and railways that had connected towns north and south of the parallel were severed, and in some cases communities found themselves on the opposite side of a militarized boundary from fields, family tombs, and relatives.

The zone's evolution after 1953 belongs to later chapters, but two features deserve mention here. Within the Demilitarized Zone, the armistice allowed a small number of civilians to remain, and two villages, one on each side, became the only settlements within the zone, an arrangement that persisted through the decades. In addition, the Joint Security Area at Panmunjom, a small circular zone straddling the line where the Military Armistice Commission met, was established as a place where personnel of both sides could in the early years move relatively freely, until a violent incident in 1976 led to a physical division of the area. Outside the zone, the South Korean military established a Civilian Control Line in the mid-1950s to regulate access to areas near the zone, which created a further zone of restricted settlement. These features show how a line drawn for a specific military purpose gradually became a permanent fixture around which a landscape, a security system, and local communities were organized. The line was intended as a temporary arrangement, but it was drawn in a way that made it durable, since it followed defensible terrain and was reinforced on both sides by military installations.

Section 72.11

The Military Armistice Commission and Neutral-Nations Supervision

Article II of the armistice created the institutional machinery to maintain the ceasefire. At its center was the Military Armistice Commission, a joint commission of ten senior officers, five appointed by the United Nations Command and five by the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers. The commission was charged with supervising the implementation of the agreement and settling violations through negotiation. Its headquarters was at Panmunjom. Paragraph 31 required it originally to meet daily, while permitting recesses of up to seven days by agreement of the senior members; either senior member could end such a recess with twenty-four hours’ notice. Later meeting practices changed, but the initial schedule was not merely on request. Subordinate to the commission were Joint Observer Teams, composed of officers from both sides, which could be dispatched to investigate alleged violations within the demilitarized zone. The commission thus functioned as a standing diplomatic and investigative channel between two armies that otherwise had no regular contact.

The second institution was the Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission, a four-nation body established to carry out inspection, observation, and investigation. The United Nations Command nominated Sweden and Switzerland, and the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers nominated Poland and Czechoslovakia. The commission supervised a system of Neutral Nations Inspection Teams stationed at designated ports of entry in both North and South Korea, where they were to observe the arrival of military personnel and equipment, and mobile teams that could investigate reported violations. The purpose was to enforce the agreement's provisions limiting the introduction of reinforcements and new equipment, a subject discussed in §72.12.

Historical Vocabulary
Monitoring Commission, Armistice Violation, Neutral Nation, and Verification

Definition. A monitoring commission is a body charged with observing compliance with an agreement. An armistice violation is an act alleged to breach the agreement's terms, such as entering the demilitarized zone with prohibited forces, introducing prohibited equipment, or firing across the line. A neutral nation, in the armistice, was a state not participating in combat, nominated by one side to serve on a supervisory body. Verification is the process of establishing, by inspection or investigation, whether a party has complied.

Historical context. The armistice distinguishes the Military Armistice Commission, composed of officers of the opposing sides, from the Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission, composed of officers of four states. The former handled disputes; the latter conducted inspection and investigation. Neither had enforcement powers beyond reporting and recommendation.

Why it matters. The design shows that the framers of the armistice sought to create institutions to maintain a ceasefire without relying wholly on trust. It also shows the limits of such institutions: they could report and recommend, but not compel.

Potential misunderstanding. "Neutral" in 1953 did not mean uncontested. Each side nominated states it regarded as acceptable, and each regarded the other's nominees with suspicion. Students should not confuse the four-nation supervisory commission with the five-nation commission that handled non-repatriate prisoners, which included India and had a different task.

The neutral-nation arrangements were politically contested from the beginning. The United Nations Command considered Poland and Czechoslovakia, as members of the Soviet bloc, to be aligned with the Communist side, and the Communist side considered Sweden and Switzerland, as Western European states, to be inclined to the United Nations Command's views. The compromise that named them was therefore a negotiated balance, not an expression of impartiality. In practice, the commission's members often divided along the lines of their nominating sides, and its reports reflected this division, with Swedish and Swiss delegates frequently reporting findings that Polish and Czechoslovak delegates declined to endorse. The inspection teams, moreover, were subject to restrictions on their movements, particularly in North Korea, and the system of inspection at ports of entry proved difficult to operate in both parts of the peninsula.

The later history of these institutions, treated in Volume IV, can be outlined briefly. By 1956 the inspection teams had been withdrawn from the ports of entry in both parts of Korea, in a process driven by disagreement about whether the system was effective, and in 1957 the United Nations Command announced that it considered itself no longer bound by the provision limiting the introduction of new weapons, arguing that the North had already violated it. The Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission itself continued in reduced form: North Korea expelled the Czechoslovak delegation in 1993 and the Polish delegation in 1995, and the Swedish and Swiss delegations continued to operate on the southern side. The Military Armistice Commission, from which North Korea withdrew in 1994, ceased to function as a joint body. These developments do not mean that the armistice ceased to exist; its demarcation line, its demilitarized zone, and its recognition as the governing military instrument persisted. They do show that the supervisory institutions, designed in a particular moment of mutual suspicion, eroded as the two sides' relationship hardened.

Section 72.12

Implementing the Ceasefire: Withdrawal, Islands, Security, and Compliance

The first days after July 27 were a period of intense practical activity. Within seventy-two hours, military forces had to withdraw from the Demilitarized Zone, and the engineers, soldiers, and laborers of both armies undertook the work of dismantling positions, removing equipment, and relocating personnel. The zone had been contested ground for months, and it was littered with trenches, bunkers, wire, and ordnance. The armistice required that mines and other hazards be removed or reported to the Military Armistice Commission so that the zone could be policed without danger, though the clearance of a landscape so heavily fortified and contaminated could not be completed in days, and unexploded ordnance would remain a hazard for decades. In the weeks that followed, the Military Demarcation Line was surveyed and marked, with joint teams from both sides establishing the numbered markers that would define it.

The agreement also regulated the movement of military forces and equipment across the peninsula. Paragraph 13 prohibited the introduction into Korea of reinforcing military personnel, and it prohibited the introduction of combat aircraft, armored vehicles, weapons, and ammunition, except for replacement of worn-out or damaged items on a piece-for-piece basis. It permitted the rotation of military personnel up to a limit of thirty-five thousand per month. These provisions were designed to freeze the military balance in place, to prevent either side from using the ceasefire to prepare for renewed war. They were also, from the beginning, difficult to enforce: the definition of replacement equipment was ambiguous, the inspection system depended on neutral teams whose access was restricted, and each side had an incentive to suspect the other of cheating. The history of these provisions illustrates a common problem in armistice agreements: obligations that are easy to write are harder to verify.

The coastal islands raised a separate problem. The armistice required each side to withdraw its forces from the rear and coastal islands and waters of the other side within ten days. It defined coastal islands as those that, though occupied by one side when the armistice took effect, had been controlled by the other side on June 24, 1950, the day before the war began. It then added a specific exception for the west coast: all islands lying north and west of the provincial boundary line between Hwanghae Province and Gyeonggi Province were placed under the military control of the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers, except for five island groups that were to remain under the control of the United Nations Command. These were Baengnyeong, Daecheong, Socheong, Yeonpyeong, and U-do. The provision shows that the negotiators had considered the geography of the western seas and had resolved the control of specific islands, while leaving the water between them largely unaddressed.

The agreement also addressed the waters of the Han River estuary, where the river meets the Yellow Sea near the western end of the line. Under Article II, the waters of the estuary were to be open to civil shipping of both sides wherever one bank was controlled by one side and the other bank by the other. The provision was practical, reflecting the fact that the estuary was a shared waterway at the boundary. The armistice also stated that naval forces should respect the waters contiguous to the Demilitarized Zone and the land area controlled by the other side and should not engage in any blockade of Korea. Students should note what the text does not do. It does not draw a boundary line at sea or define territorial waters. The armistice's naval and island provisions address specific questions, such as who held which islands and whether civil shipping could use the estuary. The broader maritime boundary was left undefined, a gap that led to disputes in later decades. The Northern Limit Line, an administrative line drawn by the United Nations Command in the weeks after the armistice to restrict the movements of its own naval and air forces, was not part of the signed agreement and was not accepted by the North, a point §72.18 examines.

Enforcing a military agreement between governments that remained hostile posed challenges from the first months. Both sides charged violations of the demilitarized zone, of airspace, and of the rules on reinforcement. The Military Armistice Commission became a venue where such charges were exchanged and argued, often in a tone of mutual accusation, and its meetings were as much exercises in public diplomacy as in practical problem-solving. Joint Observer Teams, designed to investigate, frequently could not agree on findings. The relative infrequency of large-scale violations in the early years of the armistice, however, suggests that the agreement's central provisions were observed in substance, even as lesser incidents accumulated. The comparison with the war's end is stark: fighting that had caused enormous daily casualties gave way, along the central front, to a tense but largely quiet boundary.

The ROK forces were an essential part of this system. Rhee's government had declared that it would not obstruct the armistice, and the United Nations Command exercised operational control over South Korean forces. The government of the Republic of Korea also pressed, in the autumn, for the early convening of the political conference and for guarantees of its security. The compliance of the South Korean forces was not simply a matter of orders but of the political understandings reached in June and July, which tied American support to South Korean restraint. The agreement thus operated within a political system of commitments among allies as well as between enemies. In the North, the Korean People's Army and Chinese forces likewise withdrew from the zone and accepted the new line, though Chinese forces would remain in North Korea until 1958. The armistice had produced, by the end of the first weeks, a functioning though fragile boundary.

Division IV · Sections 72.13–72.16 · August 1953–1954

Prisoner Exchanges and the Immediate Post-Armistice Settlement

How the armistice was translated into practical arrangements affecting prisoners, civilian populations, and postwar security.

Section 72.13

Operation Big Switch: The Repatriation of Prisoners, August–September 1953

Operation Big Switch was the principal exchange of prisoners of war under the armistice. It began on August 5, 1953, and concluded on September 6, and it was conducted at Panmunjom under procedures worked out by the Military Armistice Commission and the Red Cross societies of the participating sides. Prisoners who had elected repatriation were transported in trucks from their camps to the exchange site, where they were identified, processed, and handed over. On the United Nations side, prisoners held in camps across South Korea, including the compounds on Geoje Island, were gathered and moved north in convoys. The Communist side moved prisoners from its camps in North Korea and Manchuria to the exchange site by road and rail. The logistical challenge was substantial, involving tens of thousands of people processed over about a month, and it was carried out without major breakdown despite the tensions of the setting.

The numbers recorded in the official history of the Joint Chiefs of Staff are the standard operational figures. The United Nations Command repatriated 75,823 prisoners to the Communist side, of whom about 70,183 were North Korean and about 5,640 were Chinese. The Communist side returned 12,773 prisoners to the United Nations Command, of whom 7,862 were South Koreans, 3,597 were Americans, and about 1,300 were from other contributing states, including the largest group of about 945 from the United Kingdom. These figures should be read as the totals of the Big Switch operation specifically, not as the total number of prisoners held during the war and not as the final repatriation outcome, since prisoners who declined repatriation and the questions of those who died or went unaccounted for during captivity lie outside them. They also exclude the 6,670 and 684 prisoners exchanged in Little Switch and civilian internees handled separately.

The disparity between the two sides' returned totals was stark: the Communist side received roughly six times as many prisoners as it returned. The explanation lies partly in the different size of the prisoner populations. The United Nations Command held more than a hundred thousand prisoners from the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers in 1951 and early 1952, while the Communist side reported holding only about eleven thousand. The disparity also reflects the different fates of prisoners. Many of those who fell into Communist hands in the early months of the war, particularly during the retreats of 1950, died of exposure, disease, hunger, or violence, and many South Koreans were incorporated into the North Korean army or forced into labor. The question of how many captives were never accounted for remained contested. South Korean and United Nations authorities believed that tens of thousands of South Korean soldiers and civilians remained unaccounted for, while the Communist side maintained that it had returned all who wished to return. The unresolved fate of South Korean prisoners and abductees would continue to trouble relations in later decades; from the 1990s a small number of elderly former South Korean prisoners who had remained in the North escaped to the South and gave testimony about their lives there.

The political presentation of Big Switch was carefully managed. At the exchange site, returning Communist prisoners were met by officials and journalists, and some were shown to the press, in scenes the Communist media presented as homecomings of heroes. A number of prisoners on the United Nations side, including some Chinese prisoners, reportedly removed their prison garments or made demonstrations as they crossed. In the United States and other contributing countries, the return of former prisoners prompted intense public interest, partly because of widely circulated concerns about indoctrination during captivity. Newspapers speculated about the effects on returnees of the Communist reeducation programs that some prisoners had experienced, and government authorities conducted debriefings, interrogations, and in some cases investigations of conduct in captivity. For the Communist governments, the returnees were also screened. Chinese and North Korean returnees were required to account for their time in captivity, and some who had not resisted sufficiently or who had acted in ways that seemed disloyal faced investigation and punishment. The record on this is incomplete, since Chinese and North Korean archives are not open to systematic research, but memoirs and later testimony suggest that the return to home was not uniformly a happy one.

Analytically, Big Switch matters because it was where the armistice's promise became concrete for ordinary people. For tens of thousands of families, the news that a son, husband, or father was among the returned or the missing was the war's final verdict on their household. For individuals who crossed, the experience was both a liberation and a shock, a return to a home that might no longer exist or recognize them. The exchange was therefore both a logistical achievement and a deeply human event, and its details help students see the armistice as lived experience as well as legal text.

Section 72.14

Non-Repatriates, Indian Custody, and the Problem of Free Choice

Not all prisoners were returned in Big Switch. The terms of June 8 required that those who declined repatriation be transferred to the custody of the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission, where representatives of their home governments would explain their situation. In September 1953, the United Nations Command moved about 22,600 prisoners who had refused repatriation, of whom about 14,700 were Chinese and about 7,900 were North Koreans, to the neutral zone at Panmunjom, where they were received by the Indian Custodial Force. The Communist side transferred a much smaller group, roughly 350 prisoners, mostly South Koreans but including 21 Americans and one British soldier who had declined to return. The numbers show again the asymmetry between the two sides, though the smaller group represented a particular political and human drama, since the United States and Britain were deeply troubled by the possibility that their soldiers had chosen to remain in Communist countries.

The explanation period, which began in the autumn, proved far more contentious than the negotiators had anticipated. Representatives of North Korea and China sought to persuade prisoners to change their minds, and the procedures for doing so were disputed from the start. Communist representatives complained that prisoners were not being presented to them in appropriate conditions, that prisoners were intimidated by fellow prisoners, and that the explanations were not given adequate time. United Nations Command representatives complained of the opposite: that the explanations were being used as occasions for pressure, threats, and propaganda. The Chinese and North Korean prisoners in many cases refused to attend the sessions or demonstrated hostility to the explainers, and in some compounds violence or the threat of violence erupted. The Indian forces, who were armed principally with sticks and unprepared for the political fury of the compounds, faced difficult decisions about when to use force. The NNRC itself divided, with Poland and Czechoslovakia pressing for continued and longer explanations, Sweden and Switzerland resisting what they saw as pressure, and India, as chair, attempting to hold the process within the procedures. The disagreements prevented the commission from reaching consensus on many issues.

The results of the explanations were that only a small proportion of prisoners changed their preferences. Several hundred chose to be repatriated after hearing from their own side's representatives, and a smaller number moved in the other direction. For most of those who had refused repatriation, the explanations did not alter their position. In January 1954, when the periods allowed by the agreement expired without a political conference having produced a solution, the Indian custodians, following the terms, released the remaining prisoners from prisoner-of-war status. Roughly fourteen thousand Chinese prisoners were then transferred to the custody of the United Nations Command and flown or shipped to Taiwan, where they were received by the Republic of China government as "anti-Communist fighters." Nearly eight thousand North Koreans were released in South Korea. The United Nations subsequently reported 89 prisoners requesting neutral-country destinations; the final Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission disposition table separately records 86 prisoners, twelve Chinese and seventy-four Koreans, who went to India with the custodial force. These are date- and disposition-specific figures, not contradictory counts of one unchanging group. Of the twenty-one Americans who initially refused repatriation, most did not remain permanently in China: a retrospective published in 1980 reported fifteen had returned to the United States, three had gone to Europe, one had died, and two remained in China. Their immediate 1954 decisions and their subsequent lives must therefore be kept separate. The Communist governments denounced the outcome as a violation of the agreement and regarded the prisoners as having been coerced.

The problem of free choice that these episodes raise is not easily resolved. Section 72.15

Civilians after the Ceasefire: Repatriation, Resettlement, and Separated Families

The armistice included provisions for civilians as well as prisoners. Article III required each commander to permit civilians who had been resident in the territory under the other side's military control on June 24, 1950, and who wished to return, to do so, and it provided for the assistance of Red Cross societies. The provision acknowledged that the war had displaced and divided civilian populations, and it attempted to provide a mechanism for those who wished to return home. In practice, the provision was limited by its voluntary character, by the administrative apparatus of the military commands, and by the hostility between the governments. Evidence of how many people used it is thin, and its effect fell far short of the scale of separation the war had produced.

For civilians, the immediate post-armistice period was a time of both relief and continuing hardship. In the South, the government of the Republic of Korea, which had been based in Busan since the early months of the war, returned to Seoul in mid-August 1953. The capital, which had changed hands four times, was heavily damaged, and its population, which had fled or been displaced, began to return. In the North, Pyongyang had been devastated by bombing, and the government and the population turned to the problem of shelter and rebuilding. Across both parts of the peninsula, towns and villages had been destroyed or damaged, and agricultural land was littered with mines and unexploded ordnance. Emergency relief, including food, clothing, and medical supplies, was provided by the governments, by the United Nations Korean Reconstruction Agency and other international organizations in the South, and by assistance from the Soviet bloc in the North, though the full account of reconstruction belongs to Chapter 73.

The new line made the problem of separated families a permanent feature of Korean life. Wartime displacement had taken many forms: civilians who fled southward ahead of Chinese and North Korean advances, including tens of thousands evacuated from Hungnam in December 1950; people who moved north, voluntarily or under pressure, during the periods of Communist control or later; soldiers and laborers conscripted by one side or the other; and people who were killed, abducted, or went missing. Estimates of the total number of Koreans separated from relatives by the war and the division vary widely and are politically contested. The oft-cited figure of ten million people affected is a rough extrapolation rather than a documented count. What is clear is that the number was very large, that the new line prevented ordinary correspondence, travel, and visits, and that families often did not know whether relatives were alive. The first official exchange of visits between the two Koreas did not occur until 1985, and later reunion events of the 2000s reached only a small fraction of those who applied, with many applicants dying before they could be selected.

The father, who had worked for an organization associated with the earlier administration and feared reprisal, joined the crowds seeking passage on ships leaving the harbor. The mother and the children, with the grandmother too frail to travel, stayed with the expectation that he would return once the situation settled, and he too believed his departure would last weeks. He reached the South, and for months he worked in a refugee camp near Busan, sending inquiries through relief organizations that went unanswered.

By the time of the armistice, the family had been apart for more than two and a half years. The new line meant that the father could not simply go back, and ordinary mail did not pass. The mother meanwhile faced her own difficulties: raising children in a city that had been bombed, with a husband whose absence could mark the family as suspect in the local political environment, in a society where status depended on the government's judgment of family background. Neither parent knew whether the other was alive. Over the following decades, the father remarried in the South, found work, and raised a second family, and he spoke little of the first. The mother lived out her life in the North. The children, adults by the 1980s and 1990s, in time learned of their father's existence from relatives, and some families like theirs registered for reunion programs that reached few of the applicants. The experience of such households was not uniform. Some families were separated more briefly, some were divided by the deaths of members, and others never discovered what had become of their missing relatives. But the pattern of a temporary separation that hardened into a permanent one was widely shared.

The difference between the immediate humanitarian transition after July 1953 and the longer reconstruction history deserves emphasis. In the first months, the main tasks were relief, the return of the displaced, the restoration of basic administration, the clearing of hazards, and the exchange of prisoners and remains. The longer questions of economic rebuilding, foreign aid, industrial recovery, land policy, and the construction of two rival postwar societies belong to Chapter 73. What this chapter establishes is the condition in which that rebuilding began: a population that had endured extraordinary loss, a landscape marked by destruction and by a new militarized line, and a set of institutions designed to manage a continued hostility. The humanitarian consequences of the armistice were mixed. It ended the bombing and the mass killing of the battlefield, which was an incalculable relief to civilians, and it left in place a line that separated them from relatives and homes.

Section 72.16

The U.S.–ROK Mutual Defense Treaty and the Security of Postwar South Korea

The security treaty that Rhee had demanded as the price of his acquiescence was negotiated in the weeks after the armistice. John Foster Dulles traveled to Seoul in early August, and on August 7 he and Rhee issued a joint statement reflecting the understandings reached in the previous weeks; the draft treaty was initialed the next day. The treaty itself was signed in Washington on October 1, 1953, by Dulles for the United States and by the Republic of Korea's foreign minister, Byun Yung-tai. It was approved by the U.S. Senate in January 1954 with an understanding that the United States would not be obliged to act except in the case of an external armed attack on the Republic of Korea, a reservation meant to reassure Americans that the treaty would not commit them to support a South Korean offensive. It entered into force on November 17, 1954, upon the exchange of instruments of ratification, and a separate agreed minute concluded the same day addressed economic aid and the relationship between the Republic of Korea's forces and the United Nations Command.

Selected excerpts and paraphrases: The excerpts below are short quotations from the English text; students should consult the full text.

Preamble (excerpt): the parties declare their "common determination to defend themselves against external armed attack so that no potential aggressor could be under the illusion that either of them stands alone in the Pacific area."
Article III (excerpt): each party "recognizes that an armed attack in the Pacific area on either of the Parties in territories now under their respective administrative control... would be dangerous to its own peace and safety and declares that it would act to meet the common danger in accordance with its constitutional processes."

Analysis: The treaty is carefully worded. The language of Article III commits each party to act "in accordance with its constitutional processes," which preserves the American Congress's authority over war and thus does not guarantee automatic military response. The reference to "territories now under their respective administrative control" limited the commitment to the territory the Republic of Korea actually governed in 1953, and not to the whole of Korea that Rhee claimed. Article IV provides the legal basis for the continued stationing of American forces. Taken together, the provisions create a durable alliance while constraining the Republic of Korea from using it for offensive purposes.

Limitations: The treaty states commitments but not how they would be implemented, and it cannot show the military planning, command arrangements, or political bargaining that gave the alliance practical form. It does not describe the separate understanding about command of South Korean forces. The text cannot indicate how the Republic of Korea's public experienced the treaty or the alliance.

Perspective: The text represents the diplomatic formulations of two governments and, particularly in its limiting language, the preferences of the United States as the stronger party. It does not represent North Korea, China, or the Soviet Union, whose security arrangements were separate, nor Korean critics of the alliance.

The treaty institutionalized a pattern that would shape South Korean security for the rest of the century. United States forces remained in Korea in substantial numbers, reduced from their wartime peak but far larger than any prewar presence, and the combined military relationship deepened through the supply of equipment, training, and planning. The Republic of Korea's army, expanded during the war and sustained by American aid, became one of the largest in Asia. The alliance gave Rhee, and later South Korean governments, a security guarantee, and it gave the United States a forward position on the Asian mainland. It also created dependencies: South Korea's military capacity rested on American equipment and funding, its economy relied heavily on American aid for years, and its political leaders had to consider the interests of an ally whose global priorities did not always coincide with their own. The meaning of the alliance for South Korean sovereignty and politics would be debated throughout the following decades, as later chapters explain.

North Korea's security arrangements developed differently and should not be read through the lens of later treaties. In 1953 and 1954 the Democratic People's Republic of Korea did not have a formal mutual defense treaty with either the Soviet Union or China; those treaties were signed in 1961. Its security rested on the continuing presence of Chinese forces, which remained in the country until 1958, on the Soviet Union's supply of arms and economic assistance, and on the political relationship of the Korean Workers' Party with Moscow and Beijing. After the armistice, Kim Il Sung traveled to Moscow and Beijing, and both governments pledged significant reconstruction assistance, announced in the form of a Soviet pledge on the order of a billion rubles and a Chinese pledge of hundreds of millions of renminbi, though the precise amounts and terms are subjects for Chapter 73. The comparison highlights an asymmetry. South Korea's alliance was formalized in a bilateral treaty with a single patron, while North Korea's dependence on two Communist patrons was embedded in party relations and aid agreements, with a more complicated and, over time, more contested balance between them. Both Korean governments emerged from the war dependent on larger allies, and both would spend decades trying to manage that dependence.

Section 72.17

The Geneva Conference of 1954 and the Failure of Political Settlement

The armistice itself anticipated that it would be followed by a political settlement. Paragraph 60 recommended that, within three months of the armistice becoming effective, a higher-level political conference be convened by the governments concerned to negotiate the withdrawal of all foreign forces from Korea and the peaceful settlement of the Korean question. The recommendation was an acknowledgment that the military agreement could not itself resolve the questions that had caused the war. It was also a recommendation, not an obligation: the commanders could suggest a conference, but only governments could convene one, and the governments that would have to attend disagreed about almost everything that mattered, including who should attend.

The three-month period passed without a conference. In the autumn of 1953, preliminary talks at Panmunjom between American and Communist representatives foundered over the composition of the conference. The Communist side insisted that neutral states, including the Soviet Union, should attend as participants, while the United States and its allies insisted that the Soviet Union was a belligerent in practical terms and should not sit as a neutral. The disagreement delayed the conference until the foreign ministers of the United States, the United Kingdom, France, and the Soviet Union, meeting in Berlin in February 1954, agreed to hold a conference in Geneva in the spring that would address Korea and, in a second phase, Indochina. The Korean phase opened on April 26, 1954, and ran until June 15. Nineteen delegations took part in the Korean discussions: sixteen on the allied side—the Republic of Korea and fifteen states that had contributed forces to the United Nations Command, including the United States—and three on the Communist side, representing the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, the People’s Republic of China, and the Soviet Union. South Africa, although a contributor to UN military operations, was not among the Geneva participants. The identities and grouping are documented in the published conference record.

The conference revealed a deep disagreement about the principles of a settlement, and the competing proposals deserve to be understood on their own terms. The Republic of Korea's foreign minister, Byun Yung-tai, presented a fourteen-point proposal rooted in the position that the Republic of Korea was the only lawful government in Korea. It called for the withdrawal of Chinese forces before any other step, for the holding of elections in North Korea under United Nations supervision to fill vacant seats in the Republic of Korea's National Assembly, in conformity with the Republic of Korea's constitution, and for the retention of United Nations forces until unification and security were assured. For the Republic of Korea, the United Nations had already declared the Republic of Korea the lawful government, and unification meant extending that government's authority northward. The Democratic People's Republic of Korea's foreign minister, Nam Il, advanced a different framework. He proposed that all foreign forces withdraw within six months, that an all-Korean commission with equal representation from North and South organize free elections throughout Korea, and that a neutral nations commission supervise. Chinese foreign minister Zhou Enlai and the Soviet foreign minister Vyacheslav Molotov supported this approach. For the North Korean side, no party had the right to impose its constitutional order on the other, and the United Nations, having fought as a belligerent, lacked standing as an impartial supervisor.

The disagreement was thus not simply a refusal by one side to make peace; it reflected incompatible claims about legitimacy, supervision, and representation. The Western and South Korean side insisted on United Nations authority, on elections under the Republic of Korea's constitutional framework, and on representation proportional to population, which would favor the more populous South. The Communist side rejected United Nations competence, insisted on equal North–South representation in an all-Korean body, and demanded the early withdrawal of all foreign forces, including Chinese, as part of a package. Each set of proposals was framed in terms of democratic and national principle, and each would have produced an outcome favorable to its proponents. Efforts at compromise were limited, partly because the delegates had little room to maneuver and partly because the conference's attention was increasingly drawn to Indochina, where the French position at Dien Bien Phu collapsed in May and the second phase of the conference began. Secretary of State Dulles, who had attended the early sessions, left Geneva in early May. On June 15, the sixteen states that had contributed to the United Nations Command issued a declaration reaffirming their positions and regretting that the conference had not achieved a settlement, and the conference ended without agreement.

The consequence of Geneva's failure was that the armistice remained the central operative instrument governing the Korean peninsula's military order. Paragraph 62 had stated that the agreement would remain in effect until expressly superseded, and no superseding agreement was reached. The 1954 Geneva Conference did convene and substantively address the Korean question, and the allied delegations subsequently treated it as the political conference contemplated by Paragraph 60; what failed was agreement on a political settlement. The meeting also took place well beyond the three-month period recommended in 1953. The United States and the Republic of Korea, following their joint statement, withdrew their support for further conferences. The consequence was a settlement in which the military arrangements endured and the political questions remained open. But the structure of the positions made agreement unlikely. Each side held that its proposed unification would be consistent with democracy and legitimacy, and each regarded the other's as a means of absorption. The armistice thus became not a stage in a process of settlement but the framework of an enduring arrangement.

Section 72.18

The New Geography of Division: Frontiers, Islands, and the Unfinished Boundary

The geography of the divided peninsula after 1953 was more complicated than the image of a single line suggests. On land, the Military Demarcation Line and the Demilitarized Zone formed a boundary that was heavily militarized on both sides, and in which the only regular contact took place at Panmunjom. At the center of this system, the Joint Security Area at Panmunjom, a small area straddling the line, housed the Military Armistice Commission's meeting facilities and the conference buildings where delegates of the two sides, and later their officials, continued to meet. The line was crossed at Panmunjom by a bridge over a small stream, used during the prisoner exchanges and later known in English as the Bridge of No Return. The Joint Security Area was, by design, a place of controlled encounter. It would later become the most famous and, at times, the most dangerous site on the peninsula, as chapters on Cold War confrontation and later diplomacy describe.

The land boundary also created a sequence of zones on the southern side. Beyond the Demilitarized Zone, the South Korean military established a Civilian Control Line, a restricted area in which access and settlement were regulated, and further south a belt of military installations, fortifications, and anti-tank defenses developed over the following decades. Villages and farms in the border counties of Gyeonggi and Gangwon provinces lived within this regime, and their residents' movements, farming, and building were controlled by the military. Similar restrictions applied on the northern side, though they are much less documented. The border communities of both Koreas had distinct histories shaped by military regulation, relocation, and the symbolic burden of living on the line. Villagers who had once farmed fields now behind the line, or whose ancestral graves lay in the zone or beyond it, had to seek permission for access or lost it altogether.

The maritime picture was more ambiguous still. The armistice assigned specific islands and addressed civil use of the Han estuary, but it did not define a sea boundary. In the weeks after the armistice, the commander of the United Nations Command established an administrative line in the Yellow Sea, usually dated to August 1953, to keep his own naval and air forces from approaching the northern coast and thereby to reduce the risk of incidents. The line, which came to be called the Northern Limit Line, was not negotiated with the Korean People's Army or the Chinese forces and was not part of the armistice text. For years it attracted little notice, but from the early 1970s North Korea rejected it, and in 1999 it announced its own claimed boundary farther south. The line became a flashpoint of repeated naval clashes in the following decades, discussed in later chapters. The history is a reminder that a land-based demarcation line is not the same as a fully settled maritime boundary: the armistice resolved who held which islands, but not who controlled the water that surrounded them.

The consequences for border communities, fishing, and settlement were direct. Fishermen of the western islands and the coastal counties faced restrictions on where they could operate, and the rich crab and fish stocks in disputed waters became a source of recurring tension. Islanders on Baengnyeong and Yeonpyeong lived near a militarized frontier, and their economy, security, and daily lives were shaped by it. On land, the severance of roads and rail lines changed economic geography: the old rail line between Seoul and the north terminated at the line, and the region around the former capital Kaesong, once part of the commercial heartland of the south, was now in the North. The cumulative effect was to convert a temporary military arrangement into a lasting geography of division in which local life was organized around the line, even as the governments on either side continued to assert that the boundary was provisional.

Section 72.19

An Unfinished War: Political Legitimacy, Memory, and the Meaning of July 27

Each of the parties to the armistice has interpreted it differently, and the interpretations reflect their political traditions as much as the text. In the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, July 27 is commemorated as the Day of Victory in the Fatherland Liberation War. The official narrative holds that the North, with Chinese support, defeated an American invasion and forced the United States to sign an armistice. The date is a national holiday, and the narrative is reinforced by monuments, museums, school curricula, and ritual. The war's memory is intimately tied to the legitimacy of the Kim family's rule, to the justification of military priority, and to the claim that the North has defended the nation against foreign aggression. On several later occasions the North Korean government has declared that it no longer considers itself bound by the armistice, though it has also continued to regard the line as a practical reality.

In the Republic of Korea, the war is remembered primarily through June 25, the date of the invasion, which is commemorated as a day of remembrance, and the armistice has been viewed with greater ambivalence. For Rhee and his successors, it was an arrangement forced on Korea by larger powers that preserved the nation's division. In South Korean public memory it has been remembered as the end of the fighting and also as the start of a long separation. The date July 27 has been recognized since 2013 as a day honoring the participation of United Nations forces, which emphasizes the international coalition's role. For many South Koreans, the memory of the war is carried through the experience of separated families, veterans, and survivors of civilian violence, memories that do not map neatly onto official narratives. South Korea's political debates over how to interpret the war, the armistice, and the relationship with the North have been intense and, from the 1990s, increasingly open.

In the United States, the armistice has been remembered mainly through the veterans who fought the war and through the phrase "the Forgotten War," used to describe its relatively limited place in public memory compared with the Second World War and the Vietnam War. The Korean War Veterans Memorial, dedicated in Washington in 1995, and the recognition of July 27 as a day honoring Korean War veterans reflect a renewed attention. American interpretations have varied between seeing the war as a successful defense of South Korea and containment of Communist expansion and seeing it as a costly stalemate that left the underlying problems unresolved. In China, the war is officially called the War to Resist U.S. Aggression and Aid Korea, and it is remembered as a victory that demonstrated the strength of the new state and defended its security, a view reinforced by official history, film, and commemoration. The experience of the Chinese soldiers, whose losses were large, has entered public memory more gradually and with controlled emphasis. Soviet and later Russian memory of the war has been more muted. These different national memories show the same historical event serving different political purposes, and students should treat each as a historical phenomenon requiring explanation, not as an established account.

The armistice institutions and commemorations have themselves shaped political memory. The Military Armistice Commission's meetings at Panmunjom, the guard posts of the Joint Security Area, the markers along the line, and the annual rituals on both sides have produced a shared though contested symbolism. The persistence of the armistice, rather than a peace treaty, has meant that both Koreas have lived for generations within a framework that defines their relationship as unresolved and provisional. At the same time, the armistice has also shaped the practical security order. It has constrained the scale of conventional conflict, and its demarcation line has remained the effective boundary between the two states. The competing reunification claims of both governments, each asserting itself as the legitimate representative of the Korean nation, were not altered by the armistice; they were preserved by it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Temporary Division in 1945 to Military Division in 1953 and the Security System That Followed

What Changed

  • The dividing line moved from a straight parallel drawn for the acceptance of a surrender to an irregular military line shaped by three years of war
  • The division changed from a temporary arrangement between occupying armies to a boundary between two sovereign-claiming states, each with its own army, alliance, and ideology
  • The mechanisms of management changed from occupation authorities and a United Nations process to the armistice commissions and, in the South, a bilateral defense treaty
  • The expectation of the temporary changed: by 1954, few observers could imagine a swift reunification, and the line began to organize local life and national politics

What Stayed the Same

  • Both governments continued to claim to represent all of Korea
  • The great powers continued to shape Korean outcomes, with the United States, China, and the Soviet Union all committed to their respective clients
  • The peninsula's position at the intersection of continental and maritime rivalries remained
  • The expectation that the division was provisional persisted in official rhetoric even as it hardened in practice

When and how change occurred: The change was uneven. The shift from temporary to durable division occurred over a decade, not in a single moment, and it was driven by the war, the armistice, the failure of Geneva, and the alliance systems that followed. Those who experienced it most directly were the families separated by the line and the communities along it, while elites in both governments experienced greater continuity in their claims. Why the pattern matters: It demonstrates that the permanence of division was a product of specific decisions and failures, not a natural condition.

Historical Debate

Was the 1953 Armistice a Successful Peace-Preserving Compromise or a Settlement That Institutionalized Division?

The question: Historians and commentators disagree about how to evaluate the armistice. One view stresses that it ended a devastating war, prevented a return to continuous general fighting for more than seven decades, and created institutions to manage a hostile relationship. Another stresses that it froze a political division that had caused the war, entrenched two rival states, and failed to produce a settlement, leaving a permanent source of tension.

Interpretation A: a successful compromise. The armistice stopped the killing, stabilized the military situation, returned prisoners, and kept the peace in a region where war could have resumed or widened. In this view, the standard of success should be what was realistically achievable in 1953, when neither side could win and both leaderships were exhausted. The durability of the ceasefire is evidence of the agreement's effectiveness.
Interpretation B: a settlement that institutionalized division. By fixing a line, recognizing two armies, and deferring political questions, the armistice made the division durable. The failure of Geneva and the strengthening of two alliance systems meant that a temporary arrangement became permanent, while families remained separated and militarization continued. In this view, the ceasefire's durability reflects mutual deterrence and not resolution.
Interpretation C: both, and the question may be misframed. Some historians argue that the armistice cannot be evaluated apart from the political settlement it could not provide, and that its success in preventing war and its role in entrenching division were two aspects of the same outcome. They also note that the armistice did not prevent lesser violence, including clashes and assassination attempts, and that its record of preventing war must be weighed against the persistent insecurity it left.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the armistice text, diplomatic records, the history of incidents along the line, the course of the Geneva Conference, and later accounts of whether a peace settlement was ever realistically available. Counterfactual claims, such as whether a different compromise in 1951 or 1953 could have produced reunification, rest on limited evidence and should be treated with care.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and standards of evaluation more than established facts. Historians agree on the sequence of events; they differ on what counts as success and on how far the division was determined by the armistice as opposed to the broader Cold War and the commitments of the two Korean regimes.

Why it matters: How one evaluates the armistice shapes how one understands contemporary proposals to replace it with a peace agreement and the relationship between security and political settlement on the peninsula.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in this chapter, write a short assessment of which interpretation you find most persuasive. Identify evidence that would strengthen or weaken each, and explain what standard of success you are applying.

A concise retrospective shows how the armistice continued to structure the peninsula's security environment, without anticipating the political histories of Volume IV. The armistice's supervisory machinery eroded, as noted in §72.11, and the Chinese forces left North Korea in 1958. Incidents along the line and at sea, including the confrontations discussed in Chapter 78, tested the agreement but did not end it. In 1991 the two Koreas signed an agreement on reconciliation and non-aggression that referred to the armistice as the framework to be respected until a peace regime was established, and later chapters discuss the inter-Korean summits, the nuclear crisis, and the diplomacy of the 2010s. Through these changes the armistice remained the foundational document of the peninsula's military order. It is both a reminder of a war whose political questions were never settled and a framework that has, with all its imperfections, helped to prevent that war from resuming.

The meaning of the armistice for the history of Korea is therefore double. It was a success in the narrow sense that it ended the continuous large-scale fighting that had devastated the peninsula, and it created institutions and a boundary that proved durable. It was a failure in the sense that it did not settle the struggle over Korean sovereignty, and its durability helped consolidate the division that it was intended to bridge. Neither description alone captures its historical significance. What began as a temporary military arrangement became a defining condition of modern Korean history, and understanding how that happened, and why neither the Korean governments nor their allies were able to alter it, is essential to understanding the two Koreas that the next volume examines.

Section 72.20

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

Chapter Summary

Chapter 72 has explained why the Korean War ended in an armistice rather than a decisive victory or a peace treaty, and how the military settlement of July 1953 transformed wartime division into an enduring political and security system. The central argument is that the Korean Armistice Agreement ended large-scale organized fighting without resolving the political conflict over Korean sovereignty. It preserved two rival states, created mechanisms for maintaining a ceasefire, and established a military boundary that became an enduring feature of the peninsula.

The first division showed how a war that had become difficult to win took about two years to negotiate to a halt. By mid-1951, the failure of successive offensives, military exhaustion, American fear of escalation, and Communist reassessment produced Yakov Malik's broadcast of June 23 and the opening of talks at Kaesong on July 10. Fighting continued while delegates met, because the United Nations Command refused a ceasefire before terms were agreed. The negotiators settled the demarcation line on the principle of the line of contact, but prisoner repatriation became the principal unresolved issue. The dispute between compulsory and voluntary repatriation, the 1952 screening, and the Geoje-do crisis turned a humanitarian question into one of political principle and regime legitimacy. The chapter distinguished the military stalemate, the diplomatic deadlock, and the underlying political disagreement.

The second division examined the breakthrough of 1953. Eisenhower's inauguration, Stalin's death, Chinese and North Korean reassessments, and a reformulation of the prisoner question in the form of neutral custody combined to make agreement possible. No single event caused the settlement. Operation Little Switch demonstrated that exchange machinery could work, and the Terms of Reference of June 8 created the Neutral Nations Repatriation Commission. Syngman Rhee's opposition and his unilateral release of prisoners on June 18 forced the United States to bargain with its own ally, producing the commitments of a mutual defense treaty, aid, and army expansion in return for his decision not to obstruct implementation.

The third division reconstructed the armistice itself. Signed at Panmunjom on the morning of July 27, 1953 by delegates and then by the three commanders, it took effect at 10 p.m. It established the Military Demarcation Line along the final front, a four-kilometer Demilitarized Zone with two kilometers of withdrawal on each side, a ten-officer Military Armistice Commission, and a four-nation Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission. It regulated reinforcement and rotation, allocated coastal islands, opened the Han estuary to civil shipping, and recommended a political conference. The Republic of Korea did not sign, but its forces, under the United Nations Command, complied. The agreement was a military instrument and not a peace treaty.

The fourth division followed the translation of the armistice into practice. Operation Big Switch returned 75,823 prisoners to the Communist side and 12,773 to the United Nations side, according to the Joint Chiefs of Staff history, while roughly 22,600 non-repatriates entered Indian custody in a process that revealed the limits of the idea of free choice. Civilians faced a mix of relief and lasting separation, as the new line turned wartime displacement into permanent division for countless families. The Mutual Defense Treaty of October 1, 1953, which entered into force on November 17, 1954, gave South Korea a lasting security guarantee, while North Korea relied on Chinese and Soviet support through party relations and aid.

The fifth division explained why a political settlement did not follow. The Geneva Conference of April 26 to June 15, 1954 failed because the two sides held incompatible views of legitimacy, United Nations authority, representation in an all-Korean body, and the timing of foreign withdrawal. The armistice remained the governing instrument. Its geography included the DMZ, the Joint Security Area, and an unfinished maritime boundary in which the later Northern Limit Line was not part of the signed text. Memory of July 27 differed sharply among North Korea, South Korea, the United States, and China.

Several analytical themes cut across the chapter. First, evidence is partisan and incomplete: each side's records and narratives served political purposes, and Soviet, Chinese, and North Korean archives remain only partly accessible. Second, causation was multiple, and the settlement of 1953 resulted from the convergence of military, diplomatic, political, and ideological factors. Third, individuals, including prisoners and civilians, were not simply objects of policy; their choices and suffering shaped the settlement and its aftermath.

The chapter also showed the double character of the armistice. It was a success in ending continuous large-scale fighting and in creating a durable framework, and it was a failure in leaving the struggle over sovereignty unresolved and helping to consolidate division. The Historical Debate presented this as a genuine disagreement over standards of evaluation, not over the sequence of events.

As the concluding chapter of Volume III, Chapter 72 closes a long sequence that began with the forced opening of 1876. Over those seven decades Korea passed through reform, empire, colonization, liberation, occupation, and civil war, and emerged in 1953 with two rival states and a fortified line. Volume IV, beginning with Chapter 73, examines how North and South Korea rebuilt and developed within rival political and international security systems, treating them as parallel Korean histories.

Armistice
A military agreement between commanders suspending hostilities and fixing lines pending a political settlement; the Korean Armistice remains in effect until expressly superseded.

The halt in organized fighting effected under Paragraph 12, twelve hours after signature.

Islands subject to the armistice's withdrawal rule, with an exception placing five western island groups under UNC control.

The October 1, 1953 U.S.–ROK security treaty, in force November 17, 1954.

The higher-level conference recommended in Paragraph 60 to settle the Korean question.
Geneva Conference on Korea
The April 26–June 15, 1954 conference that failed to produce a political settlement.
Civilian Control Line
A restricted-access line established by the South Korean military south of the DMZ in the mid-1950s.
Separated families
Households divided by war and the line, whose communication and reunion were prevented for decades.
Historical memory
The selective commemoration and contestation of past events by states and communities.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a war ended without its central political question being answered. The Korean Armistice stopped one of the most destructive wars of the twentieth century and did so by a negotiated process whose difficulties, prisoners, lines, inspectors, and an unruly ally, reveal how modern wars end: through bargaining shaped by military pressure, domestic politics, alliance management, and competing principles. It shows that endings are historical processes with their own contingencies, and that the shape of an ending can determine the shape of what follows.

The chapter also shows how a temporary arrangement can acquire permanence. The line of 1953 was drawn for military reasons and described by its authors as provisional, yet its durability came from the commitments made around it: the security treaty in the South, the support of the Communist patrons in the North, the failure of Geneva, and the institutions and habits that grew up along the line. The history of the armistice is therefore also a history of how provisional things become structural, a pattern that students will see again in the stories of reconstruction and of rival regimes that follow.

The chapter's themes reach back across Volume III. The question of who has the right to determine Korea's future, raised by the opening of 1876, by colonial rule, and by the occupation of 1945, was not answered by the armistice but was frozen by it. The two Korean governments that emerged in 1948 and survived the war each continued to claim the whole nation. Ordinary Koreans, who had endured colonial rule, liberation, civil war, and bombing, entered the postwar years with enormous losses and with a line across their country.

Volume IV begins from this condition. Chapter 73 examines how North and South Korea rebuilt from devastation, with aid from rival blocs and within competing political systems, and later chapters follow their diverging histories to 2026. The armistice remains in the background of that story as the framework of the peninsula's military order and as a reminder that the war's political questions were postponed and not resolved. Understanding the armistice is therefore the necessary first step to understanding the Two Koreas.
