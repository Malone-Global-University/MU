Summits, Missiles, and the Collapse of Inter-Korean Détente

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the morning of April 27, 2018, the leader of North Korea walked across a low concrete curb at Panmunjom, the border village where the 1953 Armistice had been signed, and stepped onto the southern side of the Military Demarcation Line. Minutes later, at his invitation, South Korea’s president stepped briefly across in the other direction. The gesture took seconds. The question this chapter pursues is why so much of what followed it took only a few years to come undone.

Kim Jong Un’s crossing was the first by a North Korean leader since the Armistice took effect in July 1953. Moon Jae-in greeted him on the southern side, and the two men joined hands while cameras relayed the scene live to audiences in both Koreas and around the world. Later in the day they held a conversation on a small footbridge near the Joint Security Area, out of earshot of the press, and signed the Panmunjom Declaration. Only months earlier, the same peninsula had seemed to many observers to be edging toward war: North Korea had conducted its sixth and most powerful nuclear test in September 2017, tested missiles capable of reaching far beyond Japan, and traded public threats with the American president.

The sequence from that crisis to the summitry of 2018 was extraordinarily rapid. Within roughly a year, the peninsula saw a Winter Olympics in which athletes from both Korean states marched together, three meetings between Moon and Kim, a summit in Singapore between Kim and Donald Trump (the first ever between a sitting American president and a North Korean leader), reunions of families separated by the Korean War, a joint liaison office in the North Korean city of Kaesong, and a military agreement that pulled back guard posts and restricted activity along the border. For many Koreans, particularly elderly people who remembered the war, these events seemed to promise a different future. For others, in South Korea, North Korea, and the United States, they raised questions about whether declarations could alter the underlying military and political confrontation.

This chapter argues that the second group of observers saw something important, although the opening was not an illusion. The diplomacy of 2018 produced real achievements: it lowered immediate military tension, gave families a few days together, and opened channels that had been closed. But the principal agreements were declarations of intent. They named goals, including a nuclear-free peninsula and a lasting peace regime, without establishing a verified definition of denuclearization, a schedule, a sequence of sanctions relief, or independent monitoring. When the Hanoi summit of February 2019 failed over exactly these issues, the structures built on top of the summitry proved fragile.

The history that follows is therefore not simply a story of leaders who succeeded and then failed. It involves a pandemic that closed North Korea’s borders, a dispute over leaflets that became the stated occasion for the destruction of a joint liaison office in June 2020, a change of government in Seoul in 2022 that altered South Korean policy, North Korea’s decision to write its nuclear forces into law and later its constitution, the suspension of the 2018 military agreement in 2023–2024, and Kim Jong Un’s declaration in December 2023 and January 2024 that the two Koreas were separate and hostile states. North Korea’s official language of national reunification, in use for decades, was formally set aside.

The chapter ends in November 2026. By then South Korea, under President Lee Jae-myung, had adopted an explicit policy of peaceful coexistence and had suspended its border loudspeaker broadcasts. North Korea had not accepted that approach. A landmine incident in the Demilitarized Zone in September 2026 injured three South Korean soldiers, with Seoul and Pyongyang offering conflicting accounts, and on October 23 South Korea proposed military talks that had not, by the chapter’s endpoint, produced sustained negotiations. The 1953 Armistice remained the only framework governing the ceasefire. No peace treaty had replaced it.

Historians of this period work with unusual evidence problems. Many central events are documented by official declarations whose texts are public, such as the Panmunjom Declaration and the Singapore Joint Statement. Yet the private conversations behind them are known mainly through the accounts of participants with obvious reasons to present their own positions favorably. North Korean sources, principally the state news agency KCNA and statements by the party and foreign ministry, are authoritative about what the government chose to announce and much less so about its internal deliberations. South Korean government sources are extensive but also reflect the policy commitments of successive administrations. This chapter attributes competing claims to their sources rather than resolving them silently.

The chapter builds on Chapter 85 (the Sunshine Policy), Chapter 86 (North Korea’s nuclear program), and Chapter 92 (Kim Jong Un’s domestic system), and it leaves the great-power dimension to Chapter 94, South Korean domestic politics to Chapter 95, and the final synthesis to Chapter 96. Its own responsibility is narrower: to explain what happened between the two Korean states, and between them and the United States, and why a moment that looked like the start of a settlement ended as a militarized separation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the nuclear and missile confrontation of 2016–2017 shaped the conditions for the diplomacy of 2018.
  2. Trace the sequence of inter-Korean and U.S.–North Korean meetings from the PyeongChang Olympics to the Stockholm talks of October 2019.
  3. Distinguish a declaration of objectives from a verified, implemented, and binding agreement.
  4. Analyze the Panmunjom Declaration, Singapore Joint Statement, and Pyongyang Joint Declaration as documents, identifying what each specified and what each left undefined.
  5. Compare the meanings that the parties attached to “denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula.”
  6. Evaluate the 2018 military agreement as a confidence-building framework, including its achievements, geographic limits, and eventual collapse.
  7. Assess competing American and North Korean accounts of the Hanoi summit without silently resolving them.
  8. Explain why the destruction of the Kaesong liaison office in June 2020 mattered beyond its stated cause.
  9. Contextualize Yoon Suk Yeol’s deterrence-centered policy and Lee Jae-myung’s peaceful-coexistence policy within the longer history of South Korean approaches to the North.
  10. Interpret North Korea’s hostile two-state doctrine and distinguish what has been directly published, what has been reported, and what remains unavailable.
  11. Describe the human and social costs of the collapse of engagement for separated families and border communities.
  12. Evaluate competing explanations for the failure of détente, identifying the evidence that supports each.

Historical Context

The Korean War ended in 1953 with an armistice rather than a peace treaty, as Chapter 72 explained. The agreement was signed by military commanders of the United Nations Command, the Korean People’s Army, and the Chinese People’s Volunteers; South Korea’s government did not sign it. It established a ceasefire, a Military Demarcation Line, and a Demilitarized Zone roughly four kilometers wide, and it called for a political conference on a settlement that never produced one. Technically, therefore, the two Korean states remained at war, and the border they share has remained among the most heavily armed in the world. The maritime boundary in the West Sea, the Northern Limit Line drawn unilaterally by the United Nations Command after the Armistice, was never agreed by North Korea and has been the site of naval clashes.

Efforts at reconciliation preceded 2018 by decades. A joint statement of July 4, 1972, and the Basic Agreement of December 1991 (together with a joint declaration on denuclearization of the peninsula early in 1992) established principles of non-aggression and exchange that were only partially implemented. The Sunshine Policy of Kim Dae-jung and the summits of June 2000 and October 2007, discussed in Chapter 85, produced tourism at Mount Kumgang, the Kaesong Industrial Complex, family reunions, and ministerial talks. Those cooperative arrangements proved vulnerable to political change, the shooting of a South Korean tourist at Mount Kumgang in 2008, naval clashes, and the 2010 sinking of the corvette Cheonan and shelling of Yeonpyeong Island. In February 2016, following North Korea’s fourth nuclear test and a satellite launch, the South Korean government of Park Geun-hye closed the Kaesong complex.

Nuclear negotiations form a second background strand. The 1994 Agreed Framework, the Six-Party Talks of 2003–2009, and the February 2012 “Leap Day” understanding all involved attempts to trade restraints on North Korea’s nuclear program for energy, aid, security assurances, or sanctions relief, as Chapter 86 explained. Each broke down amid disagreements about verification, sequencing, and compliance. Those earlier failures matter because the participants of 2018–2019 carried memories of them: Washington was wary of concessions made in advance of verified action, while Pyongyang was wary of disarming without guarantees of security.

The immediate setting was the confrontation of 2016–2017. After North Korea’s nuclear tests of January and September 2016, the United Nations Security Council adopted successively tighter sanctions. Kim Jong Un, as Chapter 92 showed, had consolidated authority and set a priority of completing a nuclear deterrent. The election of Donald Trump in November 2016 and of Moon Jae-in in May 2017, after the impeachment of Park Geun-hye (Chapter 95), brought to office two leaders with very different instincts toward North Korea. Moon, whose political roots lay in the engagement tradition, sought to use the crisis to re-open dialogue. Trump alternated between threats and expressions of willingness to meet Kim. The chapter begins in that tension.

Part I

From Nuclear Confrontation to Summit Diplomacy, 2016–2018

The crisis that preceded détente, the Olympic opening, and the diplomatic breakthroughs of 2018.

Section 93.1

The Peninsula on the Brink: Nuclear Tests, Missile Launches, and Escalation, 2016–2017

North Korea conducted its fourth nuclear test in January 2016 and its fifth in September of that year. In 2017 it launched missiles of steadily greater range: the Hwasong-12 intermediate-range missile, two launches in July of the Hwasong-14, which North Korea described as an intercontinental ballistic missile, and in November the Hwasong-15. On September 3, 2017, it conducted a sixth nuclear test, which it described as a hydrogen bomb. Outside assessments of yield and design varied widely and remained uncertain, and this chapter does not rely on any single figure. The technical history belongs to Chapter 86; what matters here is that, by the end of 2017, governments in Seoul, Tokyo, and Washington regarded North Korea as nearing a capacity to threaten the continental United States.

Public rhetoric escalated alongside the testing. In August 2017 President Trump warned that further threats would meet with “fire and fury,” and North Korean military spokesmen answered by describing plans for missile launches toward waters near Guam. In September Trump told the United Nations General Assembly that the United States might have to destroy North Korea if it were forced to defend itself or its allies, and Kim Jong Un responded with a personal statement. The historian must distinguish between this language and action. No American strike occurred, and the Guam launch plan was not carried out. Two missiles did overfly Japan in August and September, and the United States and South Korea held large-scale exercises and flew strategic bombers near the peninsula. Rhetoric signaled risk; the military actions were the more concrete forms of signaling.

Sanctions tightened in parallel. The UN Security Council adopted Resolutions 2270 and 2321 in 2016, and Resolutions 2371, 2375, and 2397 in 2017. Together they restricted North Korean exports such as coal, iron, seafood, and textiles, capped imports of refined petroleum, and moved toward repatriating North Korean workers abroad. Their breadth would matter later, because the question of how much sanctions relief North Korea should receive in return for what concessions became a core issue at Hanoi. The sanctions also bore on the daily economy of North Korea, which Chapter 92 examined, and on its trade with China, whose enforcement varied.

South Korea’s position was especially difficult. Seoul, with a metropolitan area of more than twenty million people, lies within range of North Korean artillery and rockets near the border, and any military conflict would have exposed South Korean civilians to enormous risk. President Moon, elected in May 2017 after Park Geun-hye’s impeachment, was committed to the alliance with the United States but also to preventing a war. He argued publicly that no military action should be taken on the peninsula without South Korea’s consent, and in a July 2017 speech in Berlin proposed dialogue and a reduction in tension. The deployment of the American THAAD missile defense system on South Korean territory in 2017 added tension with China, a subject for Chapter 94, and demonstrated how Seoul’s security choices drew simultaneous pressure from several directions.

Pyongyang’s calculations are known mainly through its public statements, which presented the nuclear program as a deterrent against American attack and as a permanent achievement. In November 2017 Kim Jong Un declared the completion of the state’s nuclear force. Many analysts interpreted the declaration as the end of a phase of testing and an opening for negotiation from a position of claimed strength, though the inference rests on the timing and not on documentary evidence of motive. Thus a year that began with fears of war ended with a leader who could present himself as having secured a deterrent. That combination helps explain why the diplomacy of 2018 appeared so remarkable: it began not after a diplomatic settlement of the confrontation but directly after its peak.

Section 93.2

The PyeongChang Winter Olympics and the Opening of Diplomacy, January–February 2018

In his New Year address on January 1, 2018, Kim Jong Un said that North Korea was willing to send a delegation to the PyeongChang Winter Olympics and urged improved inter-Korean relations. In the same address he claimed that a nuclear button was on his desk, a reminder that the offer came with deterrence still in place. Moon’s government responded quickly, and on January 9 officials from the two sides met at the Peace House in the South Korean sector of Panmunjom, the first high-level talks in more than two years. The Olympics, scheduled for February 9–25, gave both governments a limited, time-bound occasion that did not require immediate agreement on the larger nuclear dispute.

On January 20 in Lausanne, the International Olympic Committee announced arrangements with the two Koreas for joint participation, including a march of both delegations under the Korean Unification Flag at the opening ceremony and a unified women’s ice hockey team to which twelve North Korean players would be added. Those arrangements are examined in Source Window 1. They required exemptions from normal Olympic procedures and were negotiated with international sports officials as well as with the two governments.

The opening ceremony on February 9 showed the political effect. Athletes from both states entered the stadium together beneath the white flag showing a blue peninsula, and a North Korean delegation led by Kim Yong Nam, the ceremonial head of state, and Kim Jong Un’s sister Kim Yo Jong attended. Kim Yo Jong was the first member of the ruling family to visit South Korea since the division. On February 10 Moon met the delegation and received an invitation to visit Pyongyang, delivered in a personal letter. North Korean artistic ensembles performed in South Korea, and a hockey team of combined players took the ice, losing its early matches. For the public, the games supplied images that few had thought possible a few weeks earlier.

The responses within South Korea were not uniform. Many people welcomed the reduced tension. Others, particularly younger South Koreans, objected that the unified hockey team had displaced athletes who had trained for years, treating the issue as one of fairness in a competitive society, and opinion surveys at the time showed notable skepticism about the joint team even among supporters of engagement. Conservative critics argued that the government was allowing the North to use the Olympics for propaganda and to ease the pressure of sanctions. International observers, including the United States, whose Vice President Mike Pence attended the opening ceremony, remained wary that cultural cooperation might soften the sanctions regime without producing nuclear concessions.

Did Olympic cooperation reduce hostility, or did it create an opportunity for governments to begin negotiating? The evidence supports the second answer more strongly. The games did not remove the military confrontation, and North Korea’s political goals were not altered by the ceremonies. But the joint participation created a venue in which leaders on both sides could take a political risk with limited commitment, and it produced a personal channel through the invitation delivered by Kim Yo Jong. Its practical effect was to turn a period of confrontation into one of preparation: in March South Korean envoys visited Pyongyang, reported that Kim Jong Un was willing to discuss denuclearization and to meet the American president, and carried that message to Washington.

Section 93.3

The Panmunjom Summit and the Declaration of April 27, 2018

After the Olympics, South Korean envoys Chung Eui-yong and Suh Hoon visited Pyongyang in March and returned reporting that Kim Jong Un would be willing to discuss denuclearization and meet the American president. Kim traveled to Beijing in late March, his first known trip abroad as leader, before meeting Moon. These preparatory moves show that the April summit was embedded in a wider diplomatic sequence, not a freestanding event.

The meeting on April 27 was held at Peace House, on the southern side of the Joint Security Area. The first steps across the Military Demarcation Line were broadcast live. After formal talks, Moon and Kim spent time on a footbridge, with no aides in earshot, an episode that became one of the most reproduced images of the summit. The substance of that conversation is known only from later accounts. South Korean officials later reported that Kim had expressed willingness to discuss giving up nuclear weapons if security guarantees were given, a report that illustrates how the content of private conversations enters the historical record through participants’ own statements.

The Panmunjom Declaration, analyzed in Source Window 2, committed the two sides to improve relations, to cease hostile acts including loudspeaker broadcasts and leaflets, to turn the area around the Northern Limit Line into a maritime peace zone, to hold family reunions, and to reconnect rail and road links. It stated a shared goal of a nuclear-free Korean Peninsula through complete denuclearization, and it said the two sides would work toward a declaration ending the Korean War in 2018 and would seek to convert the Armistice into a peace treaty with the participation of the United States and China.

Two features of the text are historically crucial. First, the commitment to a nuclear-free peninsula was stated as a common goal; the declaration did not define denuclearization, set a schedule, or create verification. Second, it envisioned a declaration ending the war and a peace treaty, but neither occurred. A summit is not a treaty, and an agreed objective is not an implemented obligation. These distinctions, which the Moon government’s critics and supporters both used to argue their positions, remain essential to understanding why the declaration’s significance was both genuine and limited.

Within weeks, the diplomatic structure displayed its fragility. After North Korea demonstrated the dismantling of its Punggye-ri nuclear test site in late May, in a ceremony for foreign journalists but without independent expert inspection, Trump on May 24 sent a letter cancelling the planned meeting with Kim, citing hostility in a North Korean statement. Moon and Kim met again at Panmunjom on May 26, and the Singapore summit was restored. The episode showed both the importance of the inter-Korean channel as a mediator and the speed with which the process could be disrupted.

Historical Vocabulary
Armistice, End-of-War Declaration, and Peace Regime

Definition. An armistice is a military agreement to cease fighting; it is not a treaty resolving political questions. An end-of-war declaration is a political statement that a war is over. A peace regime is a broader set of arrangements, which may include a treaty, institutions, and security guarantees.

Historical context. The 1953 Armistice called for a political conference that never settled a peace. In 2018 the Panmunjom Declaration proposed a declaration ending the war that year and a peace treaty. Debates followed over whether a declaration would be a symbolic step, a means of building trust, or a risk to the legal basis for the United Nations Command and the American military presence.

Why it matters. The sequence of an end-of-war declaration, nuclear steps, and a peace treaty became a core question in later negotiations, and was a central theme of Moon’s efforts through 2022.

Potential misunderstanding. A declaration ending the war would not by itself withdraw forces, replace the Armistice’s legal arrangements, or change the military balance. It would be a political act whose practical effect depended on what accompanied it.

Primary Source Window

Source Window 2 — The Panmunjom Declaration for Peace, Prosperity and Unification of the Korean Peninsula, April 27, 2018

Creator: Moon Jae-in and Kim Jong Un · Date: April 27, 2018 · Place: Peace House, Panmunjom · Reference: text published by the South Korean Ministry of Foreign Affairs

Context: The declaration followed the Olympic talks, the March envoy visits, and preparatory working-level meetings. It was signed in the presence of cameras and issued as a short document with three main sections: improving relations, easing military tension, and building a peace regime.

Source type: Bilateral political declaration between the leaders of two governments.

Paraphrase (not a quotation):

The two leaders affirmed the principle of Korean self-determination, agreed to improve relations including through a liaison office, to hold reunions of separated families, and to restore rail and road links. They agreed to stop hostile acts near the border, including loudspeaker broadcasts and leaflet distribution, and to convert the area near the Northern Limit Line into a peace zone. They stated a common goal of a nuclear-free peninsula through complete denuclearization, and a commitment to seek a declaration ending the war in 2018 and a peace treaty, with the United States and China.

Analysis: The document is significant for what it formally commits the two leaders to attempt. It also shows differences in vocabulary: it describes denuclearization as a goal, without specifying whose weapons and facilities, in what order, and under what inspection.

Limitations: It lacks verification, a timetable, and definitions. It also depends on third parties, notably the United States and China, who were not signatories, for the peace treaty it envisions.

Perspective: It represents the two governments’ official positions at the moment of the summit. The views of the United States and China, and of South Korean and North Korean citizens, are absent.

Section 93.4

The Singapore Summit: Trump, Kim Jong Un, and the Promise of Denuclearization, June 2018

On June 12, 2018, Donald Trump and Kim Jong Un met at the Capella Hotel on Sentosa Island in Singapore. It was the first meeting between a sitting American president and a North Korean leader. The summit was prepared in an unusual way: much of the pre-negotiation was conducted through the South Korean intelligence and security channel, through the personal channel of Secretary of State Mike Pompeo, and through direct letters, rather than through the lengthy working-level negotiation that earlier American nuclear agreements had relied on.

Moon Jae-in’s role was that of an intermediary. His envoys carried Kim’s statements to Washington in March, his May 26 meeting with Kim restored the summit after Trump’s cancellation, and his government publicly framed the Singapore meeting as a step in a larger sequence that would include the inter-Korean process. This position gave Seoul influence over the pace of events but also exposed it to the risk that disagreements between Washington and Pyongyang would rebound on its own policy.

The Joint Statement, examined in Source Window 3, had four elements. The two leaders committed to establishing new bilateral relations, to building a lasting and stable peace regime on the Korean Peninsula, to North Korea’s reaffirmation of the April 27 commitment to work toward complete denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula, and to recovering the remains of prisoners of war and those missing in action. The United States also offered security guarantees in the statement’s language.

What the document omitted was equally important. It contained no nuclear declaration, no inventory of facilities, no schedule, no definition of denuclearization, no verification procedure, and no sanctions-relief sequence. The two sides’ understandings of “denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula” differed in ways that public statements later revealed. Washington generally emphasized the dismantlement of North Korea’s nuclear weapons and programs, often with the formula “complete, verifiable, and irreversible.” North Korean statements, drawing on earlier positions, tended to link denuclearization to the removal of what Pyongyang described as American nuclear threats, including the American military presence and alliance guarantees, and to reciprocal steps. Students should therefore treat the phrase as a contested term, not an agreed meaning.

Trump also announced after the summit that he would suspend large combined exercises with South Korea, which he described as expensive and provocative. South Korean and American officials subsequently suspended and scaled back some exercises. This decision became part of the story of inter-Korean military confidence-building, and it reflected the way the American domestic and alliance agenda intersected with the peninsula’s diplomacy. The Joint Statement’s political significance was considerable, since it showed that the two governments could meet; but its operational thinness left the negotiation of substance to later stages that proved far harder.

Historical Vocabulary
Denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula

Definition. A phrase used in the 1992 inter-Korean joint declaration, the 2018 declarations, and the Singapore statement for the goal of a peninsula without nuclear weapons. Its meaning depends on who is speaking.

Historical context. For the United States and South Korea, the phrase has generally meant the elimination of North Korea’s nuclear weapons and production capacity. For North Korea, statements have linked it to ending what it calls American nuclear threats, which in its public language may include extended deterrence, military exercises, and the broader alliance relationship.

Why it matters. The ambiguity allowed leaders to sign documents without resolving the deepest disagreement. It meant that the Hanoi negotiations began with different assumptions about what would be traded for what.

Potential misunderstanding. The use of identical words in separate documents did not mean agreement on definitions, scope, sequence, or verification.

Section 93.5

The Pyongyang Summit and the September 19 Military Agreement, 2018

Moon Jae-in traveled to Pyongyang on September 18–20, 2018, accompanied by a delegation that included business leaders and cultural figures. Public ceremonies featured large crowds along the route and a stadium appearance in which Moon addressed a North Korean audience. On September 20 Moon and Kim visited Mount Paektu together, a mountain of symbolic importance in both Koreas’ historical narratives. The scenes continued the pattern of public image-making evident in April, and for many South Koreans the visit showed an unusual openness.

The Pyongyang Joint Declaration of September 19, examined in Source Window 4, repeated the earlier commitments and added new ones. It committed the two sides to expand economic exchanges, to reopen rail and road links, to resume the Kaesong complex and Mount Kumgang tourism when conditions permitted, to hold further reunions, and to pursue joint participation in international events, including a bid for the 2032 Olympics. It stated that Kim Jong Un would visit Seoul in the near future. That visit never took place.

The declaration’s nuclear provisions were conditional. North Korea said it would permanently dismantle the Dongchang-ri engine test site and launch platform with the participation of foreign experts, and said it was prepared to take additional measures, such as the permanent dismantlement of nuclear facilities at Yongbyon, if the United States took corresponding measures. The text explicitly ties the Yongbyon proposal to American reciprocity. It is not an unconditional pledge to dismantle the entire North Korean nuclear program. This conditionality became central to the Hanoi negotiations five months later.

Alongside the declaration, the defense ministers of the two Koreas signed the Comprehensive Military Agreement, the first detailed inter-Korean military accord in years. It created buffer zones along the Military Demarcation Line and at sea in which live-fire artillery drills and certain maneuvers were to cease, established no-fly zones for certain aircraft near the line, called for removal of mines and guard posts in certain areas, and planned a pilot demilitarization of the Joint Security Area. It also provided for communication mechanisms. Its content is examined in the next section.

Did September create the foundation of a lasting settlement? The military agreement was more concrete than most earlier inter-Korean accords, and it contained procedures that could be implemented without prior agreement on nuclear questions. The nuclear provisions, by contrast, depended on diplomatic progress between Pyongyang and Washington that had not yet occurred. Thus the September agreements combined a relatively self-contained military-confidence framework with a political structure dependent on a negotiation outside the two Koreas’ control.

Section 93.6

Implementing the 2018 Military Agreement: Guard Posts, the DMZ, and Confidence-Building

The Comprehensive Military Agreement was implemented in stages beginning in the autumn of 2018. In October the two sides cleared mines from part of the Joint Security Area and withdrew weapons and guard posts there, and in the weeks that followed they conducted a pilot withdrawal of guard posts from the Demilitarized Zone. Each side withdrew eleven posts from the front line, of which ten per side were dismantled and one on each side was retained for preservation. The two sides verified each other’s withdrawals, including in joint visits, an unusual degree of transparency. In November a joint survey of the Han River estuary began, a step toward making the waterway navigable for civilians.

The agreement’s zones restricted several kinds of military activity. Live-fire artillery drills and regimental-level maneuvers were to cease within a belt along the Military Demarcation Line; fixed-wing aircraft were barred from flying within specified distances of the line, with different limits for different aircraft types and regions; and the two navies were to stop live-fire and maritime maneuvers in buffer zones in the West and East seas. Its aim was to lower the risk of a clash being triggered by routine training or surveillance, rather than to alter either side’s force posture or ability to fight.

Military communication was a central part of the framework. In 2018 the two sides restored direct lines, including in the West Sea, and the agreement set out a consultation structure through a military committee that was to meet regularly. Such mechanisms are the practical core of crisis management: they enable officers to explain an ambiguous act before it becomes an incident. An important piece, however, was not completed. A joint military committee was envisioned but never established, and the disputed maritime boundary was not resolved, meaning the sea buffer zones coexisted with unaddressed differences over the line they were meant to stabilize.

The agreement had geographic and substantive limits. It applied to defined distances from the line and to specified areas of the sea; it did not reduce force levels, change deployments, or limit weapons development. Its restrictions on aerial surveillance, which South Korea considered a reasonable price for stability, were criticized by South Korean conservatives, who argued that the agreement reduced the South’s ability to monitor North Korean forces near the border and that the North gave up less than the South. Supporters answered that the zones were symmetrical in distance, and that incidents and exercises near the border were in themselves destabilizing.

Some measures were therefore implemented and others required continued negotiation. Guard-post withdrawal, mine clearance in the Joint Security Area, and altered training were implemented. Joint remains recovery in the Demilitarized Zone began in April 2019 when South Korea started excavation at Arrowhead Hill in Cheorwon after North Korea did not respond to proposals for joint work. The framework reduced immediate risks for a time, but it did not remove the underlying confrontation. As the following sections show, its effectiveness depended on political conditions that proved temporary.

Historical Vocabulary
Confidence-Building Measures

Definition. Confidence-building measures are steps taken by adversaries to reduce the risk of surprise attack, accident, or miscalculation. They include communications links, notification of exercises, restricted zones, observers, and information exchange.

Historical context. The 2018 Comprehensive Military Agreement is an example. Earlier arrangements, such as inter-Korean military talks in the 1990s and 2000s, produced similar measures that were also suspended when politics deteriorated.

Why it matters. Such measures can reduce risk even without political reconciliation or disarmament, but they depend on mutual compliance and are vulnerable when trust collapses.

Potential misunderstanding. They are not disarmament and do not change the balance of forces. Their value lies in preventing unintended escalation.

Section 93.7

Family Reunions, the Kaesong Liaison Office, Railways, and the Limits of Reconciliation

The most humanly significant achievement of the détente was the reunion of separated families at Mount Kumgang from August 20 to 26, 2018, held in two rounds after an inter-Korean Red Cross agreement of June 22. The meetings were the first since October 2015. Participants, nearly all aged in their eighties or nineties, had been separated since the Korean War, in some cases for nearly seven decades. They were chosen by lottery and health screening from a pool of applicants registered with the South Korean government since 1988, which has numbered well over one hundred thousand, the large majority of whom have since died.

The reunions were brief. Families met in a few sessions over several days, in a monitored setting, and then parted, with no assurance of seeing each other again. Many reunions involved people meeting siblings, children, and spouses they had not seen since the war or recognized only through photographs. The meetings showed both the depth of what the division had cost and the shortage of time. The reunions also exposed a structural asymmetry: South Korea has a registry of applicants who are dying, whereas North Korean participants were selected by the government and their circumstances are poorly known outside the country. Nonetheless, the reunions were an achievement of the 2018 process and are among the few that depended less on nuclear diplomacy.

The joint liaison office opened at Kaesong in September 2018 in a building that had been part of the former industrial complex. It was meant to allow officials of the two governments to communicate continuously in person, a notable advance over the telephone channels that had been repeatedly cut during earlier crises. It had a regular schedule of meetings between its co-heads and daily contact. Its significance was institutional: it created a permanent presence of each side’s officials in the territory of the other.

Plans for rail and road connections proved harder to realize. South Korean and North Korean teams conducted technical surveys of the North Korean sections of the Gyeongui and Donghae lines in the autumn of 2018, and a ceremony was held at Kaesong in December. But construction required equipment and materials that were affected by UN and U.S. sanctions, and the South Korean government needed exemptions and consultations with Washington. The United States and South Korea created a working group on sanctions coordination, which North Korea criticized as interference. The Kaesong Industrial Complex and Mount Kumgang tourism likewise remained closed. Sanctions were not the only obstacle; no political agreement made the financing, security, or legal framework clear.

The analytical point is that these four kinds of achievement were different in durability. Symbolic reconciliation, such as summit images, depended on leaders’ will. Humanitarian achievements, such as the reunions, could occur without larger changes but were small and not self-sustaining, since no regular schedule was established. Administrative institutions, such as the liaison office, could function only while both governments wished them to. Economically sustainable cooperation required a stable legal and sanctions framework, which a summit declaration could not provide. The 2018 détente produced significant results in the first three categories and almost none in the fourth.

Section 93.8

The Hanoi Summit and the Failure of Nuclear Negotiations, February 2019

After Singapore, working-level negotiations between the United States and North Korea proceeded slowly. Secretary of State Pompeo visited Pyongyang several times in 2018, and in 2019 Stephen Biegun became the special representative for North Korea. The two sides agreed to a second summit in Hanoi, Vietnam, on February 27–28, 2019. Expectations were high; many commentators expected a declaration of some form, possibly including an end-of-war declaration or an exchange of liaison offices. But the preparatory work did not resolve the central question of what each side would exchange.

The core issue was the relationship between nuclear steps and sanctions relief. North Korea proposed the dismantlement of the Yongbyon nuclear complex, which it had offered conditionally in September 2018, in return for the lifting of UN sanctions imposed from 2016 onward. The American side, according to its public accounts, wanted a broader commitment that included other facilities, including an uranium-enrichment site the United States said North Korea had not disclosed, and a more comprehensive approach to denuclearization. Reports at the time also described an American proposal for a wide-ranging agreement.

After the talks, a planned lunch and signing ceremony were cancelled and the leaders left without an agreement. At his press conference, Trump said that North Korea had asked for the lifting of sanctions in their entirety and that the United States could not agree, and that Yongbyon alone was not enough. Hours later, Foreign Minister Ri Yong Ho said at a late-night briefing that North Korea had asked only for partial relief, specifically of five UN resolutions adopted between 2016 and 2017 that affected the civilian economy and people’s livelihoods, in exchange for permanently and completely dismantling the nuclear production facilities at Yongbyon under American observation. The competing accounts are analyzed in Source Window 5.

The disagreement can be seen as a conflict between two logics. The American approach sought a broad denuclearization commitment before major sanctions relief, because the United States regarded sanctions as its principal leverage and worried that early relief would be irreversible while North Korean steps could be reversed. North Korea’s approach was phased: it offered a significant but limited step and expected a significant return, reflecting its distrust of disarming before receiving guarantees. The two approaches could in principle have been bridged by a roadmap with agreed stages, but that roadmap had not been prepared.

Hanoi became a turning point. North Korean statements after the summit grew more critical of what they described as American demands. In April, Kim Jong Un told the Supreme People’s Assembly that the United States should adopt a new approach by the end of the year. For South Korea, the failure removed the foundation for the inter-Korean plans that had assumed progress in the nuclear talks. Moon’s government, which had attempted to mediate, found that its leverage had decreased, since the main issues were bilateral between Washington and Pyongyang.

Section 93.9

The Panmunjom Meeting of June 2019 and the Failure of Working-Level Diplomacy

On June 29, 2019, while in Asia for a G20 meeting, Donald Trump posted a message inviting Kim Jong Un to meet at the Demilitarized Zone. The next day the two men met at Panmunjom, and Trump crossed the Military Demarcation Line into North Korea for a few steps, the first time a sitting American president had stood on North Korean soil. Moon Jae-in was present at the Joint Security Area but did not take part in the private talks between the two leaders. The meeting lasted roughly an hour.

The symbolism was considerable, particularly the image of an American president crossing the line. The substantive result was an agreement to resume working-level negotiations, which had stalled since Hanoi. The meeting illustrates a recurring feature of the period: highly publicized leadership encounters produced openings, but the detailed work of implementing any agreement required prepared negotiations between technical teams, which were harder to organize and more vulnerable to the original disagreements.

The working-level talks took place in Stockholm in early October 2019. North Korea’s delegation, led by Kim Myong Gil, left the talks and issued a statement saying they had broken down because the United States had not brought new proposals. The American side described the discussions as good and said it had offered creative ideas and invited further talks. The accounts differ, but neither side reported a breakthrough, and the talks did not restart in an agreed form.

Several disagreements underlay the failure. Pyongyang wanted a security guarantee and sanctions relief before further steps on denuclearization, and it argued that it had already taken steps such as the moratorium on nuclear and long-range missile testing and the dismantling of the Punggye-ri test site. Washington wanted a more complete statement of North Korea’s program and a roadmap before relief. There was no accepted implementation roadmap that specified stages, verification, and corresponding concessions. Kim Jong Un had also set a deadline of the end of 2019 for the United States to offer a new approach, and at a party meeting at the end of the year he indicated that North Korea would no longer consider itself bound by its testing moratorium.

The section demonstrates that summit diplomacy can create openings but cannot substitute for sustained institutional negotiation. The three Trump–Kim meetings showed that leaders could meet and make announcements. They did not produce the documents, staff work, and verification arrangements that earlier agreements, despite their own failures, had involved. The significance of working-level processes lies in their capacity to translate political statements into definitions, schedules, and monitoring, and the absence of that process left the leaders’ declarations unsupported when their personal diplomacy stalled.

Historical Debate

Why Did the 2018–2019 Diplomatic Opening Fail?

The question: Was the failure of the 2018–2019 opening primarily the result of structural disagreement over disarmament and sanctions, of a method of negotiation that relied on leaders without sufficient preparation, or of wider problems of trust, verification, and domestic politics? Students should also ask whether different decisions could plausibly have produced a more durable agreement.

Interpretation A — Fundamental disagreement over disarmament and sanctions. Some analysts argue that North Korea sought recognition as a nuclear state and the gradual easing of sanctions, while the United States and South Korea sought the elimination of its arsenal, and that this difference could not be bridged by better procedure. On this view the leaders’ warm rhetoric concealed an unresolved contradiction.
Interpretation B — Personalized summit diplomacy without working-level preparation. Others argue that the leaders’ meetings produced headlines but lacked the staff work needed to define terms, stage concessions, and agree on verification. On this view a prepared roadmap, with specified reciprocal steps, might have made a limited deal possible at Hanoi.
Interpretation C — Security guarantees, verification, distrust, and domestic constraints. A third group stresses the memory of earlier failed agreements, American and North Korean domestic political constraints, and the absence of verification systems and mutual trust that could support early steps. On this view, structural and procedural factors reinforced one another.

Evidence: The public texts of the declarations, the competing accounts of Hanoi, subsequent statements by Pyongyang and Washington, and memoirs and interviews by participants, which should be treated as evidence of perspective, not as neutral accounts.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and counterfactuals. Historians agree that Hanoi ended without agreement and disagree about whether a different structure of talks could have produced a different result.

Why it matters: If the failure was structural, later diplomacy would require a change in North Korea’s or America’s goals; if procedural, better-designed negotiations may in principle succeed.

Student task: Evaluate the three interpretations. Identify evidence that would help distinguish them, and explain why historians cannot test counterfactuals directly. Do not treat any interpretation as established fact.

Section 93.10

The Collapse of Engagement: Pandemic Isolation, Leaflets, and the Destruction of the Liaison Office, 2020

In January 2020, as COVID-19 spread, North Korea closed its borders to nearly all traffic, a closure that lasted for years and further limited exchange. South Korean staff at the Kaesong liaison office were withdrawn temporarily, and normal operations were suspended. The closure removed the practical basis for many exchanges that had been planned, and it intensified North Korea’s economic strain, which also resulted from sanctions and from weather-related damage in 2020.

Leaflets became the immediate dispute. Activists, many of them defectors living in South Korea, sent balloons carrying anti-North Korean leaflets across the border, in spite of the April 2018 agreement to stop propaganda activities. The South Korean government faced a dilemma between its commitment to freedom of expression and its agreement with the North. In early June 2020, Kim Yo Jong issued statements warning of consequences. On June 9 North Korea cut off all inter-Korean communication lines, and on June 13 Kim Yo Jong threatened to destroy the liaison office.

On June 16, 2020, at about 2:49 p.m., according to South Korea’s Ministry of Unification, the building at Kaesong was destroyed in an explosion that North Korean media described as a response to leaflet distribution. The building had opened less than two years earlier as an expression of the 2018 rapprochement. It was unoccupied at the time. The demolition, examined in Source Window 6, was a deliberate act against an institution created by mutual agreement.

Its significance lay in the symbolic and practical meaning of destroying a shared institution. A suspension of talks could be reversed; demolishing a building was a visible, irreversible rejection of what the building represented. South Korea’s government criticized the act, and for a short time the leadership in Pyongyang raised the prospect of military action, which Kim Jong Un later suspended. In December 2020, South Korea’s National Assembly passed an amendment to the law governing inter-Korean relations that criminalized leaflet launches, a measure criticized by human rights organizations and later, in September 2023, ruled unconstitutional by the Constitutional Court.

The stated grievance concerned leaflets, but historians distinguish the occasion from the causes. The wider context included the failure of Hanoi and Stockholm, the absence of sanctions relief, North Korea’s decision that South Korea could not deliver the economic benefits it had promised, and the economic pressures of the pandemic. In September 2020 a South Korean fisheries official who had gone missing was shot by North Korean forces, an incident that deepened distrust and about which South Korean and North Korean accounts also differed. By the end of 2020 many practical achievements of 2018 had disappeared: the liaison office, the lines of communication, and political momentum.

Part III

The Return to Confrontation and the Abandonment of Reunification, 2021–2024

The failure of renewed dialogue, changes in South Korean policy, the deterioration of military confidence-building, and North Korea’s hostile two-state doctrine.

Section 93.11

Moon Jae-in’s Final Diplomatic Efforts and the Fragility of Communication, 2021–2022

Moon’s last year and a half in office began with an unfavorable setting. At the Eighth Party Congress in January 2021 Kim Jong Un presented a new five-year plan for military development and described relations with South Korea in sharply critical terms. The American administration of Joe Biden, which took office the same month, reviewed policy and described a calibrated, practical approach that left the door open to diplomacy. At a May 2021 summit Biden and Moon reaffirmed the Singapore and Panmunjom statements. North Korea did not take up American offers of talks, and its officials said the United States must first abandon what they called hostile policy.

A modest recovery in communication occurred in the summer. On July 27, 2021, the two Koreas announced the restoration of communication lines, including military hotlines, which North Korea had cut for about thirteen months. The two governments said that Moon and Kim had been exchanging letters since April. But the restoration was fragile. North Korea stopped answering calls in August when American and South Korean exercises began, and the lines were restored again in early October after Kim Jong Un announced in a speech that he would restore them.

Moon’s main initiative in this period was an end-of-war declaration. At the UN General Assembly in September 2021 he proposed that the parties to the Korean War declare the war over, arguing that it would create momentum for denuclearization and peace. Kim Yo Jong replied that the idea was interesting and worthy of discussion, but that conditions, including an end to what Pyongyang called double standards and hostile policy, would have to be met first. Washington was cautious about whether a declaration would be accompanied by North Korean steps. The proposal did not advance.

The distinction between restoring a telephone connection and restoring trust is central. A hotline can allow officers to transmit routine messages and prevent misunderstanding, and it has real value. But it is not a negotiation, and North Korea showed it would turn the line on and off in response to political events. In January 2022 North Korea resumed missile testing on a large scale, including a long-range missile test in March, ending the moratorium announced in 2018. Moon and Kim exchanged personal letters in April 2022, shortly before Moon left office, but these did not alter the course of events.

Several constraints limited Moon’s options. Domestically, his government faced opposition from conservative parties that criticized engagement, and the National Assembly was polarized, as Chapter 95 explains. North Korea’s priorities were focused on military development and domestic control, its border was closed because of the pandemic, and its officials had stated that they would not engage while sanctions and exercises continued. The core nuclear disagreement remained unresolved. When Moon left office in May 2022 he had kept channels intermittently open, but he had not restored negotiations.

Section 93.12

Yoon Suk Yeol, Deterrence, and the Shift in South Korean Policy, 2022–2023

Yoon Suk Yeol took office on May 10, 2022, after campaigning on a stronger posture toward the North. His administration argued that the engagement policies of the preceding government had failed to restrain North Korea’s nuclear development and that deterrence and alliance strength should take priority, with dialogue offered on conditions. It restored the use of the term enemy for North Korea’s military in some official documents and increased defense planning against North Korean nuclear and missile threats.

On August 15, 2022, Yoon presented what he called an audacious initiative: if North Korea took steps toward denuclearization, South Korea would provide assistance, including in food, energy, infrastructure, and health. The proposal followed a long tradition of conditional offers of economic benefit in return for nuclear steps. Kim Yo Jong responded within days with a dismissive statement rejecting the offer and saying North Korea would not trade its nuclear weapons for economic aid.

The interpretation of the two sides’ security policies diverged. Seoul and Washington argued that expanded exercises, including field training that had been scaled back after 2018 and the 2023 Washington Declaration establishing a Nuclear Consultative Group, were necessary responses to North Korean weapons development and would strengthen deterrence. Pyongyang characterized the exercises as rehearsals for invasion and used them to justify its own tests. The South Korean president’s remarks in early 2023 that South Korea might consider its own nuclear weapons or request redeployment of American nuclear weapons, followed by official clarification that Seoul remained committed to the nonproliferation regime, contributed to a debate about the credibility of extended deterrence.

Sanctions enforcement and diplomacy at the United Nations also changed. The Security Council had been unable to adopt new sanctions after Russia and China vetoed a resolution in May 2022. The Yoon government, along with the United States and Japan, shifted to unilateral and coordinated sanctions and to a more public effort to implement existing measures. The broader trilateral relationship among the United States, South Korea, and Japan, including the Camp David summit of August 2023, belongs principally to Chapter 94.

It would be an error to treat the change of government in Seoul as the sole cause of renewed confrontation. North Korea had resumed missile testing in January 2022, before Yoon took office, and its leadership had already announced a major military buildup and the closure of diplomatic channels. Its law on nuclear forces came in September 2022. The conservative government’s policy and North Korea’s decisions reinforced each other: Seoul’s emphasis on deterrence and conditionality gave Pyongyang justification for rejection, while Pyongyang’s tests and statements gave Seoul justification for its approach. Historians continue to debate the relative weight of each, but the evidence supports an interactive explanation more than a one-sided one.

Section 93.13

Missiles, Nuclear Doctrine, and the Breakdown of Negotiated Restraint, 2022–2024

North Korea’s missile activity after 2021 was more frequent and more varied than in 2018–2019. In 2022 it conducted a record number of launches, and on November 2 of that year fired a large number of missiles in a single day, one of which landed in waters south of the Northern Limit Line, the first such landing since the division. In 2023 it tested solid-fuel long-range missiles, and in November 2023 it launched a reconnaissance satellite successfully. The technical details belong to Chapters 86 and 92; for this chapter, the key point is the way testing narrowed the space for negotiated restraint and increased the demands on crisis management.

The legal institutionalization of nuclear status was more consequential for diplomacy than any single test. In September 2022 the Supreme People’s Assembly adopted a law on nuclear forces policy that described the nuclear status as irreversible, set conditions for use including in response to an attack on the leadership, and said North Korea would not negotiate on denuclearization. In September 2023 the assembly amended the constitution to include the development of nuclear forces. These measures, announced by the state’s own media, meant that any renewed diplomacy would have to confront a stated position that disarmament was off the table, a position very different from the conditional offers of 2018.

The international response was constrained. The Security Council could not adopt new sanctions after Russian and Chinese vetoes in 2022, and in March 2024 Russia’s veto ended the mandate of the panel of experts that had monitored sanctions implementation. These developments, linked to the growth of North Korean–Russian relations explored in Chapter 94, weakened an important evidence-gathering and enforcement mechanism. South Korea and the United States responded with additional exercises, displays of strategic assets, and enhanced consultation.

Arguments over how to respond divide into several positions. Advocates of deterrence argue that credible alliance capabilities are essential to prevent attack and coercion. Advocates of pressure argue that sanctions and isolation must be maintained to prevent normalization of nuclear status. Advocates of arms control argue that, because denuclearization seems unreachable in the near term, the parties should negotiate limits and risk reduction. Advocates of complete denuclearization argue that any acceptance of nuclear status would encourage proliferation. Some South Koreans argue for an independent nuclear deterrent, and public opinion polls in the 2020s repeatedly showed majority support in South Korea for the idea, although support has varied with question wording.

For the history of diplomacy, the central implication is that earlier assumptions, that North Korea might trade its nuclear program for security and economic benefits, became harder to sustain once nuclear status was legislated and tied to state identity. This did not make talks impossible in principle, but it changed what negotiation could be about. The risk of armed confrontation also grew as testing continued and as the military confidence-building measures that might have limited miscalculation were abandoned, a process traced in the next section.

Section 93.14

The Erosion and Suspension of the September 19 Military Agreement, 2023–2024

The Comprehensive Military Agreement had survived the breakdown of nuclear diplomacy, but by 2022–2023 it was under strain. South Korean officials alleged that North Korea violated its provisions, for example through drone incursions in December 2022 and artillery fire near the maritime boundary, while North Korea accused South Korea and the United States of hostile exercises. Each side’s characterization of the other’s activities as violations or provocations weakened the basis for compliance.

The November 2023 launch of the Malligyong-1 reconnaissance satellite became the turning point. South Korea argued that the launch violated UN Security Council resolutions and that North Korea’s possession of reconnaissance capabilities made restrictions on South Korean aerial surveillance under the 2018 agreement untenable. On November 22 South Korea suspended the part of the agreement limiting reconnaissance flights near the line. The next day North Korea announced that it would no longer be bound by the agreement and would restore the military measures along the border that it had taken down in 2018.

The agreement remained partly alive until mid-2024, when balloons carrying waste, launched by North Korea, began arriving in South Korea in late May. On June 4, 2024, South Korea formally suspended the agreement in full, saying that North Korea had effectively repudiated it and that the South could not remain bound by it. In the days that followed, South Korea resumed loudspeaker broadcasts and exercises near the border that the agreement had limited.

The consequences were tangible. Border surveillance and live-fire exercises resumed in areas that had been restricted. Communication was impaired, since the military channels established in 2018 and in 2021 were no longer reliably answered. North Korean troops conducted construction and mining work along the line, and South Korean units responded with warning shots on several occasions, as the next sections show. The risk of misunderstanding, particularly where the line is not marked, grew as the framework designed to prevent it vanished.

How did a military agreement retain political significance while losing the compliance and trust required to operate? In part its significance was as a symbol of 2018 and as a point of reference in domestic debate: South Korean supporters and critics both invoked it. Its practical operation depended on both governments’ willingness to treat it as binding, and when one repudiated it and the other suspended it, no enforcement mechanism existed. The experience is an example of a confidence-building measure that was only as strong as the political relationship beneath it.

Section 93.15

Two Hostile States: North Korea’s Rejection of Reunification, 2023–2024

At a plenary meeting of the Workers’ Party Central Committee at the end of December 2023, Kim Jong Un described inter-Korean relations as no longer those of compatriots or of a single people, but of two hostile states. On January 15, 2024, in a speech to the Supreme People’s Assembly, he went further, saying that North Korea would no longer pursue reconciliation or reunification and that the concept of unification should be removed from its national vocabulary. He also said that the South was the primary foe and the main enemy and that any violation of what North Korea regards as its territory would be treated as a cause for war. The speech is examined in Source Window 7.

The announcements were accompanied by institutional changes. North Korean state media reported the abolition of the organizations that had been responsible for inter-Korean policy, including the committee for peaceful reunification, the bureau for economic cooperation with South Korea, and the administration of Mount Kumgang tourism. Satellite imagery in early 2024 indicated that the Arch of Reunification, a monument in Pyongyang celebrating the idea of Korean unification, had been demolished. References to unification were removed from signage and publications, according to reporting by South Korean and foreign media.

The decision had significance for decades of official rhetoric. North Korea’s founding leaders had presented the division as temporary and had proposed various formulas for reunification, including a confederal structure in 1980. The idea of a single Korean nation, the minjok, was central to both states’ legitimacy narratives. Kim Jong Un’s statements reversed this language and presented the North and South as separate states with different systems that had to be treated as hostile. The change affected both the foreign policy and the domestic ideological education of North Korea.

In October 2024, North Korean state media said that the country’s constitution defined the Republic of Korea as a hostile state, following amendments reportedly adopted by the Supreme People’s Assembly. An important source distinction is needed. In the sources examined for this chapter, the complete text of the amended constitution was not available for public examination. The chapter therefore relies on North Korea’s reported statements about the constitutional position, and not on an independent reading of the document, and it treats the details of the constitutional language as unconfirmed.

Was the doctrine a fundamental change or a more explicit statement of long-standing antagonism? Evidence supports both readings. North Korea’s official rhetoric had long combined calls for unification with intense hostility toward the South’s government, military exercises, and alliance with the United States, and the two states had fought a war and engaged in repeated clashes. At the same time, the formal abandonment of unification as an official goal, the abolition of institutions, and the redefinition of the South as a separate enemy state removed a rhetorical and legal framework that had structured inter-Korean relations since 1972. Some analysts have also suggested that the doctrine served domestic purposes, including reducing the influence of South Korean culture and information on North Korean society, an interpretation consistent with the information controls examined in Chapter 92, though internal motives cannot be documented from the available evidence.

The doctrine also affected South Korea. The Republic of Korea’s constitution continued to commit the state to peaceful unification, and the question of how a government committed to unification should respond to a counterpart that rejected it became an issue in South Korean politics, as the Lee government’s policy shows. The two states’ official positions, in other words, were no longer symmetrical: one defined the relationship as between two hostile states, the other retained a constitutional aspiration to unification.

Historical Vocabulary
Two Hostile States, Minjok, and Reunification

Definition. Two hostile states is the phrase North Korea’s leader used from late 2023 to describe the relationship between the DPRK and the Republic of Korea. Minjok is the Korean term for nation or ethnic nation. Reunification refers to the political union of the two states.

Historical context. Both states historically described Korea as one nation temporarily divided and claimed to represent it. The 2024 North Korean position abandoned that claim in official policy, while South Korea’s constitution continued to describe the peninsula and peaceful unification as its objective.

Why it matters. The phrase marks a rejection of the shared framework that had structured inter-Korean agreements from 1972 through 2018, including the Panmunjom Declaration’s emphasis on self-determination of the Korean nation.

Potential misunderstanding. It does not necessarily mean that North Korea has rejected the idea of Korean cultural or historical connection in all contexts, nor that unification by force has been formally adopted as policy; students should read the sources for what they actually state.

Part IV

Militarized Borders, Renewed Peace Initiatives, and the Peninsula in 2026

The physical dismantling of inter-Korean connections, the contrasting policies of the two governments, the human consequences of division, and developments to November 2026.

Section 93.16

Balloons, Loudspeakers, Border Fortifications, and the Destruction of Connections, 2024

Confrontation along the border intensified during 2024. From late May North Korea launched large numbers of balloons carrying trash and waste into South Korea, which it described as a response to leaflets sent north by South Korean activists. The balloons landed on roads, buildings, and farmland, and South Korean authorities warned residents to avoid contact. South Korea responded by suspending the 2018 military agreement in June and resuming loudspeaker broadcasts toward the North, which North Korea denounced and which both sides had ceased in 2018.

The distinction between private and government action matters. Leaflet launches were carried out by civil-society groups and defectors, and the South Korean government’s legal ability to stop them was limited after the Constitutional Court’s 2023 ruling against the leaflet law. The balloons carrying waste, by contrast, were launched by North Korea and described by its officials as retaliation. Competing accusations about drones added to the confusion: in October 2024 North Korea said that drones had been flown over Pyongyang from South Korea, and South Korea’s government said it could not confirm that the military had been involved. Claims on both sides could not be independently verified from public sources.

North Korea also expanded border defenses, with reports of walls, anti-tank barriers, and mines along the line, and on October 15, 2024, it blew up sections of the Gyeongui and Donghae roads that connected the two states. South Korea’s military fired warning shots in response and increased readiness. The roads had been symbols of the reconnection envisioned in 2000 and 2007 and in the 2018 declarations. Their destruction was a physical rejection of the plans they had represented.

The effects of confrontation fell on specific groups. Residents of border villages dealt with loudspeaker noise, balloon debris, and restrictions on movement. Military personnel faced heightened alert and the danger of mines and incidents, and South Korean civilians in frontline communities were reminded of vulnerability to artillery. In the West Sea, fishing communities near the Northern Limit Line experienced renewed restrictions and uncertainty.

Not every incident indicated that war was imminent, and a history of the period should not treat them that way. Most were symbolic, low-casualty actions. Their significance lay in cumulative effect: repeated incidents, in the absence of reliable communication and the limits of the 2018 agreement, increased the possibility that a misreading of intentions or an accident would escalate. Participants on both sides expressed limited interest in a major war, but the mechanisms for stopping a small incident from growing had been removed.

Section 93.17

Lee Jae-myung and the Return to a Policy of Peaceful Coexistence, 2025–2026

The December 2024 martial-law crisis and the removal of Yoon Suk Yeol in 2025, discussed in Chapter 95, led to an election in which Lee Jae-myung became president in June 2025. His government quickly signaled a change in approach. On June 11, 2025, South Korea suspended its military loudspeaker broadcasts toward the North, as an early measure intended to reduce tension. North Korean statements through 2025, including statements by Kim Yo Jong, indicated that Pyongyang saw no reason to engage with Seoul whatever its policy.

Lee’s approach, described by his government as peaceful coexistence, rested on several principles: respect for North Korea’s existing system, rejection of unification by force or absorption, and a commitment to reduce hostile activity and build trust step by step. It avoided short-term demands for denuclearization as a precondition for engagement, while keeping denuclearization as a longer-term goal and maintaining the alliance with the United States.

On February 3, 2026, the Ministry of Unification published the government’s Policy for Peaceful Coexistence on the Korean Peninsula, followed by a work plan in August 2026. The documents, examined in Source Window 8, set out objectives for military confidence-building, restoration of communication channels, humanitarian engagement, and the eventual transformation of the Armistice into a durable peace arrangement. They are statements of South Korean policy. They are not agreements with North Korea.

The distinction between policy proposals and bilateral agreements is essential. A government can announce goals, suspend its own activities, and offer talks, but cooperation requires a partner. By the end of this chapter’s period, North Korea had not accepted the proposals, and its hostile-state doctrine continued. The defining question for the Lee policy is whether unilateral tension-reduction measures can rebuild the conditions for bilateral cooperation when the other government remains unwilling to engage.

The policy also raised questions in South Korean politics. Critics, mainly on the conservative side, argued that unilateral measures such as suspending broadcasts risked giving up leverage without reciprocity and might weaken deterrence. Supporters argued that reducing friction, especially along the border, was valuable in itself and that the previous government’s hard line had not produced results. The disagreement mirrors the earlier debate between engagement and deterrence, now conducted with a counterpart that had formally abandoned reunification.

Historical Debate

Can Peaceful Coexistence Replace Reunification as the Immediate Framework for Inter-Korean Relations?

The question: North Korea defines the relationship as one between two hostile states. South Korea’s constitution still commits it to peaceful unification, while the Lee government seeks peaceful coexistence between two systems with eventual unification as a long-term aspiration. Can coexistence serve as the immediate framework, and what is gained or lost?

Interpretation A — Coexistence is a realistic and necessary step. Supporters argue that recognizing the other state’s existence and system, reducing hostile acts, and building communication reduces risk and may restore conditions for later cooperation. They note that the two states have been members of the United Nations since 1991 and that stable coexistence has precedents in other divided or hostile relationships.
Interpretation B — Coexistence risks abandoning the unification goal and leverage. Critics argue that accepting a two-state relationship weakens South Korea’s constitutional commitment, undermines support for North Koreans’ human rights and access to information, and rewards a hostile doctrine. They also note that coexistence requires reciprocity that has not appeared.
Interpretation C — The question mixes distinct issues. Others argue that legal recognition, diplomatic engagement, deterrence, humanitarian cooperation, and long-term unification are separable. A government can pursue deterrence and humanitarian contact while keeping unification as a distant aspiration without treating them as alternatives.

Evidence: The text of the South Korean constitution, the 2026 policy documents, North Korean statements of 2023–2024, and public opinion surveys on attitudes toward unification and the North.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and policy preference, not facts alone. Neither debate side should be treated as established history.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how South Korea approaches security, humanitarian policy, and diplomacy, and how historians describe the legacy of the 2018–2026 period.

Student task: Distinguish legal recognition, diplomatic engagement, deterrence, humanitarian cooperation, and unification in your analysis, and explain which interpretation the available evidence best supports. Do not treat a policy preference as a historical fact.

Section 93.18

Families, Border Communities, Humanitarian Issues, and the Social Cost of Division

The collapse of engagement fell hardest on people with the least power over it. After the August 2018 reunions, no further reunion program was held, and the registry of separated family members continued to shrink as applicants died. South Korean government figures in recent years have reported that the large majority of registered applicants are deceased and that most survivors are in their eighties or nineties. Without a program, the possibility of a meeting, even a brief one, disappears for many each year.

Other forms of contact were also lost. Letters, video meetings, and the exchange of information about the survival of relatives were not established as a regular system, and after 2020 the communication lines were largely unavailable. Families sometimes used private brokers and phone connections across the Chinese border, at risk to relatives in the North, but those channels were affected by the border closure and tightened North Korean surveillance. Separated families therefore had fewer options at the end of the period than in 2018.

Residents near the Demilitarized Zone and the Northern Limit Line experience the border as an everyday restriction. Civilian access to land near the line, farming, fishing, and tourism are regulated by military requirements, which tightened or loosened with political conditions. The 2018 agreement had brought hopes of expanded fishing areas and of tourism and transport connections. Their absence, along with loudspeaker noise, balloon debris, and the danger of mines, meant that the costs of confrontation fell on specific communities.

South Korean civil-society organizations, humanitarian agencies, and North Korean escapees expressed different priorities. Humanitarian groups argued for access to deliver aid and to maintain contact regardless of politics. Defector groups argued that information flows into North Korea, including leaflets, were a form of free expression and a way to inform North Koreans. Some residents of border areas objected to leaflet launches because of North Korean retaliation. Security restrictions and humanitarian access were repeatedly in conflict, as when border closures prevented aid or when the government restricted civil-society contacts.

Public attitudes toward reunification have changed. Surveys by South Korean research institutions, including the Korea Institute for National Unification and the Seoul National University Institute for Peace and Unification Studies, have reported declining support for unification among younger respondents and growing concern about its economic costs, while many respondents continue to support reducing tensions. Students should consult the specific survey, wording, and year, and not rely on a general claim. The decline in emphasis on unification in South Korean society, alongside North Korea’s own abandonment of it, is a significant social change that Chapter 96 will consider in a larger synthesis.

Section 93.19

The Military Demarcation Line and the Danger of Accidental Conflict, 2026

By 2026 the border remained fragile. South Korean military officials reported North Korean soldiers crossing the Military Demarcation Line during construction and mine-laying work, resulting in warning shots on several occasions. The line is marked by markers in many places that are old, weathered, or absent, and the two sides’ maps do not always agree, a condition that makes inadvertent crossings possible. North Korea’s fortification work, including fencing, walls, and mining, compounded this uncertainty.

On September 21, 2026, landmine explosions injured three South Korean soldiers. South Korean authorities attributed the mines to North Korean forces operating south of the Military Demarcation Line; North Korea denied responsibility. These two positions should be attributed to their sources and not treated as an uncontested determination. The incident illustrates the particular danger of a boundary that is physically ambiguous and heavily mined.

On October 23, South Korea formally proposed military talks to clarify the boundary, restore military communication channels, and establish measures to prevent accidental clashes. The proposal was an offer, not a negotiation. By November 2026, direct communication remained severely impaired, and there was no confirmed restoration of sustained inter-Korean talks.

Three boundaries should be kept distinct. The Military Demarcation Line is the ceasefire line of the 1953 Armistice, marked by posts along its length. The Demilitarized Zone is the buffer extending two kilometers on each side of that line. The Northern Limit Line is a maritime boundary in the West Sea drawn by the United Nations Command after the Armistice and not accepted by North Korea, which has proposed its own line. They have different legal origins, different geographic characters, and different histories of incidents.

The central analytical point is that crisis-management mechanisms matter even when reconciliation and disarmament are distant. Hotlines, notification procedures, a shared understanding of the line, and a forum for incidents do not resolve political disputes. But they reduce the chance that a mine, a patrol, or a warning shot will become a larger conflict. The loss of those mechanisms through the unwinding of the 2018 agreement meant that the border in 2026 depended on restraint and chance more than on institutions.

Section 93.20

The Korean Peninsula in November 2026: Why Détente Failed and What Peace Would Require

The history of inter-Korean diplomacy from 2016 to November 2026 shows both the speed with which an opening can occur and the speed with which it can close. In 2018 the peninsula moved from the nuclear confrontation of 2017 to summits, family reunions, a liaison office, reopened communication, military de-escalation, and declarations of shared objectives. These were not trivial, and they included real reductions in risk for a time.

They were followed by the failure of nuclear negotiations at Hanoi and Stockholm, the loss of the liaison office, the deterioration of the military agreement, border confrontations, and North Korea’s rejection of reunification as an objective. Evaluating the relative importance of causes requires attention to several factors: leadership decisions, security disagreements, sanctions, domestic political changes in South Korea and the United States, institutional weaknesses, and the lack of sustained implementation. The evidence does not allow a single ranking, but the pattern suggests that the absence of agreed definitions and sequencing for denuclearization and sanctions relief was the structural weakness that other factors exploited.

South Korea’s renewed peaceful-coexistence policy shows both the continuing appeal of engagement and its limits. A government can reduce hostile acts and propose talks, but if the other government does not reciprocate, it cannot by itself restore cooperation. At the same time, the proposal for military talks reflects a reasonable judgment that risk-reduction measures are worthwhile even without political reconciliation.

The chapter’s conclusion is that the absence of an active war is not the same as a settled peace. The 1953 Armistice remained the foundation of the military ceasefire in November 2026, and no comprehensive peace treaty had replaced it. Sustained diplomacy requires institutions, communication, verification, and credible reciprocal commitments, not simply meetings between leaders.

Nothing in this history shows that diplomacy is futile. The 2018 summits demonstrated that leaders could negotiate. What they did not do was establish the machinery to carry agreements through changes in politics, so that when conditions changed the commitments disappeared with them. Future developments after November 2026 lie beyond this chapter and belong to a later edition.

Everyday Life and Social History

Separated Families and Border Communities

Diplomatic history can seem remote from daily life, but the collapse of engagement had direct effects. For separated families, the 2018 reunions were a few days of supervised meetings after many decades. Many participants had no photographs of relatives other than those they carried from the war years, and many had children and grandchildren in the other state they had never met. When the reunion program stopped, the registry of applicants continued to shrink year by year.

For residents of frontier villages, the border is a work environment. Farmers near the Demilitarized Zone coordinate their planting with military regulations; fishers near the Northern Limit Line plan around naval patrols and restricted areas; and tourism depends on political conditions. Loudspeaker broadcasts, balloon debris, and warning shots are experienced as daily disturbances. Historians must read official documents and press accounts for indirect evidence about these groups, supplementing them with surveys, interviews, and oral histories, since many of those affected, especially in North Korea, left few published accounts.

Cause and Effect

Why the 2018 Opening Failed to Become Sustained Détente

Long-term causes

Decades of mutual distrust, the unresolved Korean War armistice, the failed agreements of 1994, 2005, and 2012, and a North Korean state that had made nuclear weapons central to its security identity.

Short-term causes

The absence of agreed definitions of denuclearization, a schedule, verification, and a sanctions-relief sequence; limited working-level preparation; and the dependence of inter-Korean projects on sanctions policy.

Immediate trigger or context

The failure of the Hanoi summit in February 2019, followed by the stalled Stockholm talks and the pandemic closure of North Korea’s borders.

Event or transformation

The loss of the liaison office in 2020, the gradual suspension of the military agreement in 2023–2024, and North Korea’s hostile-state doctrine.

Immediate effects

Cessation of family reunions and exchanges; return of loudspeakers, balloons, and border fortification; intermittent loss of military communication.

Long-term consequences

A militarized border without functioning crisis-management mechanisms and with a counterpart that rejects reunification, leaving South Korea to pursue coexistence unilaterally.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the failure. Structural disagreement, procedure, domestic politics in several states, the pandemic, and choices made in Pyongyang, Seoul, and Washington interacted, and historians disagree about their relative weight, as Historical Debate 1 shows.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the movement of the Korean Peninsula from the nuclear and military confrontation of 2016–2017 to the diplomacy of 2018, and from that diplomacy to a militarized separation in which North Korea formally rejected reunification. Its central argument is that the declarations of 2018 were genuine breakthroughs in tone and in some practical respects, but that they did not establish the definitions, sequencing, verification, and implementation mechanisms needed to survive political change.

The crisis of 2016–2017, with its nuclear tests, long-range missile launches, expanded UN sanctions, and mutual threats between Washington and Pyongyang, formed the immediate background. Seoul’s position was uniquely exposed, and Moon Jae-in sought to prevent war while remaining within the alliance. The speed with which diplomacy followed the crisis was remarkable, but it built on earlier efforts, including the 1972 statement, the 1991–1992 agreements, the Sunshine Policy summits, and the failed nuclear accords.

The PyeongChang Olympics provided a low-commitment occasion for engagement. Joint participation, the unified hockey team, and Kim Yo Jong’s visit and invitation turned a sporting event into a diplomatic opening, although the evidence suggests the games created an opportunity rather than reducing hostility directly.

The Panmunjom Declaration of April 27 committed the leaders to improved relations, reduced military tension, and a common goal of a nuclear-free peninsula and a peace regime. It also illustrated the difference between an agreed objective and an implemented obligation: it set no verification process and no treaty followed. The Singapore Joint Statement made four commitments and omitted a nuclear declaration, definition of denuclearization, schedule, verification, and sanctions-relief sequence. The two sides’ understandings of denuclearization differed.

The September 2018 Pyongyang summit added a Comprehensive Military Agreement with specific zones and procedures, and a conditional offer concerning Yongbyon. Implementation in late 2018 included guard-post withdrawal, mine clearance, and the Joint Security Area measures. Humanitarian and institutional achievements included the August family reunions and the opening of the liaison office. Economic cooperation, however, depended on sanctions and remained unrealized.

The Hanoi summit in February 2019 was the principal failure. The United States and North Korea disagreed over the scope of nuclear steps and the sanctions relief to be given, and the competing accounts of Trump and Ri Yong Ho show negotiating positions, not a complete reconstruction. The Panmunjom meeting of June 2019 provided a symbolic restart, and the Stockholm talks of October 2019 showed the absence of a prepared roadmap.

In 2020 the pandemic closed North Korea’s borders, the leaflet dispute escalated, communication lines were cut, and the liaison office was demolished on June 16. The destruction of a shared institution carried greater political meaning than suspension of talks, and it effectively ended most of the practical results of 2018 apart from the formally continuing military agreement.

From 2021 to 2024 the relationship deteriorated further. Moon’s late efforts, including the temporary restoration of communication lines and the end-of-war proposal, did not restore negotiations. Yoon’s government emphasized deterrence and conditional aid; North Korea rejected the offers, legislated and constitutionalized its nuclear status, and tested missiles at a high rate. The military agreement was partly suspended in November 2023, repudiated by Pyongyang, and fully suspended by Seoul in June 2024. Neither government’s actions alone explain the outcome.

North Korea’s declaration of two hostile states, its abolition of institutions for inter-Korean affairs, and its reported constitutional definition of the Republic of Korea as hostile ended decades of official reunification rhetoric. Whether this was a fundamental transformation or a more explicit statement of long-standing antagonism remains debated, and the full amended constitutional text was not available for examination. In 2024 balloons, loudspeakers, border fortification, and the destruction of road links brought tension to a high level.

Under Lee Jae-myung, South Korea adopted a peaceful-coexistence policy, suspended loudspeakers, published its policy in February 2026, and proposed military talks in October 2026 after a landmine incident whose attribution the two governments disputed. By November 2026 there was no sustained bilateral negotiation and no peace treaty. The chapter concludes that the absence of war is not a settled peace, and that diplomacy requires institutions, communication, verification, and credible reciprocal commitments.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how quickly the possibility of a different peninsula appeared and how quickly it narrowed. Within roughly eighteen months, leaders who had exchanged threats met, signed declarations, and reopened channels. Within roughly six years, many of those achievements had been destroyed, abandoned, or reversed.

It reveals a recurring pattern in the longer history of inter-Korean relations traced in this textbook, from the 1972 statement through the Sunshine Policy: agreements dependent on political will have proved more vulnerable than those with institutions, verification, and reciprocal interests. The Armistice, an imperfect arrangement, has endured for more than seventy years, partly because it included mechanisms, however limited.

The chapter also shaped what came after. North Korea’s rejection of reunification, South Korea’s peaceful-coexistence policy, and the dangers along the line are the conditions in which Chapter 94 will examine the regional powers, Chapter 95 South Korea’s democratic crisis, and Chapter 96 the divided peninsula in 2026. Whether coexistence, deterrence, or renewed negotiation will define the future lies beyond the chapter’s endpoint.

Finally, the chapter shows why history of diplomacy must include people far from the negotiating table: elderly relatives waiting for a meeting that did not come, soldiers on a poorly marked line, and villagers who live with the border. Their experiences are a reminder that peace is not only a diplomatic achievement but a daily condition.
