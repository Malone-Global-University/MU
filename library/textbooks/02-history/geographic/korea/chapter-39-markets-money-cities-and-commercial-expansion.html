Markets, Money, Cities, and Commercial Expansion

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Every fifth day, in towns across the Joseon countryside, farmers, weavers, peddlers, and brokers converged on open ground to trade grain, cloth, tools, and salted fish. The scene was not new in the eighteenth century. What was new was how many such markets existed, how closely they were connected, and how much of Joseon life now passed through them.

Chapter 38 examined what Korean scholars thought and proposed about the economy. This chapter asks how the economy actually worked and changed between roughly 1600 and 1800, with selective glances into the early nineteenth century. Its central question is how Joseon developed more extensive markets, commercial networks, specialized production, and monetary exchange, and how these changes affected different regions and social groups.

The chapter follows goods through the economy. It begins with production: recovery after the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions, agricultural improvement, and handicrafts. It then turns to exchange: government procurement, periodic markets, merchants, transport, and coinage. Next it examines institutions and cities, especially Seoul and regional trading centers, and finally the consequences: wealth, inequality, work, and the limits of change.

Historians rely on mixed evidence: household and land registers compiled for taxation, court records such as the Veritable Records (Annals) of successive kings, government procurement and tax regulations, private account books and family papers, and travel and scholarly writing. Most were produced by officials or elites, so ordinary traders, tenants, and women appear mainly indirectly. Commercial growth also must not be equated with modern capitalism; Joseon remained an agrarian, hierarchical, state-regulated society.

The chapter's evidence is varied and uneven. Tax and procurement regulations show the intentions of the state; court records show debates among officials; gazetteers and institutional histories identify markets, ports, and merchant groups; and modern studies of trade with Japan draw on the records of the Tsushima domain. Absent are the account books and letters of most farmers, peddlers, and workers, so ordinary experience must often be inferred.

A second aim is to keep two ideas apart. Commercial growth means more production for exchange, more markets, merchants, and money. Capitalism, by contrast, is a particular organization of capital and labor whose presence in Joseon is debated. The chapter treats the first as documented and the second as a question to be tested against evidence, not assumed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why demographic and agricultural recovery after the wars underlay commercial growth, and why population registers are imperfect evidence.
  2. Describe major agricultural innovations and distinguish their existence from their wide adoption.
  3. Analyze how the Daedong Law and government procurement stimulated private commerce.
  4. Trace the expansion of periodic markets and merchant networks.
  5. Assess the introduction and uneven spread of coin currency after 1678.
  6. Evaluate Seoul's growth and the conflict over licensed-merchant privileges culminating in 1791.
  7. Compare how tenants, merchants, officials, and artisans experienced commercialization.
  8. Interpret competing views of whether late Joseon underwent fundamental economic transformation.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a farmer's harvest reach a marketplace, and how did goods then move toward Seoul?
  2. In what ways did state policy create, rather than merely restrain, commercial opportunity?
  3. Why did copper coins become useful, and why did older means of payment persist?
  4. Who gained and who lost as exchange expanded?
  5. Why could a commercializing economy coexist with an agrarian and status-based order?
  6. What evidence supports claims of economic growth, and what are its limits?

Historical Context

The Imjin War (1592–1598) and the Manchu invasions of 1627 and 1636 (Chapters 31–36) devastated farmland, disrupted registers, and strained state finances. Joseon's official economic ideal remained agriculture-centered: the state taxed land and household labor, regarded commerce with suspicion, and licensed a limited number of Seoul merchants. Yet rural markets had already appeared in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century story is one of expansion and interconnection rather than invention.

Geography shaped exchange. Rice-producing southern and southwestern plains, the cotton, ginseng, and mining districts, the northwestern border with Qing China, and the southeastern gateway to Japan were linked by rivers, coastal shipping, and roads to Seoul. Mountains made overland transport costly; boats were generally cheaper for bulk goods.

The state's fiscal structure also shaped the setting. Joseon taxed land, collected tribute and labor service from households, and relied on registers that recorded people as taxpayers. Reforms of this system in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, discussed in Sections 39.4 and 39.11, did not remove that agrarian foundation but changed how the state obtained goods and how merchants related to it. The social hierarchy of yangban, commoners, and the enslaved (examined in Chapter 42) framed who could own land, hold office, or trade.

Section 39.1

Recovery, Population, and the Changing Economy

The Imjin War (1592–1598) and the Manchu invasions of 1627 and 1636 left much of the countryside damaged. Fields went uncultivated, villages emptied through death, flight, and capture, and tax registers lost touch with the people they were meant to record. Recovery was therefore partly administrative: the state needed to learn again who lived where and who farmed what. Two kinds of evidence anchor the story. Household registers (hojeok), compiled periodically, listed households and members for taxation and service; land surveys, notably those of 1634 and 1719–1720, each covering the three southern provinces of Chungcheong, Jeolla, and Gyeongsang, measured cultivated land for the land tax.

Neither source is a census in the modern sense. Registers recorded people as taxpayers and service-liable subjects, so households had reasons to under-report, and some groups, such as enslaved people and the very poor, were recorded unevenly or by categories reflecting status rather than demography. Land surveys likewise reveal what officials could measure and tax, not all land in use. For this reason historians treat reported totals as indicators of administrative reach as much as of numbers, and this chapter does not rely on precise population estimates. Rising registered figures could mean real growth, better registration, or both, and scholars weigh the two with caution.

The broad direction is nonetheless clear from several kinds of evidence: reclamation of abandoned fields, clearing of new upland plots, embankment of coastal tidal flats on the western coast, resettlement of displaced families, and growing activity in local markets all point to recovery in the later seventeenth century and expansion in the eighteenth. Pace and character varied. The southern and southwestern plains, with the most productive rice land, recovered and grew differently from the mountainous east and the north, where agriculture was more constrained and population movement into uplands, including slash-and-burn fields, was significant.

Recovery was repeatedly interrupted. Severe famines struck in 1670–1671 and 1695–1696, and epidemic disease commonly accompanied famine, weakening households already short of food. Such crises killed many and drove survivors from their land; they also fell hardest on poor tenants and smallholders, who had least reserve, while wealthier households could buy land cheaply from the distressed. The recovery was thus not a smooth rise but a cycle of growth and setback with unequal consequences.

Why does this matter for commerce? A larger, more settled rural population produced more grain and cloth, paid more taxes, and needed more goods it could not make itself, such as salt, iron implements, and fish. Recovery also strengthened state finances, enabling the fiscal reforms discussed in Section 39.4. Demographic and agricultural change did not by itself create markets, and, as Section 39.5 shows, markets already existed, but it supplied conditions in which exchange could expand.

Section 39.2

Agricultural Productivity and Rural Innovation

Agricultural improvement rested on water. Farmers and local communities built and repaired reservoirs (jeeon), embankments, and small weirs to store and divert water, and the state encouraged such works, since irrigated paddies yielded more reliably and supported tax collection. Irrigation required labor and coordination: a reservoir served many households, and disputes over water rights, maintenance duties, and who contributed labor could be as important as the engineering. Wealthier landowners could often secure better-watered land, while smallholders depended on shared systems and tenants on their landlords' arrangements.

The most discussed innovation is rice transplanting (iangbeop), in which seedlings are raised in a nursery bed and moved to the paddy. It saved labor on weeding and allowed the field to be used for another crop during the early season, but it carried risk: if rains failed at transplanting time, seedlings could be lost. Officials had earlier discouraged it for that reason. The technique was known before the seventeenth century, and the historical change lay in its wider adoption, especially in regions with dependable irrigation. Where water was uncertain, farmers continued direct sowing, so regional variation was large.

Transplanting made double-cropping more practical in suitable areas, with barley or wheat grown over winter and rice in summer. This raised total output and helped households through the lean spring months, though it also demanded more labor and manure. Fertilizer came from human and animal waste, household ash, straw, and green manure; fuel, fodder, and manure competed for the same biomass, and access to grass and woodland affected how much a household could fertilize. Improved iron tools and draft animals aided plowing, but cattle were costly, and many poor households relied on hand tools or borrowed or rented animals.

Knowledge circulated through experience and, increasingly, writing. Agricultural manuals compiled in Joseon, including works of the early eighteenth century, record regional practices and recommend methods, but historians use them as evidence of what writers thought worth recording, not necessarily of what most farmers did. Environmental limits remained decisive: rainfall variability, droughts, floods, soil quality, and short growing seasons in the north constrained what any technique could achieve.

Gains were therefore uneven. Landowners with water and labor could increase surplus and, by selling it, gain from rising demand. Smallholders could benefit but remained exposed to bad harvests and debt. Tenants, who paid a large share of the crop as rent, often saw improvements captured partly by landlords, though a tenant with secure terms could keep part of extra output. The distinction between knowledge and adoption, and between who owned land and who farmed it, is essential to judging what agricultural change meant.

Section 39.3

Agricultural Specialization and Production for Markets

Surplus beyond the household's needs and obligations could be sold, and many farmers grew crops with market demand. Cotton was central: cotton cloth was widely used for clothing and, in many places, as a medium of payment, so cotton cultivation and weaving linked farm households to markets and tax obligations alike. Tobacco, introduced to Korea in the early seventeenth century, spread quickly and became a significant cash crop. Vegetables grown near towns, notably around Seoul, supplied urban demand, and ginseng, long traded as a valuable commodity at home and abroad, was increasingly cultivated rather than only gathered from the later eighteenth century; larger-scale commercial production around Kaesong belongs mainly to the nineteenth century.

Production for markets varied by region. Areas close to cities, river ports, or major markets could specialize more readily than remote mountain districts, where transport costs made sale of bulky goods unprofitable. Specialization also depended on household decisions about land and labor: planting cotton or tobacco meant planting less grain, which was a risk if prices fell or harvests failed, so many households combined subsistence grain with a cash crop rather than abandoning one for the other.

Women's labor was central to household manufacturing. Spinning, weaving, and sewing produced cloth for use, tax payment, and sale, and women's work was often essential to a household's cash income, though it is poorly recorded because most surviving sources were written by officials and men. Chapter 42 considers gender and household roles more broadly.

Land tenure shaped who profited. Landlords often rented land to tenants for a share of the crop or a fixed amount, and terms varied by place and period. Tenants paid rent in kind and might also owe labor or obligations, leaving less to sell. Debt compounded the disadvantage: households short of grain borrowed against the next harvest, sometimes at high interest, and defaulting could lead to loss of land or entry into dependent labor. By contrast, landowning farmers and some prosperous tenants could invest in cash crops, buy land, or lend. Commercial crops therefore did not simply raise living standards; they could widen differences within villages.

The balance between subsistence and market was thus a question of position. For many households, selling a crop or cloth was a way to meet tax, rent, and debt obligations, not an exercise of entrepreneurial choice; for a smaller group, it was a source of accumulation. Evidence consists largely of government records, land documents, and elite commentary, so it shows broad patterns and prominent regions better than the finances of individual poor households.

Section 39.4

The Daedong Law, Government Procurement, and Commercial Growth

Before the reform, localities owed tribute (gongnap) in assorted local products, which central offices received through intermediaries. Complaints centered on abuse: agents could substitute payments at inflated prices, demand goods a district did not produce, and shift costs onto poorer households. After the wartime devastation, such burdens fell on a depleted peasantry. Reformers, notably the official Yi Won-ik, argued for replacing product tribute with a levy on land, which would tie burdens to landholding, reduce the room for intermediaries, and simplify collection.

In 1608 an experiment called the Seonhye system was introduced in Gyeonggi province, collecting a fixed amount of rice per unit of land in place of tribute. It was a predecessor, not the finished Daedong Law. Opposition from officials and interests that profited from the old system slowed extension, and adoption proceeded in stages over about a century, province by province, so that policy was uneven across the kingdom. Provincial terms differed: rates, timing, and forms of payment varied, and in many places rice, cloth, or money could be accepted depending on locality, so there was never one uniform rice tax.

Administration centered on the Seonhyecheong, which collected the levy and handled purchasing. The state then bought what it needed from licensed suppliers (gongin), paying them from Daedong revenue. This created steady public demand: instead of receiving goods as tribute, offices purchased through the market, so supplies had to be drawn from producers and merchants. Some gongin grew wealthy, though their position combined privilege with obligation, and their relationship with unlicensed traders became contested, as Section 39.11 shows.

For taxpayers the effects were mixed. The levy tied burdens to land and curbed some abuses, which benefited many landowning households; yet other levies persisted, and the landless and tenants, who paid rent to landlords who in turn owed the tax, benefited less directly. For merchants, public purchasing created opportunity, especially for those near the capital and major supply routes.

Historians distinguish three levels: policy design, as stated in proposals and regulations; implementation, which varied by region and over time; and surviving evidence, which comes mostly from fiscal regulations and court debates and so reveals intent better than local practice. The reform's commercial effects are therefore inferred from its design, from the growth of suppliers and markets, and from records of procurement, not from direct measurement of sales.

Historical Vocabulary
Daedongbeop and Gongin

Definition: The Daedong Law ("Great Uniformity Law") converted local-product tribute into a land-based levy, developing from the 1608 Seonhye experiment in Gyeonggi and accepting rice, cloth, or money by locality; gongin were licensed purveyors who supplied government offices. Why it matters: State purchasing channelled public revenue into private markets. Misunderstanding: It did not abolish all service or tribute obligations, and it was not adopted everywhere at once.

Section 39.5

Jangsi: Periodic Markets and Rural Exchange

Periodic markets (jangsi) were not a seventeenth-century invention. Records place their emergence in the late fifteenth century, first notably in Jeolla province, where records from the 1470s describe markets around Muan and Naju and famine-era trading and officials worried that it drew farmers from the land. Markets spread through the sixteenth century, and the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries saw their multiplication, closer interconnection, and growing role in long-distance trade.

Market schedules evolved. Early markets sometimes met only about twice a month, and five-day schedules developed later as markets multiplied. Neighboring markets coordinated their dates so that they did not coincide, which let peddlers move through a circuit and producers choose among several markets in a week. Historical references estimate approximately 1,000 markets by the later eighteenth century; this is an estimate, not a census, and it shows the scale of expansion from the few markets of the fifteenth century.

Markets formed a hierarchy. Small local markets served nearby households; county-seat markets drew wider custom; and a few large regional markets, such as those of Daegu, Songpa, and Gangyeong, linked producing districts with ports, rivers, and the capital. Regional differences were considerable: the dense, productive south supported more and larger markets, while mountainous districts had fewer, less frequent ones, and some northern and eastern communities depended heavily on peddlers.

The state's attitude shifted from suspicion toward practical tolerance. Authorities sometimes tried to restrict markets, and local magistrates supervised them and could tax or regulate trade, but the persistence of markets and their usefulness for supply made suppression impractical. Evidence for markets comes from official records, gazetteers, and scholarly commentary, which show their existence and location better than the daily lives of the many ordinary traders who used them.

Markets did more than exchange goods. They transmitted news and price information between districts, and they were places where communities gathered and communal cultural activities took place. A household preparing cloth or grain for market day therefore engaged with a social institution as well as a commercial one, and market towns became points through which villages learned about prices, officials, and events elsewhere.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Rural Household and the Five-Day Market

This is a composite built from the institutions described in this chapter, not a record of any actual family. A household of farmers might prepare for market day by finishing woven cloth and bundling surplus grain or vegetables, timing the trip to one of several markets within reach. At the market the household sold for coin or goods, bargained with peddlers who carried goods onward, and bought salt, fish, and iron tools.

Obligations shaped these choices. A landowner owed the land levy, collected under the Daedong system in rice, cloth, or coin depending on locality, while a tenant owed rent in kind to the landlord, leaving less to sell. A household short of grain might borrow against the next harvest, and a coin shortage or a price fall could make repayment harder, depending on how the debt was denominated. Women's weaving often supplied cloth for sale, and enslaved and hired labor worked on larger holdings. Evidence is indirect, drawn from regulations, registers, and elite writing.

Section 39.6

Peddlers, Brokers, and Merchant Networks

Several kinds of traders operated. Itinerant peddlers carried goods between markets, some as burden-carriers who traveled with loads on their backs; private merchants (sasang) bought and sold on their own account; and wholesale merchants distributed larger quantities. Regional merchant groups gained reputations: Kaesong merchants (Songsang) were known for extensive domestic and foreign networks, Han River merchants (Gyeonggang) for river trade supplying Seoul, and merchants of Uiju and Dongnae for trade with China and Japan.

Brokers were central. Gaekju and yeogak lodged traveling merchants, stored their goods, sold on commission, and arranged introductions, and in doing so provided credit and commercial information. Because few traders could judge distant prices or trustworthy partners, brokers who knew local conditions became powerful intermediaries, shaping terms of exchange and, where they dominated a port or market, control of distribution. Credit often rested on personal trust and advances of goods or money; Kaesong merchants are associated with a distinctive double-entry method known as sagae chibubeop (Songdo bookkeeping), which recorded transactions and accounts in a systematic way; surviving evidence does not establish an exact date for its invention.

Competition took several forms. Private traders competed with each other, with licensed Seoul shops that held privileges, and with local brokers who sought to control access, a conflict that came to a head in 1791 (Section 39.11). Organization also changed over time. Peddling and loose groupings of burden-carriers had earlier precedents, but efforts at national organization of peddler associations became prominent only in 1879, followed by a government-supervised institution in 1883. The formal structures of the nineteenth century should therefore not be projected back onto eighteenth-century trading.

Merchant institutions divided the work of distribution. Brokers handled wholesale distribution, storage, consignment sales, transport, and financial services such as advances, and received fees or commissions in return. Gaekju and yeogak combined lodging with these functions, while peddlers carried goods the last distance to villages. Kaesong merchants are documented as employing branch agents, bookkeeping systems, and credit arrangements. Much of the fullest documentation of such practices comes from later periods, so their eighteenth-century form should be inferred cautiously. Risk lay in price falls, damage in transit, and default; arrangements of trust and commission let traders operate with limited capital but concentrated influence in brokers and large merchants.

Kaesong merchants also built institutions of training and credit. Boy apprentices (sahwan) learned bookkeeping and trading, and around age twenty a trained agent (chain) could go to another region and trade independently on funds lent by a master, in a kind of partnership. Credit instruments, bills of exchange called hwan and eoeum, became common in large cities such as Seoul, Pyeongyang, and Kaesong by the late eighteenth century, and Kaesong had a system of unsecured, trust-based lending (sibyeonje), thought to date from about the seventeenth century. The interest rates reported for it come from later records, so they are not used here as eighteenth-century evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Jangsi, Bobusang, and Gaekju

Jangsi are periodic markets; bobusang are itinerant traders; gaekju and yeogak are brokers who also lodged and financed merchants. Misunderstanding: Brokers were not mere innkeepers; they structured trade and credit.

Section 39.7

Rivers, Roads, Ports, and Commercial Transportation

Bulk goods moved most cheaply by water. Rivers and the sea carried grain, salt, timber, and fish, while roads and pack animals or porters carried lighter or shorter-distance loads. The Han River was the principal artery into Seoul, and coastal shipping brought grain from the southern provinces up the western coast to river landing places near the capital. Overland routes, such as the great road crossing the peninsula's southern interior, linked towns but were slow and expensive for heavy goods.

Fiscal administration shaped shipping. Tax grain from the southern provinces was gathered at coastal and river warehouses and carried toward Seoul. Much of the carrying was done aboard privately operated vessels hired for payment, notably by Gyeonggang merchants. The government's establishment of grain warehouses at Masan and Gasan in 1760, followed by Samnang in 1765, was intended to address delays and damage in tax-grain transport and formed part of an effort to reduce dependence on private shipping. In 1785 Jeongjo's Jakdae samok organized Gyeonggang merchants into groups with collective shipping responsibilities. In return for providing boats for royal pontoon bridges and accepting regulatory obligations, participating merchants received protections for grain shipping and Han River commerce. The relationship shows the state both relying on and constraining private enterprise.

Geography and season shaped cost and risk. Western coastal waters had strong tides and treacherous stretches, shipwreck was a recurring hazard, winter ice and summer floods interrupted river traffic, and mountains made some districts costly to reach. Transport cost therefore influenced prices: goods from remote districts sold at a disadvantage, while ports and river landings gained as transfer points where cargo moved between boats, carts, and animals, attracting brokers, storage, and markets. Evidence consists of official shipping regulations, tax records, and later descriptions, which do not yield reliable freight rates, so this chapter gives none.

Hired carriage of tax grain should be distinguished from merchants' separate private trade: Gyeonggang merchants also dealt in rice and other goods on their own account and competed for the supply of the capital. The evidence comes from fiscal regulations and institutional histories, which do not yield complete freight records.

The Han River gave Gyeonggang merchants a chain of commercial locations: Mapo, Seogang, Yongsan, Dumopo, and Ttukseom served as important landing and trading places. There, warehousing, commissioned sales, and shipping supported distinct intermediary occupations, linking riverside storage to shippers, brokers, and Seoul's markets. The evidence documents these places' functions; it does not provide complete volumes handled at each.

A documented voyage of 1753 shows how finance, shipping, and regional trade fitted together. The Kaesong sailor Kim Jung-jae, acting as agent for the merchant-investor (mulju) Kim Jin-cheol, set out from the Yeseong River with 2,200 nyang of the investor's money and traded at Gangyeong-po in Chungcheong, Pohang in Gyeongsang, and Samcheok in Gangwon, using 540 seok of rice as a basis for buying marine products at each place. The account, in the Academy of Korean Studies' entry on Kaesong merchants, shows capital from a merchant house, an agent who captained the boat, rice carried as trade goods, and a chain of ports from the west to the east coast. It is one documented case; it does not show profits, how typical such voyages were, or how often agents failed.

Section 39.8

Coins, Credit, Prices, and the Monetary Economy

Earlier attempts to establish a copper coinage had not taken lasting hold, and cloth and grain served as everyday means of payment. In 1678 the government issued Sangpyeong tongbo, minted by government offices, and over the following decades the coin gradually became important in everyday transactions and in paying taxes. Cloth and grain nonetheless persisted, especially in poorer and more remote areas, so monetization was a gradual, uneven process rather than a sudden replacement.

An important distinction separates commodity money from coin. Cloth and grain were useful in themselves and varied in quality, so valuing them required judgement; copper coins were standardized and divisible, suiting small purchases, tax payments, and long-distance trade. Markets raised demand for such a medium, while wider coin circulation made it easier to buy and sell, so commerce and coinage reinforced each other.

Credit and debt grew alongside money. Merchants advanced goods or money on trust, and farmers borrowed against harvests, sometimes at high interest. Coin scarcity became a recurrent problem that contemporaries criticized. It could make debts denominated in coin harder to repay, but the effect depended on contract terms, on whether obligations were counted in coin, grain, or cloth, and on relative price movements, so borrowers were not affected uniformly. Historians reconstruct these mechanisms mainly from official debates and incomplete records; complete price series are lacking, and this chapter gives none. Regional variation was marked: coin circulated most densely around Seoul, in Gyeonggi, and near major markets and ports, and least in remote areas.

The beneficiaries and losers differed. Merchants, brokers, and market-oriented farmers handled coin readily and could profit from it, while tenants and small borrowers, who had to obtain coin to pay rent, taxes, or debts, could bear costs of scarcity, depending on how their obligations were denominated. The monetary economy thus widened commercial opportunity while also exposing the poorest households to market conditions they could not control.

Circulation of coins should be distinguished from production of new ones. Japanese copper imports supplied government minting, and a disruption in the copper trade is documented for 1698–1707. Official minting of Sangpyeong tongbo was suspended around 1697–1698 and resumed in 1731. Coins already produced remained in circulation, and expanding demand for money contributed to coin shortages. Interrupted imports, suspended minting, and continuing circulation are therefore related but separate developments, and their combined effect on prices and debts is reconstructed from incomplete records.

Historical Vocabulary
Sangpyeong Tongbo and Monetary Exchange

Sangpyeong tongbo ("commonly circulating treasure") was the copper coin issued in 1678. Why it matters: It eased small transactions and tax payment. Misunderstanding: Coins supplemented rather than immediately replaced grain and cloth.

Cause and Effect

Market Expansion

Long-term causes: post-war agricultural recovery, irrigation, and wider adoption of transplanting; the earlier existence of rural markets. Short-term causes: the Daedong reform and public purchasing through gongin; growing demand from Seoul; coinage from 1678.

Context and event: markets, merchant networks, transport, and money developed together; imported Japanese copper supported minting; private shipping carried tax grain. Immediate effects: more and larger markets, regional centers, brokers and credit, and more households selling and buying. Contested institutions: licensed-shop privileges, challenged by independent traders and adjusted in 1786–1794.

Long-term consequences: a more commercialized economy with unequal gains for landowners, merchants, tenants, debtors, and laborers, and a state that depended on and regulated private commerce. Historical complexity: each element reinforced the others, markets predated coin, and commerce itself raised demand for coin, so no single factor suffices.

Section 39.9

Handicrafts, Workshops, and Mining

Most manufacturing was done in households. Rural families spun and wove cloth, made straw goods, and produced tools and pottery for their own use and for sale, with women's weaving especially important. Alongside this, the state employed registered artisans who owed labor service to government workshops producing items such as weapons, ceremonial goods, and fine ceramics; the official kilns at Gwangju in Gyeonggi, which made white porcelain for the court, are a well-known example.

Over time the relationship between state production and private manufacturing shifted. As procurement increasingly worked through purchase (Section 39.4), the government relied more on paid commissions to private producers, and compulsory service by registered artisans became less central. Private workshops and kilns produced for markets: ceramics, paper, iron tools, and brass goods, with certain localities gaining reputations for particular products. Specialization remained limited, since most producers were small, household-based, and often combined craft work with farming.

Mining expanded in the eighteenth century. Silver, copper, gold, and lead were extracted, and iron was smelted for tools and implements. The state regulated mining through permits and taxes and at times restricted it from fear of disorder and cost, but private operators and investors increasingly organized work. Mining employed skilled workers, such as smelters and refiners, and large numbers of unskilled laborers, some paid wages in coin or kind. The scale of production differed considerably between industries. Many household crafts and private workshops remained small, but some mining enterprises, particularly from the late eighteenth century into the nineteenth, brought together substantial investment, specialized workers for extraction, transport, crushing, and smelting, and large hired labor forces. These developments demonstrate commercial investment and increasingly organized production without implying that Joseon had developed a modern mechanized factory system.

Mining investment was organized through a division of roles: the capital provider (mulju) financed the enterprise and the mine operator (deokdae) managed excavation, labor, and processing. This arrangement is best documented from the late eighteenth century into the nineteenth; earlier mining was more heavily controlled or restricted by the state, so the investor-operator system should not be projected back across the whole period.

Section 39.10

Seoul: The Growth of a Commercial Capital

Seoul was the political center of Joseon and its largest consumer market. The court, ministries, garrisons, and the households of officials and scholars required food, fuel, clothing, paper, and tools, and government spending, including rice stipends and purchases through the gongin, converted state revenue into urban demand. The city's growth, which drew migrants from the countryside to work as laborers, carriers, craftspeople, and traders, makes Seoul a key link between regional economies, though this chapter gives no population estimates because reliable counts are lacking.

Commercial geography reflected privilege. Along Jongno, the main thoroughfare, licensed shops (sijeon) supplied goods such as silk, cotton, hemp cloth, paper, and fish to the court and the city, in return for obligations to the state, with the Six Shops highest in standing. Their commodity categories and membership varied across periods; records mention shops dealing in silk, cotton cloth, other textiles, paper, ramie, and fish products, and counts differ. By the late eighteenth century Jongno, Ihyeon (Baeogae), and Chilpae formed Seoul's three major commercial districts. Ihyeon and Chilpae lay outside the licensed core and served ordinary consumers, and Chilpae was especially important for the fish trade. These unlicensed markets drew private merchants and increasingly competed with the traditional shops. Han River landings and districts such as Mapo and Yongsan received grain, firewood, salt, and fish from many regions, and merchants of the Gyeonggang network were involved in private trade and, in some periods, in government grain transport and supplying the city.

Provisioning created connections far beyond the walls. Grain came largely from the southern provinces by coastal ships and the Han River, fuel from nearby hills and forests, cloth and paper from provincial producers, and specialty goods from distant regions. Storage and brokerage at the landings smoothed seasonal supply. Seoul's demand thus tied agricultural districts, ports, and merchant groups into a network, while competition between privileged shops and independent traders over who could supply the city became a major political issue.

Seoul's commerce also depended on labor that sources record only indirectly. Porters, boatmen, and carriers handled goods at the Han River landings and between markets, and shop assistants and hired hands worked for licensed and unlicensed traders alike. Such work was probably insecure and tied to the seasons of shipping and market, but surviving records say more about shops and regulations than about these workers. The licensed core, Jongno, and the unlicensed markets of Ihyeon and Chilpae therefore differed in privilege, clientele, and function, yet drew on the same landings and workforce.

Section 39.11

Merchant Privileges, Monopoly, and the Reform of 1791

The licensed-shop system gave certain Seoul merchants the right to supply the government and the court, and in return they owed obligations to the state. To protect their position they held geumnanjeonggwon, the right to suppress unlicensed traders selling the same goods. As the city grew and independent traders multiplied, the licensed merchants used this power against them, and critics charged that monopoly raised prices for consumers.

The question became pressing in the late eighteenth century. In 1791 (Jeongjo's fifteenth year) the government adopted Sinhae tonggong, a policy associated with the minister Chae Je-gong, who criticized monopolization and rising prices and argued that the exclusion rights of most licensed shops should be ended. Under the decision, most licensed shops lost the right to suppress competitors, while the Six Shops retained theirs. Legally, the reform opened trade in many goods to unlicensed merchants; it did not establish free trade, because the most privileged shops kept their rights. Independent traders selling the goods of other shops could no longer be suppressed by them, while the Six Shops kept their supply obligations to the state.

What can be demonstrated about the effects is more limited than the decree itself. The record shows the policy and its rationale; it does not by itself measure enforcement, price changes, or the later fortunes of the shops, and later conditions varied. Historians generally read it as a response to commercial competition and political concern over prices rather than as a commitment to free markets, a distinction central to the debate considered at the end of this chapter.

The reform belonged to a sequence of related measures. In 1786 (Byeongo tonggong) the dispute concerned enforcement of exclusion privileges against merchants outside certain licensed shops. In 1787 (Jeongmi tonggong) officials examined specific abuses involving textile, fish, and iron merchants; in one case, merchants were accused of buying up and stockpiling iron and treating competing blacksmiths as unauthorized traders. In 1791 (Sinhae tonggong) exclusion rights were abolished for most licensed shops, with the Six Shops exempted. In 1794 (Gabin tonggong) the Six Shops' commodity categories were reorganized, with the fish shops removed from the privileged group, and certain commercial taxes were reduced or reallocated. These cases show commercial policy affecting concrete economic interests, such as blacksmiths' livelihoods and traders' access to markets. They are known mainly from policy records, which show the rulings but not how consistently they were enforced.

Section 39.12

Regional Towns and Commercial Centers

Regional centers differed less in their names than in their institutions. Kaesong, the former Goryeo capital, was the base of the Songsang merchants, whose branch offices, called Songbang, were established in important trading places across the country. Through such offices and commercial agents they organized distribution, and by the eighteenth century some Kaesong merchants advanced funds to producers and purchased goods ahead of sale. This was merchant capital at work: investment in future output, not only trade in finished goods. It should be distinguished from ginseng itself, which was long traded, with larger-scale cultivation and processing around Kaesong belonging mainly to the nineteenth century.

Pyeongyang, on the Taedong River, was a leading northwestern administrative and commercial center, home to the Yusang merchants. Its opportunities came from administrative demand, river routes, and roads toward China and the south. Daegu lay on the main routes of the southeast and served as a provincial administrative center; its medicinal-herb market is traditionally dated to 1658, though its precise origins are debated. Songpa, at a Han River crossing outside Seoul, became a major market where private merchants competed with the privileged Seoul shops.

Gangyeong, on the Geum River, was a river-trade market known for fish and salt. It had an established town and market by the eighteenth century, but its growth as a major commercial center is especially well documented for the nineteenth. Masanpo, on the southern coast, owed its role largely to the state: the establishment of the Masan warehouse in 1760 made it a collection point for tax grain, which drew shipping and brokers.

These cases show that commercial centers differed in function. Kaesong's strength lay in a merchant network with branch offices across the country and in credit; Pyeongyang's in regional commerce linked to the northwest and to China; Gangyeong's and the Han River landings' in river-port functions of warehousing, commissioned sales, and shipping; Masanpo's in fiscal logistics. None should be treated as typical. Institutional histories and official records describe markets and functions more fully than ordinary traders' lives, and they do not support named merchant houses or transaction histories here.

Section 39.13

Foreign Commerce: Qing China, Japan, and Border Markets

Foreign trade was tied to diplomacy but followed distinct routes and chronologies. With Qing China, regular missions to Beijing carried private trade by merchants and attendants, and border markets supplemented them, though they did not operate continuously. The Hoeryeong market in the northeast began officially in 1638 and Gyeongwon's in 1645, on different schedules; the Uiju–Junggang market in the northwest reopened in 1646 under Qing pressure and closed in 1700. Uiju merchants, called Mansang, were prominent intermediaries. Joseon exported ginseng, cotton cloth, paper, and hides to China and imported silk, medicines, books, and dyes, with some goods restricted.

Frontier commerce did not end with the Junggang market. Private Chaekmun trade, conducted at the frontier, was operating from approximately 1660, and Joseon merchants used the transport arrangements of diplomatic missions. On the Qing side they dealt with Chinese transport intermediaries (欄頭輩, Korean nandubae), including a group of twelve transport operators identified in records from 1690. In 1754 the government regulated Mansang trade, and a detailed reference dates formal authorization and taxation of Chaekmun trade to 1755, although accounts differ in dating and characterizing the measures. Competition between merchants and interpreters helped generate the 1787 prohibition, and trade was authorized again in 1795. The sequence shows that trade regulation was repeatedly renegotiated among merchants, interpreters, and the state.

Trade with Japan ran through the Japanese settlement (waegwan) at Dongnae, near Busan, and the Tsushima domain, which mediated relations with the Tokugawa shogunate. Three kinds of trade should be distinguished: official trade, trade by licensed private merchants, and illicit exchange; not all private trade was smuggling. Licensed private markets operated at the waegwan on the 3rd, 8th, 13th, 18th, 23rd, and 28th of each month; Joseon merchants needed authorization, and regulations at times changed the number of merchants admitted. A separate daily morning market supplied provisions. Government-approved Dongnae merchants (Naesang) handled much of this exchange. In 1683 Tsushima established a trade-management office, the motokatayaku, and a study of its operations in 1684–1711 documents the handling of silver, silk, and ginseng transactions. Joseon sold ginseng and Chinese-origin silk and received Japanese silver and, later, copper, and the composition of major exports and imports changed during the eighteenth century.

These routes were linked. Silver from Japan could finance Joseon's purchases of silk in China, making Joseon an intermediary, but scholarship indicates that this intermediary trade weakened in the 1740s–1750s, so the pattern did not hold unchanged across the period. Japanese copper was essential to government coin production; a study of the copper trade documents serious supply difficulties around 1698–1707, which shows how external supply could constrain domestic minting (Section 39.8). The state controlled missions, border markets, and the waegwan, yet unofficial exchange appears in records that cannot measure its scale, and this chapter gives no estimates of total trade volume.

Private traders' access to foreign commerce had itself been restricted and then opened by the state. Private trade abroad had been treated as illegal and tightly controlled, but from 1681 the government appointed wealthy merchants as trade managers (mupan byeoljang) to fund missions and procure military supplies, opening a path for private merchants. Kaesong merchants gained experience at the Junggang market, which reopened in 1646, and in the mid-seventeenth to early eighteenth century, when direct China–Japan trade was cut off, they used ginseng to mediate between the two, with the Kaesong authority granting them the right to circulate ginseng. Alongside licensed trade they also took part in illicit trade; a record of 1821 states that official red-ginseng exports were 200 geun a year while illicitly made red ginseng exported to China amounted to thousands of geun, though the figure is a contemporary claim, not a measured total.

Section 39.14

Consequences and Limits of Commercialization

The effects of commercial growth were unequal. Landowners with surplus, merchants, brokers, skilled artisans, shipowners, and transport workers could gain, and some commoners prospered. Tenants, indebted households, and small producers faced pressure from rent, debt, and market risks, and wage laborers in mining, transport, and the city depended on uncertain work. Women's household production contributed to many households' incomes, and enslaved and commoner laborers worked within this economy, though Chapter 42 treats gender, family, and social status more fully.

The chapter's examples show what commercialization involved. Songbang offices and advances to producers show merchant capital organizing distribution; Gyeonggang shipping shows private operators carrying state tax grain; mulju–deokdae mines show investment and large hired workforces; the licensed shops and the 1791 reform show privilege being contested; coin circulation shows monetization; and the silver, silk, and copper trades show Joseon linked to China and Japan. Regional differences were large: the southern plains, river and coastal districts, and the Seoul region experienced these developments most directly, remote mountain districts much less.

What these developments do not establish is equally important. They do not show that most households depended on wages or markets for survival; most people still farmed, land-based taxation remained central, and the status hierarchy persisted, with wealthy merchants not displacing the landed, office-holding elite. State regulation continued through licenses, taxes, and privileges. The crises of the nineteenth century, treated in Chapters 43–44, arose in this setting.

Historians therefore ask how fundamental the change was. Commercialization is not synonymous with capitalism, and the absence of mechanized factories alone does not settle the question. Capital investment, wage labor, and merchant credit existed, and capitalist relationships could emerge within an agrarian society; the disagreement concerns how widespread, organized, and transformative they were. The pattern the chapter has traced is of production, procurement, transport, merchant networks, and coinage reinforcing one another without eliminating the agrarian hierarchy.

The state's own role was multiple. It was a tax collector through the Daedong levy, a customer through the gongin, a licensor of the Six Shops and other shops, a regulator of shipping through the Jakdae samok, and a builder of grain warehouses such as Masan. These functions sometimes encouraged private commerce and sometimes limited it. Commercial growth in Joseon therefore cannot be told as the market escaping the state; state institutions were among the channels through which markets grew.

Historical Debate

Transformation or Growth within an Agrarian Order?

The question: Did commercial expansion fundamentally transform the economy, or was it growth within an enduring agrarian, hierarchical order? Many historians avoid a simple binary and ask which relationships changed, for whom, and how far.

Transformation view: Investor-run mines with large hired workforces, merchant capital such as Songbang advances to producers, the spread of coin, and private carriage of tax grain suggest relationships moving beyond a subsistence-and-tribute order. Some scholars read these as early forms of capitalist development.
Continuity view: Land-based taxation, status hierarchy, state licensing and regulation, small-scale household production, and the fact that most households farmed suggest commerce expanded within an agrarian framework, and commercial growth does not by itself show a structural break.

Evidence and disagreement: Both sides use the same records. The disagreement concerns scale, how widespread wage labor and merchant capital were, and the meaning of terms such as capitalism; records are incomplete and regions differed. Why it matters: It determines whether late Joseon is read as moving toward a different order or adapting within its own. Student task: Identify evidence, such as records of hired labor, coin use, and landholding, that could distinguish the positions, and explain what each could not show.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Agrarian Ideals and Commercial Relationships

Before: after the wars, tribute in local products, scattered markets, and payment largely in grain and cloth. After: a land-based levy with public purchasing, many linked markets, coin alongside older payment, merchant networks with credit, and a larger commercial capital.

Continuities: land as the basis of taxation, the status hierarchy, state licensing and regulation, tenancy, and most households living by farming. Changes in everyday economic relationships: more households sold or bought at markets, used coin, borrowed against harvests, and worked for hire. Change was gradual, uneven, and contested; merchants, brokers, and market-oriented farmers felt it most directly, subsistence tenants and remote communities least. It shows change within, not simply against, an agrarian order.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 39 asks how Joseon developed more extensive markets, merchant networks, specialized production, and monetary exchange between about 1600 and 1800, and how those changes affected different regions and social groups. Its answer is that production, procurement, exchange, and money reinforced one another while the agrarian and hierarchical order endured.

The starting point was recovery. After the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions, fields were reclaimed, households resettled, and registers rebuilt. Household registers served taxation and under-register people, while land surveys, which covered the three southern provinces in 1634 and 1719–1720, have their own limits of measurement, coverage, and taxation; historians infer growth cautiously, and famines such as those of 1670–1671 and 1695–1696 interrupted it.

Agricultural change rested on irrigation works (jeeon), wider adoption of rice transplanting (iangbeop), double-cropping, and fertilizer. Technique was known before it was widely used, and gains depended on water, labor, and land. Owners, smallholders, and tenants experienced them differently, and cotton, tobacco, vegetables, and ginseng offered market opportunity mainly to households with surplus, while tenancy and debt bound others.

State finance helped create commerce. The Daedong system, developing from the 1608 Seonhye experiment and adopted province by province, replaced product tribute with a land-based levy payable in rice, cloth, or money by locality. Through the Seonhyecheong and licensed suppliers (gongin), public demand moved into private markets. Policy design, implementation, and evidence must be kept distinct.

Exchange expanded through markets, merchants, and transport. Jangsi, which emerged in the late fifteenth century and later settled into five-day circuits, linked villages to regional markets. Peddlers, brokers, and merchant groups supplied credit and information, while rivers and coasts carried grain, as in the Masan warehouse of 1760 and the private shipping of Gyeonggang merchants regulated in 1785.

Money and production developed together. Sangpyeong tongbo, issued in 1678, spread unevenly beside grain and cloth, and coin scarcity affected coin-denominated debts depending on terms and prices. Households, government artisans, and private workshops made goods, and mining, financed by mulju and run by deokdae, shows large hired workforces from the late eighteenth century without implying modern factory industry.

Seoul tied regions together. Court and government spending created demand, Jongno's licensed shops, Ihyeon, and Chilpae formed its main markets, and Han River landings brought supplies. Privileged merchants held exclusion rights against independent traders, and a sequence of measures from 1786 to 1794 adjusted the system: in 1791 Sinhae tonggong ended most of those rights while the Six Shops kept theirs, and in 1794 their commodity categories and taxes were revised. The record shows the legal change, not its enforcement or price effects.

Regional centers grew for different reasons: Kaesong through Songsang merchant capital and Songbang offices, Pyeongyang through administration and routes to China, Masanpo through fiscal logistics, Gangyeong, Songpa, and Daegu through rivers, crossings, and markets. Foreign trade followed separate Qing and Japanese routes, with changing Chaekmun policy, Tsushima's trade office, silver, silk, and ginseng, and Japanese copper supporting coin production; the Qing side involved transport intermediaries, and Japan trade ran through licensed waegwan markets.

Consequences were unequal. Landowners, merchants, brokers, skilled artisans, and shipowners could gain; tenants, debtors, small producers, and laborers faced pressure. Regional variation was large, and most people still farmed. Historians disagree about how fundamental the change was, and commercialization should not be equated with capitalism or dismissed as irrelevant to it.

Throughout, evidence limits the story. Most sources come from officials and elites, ordinary traders and women appear indirectly, and complete price, freight, population, and trade-volume series are lacking, so this chapter gives none. Continuity, in land taxation, status, and state regulation, accompanied change, and later strains are the subject of Chapters 43–44.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 39 shows an economy changing through accumulated adaptation: fiscal reform created demand, markets and merchants distributed goods, money eased exchange, and foreign trade supplied silver, silk, and copper. State institutions were not only brakes on commerce but also among its causes.

The gains were uneven. Landowners, brokers, merchants, and skilled workers had opportunities that tenants, debtors, and laborers often lacked, and regions differed greatly. The evidence itself is uneven: it tells us more about regulation and elites than about ordinary traders, so many conclusions are inferences.

The agrarian hierarchy persisted alongside commerce, which is why historians disagree about how to describe the change. Chapter 42 examines status, gender, and family in this society, and Chapters 43–44 show how fiscal and social strains grew within it.

The chapter also teaches a method. Claims about commerce rest on regulations, institutional histories, and scholarly studies, each with its own perspective, and wherever records are missing, such as prices, freight, and trade volumes, the responsible conclusion is that they are unknown. Distinguishing documented institutions from reconstruction is part of understanding the period.
