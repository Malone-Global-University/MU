Kim Jong Un's North Korea

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On December 19, 2011, North Korean state media announced that Kim Jong Il had died two days earlier. The announcement named a successor in the same breath. A state whose founding leader had died in 1994 now had to show, within days, that power could pass to a third member of the same family, and that the party, army, and security services would accept it. How that transfer turned into durable personal and institutional authority is the subject of this chapter.

The notice read over North Korean broadcasts that December announced the death of a leader who had governed since 1994 and presented his son, Kim Jong Un, as the person around whom the party, army, and people were to rally. The announcement was a political document as much as a report of a death. It listed the institutions that issued it, described the dead leader's work in the language of revolutionary sacrifice, and told the population what loyalty required. Historians read it as evidence of what the state wished citizens to believe and how it wished the succession to be understood. It is not evidence of what citizens felt, and it says little about the arrangements made among senior officials behind closed doors.

The young successor was in his late twenties, and his preparation had been brief by comparison with his father's long apprenticeship under Kim Il Sung. His public career had begun only in September 2010, when he received a four-star general's rank and a party post. Around him stood officials who had served his father for decades, including relatives by marriage, veteran generals, and security chiefs. The first historical question of this chapter is therefore not whether a hereditary succession could be announced, because it could, but whether symbolic legitimacy could be converted into practical control over the institutions that actually governed.

The answer unfolded over several years and involved appointments, reorganizations, public campaigns, and purges that included the execution of the leader's own uncle by marriage in 2013. It also involved the relationship between the party and the army, which had been reshaped under the military-first politics (Songun) of Kim Jong Il. By 2016 and 2021 the Workers' Party of Korea held congresses that gave institutional form to the new leadership, and in February 2026 a Ninth Congress set a new five-year plan. Throughout this period the formal vocabulary of government, with its committees, assemblies, and constitutional offices, coexisted with a political reality in which one man's authority was final.

Chapter 83 described how the famine of the 1990s weakened the public distribution system and drove households into informal markets. This chapter picks up that story after 2011. It asks why markets that had become indispensable to ordinary survival did not produce a market-based political order, how the state alternately tolerated and restricted commercial life, and what that oscillation meant for traders, farmers, workers, women, and families. It also asks how a state under heavy international sanctions combined a nuclear and missile program with promises of economic improvement, and how the strategy known as byungjin changed over time.

The chapter also examines the restrictions that shaped everyday life. The government used neighborhood surveillance, political classification, detention, and a series of laws against foreign culture to limit movement, speech, and information, and these measures tightened after January 2020, when the country closed its borders against COVID-19. United Nations investigations in 2014 and 2025 are major documentary anchors here. The country's relationship with the outside world also changed, with sanctions, Chinese trade, and a military partnership with Russia that had begun to alter the material basis of state power by 2025.

Evidence is a constant difficulty. North Korean official statements are primary sources for policy and ideology, but they cannot be taken at face value on production, popular support, or military performance. External statistical estimates, such as those produced by the Bank of Korea, rest on indirect measurement. Testimony from people who left the country is indispensable but comes from a self-selected group, and the state restricts independent access. The chapter therefore pays continuous attention to what kinds of evidence support each claim, and it declines to predict either imminent collapse or imminent economic transformation. Neither prediction is supported by the evidence available through November 2026, which is this chapter's fixed endpoint.

Chapter 92 treats North Korea as a functioning political and social system and not merely as the origin of missile tests or the counterpart in diplomatic summits. The summits and the collapse of inter-Korean détente belong to Chapter 93, and the wider strategic relationships with the United States, China, Japan, and Russia belong to Chapter 94. The origins of the nuclear program are in Chapter 86. Here the focus is on the governing system itself and on the people who lived within it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the symbolic legitimacy conferred by hereditary succession from the practical consolidation of control over party, military, and security institutions after December 2011.
  2. Trace Kim Jong Un's assumption of party, military, and state offices in 2012 and the later institutional changes of 2016, 2019, 2021, and 2026.
  3. Analyze the 2013 purge of Jang Song Thaek and the 2017 killing of Kim Jong Nam, separating established findings from contested questions of responsibility.
  4. Explain how the Workers' Party, the State Affairs Commission, the Cabinet, the Supreme People's Assembly, and the military related to one another, and why formal institutions did not amount to political pluralism.
  5. Assess official propaganda as evidence of what the state wished citizens to believe and not as proof of popular acceptance.
  6. Evaluate the findings of the 2014 UN Commission of Inquiry and the 2025 UN review, and explain the difficulties of verifying conditions in a closed state.
  7. Explain why jangmadang markets became indispensable to household survival without producing political liberalization.
  8. Interpret byungjin as a policy that changed over time, and compare its stated aims with documented economic outcomes.
  9. Analyze the relationship among state planning, enterprises, private capital, currency, prices, and attempts at recentralization in the 2020s.
  10. Describe the effects of sanctions, border closure, and external trade on household and regional economic life, distinguishing external pressure from domestic policy.
  11. Distinguish nuclear doctrine, tested capability, and independent assessment, and evaluate competing arguments about nuclear strategy and national development.
  12. Evaluate the regime's strength along four dimensions (elite cohesion, suppression of opposition, resource mobilization, and civilian welfare) and the limits of external statistics.

Historical Context

The state Kim Jong Un inherited was the product of seven decades of development described in earlier chapters. Chapter 77 traced Kim Il Sung's construction of a party-state organized around collectivized agriculture, state industry, a classification system called songbun, and the ideology of Juche, with its emphasis on self-reliance and the centrality of the leader. By the 1970s the leader's authority had become hereditary in practice, and Chapter 80 described the debt, stagnation, and militarization of the 1970s and 1980s as the next succession was prepared.

Kim Jong Il, who assumed leadership after his father's death in 1994, governed through a very different crisis. Chapter 83 described the economic collapse after the end of Soviet support, the famine of the mid-1990s, the breakdown of public distribution, and the rise of informal markets known as jangmadang. Demographic estimates of famine deaths vary widely, and scholars disagree about their scale. Kim Jong Il responded politically with Songun, which gave the military a leading role in state ideology and resource allocation, while the government partly legalized markets in the early 2000s and then repeatedly tried to rein them in. Chapter 86 described how the nuclear program moved from negotiating chip toward declared weapons capability, with tests in 2006 and 2009.

Three structural conditions therefore shaped the new leader's position in 2011. First, the central institutions of the state existed alongside an economy in which most households relied on informal commerce for a substantial share of their income. Second, the party had been overshadowed in some respects by the army and by security institutions. Third, the country was subject to international sanctions that constrained trade and finance, and its principal economic lifelines ran through China. These conditions did not determine what Kim Jong Un would do, but they defined the problems he faced.

For a student of this period, the most important caution concerns evidence. The North Korean government controls the publication of statistics, restricts foreign researchers and journalists, and imposes severe penalties for contact with outsiders. Historians therefore combine several kinds of evidence, which differ in reliability and in what they can show. Official media show policy and ideology. Satellite imagery and trade statistics from partner countries show physical and commercial patterns. Interviews with people who left North Korea show everyday experience, though not necessarily the experience of those who stayed. International investigations aggregate such material under defined legal standards. No single type is sufficient, and the chapter attributes claims to their sources throughout.

Part I — Succession, Political Consolidation, and the Kim Jong Un State

Sections 92.1–92.5 examine the establishment of third-generation hereditary rule and the institutions of authoritarian government.

Section 92.1

The Death of Kim Jong Il and the Succession Crisis, December 2011–2012

Kim Jong Il's death was announced on December 19, 2011, with the statement that he had died on December 17 of physical and mental overwork, on a train during a field visit. Observers outside the country learned of it only through the announcement, which followed two days of silence. The delay itself drew attention, but historians cannot say from outside what discussions took place in those two days. What the announcement does establish is the official sequence of legitimacy: the dead leader's authority was transferred by name to his son, and the leading institutions of party, army, and state were presented as united behind him.

The funeral and mourning period supplied the visual language of this unity. State television showed Kim Jong Un walking beside the hearse in Pyongyang on December 28, flanked by senior military and party figures, and showed mass displays of grief. The state media used the phrase "Great Successor" and gave the young leader the political attributes of his predecessors, such as visits to military units and the claim that he carried on the leader's work. Outside observers have disputed how far the displays of grief were spontaneous and how far they were organized, and the evidence available cannot settle the question for the population as a whole. Their political function, however, is clear: they staged continuity.

Kim Jong Un faced a real difficulty. He had been publicly promoted only in September 2010, at a party conference that gave him a general's rank and a post on the Central Military Commission. His age was in the late twenties, and his exact birth year has not been consistently stated by the state. His authority rested on the dynastic prestige of the family and on the willingness of officials with long careers to defer to him. It did not rest on a personal record of service. Senior figures, including relatives, veteran generals, and heads of security bodies, therefore played a visible role in the early period and were widely assumed by outside commentators to be guiding or constraining him. How much constraint actually operated is not documented.

The consolidation of offices proceeded quickly. Within days of the funeral, a party meeting named him supreme commander of the Korean People's Army. In April 2012, a party conference named him First Secretary of the Workers' Party and conferred the title of Eternal General Secretary on his late father. A few days later he became First Chairman of the National Defence Commission, and in July he was given the rank of marshal. These offices gave him formal command of the party, the state's central military organ, and the armed forces. The ordering of the posts, with party and military first, matches the fact that these were the institutions whose acceptance mattered most.

The central analytical point is the difference between symbolic and practical authority. Holding titles did not by itself ensure that orders would be carried out, that officials would be loyal, or that the security services would not favor rivals. The next sections show how Kim Jong Un used personnel changes, party reorganization, and coercion to turn titles into control. The succession also differed from the 1994 transfer in that the new leader inherited an economy partly sustained by markets and a state already armed with nuclear devices, so his legitimacy would depend not only on lineage but also on performance, a theme that recurs throughout this chapter.

Document(s): Contemporary KCNA notice, reproduced with broadcast context by NK TechLab.

Context: The notice was issued in the name of the Workers' Party Central Committee and Central Military Commission, the National Defence Commission, the Presidium of the Supreme People's Assembly, and the Cabinet. It was transmitted by KCNA and also reproduced and recorded by outside media observers. It addressed domestic and international audiences at a moment of uncertainty about the succession.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation.

The notice reports that the leader died of overwork while traveling, praises his devotion to the people and the revolution, tells the nation to follow Kim Jong Un as its leader, and calls on the party, army, and people to unite around him while observing national mourning.

Analysis: The document names several institutions and puts the succession in the first message that most citizens heard of the death. It presents continuity as a matter of loyalty and moral duty, and it frames the new leader through the memory of the old one. Historians use it to examine how the state defined legitimacy, which here combined family lineage, institutional support, and revolutionary rhetoric.

Limitations: The notice cannot show who decided the succession, how senior officials reacted, or how the population received the news. It also says nothing about disagreements, if any, within the elite.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the leadership. The views of ordinary citizens, of officials who may have doubted the succession, and of outside governments appear only indirectly.

Section 92.2

Purges, Elite Restructuring, and the Consolidation of Personal Authority

Personnel change began early. In July 2012 state media announced that Ri Yong Ho, the army's chief of general staff and a prominent figure at the funeral, had been relieved of his posts because of illness. Outside analysts widely read the removal as political, since it came amid a broader reshuffling of senior generals, though the official reason was never contradicted by public evidence. Over the following years, the leadership of the armed forces turned over repeatedly, with senior officers promoted, demoted, and sometimes restored. The pattern is well documented in official announcements, which give titles and dates. The reasons behind each change are rarely documented.

The most dramatic episode was the fall of Jang Song Thaek. Jang was married to Kim Jong Il's sister, held senior party and state positions, and was seen as an influential figure in the early years. In early December 2013 state media announced that he had been dismissed at a Politburo meeting, and a few days later reported that a special military tribunal had convicted him of factionalism, corruption, and attempting to overthrow the state, and that he had been executed. The accusations were published in a long public indictment and photographs showed him being removed from the meeting. The regime thus made the purge public instead of concealing it, a choice that warned other officials as well as removing a rival.

The accusations against Jang cannot be accepted as an account of his actual conduct, since the tribunal was not independent and no outside observer attended. Historians therefore treat the indictment as evidence of how the regime wished to portray the case and of the kind of conduct, such as building independent power bases or controlling economic resources, that it treated as treason. Many outside analysts also connect the case to Jang's control of foreign-currency enterprises that were then reassigned. That link is plausible but inferential. Officials associated with Jang were also removed, and some reports claimed that relatives had been punished, which are difficult to verify independently.

In February 2017, Kim Jong Nam, Kim Jong Un's older half-brother, was attacked at Kuala Lumpur airport and died shortly afterward. Malaysian authorities identified the poison as VX nerve agent, and prosecuted two women who had applied it to his face. Charges against the Indonesian woman were dropped, and the Vietnamese woman pleaded guilty to a lesser charge and was released in 2019. Malaysian police sought several North Korean nationals who had left the country, and the governments of South Korea and the United States attributed the killing to North Korea. North Korea denied responsibility and disputed the cause of death. The established findings concern the method, the victim, and the judicial outcomes. Who ordered the killing has not been established in a court.

Elite management under Kim Jong Un also involved less visible methods. Officials were rotated among positions, investigated through party disciplinary channels, and publicly criticized in meetings. Some media accounts, including reports of the execution of senior officers, have come from South Korean intelligence briefings that could not be independently verified and, in a few cases, were later contradicted when individuals reappeared. The section's cautionary point follows from this: not every dismissal was a purge, not every purge was reported accurately, and official accusations are not a reliable explanation. What the evidence supports is a pattern of frequent turnover and of exemplary punishment that kept officials uncertain of their position and dependent on the leader's favor.

Section 92.3

The Workers' Party, the Military, and the Reorganization of Government

The institutions of North Korean government are numerous, and their relationships changed under Kim Jong Un. The Workers' Party sits at the center, with a Central Committee, a Politburo, a Secretariat, and departments that supervise personnel, propaganda, and economic work. The Supreme People's Assembly is the formal legislature and amends the constitution. The Cabinet administers the economy. The State Affairs Commission, created in 2016, took over from the National Defence Commission as the principal state organ. The Korean People's Army is subject to party political organs. Formal descriptions of these bodies exist in the constitution, but the practical distribution of power among them has to be reconstructed from official reports and outside analysis.

Under Kim Jong Il the army had held a privileged place under the policy of Songun, and the National Defence Commission had been the leading state organ. Under Kim Jong Un the ruling party recovered institutional prominence. Regular Politburo and Central Committee meetings were held and reported, party conferences and plenary sessions set policy directions, and the party's organizational departments gained visibility. The shift did not reduce the importance of the military or its role in nuclear and missile programs. It changed the framework in which those programs were governed, placing the military more explicitly under party leadership, a pattern supported by the frequent reporting of party meetings that discussed military affairs.

The Seventh Congress of the Workers' Party met in May 2016, the first in 36 years. It reaffirmed the byungjin line, announced a five-year strategy for economic development, and gave Kim Jong Un the title of Chairman of the Party. In June 2016 the Supreme People's Assembly replaced the National Defence Commission with the State Affairs Commission. Later constitutional revisions further defined the chairman's position as head of state in practice. The Eighth Congress in January 2021 created the title of General Secretary for Kim Jong Un, adopted a new five-year plan, and announced the previous plan's shortfalls. These congresses gave a regular rhythm to policymaking and a venue for reshuffling elites.

Formal institutions matter, but they do not establish pluralism. The Supreme People's Assembly meets rarely, and, as in earlier decades, its votes are reported as unanimous. Candidates for election are presented by the party, and there is no competing party with independent power. Official reports of party meetings are carefully edited, so historians reading them see what the leadership chose to publish. Decision-making appears to rest ultimately with Kim Jong Un and a small circle of officials. The distinction between formal offices and actual authority therefore remains central, and students should avoid assuming that the existence of committees implies independent accountability.

The Ninth Party Congress held in February 2026 is the culmination of this reorganization. Section 92.19 examines its policy outcomes in detail. For present purposes, the congress belongs to a sequence beginning with the 2012 conference and continuing through 2016 and 2021, in which the party regained a central role, the leader's titles were altered to reflect that role, and personnel were refreshed to include officials loyal to the new leadership. The sequence shows the regime making institutional adjustments while preserving the underlying concentration of authority.

Section 92.4

Juche, Dynastic Legitimacy, Propaganda, and the Construction of Kim Jong Un's Leadership

The ideology Kim Jong Un inherited combined Juche, the doctrine of self-reliance developed under Kim Il Sung, the Songun emphasis of Kim Jong Il, and a family narrative of revolutionary struggle centered on Mount Paektu, the mountain associated with the family's guerrilla past and, in official myth, with the leaders' lineage. In 2012 the party formalized "Kimilsungism-Kimjongilism" as the guiding ideology. Official histories, school textbooks, monuments, and ceremonies all reinforced the link between the ruling family and the founding of the state. The ideological framework therefore located the third leader within a sacred genealogy and not merely in a line of office-holders.

State media presented Kim Jong Un in ways that both echoed and differed from his predecessors. Photographs and film showed him visiting factories, farms, military units, and construction sites, often with officials taking notes at his side, and accompanied by workers, soldiers, and children. His public manner, including smiling and physical closeness to ordinary people, was noted by outside observers as a contrast with his father's reclusiveness, and some commentators saw an attempt to evoke Kim Il Sung. Large construction projects, such as the Masikryong ski resort, the Mirae Scientists Street, and the Ryomyong Street apartment district in Pyongyang, were presented as evidence of the leader's concern for development and modernity.

The vocabulary of legitimacy also changed over time. From the late 2010s official language placed more emphasis on the state, on national strength, and on the idea that the political system was permanent. After 2021, the phrase "people-first" politics became prominent, and the leadership increasingly spoke of the obligation of officials to deliver material improvements. These slogans did not abandon the leader cult. They added a claim to be responsible for welfare, which also created a standard against which performance could be measured, as the 2021 admission of unmet economic targets shows.

Education, monuments, and ceremonies carried the message into everyday life. Schools taught the family's biographies, public squares displayed statues and murals, and workplace and neighborhood groups held regular study sessions and mass events. Cultural production, including films, songs, novels, and mass performances, was organized by state institutions and directed toward political themes. These activities did not require private belief, and their effects on actual attitudes are difficult to measure. They did establish a shared public language in which expressions of loyalty were required and dissent was dangerous.

Students should read this material critically. Propaganda is evidence of what the state wished citizens to believe and of what the state believed would be effective. It is not by itself evidence of acceptance. Interviews with people who left the country suggest a wide range of attitudes, from genuine veneration to cynicism, though those interviewees are not a representative sample. The most defensible conclusion is that the state invested heavily in constructing and displaying devotion, and that public conformity was both encouraged and enforced, so the observable record of loyalty is a poor guide to private opinion.

Historical Vocabulary
Songbun

Definition: A system of political and family background classification, developed under Kim Il Sung, that groups citizens by the perceived loyalty of their ancestors and relatives, often described as core, wavering, and hostile categories.

Historical context: Songbun was shaped by the land reform, the Korean War, and the identification of families with colonial-era or anti-communist backgrounds. It affected access to education, employment, residence in Pyongyang, and party membership.

Why it matters: Songbun explains how political status could affect economic opportunity, and why market activity offered some people routes around official hierarchies, while others, through bribery or connections, could convert wealth into protection.

Potential misunderstanding: Songbun is not a fixed caste system with unchanging categories. Its practical importance has varied, the details are known mainly from testimony and scholarship, and the effect of markets on its relevance remains debated.

Section 92.5

Surveillance, Political Detention, Human Rights, and the Machinery of Social Control

North Korea's system of social control relies on several institutions. The Ministry of State Security investigates political crimes and runs detention facilities for political offenders. The Ministry of Social Security handles ordinary policing, controls residence and movement, and monitors markets. Military security organs watch the armed forces. At the neighborhood level, residents are organized into inminban, groups of households whose leaders report on residents and enforce regulations, and workplace and party organizations add further supervision. Together these institutions make surveillance part of everyday life, though the intensity varies by region and the practices differ across classes.

Political detention takes several forms, including the facilities known as kwanliso for political offenders, and shorter-term labor-training facilities and detention centers used for lesser offenses. Under the principle of guilt by association, family members of political offenders could be detained or have their opportunities restricted. These practices are known from testimony, from the investigations described below, and from analysis of satellite imagery of facilities, which can show physical change but not the conditions inside. The North Korean government denies the allegations of systematic abuse and describes them as politically motivated.

The United Nations Commission of Inquiry on Human Rights in the DPRK, led by Michael Kirby, released its report in February 2014. The commission was established by the UN Human Rights Council, held public hearings in several countries, and interviewed many witnesses who had left North Korea. The government refused to cooperate or to grant access. The commission documented violations of freedom of thought, expression, and movement, discrimination based on songbun, abductions of foreign nationals, and the use of forced labor, torture, and execution. It concluded that these included crimes against humanity, citing the acts of extermination, murder, enslavement, torture, imprisonment, rape and other sexual violence, persecution, enforced disappearance, and the infliction of prolonged starvation. It estimated that tens of thousands of people were held in political prison camps, and it recommended referral to international justice mechanisms.

In September 2025 the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights published a comprehensive review, A/HRC/60/58, on developments since the 2014 report. It found that the overall situation had not improved and in many respects had deteriorated. It pointed to increased surveillance, including digital tools, tighter controls on information and on foreign cultural content, restrictions that were intensified after the border closure, continuing forced labor, and an expanded use of severe penalties. The review relied on interviews with people who had recently left the country and on analysis of legal texts. Because those who left after 2020 were far fewer than earlier, the quantity of new testimony was smaller, a limitation the review itself recognizes.

Students should distinguish carefully among types of evidence. Firsthand testimony provides detail but may reflect individual memory and selection. Official laws show what the state prohibits and what penalties it announces, but not how consistently they are enforced. Satellite observation shows structures but not conditions. International investigative findings aggregate and cross-check these sources under legal standards, and the 2014 and 2025 reports are the strongest documentary foundations. Less reliable are single-source media accounts, anonymous intelligence claims, and sensational stories that circulate widely. The inability to verify conditions independently is itself a feature of the system, since the state restricts access, and absence of verification should not be mistaken for absence of abuse or for confirmation of every claim.

Part II — Markets, State Planning, and Everyday Economic Life

Sections 92.6–92.10 examine household survival, economic management, food security, inequality, and the continuing tensions between markets and state institutions.

Section 92.6

The Jangmadang Economy after Kim Jong Il: Markets and Household Survival

By 2011 the markets described in Chapter 83 were a permanent feature of North Korean life. Jangmadang markets sold grain, vegetables, clothing, household goods, imported electronics, and cooked food, and they operated in cities, towns, and many rural areas. Alongside licensed market spaces lay a larger world of informal exchange, including street sellers, home workshops, transport services, small-scale construction, and the resale of goods from China. Official wages in state enterprises were low and often irregular, so many households depended on market income for a large share of their consumption. How large that share was is difficult to measure, and surveys of people who left the country give estimates that vary by region and time.

Women were central to this economy. Because men were more likely to be assigned to state workplaces or the military and required to attend them regardless of pay, women often became the household traders, buying goods wholesale, carrying them to markets, and sometimes traveling long distances to arbitrage price differences. Testimony from those who left the country repeatedly describes the burden of this combination of commerce, housework, and care for children and elders. Women also faced the greatest direct contact with market regulators, whose inspections, fines, and demands for payment shaped the daily conditions of trade.

The legal status of market activity was layered. Some markets were officially sanctioned and charged fees for stalls. Other transactions were tolerated, and some were prohibited, depending on the goods, the location, and the moment. Officials and security agents often collected unofficial payments, and state institutions, including local governments and enterprises, depended on market-derived income. The government periodically tried to restrict or regulate markets, for example by limiting the hours or the goods that could be sold, and the enforcement was uneven. The coexistence of tolerance and restriction gave the state leverage, since vendors had to maintain good relations with officials.

The experience of the famine and the growth of markets changed the relationship between households and the state. Households learned that survival depended on their own networks and commerce rather than on rations, and many scholars argue that this reduced the state's moral claim as provider. Surveys of people who left the country often report a sense that officials and the state could not be relied on for food. They also report that officials themselves depended on markets, so the relationship was one of mutual dependence, in which state agents extracted payments while households sought protection or exemptions.

The defining historical question is why markets could become indispensable without producing a transition to a market-based political system. Several answers have been proposed. Markets provided survival without offering organized political voice, and traders had strong incentives to stay out of politics. The state retained control of key resources and the security apparatus. Officials profited from market activity and had an interest in preserving a system that rewarded their discretion. And the leadership retained the ability to curtail markets when it judged them politically threatening. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the next sections examine how the leadership used them in practice.

Historical Vocabulary
Jangmadang

Definition: Literally "market ground," a term for the informal and later partly legalized markets that grew after the famine of the 1990s.

Historical context: Markets expanded when public distribution failed. Authorities legalized "comprehensive markets" in the early 2000s while repeatedly attempting to restrict them.

Why it matters: The jangmadang explains how households survived, how women's economic roles changed, and why the state could not simply abolish commerce.

Potential misunderstanding: Jangmadang are not evidence of a free-market economy. They operate under state licensing, taxation, bribery, and periodic crackdowns, and the state continues to own the major means of production.

Section 92.7

Byungjin: The Simultaneous Pursuit of Economic and Nuclear Development

At a party plenary meeting at the end of March 2013, the leadership adopted the byungjin line, a term meaning "advancing side by side," which presented the building of the economy and the building of nuclear forces as simultaneous national objectives. The announcement followed the third nuclear test of February 2013 and rising tensions with the United States and South Korea. The policy echoed earlier party lines such as the parallel development of defense and the economy under Kim Il Sung, but it differed in giving priority to nuclear forces as a permanent feature of the state's security.

The official argument held that nuclear weapons would deter attack and so allow the country to reduce the burden of conventional forces and devote resources to economic construction. In this reasoning, nuclear weapons were a cheaper form of security than a large conventional military buildup. This claim was made repeatedly in official documents and speeches. It was a political argument and an economic claim, and it can be tested against what followed, which is one reason the source window below is useful.

The economic costs of the weapons programs and of the sanctions they provoked are difficult to quantify. Sanctions imposed by the United Nations Security Council, particularly the resolutions of 2016 and 2017, restricted exports of coal, iron, seafood, and textiles and limited imports of refined petroleum, constraining the country's access to foreign currency. The weapons program itself consumed materials, skilled labor, and funds, though the portion of national output devoted to it is not known. What can be said is that the policy accepted isolation from international finance and technology, and that its advocates argued the security benefits outweighed these costs.

In April 2018 a party plenary announced that the nuclear force had been completed and that the country would concentrate on economic construction, a shift that accompanied the diplomacy discussed in Chapter 93. The shift was soon reversed in emphasis after the breakdown of negotiations and the sanctions pressure that persisted, and by 2020 the leadership was again stressing military and nuclear development. At the Eighth Congress in January 2021, Kim Jong Un openly acknowledged that the previous five-year plan's goals had fallen substantially short in almost all sectors. The admission is rare in North Korean official speech and shows the distance between stated goals and results.

Byungjin should therefore be treated as a historically changing strategy rather than a fixed description of the regime. It began as an argument for combining two priorities, shifted in 2018 toward economics, and returned toward military emphasis, while from 2021 the leadership paired nuclear development with attempts at economic recentralization and regional development. Each shift responded to changing external pressures, domestic economic results, and political calculation. Students should evaluate it as a sequence of choices, with outcomes that the evidence allows only partly to be measured.

Section 92.8

Markets versus State Planning: Enterprises, Money, Prices, and Economic Control

Formally, the North Korean economy remains state-owned and planned. Enterprises receive production targets and are supposed to obtain inputs through the planning system. In practice, enterprises have long needed to find supplies, pay workers, and generate income through market channels. Reports from the 2010s describe changes, sometimes called the "June 28" and "May 30" measures, that gave agricultural work teams and enterprise managers more discretion over what to produce and how to distribute the proceeds. These changes are known from reports by outside researchers and from testimony, and the details are less clear than the general direction, which gave managers more autonomy while preserving state ownership.

A significant role was played by donju, or "masters of money," private individuals who had accumulated capital through trade, currency exchange, or other commerce and who financed enterprises, construction, and transport, usually under the cover of a state or party institution. State enterprises sometimes relied on donju capital, and donju relied on official sponsorship for legitimacy and protection. The relationship illustrates how private and state economic activity were intertwined, and why the formal distinction between public and private ownership does not describe actual practice.

Foreign currency, exchange rates, and prices were important indicators of the interaction. Households used Chinese yuan and U.S. dollars as stores of value and for transactions, and the exchange rate between the North Korean won and foreign currency fluctuated, with periods of high inflation. Officials tried to set prices and rates, but the official rates diverged from the market, and enforcement was uneven. Unofficial payments to officials were common. The state's difficulty enforcing economic regulations uniformly reflected the scarcity of resources, the dependence of officials on informal income, and the sheer number of transactions.

During the 2020s the government appears to have tried to strengthen centralized control over trade, distribution, foreign exchange, and commerce. Reports from outside analysts describe renewed efforts to channel grain sales through state outlets, to limit private trade in staples, to concentrate foreign exchange in state banks, and to restrict informal markets. An OECD analysis published in 2026 describes a renewed emphasis on centralized price-setting, foreign-exchange allocation, and restrictions on informal markets. These assessments are outside analyses based on indirect evidence and should be distinguished from official North Korean statements, which describe such measures as improvements to socialist economic management.

Why would a planned economy rely on market mechanisms and then try to recentralize them? A plausible explanation is that the state needed the productive flexibility of markets in periods of weakness and then sought to reclaim control when conditions allowed, partly for political reasons. Market power could generate independent wealth and influence, and rising prices or inequalities could produce discontent. The oscillation shows that policy responded to political calculation as well as economic efficiency, and that the effects on households depended on how strictly regulations were enforced in each place.

Historical Debate 1

Did Markets Weaken the North Korean State or Help It Survive?

The question: Markets grew beyond state control after the 1990s. Did they erode the state's authority, or did they sustain the population and the regime?

Interpretation A — Markets as evidence of failed planning: Some analysts argue that the rise of markets shows that central planning failed, undermined the state's role as provider, and spread outside information and values, creating long-term pressure for change.
Interpretation B — Markets as a survival mechanism: Others argue that markets kept the population fed, reduced pressure on the state, and therefore helped sustain the system and lowered the risk of collapse.
Interpretation C — Selective tolerance as political control: A third view stresses that the state tolerated, taxed, and restricted markets according to political calculation, using control over market access to maintain loyalty and to extract resources.

Evidence: Evidence includes testimony from people who left the country, surveys, price data from market observers, official laws and campaigns, and trade statistics. Each has limitations: testimony comes from a self-selected group, price data come from a limited number of locations, and official statements are not neutral.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and weight more than established facts: how large market income is relative to state income, how far officials depend on market-derived payments, and whether markets alter political attitudes.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether economic decentralization should be expected to produce political liberalization, and whether sanctions or engagement are likely to alter the system.

Student task: Choose one interpretation and identify the evidence in this chapter that supports it, then identify the evidence that would most challenge it. Explain whether economic decentralization necessarily leads to political liberalization.

Section 92.9

Agriculture, Public Distribution, Food Insecurity, and Rural Survival

Agriculture in North Korea is organized mainly through cooperative farms divided into work teams, with state procurement of a share of the harvest and an allocation to members. Rice and maize are major crops, with additional production of potatoes, soybeans, and vegetables. Production depends on fertilizer, irrigation, seeds, fuel, and machinery, and on weather. The country has limited arable land, and flooding and drought have repeatedly damaged harvests. Agricultural inputs have been constrained by shortages of fuel and fertilizer, and by the effects of sanctions on some imports, though humanitarian exemptions exist.

Household food supply came from several sources: state distribution, market purchase, cultivation of small private plots, assistance from relatives, and informal exchange. For much of the population, the public distribution system supplied only part of consumption, and the share varied across regions and occupations. Residents of Pyongyang and some privileged groups received more reliable supplies. Households elsewhere depended heavily on markets and on their own plots. Because market access depended on cash income, households with fewer commercial opportunities were more vulnerable to price increases.

Government policy has also shaped the food economy. Authorities periodically restricted private grain sales and attempted to direct sales through state outlets, limited the transport of food between regions, and drew on harvests for military and strategic stockpiles. The border closure after 2020 cut imports of food and agricultural inputs and reduced informal cross-border trade. Researchers disagree about the relative weight of sanctions, domestic policy, weather, and the border closure, and a single cause should not be assigned. Each affected different places and households differently.

Measurement is the central difficulty. The government does not publish reliable data, and foreign monitoring has been constrained since the border closure, when many international staff left. Estimates of harvests rely on satellite observation, assessments by international agencies, and partial reports. Estimates of malnutrition come from limited surveys. Prices reported by market observers suggest changes in affordability but are not a complete picture. Reports of food shortages have appeared at several points, but the evidence does not support equating them with the mass famine of the 1990s.

Students should therefore avoid two errors. One is to assume that conditions were uniform; rural communities in the east, along the border, in the major grain provinces, and in mountainous regions had different experiences. The other is to infer from declining availability in some places that a nationwide famine on the scale of the 1990s was occurring. The evidence supports persistent food insecurity for many households and a vulnerability to shocks, together with substantial uncertainty about the magnitude. Where evidence is thin, the responsible approach is to say so.

Section 92.10

Pyongyang, Provincial Inequality, Women, Families, and Everyday Life

North Korean society is geographically unequal. Pyongyang, the capital, receives priority in housing, electricity, food distribution, medical care, and consumer goods, and residence there is a privilege that generally requires political approval. Provincial cities, industrial regions, border areas, and agricultural districts differ widely from each other and from the capital. Border cities such as Sinuiju have greater access to Chinese goods, and regions with major industries or mines have experienced swings tied to production and trade. Electricity and transport are less reliable in many provincial areas, and access to health care and schooling varies.

Family background, political classification, occupation, and commercial connections shaped opportunity. Songbun affected access to education, party membership, and residence. Occupation mattered: workers in favored state institutions, such as the military, the security services, and some export enterprises, often had access to better supplies or perquisites. Households with trading networks, relatives abroad, or access to foreign currency could obtain more. Bribery and connections could soften the effects of low status, which meant that wealth and political position interacted and did not simply replace each other.

Women's work illustrates how these factors combined. In addition to trading in markets, women worked in factories, farms, services, and the party and women's organizations. They were responsible for most household work and childcare. Interviews with women who left the country describe harassment from officials and security agents, demands for payments, and in some cases sexual coercion, as well as strong networks of mutual aid among traders. These accounts come from a limited set of respondents, but the patterns appear consistently in different studies and in the UN reports.

Elite consumption and prominent construction contrast with these conditions. Pyongyang has seen the construction of high-rise apartment districts, a ski resort, amusement parks, and shopping facilities that state media present as achievements. Some residents and officials gained access to imported goods, restaurants, and luxury items. At the same time, reports from provincial areas describe shortages of electricity, food, and medicine. Such contrasts are not unique to North Korea, but they matter here because official ideology claims egalitarian aims, and because the state's promotion of regional development after 2024 acknowledged the gap.

This section relies on documented experiences and identified testimony, not on an invented typical household. Evidence comes from surveys and interviews with people who left the country, from reports by humanitarian agencies with limited access, from official media that show what the state wants to display, and from the UN reports. All have biases and gaps. Where the sources agree, such as on the importance of markets for women and on regional disparities, the chapter treats the finding as well supported. Where they diverge, such as on how widely severe hardship extended, the chapter notes uncertainty.

Everyday Life and Social History

Markets, Workplaces, and Family Economies

Studies based on interviews with people who left North Korea, including surveys conducted by outside researchers in South Korea, describe a common pattern of household economic management. A household might combine a state workplace assignment that paid little but conferred status and a ration entitlement, a woman's market trade or small business, a garden plot, and occasional support from relatives. Members of the household had to attend mandatory meetings, labor mobilizations, and political study sessions, and were liable to fines or penalties for absences. Households with members in border areas or with connections to traders had more access to goods.

These accounts are testimony, not a census. They should be read with attention to who is speaking: respondents tend to have come from the northern provinces near the Chinese border, to be disproportionately women, and to have left before 2020. Their descriptions tell us a great deal about the economy of the 2010s and less about conditions after the border closure. The documented patterns, such as the central role of women in markets and the importance of connections and payments to officials, are nonetheless consistent across many studies and across reports from international agencies and the UN.

Section 92.11

China, Border Commerce, Sanctions, and North Korea's External Economic Lifelines

China has been North Korea's largest trading partner by a wide margin, and the border along the Yalu and Tumen rivers is the main channel of commerce. Trade passes through crossing points such as the Sinuiju–Dandong bridge, and through smaller ports and river crossings. Imports from China have included fuel, machinery, fertilizer, food, textiles, and consumer goods, and North Korean exports have included minerals, processed goods, and textiles assembled from Chinese materials. Trade data come mainly from Chinese customs statistics, which show the formal flows but not all unofficial ones.

The commercial system combined several layers. State-controlled trading companies operated under official licenses and often under the sponsorship of party, army, or government institutions, which used trade to earn income. Private traders and intermediaries worked through these companies or on their own, often with relatives or contacts across the border. Smaller cross-border traders carried goods across borders, sometimes under permits and sometimes without. The border region developed its own economy, with markets, transport services, and communities whose livelihoods depended on trade, as well as on enforcement by officials who could permit or block it.

United Nations Security Council sanctions, especially those adopted in 2016 and 2017, restricted exports of coal, iron ore, seafood, textiles, and other goods, capped imports of refined petroleum, and required the repatriation of North Korean overseas workers. These measures, designed to reduce funding for weapons programs, affected industries that depended on exports and the supply of foreign currency. Reported effects included reductions in official exports and pressures on enterprises and trading companies. Humanitarian exemptions existed but operated unevenly, and some agencies reported delays in delivering aid. The Security Council's Panel of Experts, which monitored sanctions, ceased operating in 2024 after its mandate was not renewed.

Students should avoid attributing every economic difficulty to sanctions. Economic stagnation predated them, and the government's own decisions, including the border closure after 2020 and campaigns restricting markets, had large effects on trade and household incomes. Sanctions and domestic policy operated together, and their relative contributions are debated. Chinese trade fell sharply after the border closed in 2020 and recovered partially after 2023, a pattern that reflects the domestic decision as well as the international restrictions. The pattern is consistent with the position that both mattered.

This section concerns the domestic consequences of external economic relationships. Chapter 94 will take up the political and strategic relations among Pyongyang, Beijing, and other capitals. What matters here is that external commerce, whether official or unofficial, supplied goods, income, and resources on which both households and state institutions relied, so that border policy was also economic policy, and the border was a mechanism through which the state both enabled and restricted the circulation of goods and information.

Section 92.12

The COVID-19 Border Closure and the Intensification of Isolation, 2020–2023

In January 2020, as the novel coronavirus spread from China, the North Korean government closed its borders to travelers and sharply restricted cross-border trade. Passenger rail and air services were suspended, foreign diplomats and aid workers left, and quarantine rules were imposed within the country. The government's early decision was swift and unusually strict, and it was maintained far longer than in most countries. The legitimate public-health risk was real, since the country's medical system had limited capacity and little access to vaccines, and the leadership's concern about an uncontrolled outbreak is plausible.

The effects on trade and households were severe. Chinese customs data show a collapse in recorded trade in 2020, and reports from border areas describe the closure of markets, the loss of trading income, and shortages of imported goods and medicines. Vendors who depended on Chinese supplies lost their stock, transport workers lost income, and border communities were particularly affected. Reports described shoot-on-sight orders and the creation of buffer zones along the border, and Human Rights Watch's investigation of the closure documented expanded surveillance, new movement restrictions, and economic consequences for households. Not every claim can be independently verified, but the pattern is consistent across sources.

The government acknowledged a COVID-19 outbreak publicly in May 2022, reporting large numbers of "fever" cases and imposing nationwide lockdowns. In August 2022 it declared that the emergency had been overcome. Outside experts doubted the reported figures and the claimed rapid end of the outbreak, and the true toll has not been independently established. The official acknowledgment itself marked a reversal of the earlier claim that the country had no cases, which shows that the state adjusted its public account as events required.

Reopening was gradual. Starting in 2023, freight and some passenger links with China resumed, trade volumes began to recover, and some diplomatic and commercial exchanges resumed. Controls did not return to their previous level. Authorities maintained stricter checks on movement and goods, and the state appeared to use the period to strengthen control over markets and information. The pattern of partial reopening shows that the closure had become a tool of political management as well as public health.

Students should distinguish the public-health rationale from the longer-term political effects. The pandemic created genuine risks, and many governments imposed restrictions. What distinguishes the North Korean case is the duration, the severity of border enforcement, and the way the closure coincided with new laws against foreign culture, tighter market controls, and heightened surveillance, as examined in the next section. The closure reduced households' income and the flow of outside information at the same time, so its effects were both economic and political.

Section 92.13

Foreign Media, Mobile Phones, Youth Culture, and the Struggle over Information

Mobile phones spread widely in North Korea after the introduction of a domestic network in 2008. Most phones operate on a closed domestic network that allows calls and access to a state intranet but not the global internet, which is available only to a small number of authorized users. Computers, tablets, and removable storage devices, including USB drives and memory cards, also circulated, and the devices became channels through which foreign entertainment entered the country. The distinction between a tightly controlled domestic network and unrestricted internet access is fundamental to understanding the information environment.

Foreign media, particularly South Korean dramas, films, and music, circulated through informal commercial networks. Material was copied onto storage devices, smuggled across the Chinese border or copied within the country, and sold by traders. Testimony from people who left the country describes watching such material in private, sharing it with friends, and adopting speech and fashion from it. The scale of consumption is difficult to measure, and the testimony may overrepresent the experience of younger people in border areas and cities. The evidence nevertheless suggests that exposure was substantial enough for the government to treat it as a major threat.

The government responded with laws and campaigns. The Law on Rejecting Reactionary Thought and Culture, adopted in December 2020, prohibited the possession and distribution of foreign media, with heavy penalties. The Youth Education Guarantee Law of September 2021 sought to strengthen ideological education and discipline among young people. The Law on Protecting the Pyongyang Cultural Language, adopted in January 2023, targeted the use of South Korean speech patterns and vocabulary. Together, these measures aimed to restrict foreign cultural influences and to regulate language, dress, and behavior.

Enforcement relied on inspections, surveillance, and punishments. Reports from the UN and human-rights organizations describe house searches, inspections of phones and storage devices, public trials and denunciations, and penalties that include long-term labor sentences and, in some documented cases, executions. The 2025 UN review discusses the severity of these penalties. The exact application varies by place and time, and independent verification is limited, but the laws themselves are a documented expression of state intent, and the testimony about enforcement is consistent.

This section connects to Chapter 90, which showed South Korean popular culture spreading across the world. The North Korean government came to treat part of that same culture as a political threat. The state's objection was not only to foreign entertainment but to its implications: depictions of South Korean life, the speech and manners it spread, and the sense of a wider world that undercut official narratives. The laws show that the government considered cultural influence a matter of regime security, and that it was prepared to apply severe penalties to protect its control over information, even at the cost of additional constraints on young people's lives.

The Tightening of Control after 2020

Long-term causes

The hereditary system's dependence on information control, the expansion of markets after the famine, the spread of phones and storage media, and the gap between official promises and living conditions.

Short-term causes

Sanctions pressure, the failure of the economic plan acknowledged in 2021, circulation of foreign media among the young, and the pandemic emergency.

The January 2020 border closure, which created a public-health justification for strict movement controls.

Transformation

New laws on foreign culture and youth, expanded surveillance, tighter border enforcement, and efforts to restrict markets.

Collapsed border trade, lost household income, increased risk of severe penalties for consuming foreign media, and reduced flows of information and goods.

Long-term consequences

Narrower personal freedoms, a lasting recentralization effort, and the 2025 UN finding of deterioration, alongside later partial economic recovery.

Historical complexity: Public-health reasons, political calculation, and economic motives were intertwined, and the evidence does not allow historians to give each a precise weight. Reducing the outcome to a single cause would ignore this combination.

Section 92.14

Nuclear Weapons, Missiles, and the Transformation of North Korea's Military Strategy

Chapter 86 traced the nuclear program to the early 2000s and the first tests in 2006 and 2009. Under Kim Jong Un the program accelerated. North Korea conducted a third nuclear test in February 2013, two tests in 2016, and a sixth test in September 2017, which it described as a hydrogen bomb. The 2017 test produced the largest seismic signal of any North Korean test, and outside analysts judged it consistent with a significantly more powerful device, though the exact nature of the device is not publicly established. The tests were detected by seismic monitoring stations worldwide, so their occurrence is well established even though their technical details are not.

Missile development paralleled the tests. North Korea launched intermediate-range and intercontinental ballistic missiles in 2017, including the Hwasong-14 and Hwasong-15, and the government declared in November 2017 that it had completed its nuclear force. Later years saw tests of newer intercontinental systems, the introduction of solid-fuel missiles, which can be launched with less preparation, submarine-launched missiles, and efforts to diversify launch platforms. In 2023 the country launched a reconnaissance satellite after earlier failures. Tested capabilities, claimed capabilities, and independent assessments differ, and students should note which is being cited at each point.

The legal and political treatment of nuclear weapons also changed. In September 2022 the Supreme People's Assembly adopted a law on nuclear-force policy that defined the conditions for use, including in response to attack and in some circumstances preemptively, and declared the country's nuclear status irreversible. In September 2023 the assembly amended the constitution to incorporate the nuclear-force policy. These steps mean that nuclear forces were embedded in the state's formal legal structure and national identity, and that the leadership publicly rejected any prospect of denuclearization as a negotiating objective.

Nuclear weapons became central to regime security calculations. The leadership repeatedly argued that nuclear forces provided protection against external attack and invasion, citing the fates of other states that gave up weapons programs. Whether the weapons actually prevent attack cannot be proven, since deterrence involves a counterfactual, but the belief that they do shaped policy. The weapons also served domestic purposes, as symbols of national strength and of the leader's competence, and as justification for sacrifices. By 2026 the regime had reiterated its commitment to expanding nuclear capabilities, and U.S. assessments described continuing improvements in missile range and sophistication.

The chapter distinguishes between three kinds of evidence. Tests, which are observable, establish that a device was detonated or a missile launched. Official statements and laws declare objectives and doctrines but may exaggerate performance. Independent assessments, by governments and research institutions, estimate the size and quality of the arsenal and the reliability of delivery systems, with substantial uncertainty. Independent estimates of warhead numbers vary, commonly in the range of several dozen. The full history of the nuclear crisis belongs to Chapter 86 and the diplomatic consequences to Chapter 93.

Section 92.15

Sanctions Evasion, Overseas Revenue, Cyber Operations, and the Costs of Militarization

Facing sanctions, the North Korean state sought foreign currency and materials through several channels. These included overseas labor arrangements, with workers sent to countries such as China and Russia, state-linked trading entities, ship-to-ship transfers of goods at sea, and the use of foreign companies and intermediaries. The UN Panel of Experts documented many such activities in reports until its mandate ended in 2024, and various governments have issued their own findings and enforcement actions. The reports describe methods in broad terms, and this chapter does the same, because its purpose is historical and institutional.

Cyber activity has also been attributed to North Korea. Governments and security firms have attributed major thefts of funds, including from cryptocurrency platforms, to North Korean state-linked groups, and U.S. authorities attributed the February 2025 Bybit theft to such groups. These attributions come from government investigations and private analyses, and they rest on technical indicators that are not always publicly disclosed. They should be treated as official or expert findings, which vary in strength, and not all incidents attributed to North Korea have been conclusively proven.

It is difficult to trace the funds. Revenue from illicit or semi-legal activities passes through intermediaries, front companies, and accounts in several jurisdictions. The share directed to weapons programs, as opposed to the leadership's consumption or to civilian needs, is not publicly known, and estimates differ. Analysts use shipping data, financial records, and defector accounts, each with limits. The correct historical statement is that the state sought external revenue through many channels, and that the evidence supports the general pattern more firmly than it supports particular totals.

The opportunity costs of militarization are a recurring theme. Resources used for weapons, including materials, skilled labor, and fuel, are not available for civilian needs. Sanctions, in turn, limit the availability of imports. Household welfare depended on all of these, as well as on weather, trade, and policy. The evidence does not allow a precise accounting, but it supports the observation that strategic priorities and civilian needs competed for scarce resources, as the leadership itself implied in 2021 when it acknowledged economic shortfalls.

Students should keep several categories distinct. Allegations are claims by governments or researchers that have not been tested in court. Official sanctions findings are conclusions by international or national bodies, often with documented evidence. Criminal investigations produce indictments and sometimes convictions. Independently corroborated evidence is information confirmed by multiple unrelated sources. Many statements about North Korean external revenue belong in the first two categories. This section is not an operational account of cyber activity or procurement methods, and it does not need to be, since its purpose is to explain how the state's external behavior related to its domestic priorities.

Section 92.16

Economic Recovery after Isolation: Production, Trade, and the Limits of Statistical Knowledge

After the worst period of COVID-era isolation, outside estimates indicate that North Korea's economy recovered. Cross-border trade with China resumed and rose, construction expanded in Pyongyang and in provincial projects, and manufacturing, mining, and services appear to have increased. Agriculture remained a continuing difficulty, since harvests depend on weather and inputs, and household consumption lagged behind aggregate production according to several assessments. The recovery was partial and uneven, and its features differed from those of earlier growth episodes because trade now ran through a more tightly controlled system.

The most widely cited estimates come from South Korea's central bank. On July 31, 2026, the Bank of Korea announced its estimate for 2025; on August 12, it posted its updated series of North Korean economic statistics with 2020 as the base year. The series estimates real GDP growth of −0.2% in 2022, +3.1% in 2023, +3.7% in 2024, and +3.5% in 2025. These are not North Korea's official national accounts. The Bank constructs estimates using indirect production, trade, and other evidence, valued according to South Korean prices and value-added ratios. The figures permit comparison within the Bank's series but should not be treated as internationally interchangeable GDP measurements or as direct observations of North Korean household welfare; they are subject to revision.

Section 92.17

The Regional Development 20×10 Policy and the Campaign to Improve Provincial Living Standards

In January 2024 Kim Jong Un announced the Regional Development 20×10 Policy, under which modern industrial facilities were to be built in twenty counties each year for ten years. The program was a response to the stark gap between Pyongyang and the provinces. It was presented as a means of improving living standards outside the capital by establishing local factories for food, consumer goods, building materials, and other products, along with related facilities in agriculture and other sectors. State media reported on construction starts and completions, and Kim made visits to sites.

The program involved central direction of resources, including construction materials and labor mobilized through campaigns. Local factories were intended to use regional raw materials and to supply local markets and institutions, reducing the dependence of provinces on Pyongyang. The political purposes were evident: the program projected Kim Jong Un as a leader addressing inequality beyond the showcase capital, and it linked the leadership to material improvements at the local level, in keeping with the people-first language introduced earlier. Evidence of progress comes mostly from official media, which show completed buildings and ceremonies.

The difference between completing buildings and establishing viable production is important. A factory requires reliable electricity, raw materials, equipment, trained workers, transport, and distribution channels, and a way to pay workers and sell goods. Many provincial areas have unreliable power and limited transport. Without these supports, new buildings may produce little. Outside analysts note that evidence about actual output, employment, and the distribution of goods is scarce, so judgments about success rest on limited information. The record of earlier campaigns, in which announced goals were unmet, also counsels caution.

The February 2026 Ninth Party Congress renewed the emphasis on regional industrial development in its 2026–2030 plan, indicating that the leadership regarded the policy as a continuing priority. The central question is whether centralized investment can reduce regional inequality without broader changes in markets, infrastructure, and economic institutions. Supporters of the approach point to the state's capacity to mobilize resources. Skeptics note that such mobilization has limits and that provincial living standards depend on commerce, which the state was simultaneously trying to restrict. The evidence through November 2026 is insufficient to resolve the matter.

Section 92.18

North Korea's Russian Partnership and the Changing Material Foundations of State Power

After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, North Korea and Russia developed a rapidly deepening military and political relationship. On June 19, 2024, during a visit by Vladimir Putin to Pyongyang, the two countries signed a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty that included provisions for mutual military assistance if either were attacked. Congressional Research Service documentation records the treaty and describes the substantial military relationship that had emerged by 2025–2026. This chapter examines primarily the effects on North Korea's own institutions, economy, and politics. Chapter 94 provides the full strategic treatment.

Governments and monitoring bodies reported that North Korea supplied Russia with munitions and missiles, and that North Korean troops were deployed to Russia in 2024. On April 28, 2025, Pyongyang publicly acknowledged the deployment, after Russia's own acknowledgment days earlier. Reported numbers of troops and casualties vary by source, and estimates of the quantity of munitions supplied also vary. The existence of the deployment is confirmed by the governments' own statements, while the scale and the terms are subject to estimation by South Korean, Ukrainian, American, and other analysts.

The economic effects are difficult to measure. Possible benefits include payments in currency or goods, access to food and fuel, commercial opportunities, and military or technical assistance, while the costs include demands on domestic production of munitions and the human costs of casualties. Estimates of payments and of technology transfers are disputed, and some claims rest on intelligence assessments that cannot be publicly verified. Confirmed activities should be distinguished from disputed ones, and this section accordingly treats the existence of the partnership and troop deployment as established and the quantities and terms as uncertain.

The partnership also had domestic political effects. The leadership publicly celebrated the troops and framed the war as a defense of an ally, giving the military a role in national narratives and potentially reducing the sanctions pressure on the state's finances. By providing an external source of resources and a market for military production, the relationship altered the material foundations of state power, and it reduced the leverage that sanctions and Chinese trade had given others. How durable these effects are is unknown, since they depend on the course of the war and on Russian policy, and the section does not assume that the partnership will continue.

Section 92.19

The Ninth Workers' Party Congress and Kim Jong Un's Development Program, February 2026

The Ninth Congress of the Workers' Party of Korea met from February 19 to 25, 2026. Its functions included reviewing the preceding five-year period, adjusting the leadership bodies, and setting economic and military priorities for 2026–2030. Kim Jong Un continued as general secretary. Reports available to this chapter derive from North Korean announcements as summarized by international institutions, including a United Nations summary, and not from a full original transcript, so details should be treated as official claims and cross-checked against later primary publications.

The announced program emphasized economic stabilization and development, including industrial sectors such as metallurgy, chemistry, electrical power, coal, and machinery, as well as agriculture, infrastructure, and the continuation of the Regional Development 20×10 Policy. It also included military modernization. The plan therefore combined the sectoral priorities of earlier campaigns with the regional program of 2024, and with continued emphasis on defense, in a form consistent with the changing byungjin approach described in Section 92.7.

The relationship between the new plan and earlier ones is a central question. In 2021 the leadership had admitted that the previous plan fell far short. A new plan with similar sectoral targets therefore invites questions about what had changed. Possible changes include the recovery of trade, the Russian partnership, tighter central control, and the leadership's institutional consolidation. These are hypotheses, and the actual results will become visible only over the plan period. Students should compare announcements with later outcomes, as the 2021 admission shows is possible.

The revised Workers' Party charter, adopted at the Ninth Congress in February 2026, became available for detailed outside examination when South Korea's Institute for National Security Strategy, a government-affiliated research institute, disclosed the text on September 3, 2026. Its analysis reported that the provisions more explicitly authorized the party general secretary to convene key meetings, appoint and dismiss senior officials, and dissolve party organizations or departments. The charter also removed language concerning Korean reunification, whose diplomatic implications are reserved for Chapter 93. An October 15, 2026 scholarly analysis argued that these provisions largely codified authority Kim Jong Un already exercised rather than demonstrating a sudden expansion of effective power. The available text supports conclusions about formal rules, not a direct reconstruction of secret leadership deliberations or proof that the rules constrained the leader.

The public appearances of Kim Jong Un's daughter, commonly identified as Kim Ju Ae, have raised questions about dynastic continuity. She first appeared in state media in November 2022 and has accompanied her father at military and ceremonial events. Some outside analysts, including South Korean intelligence, have considered her a possible successor. No official designation of a successor has been documented, and the chapter does not assume that one has been chosen. Public appearances alone do not establish a completed succession plan.

Section 92.20

Kim Jong Un's North Korea in November 2026: Consolidation, Adaptation, and Contradiction

Fifteen years after the succession, Kim Jong Un held the top positions in party, state, and military, and no organized challenge to his authority had been publicly documented. The Workers' Party had regained a central place in governance, elite networks inherited from the previous era had been dismantled or restructured, and the security apparatus remained powerful. Dynastic legitimacy, once dependent on his father's memory, had been supplemented by an extensive record of ceremonies, construction, and military display. The consolidation of hereditary authority is the most firmly supported conclusion of the chapter.

The economy showed continued reliance on markets and household activity, alongside renewed efforts to restore centralized control. Outside estimates indicated a recovery after the isolation, and the Ninth Congress set a plan that stressed industry and regional development. Yet persistent food insecurity, regional disparities, and the opportunity costs of military priorities remained, and the leadership itself had acknowledged unmet goals in 2021. The country's economic position was shaped by sanctions, its own restrictions, Chinese trade, and a new partnership with Russia, and the evidence available does not support either a prediction of collapse or a prediction of transformation.

Information control, political detention, and restrictions on movement and expression were stronger in many respects than before 2020, according to the 2025 UN review and other sources. The laws on foreign culture and the treatment of the young showed the leadership's perception of cultural influence as a threat. Nuclear capabilities had expanded and had been written into law and the constitution, and the military partnership with Russia added a material and political dimension that the diplomatic history of Chapters 93 and 94 examines in more detail.

The chapter's analytical conclusion distinguishes four forms of regime strength. The first is the ability to maintain elite cohesion, which the record of consolidation supports. The second is the ability to suppress organized opposition, supported by the documented apparatus of surveillance and punishment. The third is the ability to mobilize resources, shown in construction campaigns, weapons programs, and the commitments to Russia. The fourth is the ability to improve civilian welfare, where the evidence is mixed and the state's own admissions point to shortfalls. Success in the first three does not establish success in the fourth.

The final historical judgment is therefore that Kim Jong Un had consolidated a durable hereditary authoritarian system by 2026, but that political continuity and military development coexisted with persistent economic constraints, social inequality, and extensive restrictions on individual freedom. North Korea's continued existence through 2026 is a historical fact, and its future is uncertain. Chapter 96 will place this account beside that of South Korea in a comparative synthesis.

  • 2013: Byungjin was adopted, a third nuclear test was held, and Jang Song Thaek was executed, showing consolidation by coercion.
  • 2017: The party's primacy had been restored through the 2016 Congress, and a sixth nuclear test and long-range missiles supported the claim of a completed nuclear force.

  • Dependence of households on markets and informal economic activity
  • Surveillance, political classification, and restrictions on movement and information
  • Sanctions pressure and reliance on external lifelines

Elites experienced change most directly, since officials were rotated and sometimes punished, while many rural and market households experienced greater continuity in the basic problem of securing a living. The pattern matters because it shows a system that adapted at the margins while preserving its core.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Kim Jong Un inherited and consolidated a hereditary authoritarian state after December 2011. The central argument is that dynastic succession did not automatically produce stable control. The official announcement of Kim Jong Il's death presented continuity, but practical authority had to be built through appointments, institutional reorganization, ideological campaigns, and coercion.

Within a few years the new leader held the top offices of party, state, and military. The 2013 execution of Jang Song Thaek and the 2017 killing of Kim Jong Nam show how rivals and potential rivals were removed, though the evidence supports firm conclusions about some elements (the fact of the execution, the method of the killing, the judicial outcomes) and not others (the motives and orders behind them). Elite turnover and exemplary punishment kept officials dependent on the leader.

Institutionally, the Workers' Party regained a central place after the Songun era. The congresses of 2016 and 2021, the creation of the State Affairs Commission, and the Ninth Congress of 2026 gave institutional form to the leadership while preserving concentrated authority. The existence of assemblies, commissions, and procedures did not create pluralism or independent accountability.

Ideology and propaganda placed Kim Jong Un within a sacred family genealogy while adding claims of responsibility for development. These materials are evidence of what the state wished citizens to believe and not of what they believed. The same applies to surveillance and detention, documented most authoritatively in the UN Commission of Inquiry report of 2014 and the review of 2025, which found that conditions had not improved and in many respects had worsened.

Economically, markets remained indispensable to household survival, and women played a central role in them. The state tolerated, taxed, restricted, and periodically tried to recentralize commerce. Markets did not produce political liberalization, because they gave survival without political voice, and because the state retained control over resources and enforcement. The historical debate over whether markets weakened or sustained the regime has no settled answer.

Byungjin tied nuclear development to economic construction in 2013, was revised toward economics in 2018, and returned toward military emphasis, while the Eighth Congress admitted that earlier economic goals had fallen well short. Nuclear weapons were written into law in 2022 and the constitution in 2023, becoming part of state identity. Students should distinguish tests, official claims, and independent assessments, and should treat the economic cost of the nuclear strategy as a matter of debate.

External pressure and domestic policy interacted. Sanctions restricted trade and finance, while the border closure of 2020 had severe economic and political effects that cannot be attributed to sanctions. New laws against foreign culture, adopted in 2020, 2021, and 2023, show that the state regarded outside information, including South Korean culture, as a threat to its control, and enforcement included severe penalties according to UN and human-rights reports.

After 2023 outside estimates indicated economic recovery, but these estimates measure aggregate output and cannot show household welfare. The Regional Development 20×10 Policy sought to improve provincial conditions, but completing factory buildings is different from establishing viable production. The Russian partnership, formalized in 2024 and followed by acknowledged troop deployment in 2025, altered the material and political foundations of state power, though its terms and scale remain uncertain.

The chapter distinguished four forms of regime strength: elite cohesion, suppression of opposition, resource mobilization, and improvement of civilian welfare. The evidence supports the first three more firmly than the fourth. By 2026 the system was durable, but the contradictions between military priorities, economic development, political control, and the welfare of the population had not been overcome.

Throughout, the chapter has emphasized evidence. Official statements, external statistics, testimony, and international investigations each answer different questions, and none is sufficient alone. It has also avoided predictions of collapse or transformation, since neither is supported by the evidence through November 2026.

Workers sent abroad to earn foreign currency for the state, restricted by UN sanctions from 2017.

Efforts to restore state control over trade, prices, and foreign exchange.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows a hereditary state surviving its third leadership transition and adapting without changing its basic structure. The processes it describes, including consolidation of personal authority, selective tolerance of markets, and tightening of information control, help explain why predictions of collapse repeatedly proved premature, while also showing that survival is not the same as welfare.

It also shows the importance of evidence. Because the state restricts access, historians must combine official statements, external statistics, testimony, and international investigations, each with its own limits. The habit of asking what a source can and cannot establish applies throughout this textbook, and nowhere more than here.

The developments examined here shaped the context for Chapters 93 and 94, which treat diplomacy and wider strategic relationships, and they set up the comparison of Chapter 96. The continuities with earlier chapters are strong: the dynastic claims of Chapter 77, the markets of Chapter 83, and the nuclear program of Chapter 86 all reappear in new forms.

Finally, the chapter belongs to the book's commitment to treat North and South Korea as parallel histories. North Korea has its own internal dynamics, which should be understood on their own terms and not merely as a foreign-policy problem. Understanding those dynamics, including the contradictions that remained unresolved in November 2026, is necessary for any serious account of the divided peninsula.
