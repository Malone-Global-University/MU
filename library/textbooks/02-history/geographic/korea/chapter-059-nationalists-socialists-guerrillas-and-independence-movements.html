Nationalists, Socialists, Guerrillas, and Independence Movements

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Korean resistance to Japanese rule was never one organization or one unbroken line of battles. It was a fragmented but interconnected struggle, conducted in courtrooms, classrooms, Manchurian valleys, Shanghai lodgings, Siberian garrisons, and Hawaiian fundraising halls.

After the March First Movement of 1919 (Chapter 56), activists faced a harder question than whether to resist: how to sustain resistance over decades against a state with police, courts, railways, and an army. Their answers differed. Some pursued diplomacy and legal organization, some preparation through education and economic self-strengthening, some clandestine party-building, some assassination and sabotage, and some armed units on the Manchurian frontier. These strategies were not merely tactical. Each carried a vision of what an independent Korea should be: a liberal republic, a socialist society, a stateless federation of mutual aid, or a restored nation under uncertain institutions.

This chapter traces those movements from 1919 to the eve of the full-scale Sino-Japanese War in 1937. It asks why nationalists, socialists, anarchists, students, women, and guerrillas sometimes cooperated and often divided, and why none of their strategies produced independence before a wider regional war transformed the situation. It distinguishes anti-colonial resistance, which could aim at many immediate goals, from full independence, which was not the declared objective of every reformist or local campaign.

The evidence is uneven. Much of what historians know about underground networks survives in colonial police and court records, which record resisters through the eyes of their pursuers. Declarations, newspapers, memoirs, and organizational papers survive in other collections, but memoirs and commemorations were often shaped by later political needs, including the rival memories of South and North Korea after 1945. The chapter treats those later narratives as historical phenomena in their own right, not as neutral descriptions of the colonial period.

Chapter 59 concentrates on interwar organization and interaction. Detailed treatment of the Provisional Government, the Korean Liberation Army, communist guerrilla biographies, and wartime legitimacy claims belongs to Chapter 63; Japan's Manchurian expansion belongs to Chapter 61; the economic and demographic setting of migration is in Chapter 58.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the aftermath of 1919 forced activists to reassess strategy.
  2. Compare diplomacy, cultural nationalism, united-front politics, revolutionary violence, and guerrilla warfare.
  3. Distinguish nationalist, communist, and anarchist programs for a future Korea.
  4. Analyze the Provisional Government's difficulties, including the 1923 conference and Rhee Syng-man's 1925 removal.
  5. Trace Shinganhoe from its 1927 founding to its 1931 dissolution.
  6. Interpret the Gwangju student movement as both a local incident and a national movement.
  7. Evaluate conflicting casualty claims for the 1920 Manchurian campaigns.
  8. Assess civilian consequences of armed resistance, including the Gando massacres.
  9. Distinguish the separate armed organizations active in the 1920s and 1930s.
  10. Contextualize Korean resistance within Chinese, Soviet, Japanese, and American politics.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did shared opposition to colonial rule not produce a unified movement?
  2. How did legal and underground resistance depend on, and endanger, one another?
  3. Why were guerrilla operations inseparable from civilian communities?
  4. How did geography and jurisdiction shape what activists could attempt?
  5. Why do casualty figures for 1920 differ, and what makes a battle successful?
  6. How have later political memories reshaped the colonial-era record?
  7. Why did united fronts attract activists yet repeatedly collapse?

Historical Context

The Government-General had responded to 1919 with a shift to so-called cultural rule: limited Korean-language newspapers, associations, and schools, alongside an expanded police apparatus (Chapters 55 and 57). This created narrow legal space for organizing and sharpened the divide between those who used it and those who rejected it. The Russian Revolution of 1917 introduced socialist ideas and Comintern networks, while Wilsonian rhetoric of self-determination had raised, and then disappointed, expectations of foreign support.

Korean communities abroad had grown through migration (Chapter 58). By 1919 substantial Korean farming settlements existed in Gando and the wider Manchurian borderland, in the Russian Far East, and in Japan; smaller exile communities existed in Shanghai and the United States. These communities supplied recruits, money, and refuge, but lived under Chinese, Russian-Soviet, or Japanese jurisdiction, each with its own interests. Japanese consular police and extradition pressure followed activists across borders.

Division I · §§59.1–59.5

Resistance after 1919: Geography, Organization, and Competing Strategies

59.1 The Independence Struggle after the March First Movement

Mass demonstrations had shown the breadth of Korean discontent without producing independence. Colonial forces suppressed the protests, the Paris peace settlement did not take up the Korean case, and the Government-General turned to a reformed but still authoritarian policy of cultural rule (Chapters 55 and 56). Activists therefore faced a long struggle. Many shifted from improvised mobilization toward durable organization, and the early 1920s were a period of institution-building, disillusion, and argument over means and ends.

The failure of the independence demonstrations to secure an international settlement created a strategic crisis rather than a disappearance of national sentiment. Activists compared what the movement had demonstrated—broad popular willingness to protest—with what it had lacked: means to compel Japan to withdraw. The new colonial policy after 1919 permitted selected newspapers and associations, yet political police and prosecution remained central instruments of rule. Legal openings could therefore support public discussion, fundraising, and education while leaving organizations vulnerable to intervention. In exile, the Provisional Government's republican claim created a different problem: how to exercise authority without territory, taxation, or diplomatic recognition. The distinction between symbolic legitimacy and organizational capacity runs through this chapter. Responses developed at different speeds. Military units assembled along the northern border; intellectuals argued for national reconstruction; communists proposed a combined anti-imperial and class struggle; students developed networks in schools; and overseas associations sought contributions and foreign sympathy. These were not merely stages in a single movement. They were competing answers formed under different jurisdictions and social conditions. Nor did all activists seek exactly the same postcolonial state. A meaningful history must ask both what each movement opposed and the institutions it hoped to create. The inability to impose a common program became a recurring weakness, but debates also generated political thought and institutions with a history extending beyond Japanese rule.

Historical change did not unfold uniformly across the early 1920s. The same movement that gave rise to an exile government also stimulated armed units, local improvement societies and socialist study circles. Where a public meeting could be licensed, activists often worked within cultural-rule regulations; where Japanese military and police power was closer, they relied on private association and secrecy. Even the memory of March First became contested political property, because organizations appealed to its legitimacy while disputing who could speak for its participants. This divergence is the setting for the attempts at national conferences and united fronts examined below.

59.2 The Geography of Korean Resistance: Peninsula, Manchuria, China, Russia, Japan, and America

Where an organization stood determined what it could do. In Gyeongseong, the colonial capital, groups worked within colonial law or underground, under the eyes of a large police force. In Manchuria, armed units relied on Korean farming villages and on the tolerance or weakness of local Chinese authorities. In Shanghai, exiles operated in the foreign concessions, where they enjoyed limited protection. In the Russian Far East, activists confronted Soviet policy, and in Japan, students and laborers organized within the metropole itself. In Hawaii and North America, organizers raised money and lobbied. These jurisdictions each imposed different legal risks and offered different resources.

Borders mattered because Korean political communities crossed them before 1919. Northern farmers had migrated into Gando and the Amnok valley; merchants and students lived in Japan; exiles made Shanghai and Beijing centers for journalism and conferences; and Korean associations raised money in Hawaii and the continental United States. A single political statement might thus circulate among several audiences and pass through Japanese, Chinese, Russian, English, and Korean languages. In Manchuria a guerrilla could rely on village support yet face Chinese military officials and Japanese consular demands. In Shanghai diplomatic activity was possible, but concession authorities did not confer immunity from arrest or expulsion. In Japan organizing was legally closer to the imperial center and exposed Koreans to anti-Korean discrimination. These differences produced distinct political cultures. American associations debated representation and fundraising; Russian-based activists confronted Bolshevik institutions and civil-war conflict; Chinese-based exiles bargained with Chinese parties and commanders; domestic students balanced public school campaigns against clandestine socialist study. Geography was therefore not background. It shaped the size of organizations, the materials they could publish, the alliances they needed, and the kinds of evidence that survived. A map of independence organizations must depict overlapping jurisdictions and political connections rather than a tidy national headquarters directing everyone else.

The distinction between eastern Manchuria's Tumen River settlements and the southern Amnok borderland is especially important. Those areas differed in routes of access to Korea and in the local Chinese authorities with whom military organizations had to negotiate. Shanghai was not simply 'China' in the same sense: its divided municipal jurisdictions affected exile politics. The Russian Far East changed dramatically as the civil war and revolutionary regime transformed local control. A historically sound map should therefore identify the specific jurisdiction and approximate date, not draw one stable border around the independence movement as if it possessed sovereign territory.

59.3 Competing Paths to Independence

Diplomacy sought recognition but required foreign governments willing to act, and none was. Cultural self-strengthening built schools, newspapers, and enterprises but operated under censorship and could be criticized as accommodation. Clandestine party-building promised social revolution at the cost of constant arrest. Revolutionary violence aimed to inspire and intimidate but invited reprisal. Independence armies required bases, weapons, and recruits, and guerrilla warfare required civilian cooperation. None of these approaches was exclusive, and individuals moved between them.

Diplomacy had an evident attraction: if powerful governments recognized a Korean republic, an unarmed movement might gain political leverage beyond the reach of Japanese colonial police. The Paris and Washington conferences instead exposed the limits of appealing to international principle when other powers prioritized their relations with Japan. Cultural self-strengthening offered a longer route through education, language, economic enterprise, and public association. Critics asked whether this preserved a nation or normalized life under continuing Japanese sovereignty. Others proposed a rapid revolutionary break, but there was no agreement on the revolution's political and social end state. Mass political organization required legal space and broad participation; armed resistance required territorial bases, supplies, and support from civilians whose exposure to retaliation could be severe. Socialist parties emphasized workers, tenants, and class relations, while nationalist formations varied between broad alliances and exclusion of class politics. Individuals also changed course. The existence of hybrid careers cautions against placing every person in an unchanging ideological box. In evaluating a strategy, students should separate declared objective, means, attainable constituency, actual results, and unintended consequences. A temporary organizational gain need not be equivalent to independence, and failure to secure sovereignty by 1937 does not erase political achievements.

The boundaries between strategies can be tested using actual careers. Ahn Chang-ho worked for education and organizational strengthening while retaining an independence commitment. Kim Gu operated within a constitutional exile institution while also sponsoring direct action. Uiyeoldan cadres later entered political coalition-building and Chinese military training. These transitions were responses to changing conditions, not evidence that all methods were equally effective. The real question is whether a movement gained resources, broadened participation, or secured a political advantage that lasted. Historical evaluation also requires looking at who absorbed the danger when a tactic prompted arrests, punitive raids or tightened surveillance.

59.4 How Independence Organizations Functioned

Resistance organizations lived on membership dues, donations, and bond sales, and on couriers, printing presses, and trusted intermediaries. Distance and surveillance made coordination slow and insecure, and messages could take weeks. Disagreements over money, leadership, and credit were chronic. The discussion in this chapter concerns the political and institutional history of these organizations, not their operational techniques.

Exile institutions were costly. Offices required rent and staff; publications demanded paper and distribution; representatives traveled between cities with uncertain funds; soldiers needed continuous maintenance. Overseas donations and independence bonds could be politically important, but the terms of their collection and distribution provoked disputes about legitimacy. The Provisional Government could call itself a national authority without possessing the ordinary coercive powers of a state. Military bodies often governed their own communities, while students and labor organizers maintained separate memberships and political obligations. Financial arrangements were therefore a test of who claimed to speak for the nation. Communications also shaped the reliability of leadership. Messages transmitted through intermediaries could arrive after decisions had changed. Arrests disrupted correspondents, and different groups could understand a shared resolution differently. The Yeontongje network exemplified both the ambition of coordinating a government abroad with supporters inside Korea and the vulnerability of such links to police discovery. Surviving papers usually overrepresent the moment of crisis: an arrest produced records, whereas ordinary correspondence may have vanished. Describing resistance through its institutions clarifies why capable individuals still struggled to produce stable organizations.

Organizational claims deserve the same scrutiny as battlefield claims. A statement of membership may include dues-paying adults, nominal supporters, local chapters or participants in several overlapping groups. An independence bond is evidence of a promise of future repayment by a prospective sovereign authority, not proof that it functioned like a state bond in an independent capital market. A letter naming a distant representative does not establish that the recipient could actually enforce decisions there. These questions help students distinguish the impressive reach of interwar political networks from their uneven institutional capacity.

59.5 Colonial Intelligence, Border Enforcement, and the Risks of Resistance

Japanese authorities relied on a dense police system, informants, consular officers, and extradition requests, and negotiated agreements with Chinese local authorities to limit Korean activity. In practice enforcement varied: Chinese officials sometimes tolerated Korean groups, sometimes cooperated with Japan, depending on local politics. The same frontier that sheltered activists also exposed them to cross-border pursuit.

The Government-General's political police classified activists and followed organizations through publications, association meetings, denunciations, and arrests. Japanese consular officials also monitored Korean communities in China, sometimes urging local military authorities to restrain anti-Japanese activity. The ability to operate across a border did not guarantee security. Chinese authorities changed their policies according to local conditions, pressure from Japan, and their own rivalries; in the Russian Far East, revolutionary institutions regarded Korean armed forces in relation to their larger military and diplomatic objectives. The same external power could provide support one year and insist on subordination the next. The documentary result is unusually complicated. Police reports preserve membership lists, meeting accounts, seized literature, and interrogation summaries that no longer survive in activist archives. Yet these records were generated by an adversary whose administrative purpose was to identify, prosecute, and classify. An activist's later memoir offers an alternative perspective but can reflect personal vindication or postliberation politics. The historian must compare types of evidence and avoid treating a successful police prosecution as proof of every allegation it made. Censorship and surveillance also help explain why legal declarations sometimes used abstract terms where participants privately intended stronger political claims.

A useful comparison is between a colonial police report and a contemporary Korean-language newspaper article about the same demonstration. The report may provide arrest records and the names of organizations but classify dissent as a security issue; the newspaper may foreground grievances while excluding prohibited slogans. Neither can simply be dismissed. Taken together they establish how the state and participants understood the event differently. This also explains why materials from the Japanese Foreign Ministry, court files, Korean exile publications and local Chinese archives all matter. The historian's task is to combine them without treating all records as equally independent.

Division II · §§59.6–59.11

Nationalist Movements and the Provisional Government’s Political Difficulties

59.6 The Korean Provisional Government after Its Establishment in 1919

The Provisional Government, founded in Shanghai in April 1919 and reorganized later that year by merging rival bodies, claimed to represent the Korean republic proclaimed by the March First Movement (Chapter 56). That claim carried real symbolic weight for many Koreans at home and abroad. Its practical authority was thin. It controlled no territory, lacked an army of its own, depended on contributions it could not compel, and operated in the Shanghai French Concession at the tolerance of French authorities, who could be pressed by Japanese diplomats. Its leaders disagreed on strategy and on how much authority the presidency should hold.

The September 1919 constitutional consolidation gave the Provisional Government a presidential framework and a claim to speak for an independent republic. This claim reached beyond its actual reach. Its offices in Shanghai could issue declarations and seek diplomatic contacts, but could not enforce decisions uniformly among Korean communities in Manchuria, Russia, Japan, or America. Leaders had different personal constituencies. Some derived authority from the independence movement inside Korea; others from earlier exile politics, military service, or overseas fundraising organizations. The government's constitutional vocabulary expressed a national project while its everyday operations resembled those of an underfunded exile association. Repeated political crises should be understood in that setting. The institution needed to make credible decisions about finance, military relations, diplomacy, and representation, but its leaders disagreed about who could authorize such decisions and how far the presidency extended. Problems grew after the hope of quick international intervention faded. Some activists proposed expanding a national assembly of resistance groups; others insisted that the established republic be preserved. Government documents provide valuable evidence of constitutional argument, but they do not automatically represent people inside Korea who could not vote or delegate representatives freely. The Provisional Government's endurance and its limited effectiveness must be assessed together.

A constitution provides a powerful symbol precisely when a movement lacks the normal features of statehood. The provisional authorities could legislate aspirations about citizenship or republican leadership, but they had no national courts or administrative departments functioning throughout Korea. Rival military units recognized the Shanghai government to varying degrees, and American organizations retained their own fundraising procedures. The constitutional disputes of the 1920s were therefore disputes about scarce practical resources as well as legal doctrine. Students should ask what a government could accomplish through a decree when compliance ultimately depended on voluntary cooperation.

59.7 Independence Funding, Overseas Contributions, and Communications with Korea

The government sold independence bonds, collected contributions from Koreans in the United States, China, and Manchuria, and built a confidential communications network with the peninsula to relay funds and instructions. The network, known as the Yeontongje, was uncovered by Japanese police in the early 1920s, and arrests cut the government’s links with domestic supporters. Overseas fundraising also created rival claims: money raised in the United States, especially through bodies associated with Rhee Syng-man, became a point of dispute over who controlled it and to whom it was accountable.

The Yeontongje, sometimes described as a secret administrative and financial connection between Shanghai and the peninsula, sought to connect the constitutional claim of the government to a supporting network at home. It could transmit political instructions and contributions through intermediaries. The discovery of portions of the network by Japanese police during the early 1920s disrupted channels and endangered participants. Even during successful periods, these communications did not convert the exile authorities into a government capable of collecting taxes across Korea. Secret subscribers differed from the population as a whole, and the degree of participation changed by district. Overseas funds produced their own constitutional questions. Communities in Hawaii, California, and China raised contributions for relief, diplomacy, education, and independence activity, sometimes through separate associations whose officers did not answer to Shanghai. Disagreements over how to account for money and who could represent donors intensified factional rivalry. The point is not simply that the movement lacked adequate finances. Money created authority, and the absence of mutually accepted accounting rules made it possible for rival leaders to claim the same national mandate. Official documents, association ledgers, and correspondence should be read together rather than treating fundraising as a peripheral topic.

Financial disagreement is not evidence that everyone involved acted in bad faith. Overseas donors could reasonably demand to know how funds were used, while leaders undertaking dangerous work might regard secrecy as necessary. Those competing needs produced accounting disputes even within a common independence commitment. Separate ledgers could undercut coordination, but a single centralized treasury was difficult to establish without stable leadership and communication. The archival problem is similar: some correspondence survived in police copies after raids, while other records were deliberately destroyed to protect contributors. A lack of surviving receipts does not demonstrate that a contribution never existed.

59.8 Diplomatic Disappointment and the Reassessment of Nationalist Strategy

Appeals to the Paris peace conference, the United States, and the League of Nations produced sympathy but no recognition, and the Washington Conference of 1921–1922 confirmed that the major powers would not challenge Japan over Korea. Responses differed. Rhee and others kept up lobbying in Washington; Ahn Chang-ho and his supporters favored patient organizational and educational preparation; others, like Shin Chae-ho, rejected dependence on foreign powers altogether and urged revolution. Rhee’s earlier proposal that Korea be placed temporarily under League of Nations mandate had already provoked fierce opposition among noncompromise nationalists, who regarded it as a surrender of sovereignty.

The Paris Peace Conference and the postwar League of Nations inspired hopes that a defeated or dismembered empire might be judged by the principle of national self-determination. But Korea was not the territory of a defeated central power, and Japan was a recognized Allied participant. Korean appeals therefore encountered an international order that prized its own stability more than the claims of colonized peoples. The Washington Conference of 1921–1922 likewise did not overturn Japanese sovereignty over Korea. The meaning of diplomatic disappointment depended on the audiences involved: it could weaken faith in foreign recognition, provoke a change of strategy, or reinforce an argument for continuing long-term persuasion. Rhee Syng-man's advocacy in the United States remained influential with parts of the diaspora, but the proposal associated with placing Korea under a League mandate exposed him to accusations that he was willing to exchange direct independence for international supervision. Ahn Chang-ho and other organizers emphasized preparation, institutions, and political education, while revolutionary critics insisted that imperial power would never voluntarily surrender control. Students should examine the difference between a proposal advanced in a particular diplomatic setting and a later summary of a leader's entire career. Neither the failure of international diplomacy nor the advocacy of armed struggle settled the question of what political institutions an independent Korea should possess.

The Washington Conference offers an especially clear way to teach the difference between public attention and diplomatic result. Korean representatives could circulate petitions and appeal to the principle of self-determination, but Japan's position in the Pacific international order remained secure. A sympathetic speech by an individual legislator did not bind a government to recognition. Within the movement, such disappointments could strengthen opposing arguments: that diplomatic work must become more professional and persistent, or that reliance on external powers had reached a dead end. The same event thus helps explain why different strategies developed simultaneously.

59.9 The National Representatives Conference of 1923

February 20, 1923: Declaration of the National Representatives Conference

Identified record: Conference representatives, Shanghai; declaration of February 20, 1923, reproduced by the National Institute of Korean History in Sources in Korean History.

What the record supports: The declaration articulates the need to rebuild and unite a weakened independence movement. Compare its public unity claims with the conference proceedings, whose delegations eventually divided between reorganization and new-government factions.

Provenance and limitation: Direct inspection of the NIKH published transcription; a translated interpretive paraphrase, not a verbatim English translation. The delegates did not represent every Korean community.

Research questions: How does a public statement of common purpose differ from records of disagreement inside the meeting?

Source: National Institute of Korean History — view the identified record or its scholarly reproduction.

The conference met for five months. Three positions emerged. Defenders of the existing government argued that its legitimacy rested on the March First Movement and should not be discarded. Advocates of reorganization, including Ahn Chang-ho’s circle, wanted to retain the government’s framework while restructuring it. Advocates of creating a new government, among them Shin Chae-ho and several delegates from Manchuria and Russia, regarded the Provisional Government as ineffective and wanted a fresh start. No compromise held. Those favoring a new government walked out and tried to form a rival body, which did not survive, while the Provisional Government continued with reduced prestige and support.

The National Representatives Conference was not a spontaneous gathering of discontented exiles; it followed efforts from 1921 to rebuild a divided independence movement. It met in Shanghai from January 3 to June 7, 1923. Research based on the proceedings identifies 135 participating regions or organizations and 158 persons appearing during the meetings, of whom 125 were finally confirmed as representatives. Such figures refer to the conference process, not to the full Korean political nation. Delegates discussed diplomacy, finance, military coordination, and the status of the Provisional Government, but the question of constitutional legitimacy became decisive. Supporters of reorganization wanted to reform an existing government whose link to the March First uprising remained meaningful. Advocates of creation argued that recognition of a new constituent authority was necessary to end paralysis. Defenders of the incumbent government rejected surrender of its founding claim. Disputes over credentials, authority, and procedure made compromise difficult. A February 20 conference declaration gives historians a view of the aspirations proclaimed publicly, while the actual proceedings show the less unified process of decision-making. The breakdown produced rival institutions with limited durability and left the Provisional Government in place but weakened. Its importance lies not only in failure: the conference demonstrates how seriously exile groups regarded representation and the political form of independence.

The distinction between delegates present and organizations represented matters for the conference's political legitimacy. It was a rare attempt to create a common decision-making forum across rival exile networks, yet it depended on disputed credentials and the willingness of organized groups to accept a result. Even if a majority agreed on a procedural resolution, those withdrawing could reject the process as illegitimate. Later historians sometimes assign a simple winner and loser; a more useful inquiry follows the fate of the institutions proposed by each faction and asks whether any acquired support from the communities in whose name they spoke.

59.10 Rhee Syng-man’s Removal in 1925 and the Reorganization of Leadership

Rhee had left Shanghai in 1921 and governed from the United States, and critics accused him of neglecting the office, acting without authority, and mishandling funds, while supporters regarded the charges as factional. In March 1925 the Provisional Assembly voted to impeach and remove him. Pak Eun-sik, a historian and veteran activist, became president, and constitutional revisions in the following years weakened the presidency in favor of cabinet and collective leadership, formalized by 1927. The changes cleared a leadership dispute but did not solve the government’s underlying poverty and isolation.

Rhee's distance from Shanghai complicated governance but was not the sole cause of his removal. The Provisional Assembly had debated presidential confidence and constitutional reform before 1925. The March 14, 1925 impeachment proposal recorded specific complaints concerning the handling of levies among overseas Koreans and attempts to separate American administration from the Shanghai government's authority. The record reflects the Assembly's case, not a neutral verdict about all of Rhee's decisions; the positions of his supporters must also be consulted. This distinction is crucial because a document containing formal charges is evidence that charges were made, not independent proof of every allegation. The removal occurred within a wider constitutional struggle. After interim arrangements, Pak Eun-sik became president; further constitutional changes shifted toward a cabinet-centered executive and eventually a more collective structure. Such revisions aimed to reduce the dependence of national leadership on a contested single figure. They also reveal the limits of constitutional form without the resources and territorial institutions to carry policy into effect. Rhee continued organizing in America, and the Provisional Government maintained its claim to national legitimacy, so the removal did not end rivalry across exile communities. The case provides a particularly clear source-based lesson in the difference between legality, authority, and effective government.

Pak Eun-sik's involvement illustrates the connection between historical writing and political legitimacy. A respected historian of the independence struggle could lend moral authority to an institution whose finances and practical control were weak. But a respected successor could not by himself secure unity among commanders or gain foreign recognition. The transition from a strong presidency to alternative executive arrangements should therefore be placed within a longer sequence of constitutional revisions and leadership negotiations. Students reading the impeachment text should distinguish its procedural language from the broader personal rivalries described in later recollections.

59.11 Cultural Nationalism, Noncompromise, and the Debate over Self-Government

Inside Korea, a cultural-nationalist current worked through newspapers, schools, and enterprises such as the Korean Products Promotion Movement of the early 1920s. Some, among them the writer Yi Gwang-su, argued in the early 1920s that Koreans should first reform themselves, and by the middle of the decade some elites discussed limited self-government under Japanese rule. Noncompromise nationalists, such as Cho Man-sik, rejected any arrangement that accepted colonial sovereignty. The dispute split nationalists, pushed some of them toward cooperation with socialists, and shaped the united front that produced Shinganhoe.

The cultural-nationalist current developed from an argument that a nation deprived of political sovereignty could nevertheless preserve its future through language, education, economic enterprise, and public character. Its adherents were not uniform. Korean Products Promotion organizers could believe in collective economic self-help without accepting self-government under Japan, while some intellectuals increasingly entertained autonomy proposals that would keep the imperial framework intact. Noncompromise nationalists objected that these policies risked making Japanese sovereignty the permanent horizon of political activity. The issue was more precise than whether schools and enterprises were useful: it concerned what political position their pursuit implied. This argument intersected with socialist criticism. Marxist writers questioned whether class divisions among Koreans could be overcome through national unity and whether elite-managed culture served landlords and business owners better than workers. Yet the strongest public united front later involved elements of noncompromise nationalism and socialism cooperating without resolving their differences. Newspaper debates are useful evidence, but censorship could prevent participants from declaring their full objective. Students should avoid a neat chronological story in which cultural nationalism automatically became collaboration or political radicalism automatically became more effective. Individual positions shifted with the changing risks and opportunities of the 1920s.

The Korean Products Promotion Movement is one useful bridge between Chapters 57 and 58: buying Korean-made goods could be framed as supporting national capacity while it also reflected differences among consumers and business owners. Socialist critics might question whether such appeals benefited workers whose wages were too low to buy the goods. Noncompromise nationalists could support economic organizing while refusing any autonomous status short of sovereignty. The political content of 'culture' thus depended on who used the word and to what end. It should not be treated as a reliable marker of loyalty to Japan or opposition to it.

Division III · §§59.12–59.17

Socialism, Communism, Anarchism, and Revolutionary Political Programs

59.12 The Russian Revolution and the Arrival of Socialist Ideas among Koreans

The Russian Revolution of 1917 and the Comintern’s founding in 1919 offered Korean activists an ideology of anti-imperialism and a source of potential support. Socialist ideas also arrived through Japanese and Chinese socialist circles. Many activists turned to socialism because the western powers had disappointed them, while others were drawn by its analysis of landlords and capitalists. Socialist internationalism and nationalist demands could coexist, but they were not identical: for some, national liberation was the immediate goal, for others, social revolution.

The Russian Revolution's influence was intellectual, organizational, and diplomatic. It offered an example of an empire overturned and a language of anti-imperialism directed against European and Japanese domination. International communism also promised institutional connections and sometimes resources unavailable from Western governments. But Bolshevik authorities placed the interests of the new Soviet state first. Korean militants in Siberia experienced revolutionary politics alongside civil war, military emergency, and struggles for control of armed units; socialists within Korea often encountered similar ideas through Japanese student circles or translations rather than direct Soviet contact. Socialist diagnoses of colonialism linked foreign sovereignty with property relations. Landlords and capitalists, including Koreans, might be seen as benefiting from an unjust system; demands for workers' organization and land reform therefore went beyond the restoration of a Korean flag. Others held that national liberation must precede deeper social transformation. These options involved different choices of coalition partner and institution. A study of socialists in this period must distinguish organizations of 1918–1923, the Seoul party founded in 1925, and Korean militants later working within Chinese organizations. The word 'communist' does not identify a single unchanging command structure throughout all three contexts.

The Comintern's authority also varied by period. The Bolsheviks had their own diplomatic need to negotiate with Japan, while Korean exiles hoped for material and political aid. Internationalist language could obscure this asymmetry. For Korean workers and peasants, by contrast, the appeal of socialism could stem from immediate injustice under landlords and employers. The movement's international and domestic dimensions should be compared rather than assumed identical. The chronology of the Russian Civil War also cautions against viewing early Soviet connections through the stable institutional arrangements of the much later 1930s.

59.13 Early Korean Socialist Organizations in Shanghai, Siberia, Japan, and Korea

The first Korean socialist party, formed in Khabarovsk in 1918, moved its base to Shanghai, while rival communists in Irkutsk built a separate network, setting up a division between Shanghai and Irkutsk groups that persisted into the early 1920s and contributed to the Free City conflict (§59.45). In Japan, Korean students formed study groups and labor associations; in Korea itself, youth associations and labor unions provided organizing ground. Different locations meant different political experiences: exiles in the Soviet Far East dealt directly with Soviet authorities, students in Japan with Japanese leftists, and activists in Korea with police.

Korean socialist politics emerged simultaneously in different centers. Groups in Khabarovsk, Irkutsk, Shanghai, Tokyo, and Seoul drew upon different mentors, exiles, military experiences, and social constituencies. The Shanghai and Irkutsk communist networks competed over recognition and authority; this rivalry contributed to the contested command arrangements around the Free City Incident. In the peninsula, socialist youth and labor organizations opened spaces for political education and organizing, but they faced police surveillance and disagreement over the priority of class versus national questions. The diversity created a recurring problem: an international party center could recognize one leadership without compelling allegiance from all Korean activists. Some rivalries involved resources and jurisdiction as much as ideological doctrine. The Comintern's changing line on anti-imperialist alliances also meant that what appeared politically sensible in 1926 might be criticized by 1929. Students should date each organization rather than constructing a direct, seamless lineage from the first Siberian groups to the later Korean Communist Party. Surviving police files can establish arrests and membership circles while reflecting official assumptions about what a communist party should look like.

In Korea itself, the development of the socialist movement depended on ordinary institutions: publishing houses, youth circles, school associations, and labor unions. Some participants had encountered Marxist literature while studying in Japan; others learned through Korean-language discussions of tenant and workplace disputes. This produced regional and class differences in emphasis. A historian cannot infer a member's position on national independence or international revolution from the group's name alone. Party origins in Shanghai and Siberia were important, but the attempts to form a durable legal or clandestine constituency inside Korea gave socialism a different social history.

The clandestine Korean Communist Party was founded in Seoul in April 1925 together with a communist youth league. Its members came from several socialist circles and aimed to organize workers and peasants for a Korean revolution under Comintern guidance. Police arrests in late 1925 and subsequent years, along with disputes among its members, repeatedly broke up the party’s leadership.

The clandestine Korean Communist Party was founded in Seoul on April 17, 1925, after negotiations among several socialist factions, with a communist youth organization established the following day. It was not a universally accepted federation of all Korean socialists: the Seoul faction remained outside the initial coalition. Its political ambition was to connect Korean liberation with the organization of laborers, tenant farmers, and revolutionary internationalism. It had to confront the dual pressure of colonial police and internal factional mistrust. The Sinuiju arrests of late 1925 severely damaged the initial party, after which new groups attempted reconstruction. The party's own declarations articulated national liberation and revolutionary coalition-building in terms that are more sophisticated than the shorthand that communists were concerned only with class. A declaration preserved and discussed in the National Institute of Korean History's account calls for assembling anti-imperialist forces while emphasizing worker and peasant leadership. Students should compare that political self-description with police reports, which classified the party through security threats and surveillance categories. The party became central to domestic socialist politics, but neither its short existence nor later claims of revolutionary continuity should be confused with sustained organizational control over the whole Korean left.

The National Institute of Korean History identifies April 17, 1925 as the first party congress and April 18 as the foundation of the youth league. These details show that organizers intended to build both a central leadership and a younger cadre. Police raids later made it difficult to preserve ordinary institutional records, which is why court and security materials are so prominent in modern accounts. Yet a 1926 party declaration printed in Bulkkot shows substantive policy thinking, including universal suffrage and labor protections. The presence of such demands should prevent students from reducing all Korean communism to an overseas conspiracy.

59.15 Factional Conflict, Comintern Policy, and the Communist Movement after 1928

Historical Vocabulary III — Comintern, Faction, Platform, and United Front

Comintern: the Communist International, whose guidance affected Korean party organization but whose directives did not control every Korean activist. Faction: an organized circle competing for influence within a movement. Platform: an explicit public statement of the state and policies a party seeks. United front: a coalition that cooperates on defined goals despite continuing disagreements.

Distinction exercise: identify which terms describe international institutions, organizational processes, written programs, and temporary political relationships. Do not confuse an announcement of unity with a successful and permanent coalition.

Successive leaderships were arrested, and the party was reconstituted several times through the late 1920s. Factional rivalry between groups of different origins made recovery difficult. In December 1928 the Comintern issued a statement on the Korean question that criticized factionalism and instructed Korean communists to rebuild on the basis of workers and peasants, and the party was not recognized as an affiliate. In practice communists continued to work through labor unions, peasant associations, and underground reading circles, and some moved away from broad alliances with nationalists as Comintern policy hardened around 1928–1931.

The years after 1925 saw multiple reorganizations, commonly described in Korean historiography as successive party formations. Leaders attempted to reconstruct networks after arrests while rival circles challenged one another's claims. Colonial police campaigns in 1925, 1927–1928, and subsequent years impeded sustained organization. The Comintern's December 1928 intervention criticized factionalism and sought reorganization from workers' and peasants' circles rather than the recognition of existing elite factions. It is too simple to say that the movement vanished in one administrative act, since communists continued political work through labor, youth, and underground networks. International directives were themselves historically contingent. The Comintern's broader shift toward sharper class confrontation reduced enthusiasm for alliances with nationalist moderates, even though local activists still had pragmatic reasons to cooperate. Historians must also distinguish actual Comintern decisions from interpretations later imposed by Korean communist or anti-communist writers. The relationship between organization and ideology is evident here: a movement may share an ideological language but lose the practical ability to coordinate under surveillance. Later guerrillas in Manchuria arose partly through a different organizational path inside Chinese communist institutions, not as an undisturbed survival of the Korean party founded in 1925.

The 1926 party declaration and the December 1928 Comintern line should not be read as products of a single stable organization. The first expressed a broad anti-imperialist and democratic program; the later intervention sought reorganization amid repeated failures, factional disputes and international policy changes. The choice of archival anchor therefore changes the apparent character of the movement. A chapter that cites only police destruction of party cells misses political thought; one that cites only ambitious declarations misses the actual weakness of clandestine institutions. Both kinds of evidence are necessary for an accurate account of the late 1920s.

59.16 Class Struggle and National Liberation: Competing Socialist Visions

Historical Debate II — Can National and Class Liberation Be Separated?

Interpretation A: Broad national solidarity was strategically necessary before any social reconstruction. Its supporters argue that a movement divided by class could not challenge the colonial state effectively.

Interpretation B: Independence that preserved landlord or capitalist domination would leave many workers and tenants without meaningful freedom. This view gives economic transformation a central place.

Interpretation C: The two aims could be pursued through a staged or overlapping program, as some revolutionary declarations proposed. Evidence task: compare the 1926 communist declaration with the 1935 National Revolutionary Party platform and identify what each would change first.

Korean socialists agreed that colonialism rested on both foreign rule and class exploitation, but disagreed on emphasis. Some argued that a national-democratic revolution, including land reform and representative government, must come first. Others pushed for early class struggle against landlords and capitalists, including Korean ones. Positions on cooperation with nationalists shifted with Comintern policy and with local conditions (see Chapter 58 for tenancy and labor).

One socialist approach emphasized a national-democratic stage in which colonial rule would be overturned through broad cooperation, with later disputes concerning land, class, and ownership to be decided in a liberated Korea. Other activists worried that such coalitions placed educated nationalists and property owners at the head of a movement dependent on workers and tenant farmers. Land reform, union rights, political freedoms, women's position, and the future ownership of major productive enterprises were consequently not secondary policy questions; they defined what freedom would mean. A nominally united movement could split as soon as these questions became politically urgent. The 1927 united-front project did not abolish these disputes. Its political significance lies precisely in the temporary agreement of actors who opposed Japanese rule but maintained divergent social programs. The collapse of Shinganhoe in 1931 cannot be explained through one ideological slogan alone: arrests, leadership changes, international directives, local rivalries, and the difficulties of legal association under colonial rule all mattered. Students should use the 1935 National Revolutionary Party platform as a comparative text because it reveals an alternative attempt to combine national independence with an explicit program of economic and social transformation.

Programs for land reform reveal that the debate was not merely abstract. Tenant associations in Chapter 58 were confronting rent, debt and insecurity, while Korean employers and landlords sometimes supported nationalist schools and newspapers. A coalition drawing from both groups could agree on opposition to Japan yet have difficulty proposing future property law. The student should compare whether a program demanded rent reduction, confiscation, nationalization, or reform through an elected assembly. Those choices help identify the specific future state being proposed. National liberation was a political aim shared by different social projects, not a complete economic constitution.

59.17 Korean Anarchists, Mutual Aid, and Revolutionary Alternatives

Anarchists such as Yi Hoe-yeong and his brothers advocated federated self-governing communities, mutual aid, and revolutionary action without centralized authority, positions that set them against both party-led communism and nationalist state-building. Around 1929 Korean anarchists and nationalists in Manchuria formed the Korean People’s Association, an experiment in self-government for Korean villages. Kim Jwa-jin was assassinated in January 1930. Accounts of the perpetrator’s identity and political affiliation differ, and collective responsibility should not be assigned to one movement without specifying the evidence. The assassination deepened recriminations amid existing divisions among Korean activists. The experiment illustrates that Korean resistance included stateless as well as state-building visions.

Korean anarchism connected anti-colonialism to distrust of centralized command and the coercive state, including states whose rulers might be Korean. Activists advocated forms of mutual aid, voluntary association, education, and local self-government, though individuals differed in their relationship to revolutionary violence and cooperation with nationalists. Yi Hoe-yeong's political trajectory illustrates how a member of an independence-minded elite could reject both colonial sovereignty and centralized party government. Anarchist ideas circulated in Beijing and Manchuria through print, personal networks, and exchanges with Chinese and Japanese radicals. The Korean People's Association in Manchuria emerged in the late 1920s among communities that needed land administration, education, food, and defense. Its associated leadership included Kim Jwa-jin, whose assassination in January 1930 became the subject of conflicting allegations concerning the assailant and political responsibility. Later narratives often assign blame according to ideological conflict; the surviving evidence does not warrant declaring a whole political faction responsible. The association also faced insecurity, changing Chinese authority, and the limits of rural resources. Anarchist history belongs in the main narrative because it presented a genuine alternative model for collective life, not merely an appendix to nationalism and Marxism.

The attribution of Kim Jwa-jin's killing became especially sensitive because his later reputation was embraced by different political traditions. A plausible account of the event must identify the witness or police record for the perpetrator, the time of its creation and subsequent contradictions. Retrospective certainty about a political faction can transform a contested incident into collective accusation. The wider anarchist experiment also deserves attention apart from the assassination: educational provision, farming settlements and local autonomy made it a social project requiring economic resources. Its institutional difficulties show how ideals of stateless cooperation met the demands of defending a vulnerable migrant community.

Division IV · §§59.18–59.23

Uiyeoldan, Revolutionary Direct Action, and the Korean Patriotic Corps

59.18 Kim Won-bong and the Founding of Uiyeoldan in 1919

Uiyeoldan (the Righteous Heroic Corps) was founded in Jilin, Manchuria, in November 1919 by a small group of young activists led by Kim Won-bong. Its members believed that petitions and appeals to foreign powers could not win independence and that a secret organization using direct action against colonial officials and institutions could. This set it apart from both conventional independence armies, which sought to fight in the field, and the Provisional Government’s diplomacy. Its membership was small and recruited through personal ties, and its operations depended on links to activists in China and Korea.

Uiyeoldan emerged in Jilin in November 1919 within a political environment shaped by exiles' disillusionment with foreign diplomacy and the demands of a long struggle. Kim Won-bong's organization attracted young men prepared to risk imprisonment or death in conspicuous actions against colonial personnel and institutions. Its aims were more ambitious than retaliation. Early accounts associate it with expelling Japanese colonial power and with social change, including opposition to inherited hierarchies. It was neither a conventional army with a protected base nor a mass association like the later Shinganhoe. Its small cells could act symbolically but had difficulty building continuous, broad membership. Its origins also demonstrate the difference between political purpose and a successful operation. Some early plans failed after arrests, and a conspicuous action could trigger police investigations far beyond its immediate participants. The 1923 manifesto produced by Shin Chae-ho gave a powerful ideological justification for popular revolution that extended beyond the method of selective attacks. Over the following decade, some Uiyeoldan members pursued military training and united-front politics, revealing that direct action was a starting strategy rather than a fixed organizational destiny. The historian should analyze the organization's declared social vision, actual recorded actions, and changing political alliances separately.

Uiyeoldan's declared political program also distinguished it from purely restorationist nationalism. References to class hierarchy and land ownership in accounts of its early aims foreshadowed the larger social programs of the 1930s. The organization operated in a landscape where a relatively small group could generate an outsized political response, but where even a famous attack did not necessarily increase membership or donations. A record of plans frustrated by police discoveries is part of the history, not an embarrassing omission. Organizations should be assessed through their failed undertakings as well as the events commemorated later.

The declaration is among the most important statements of the debate between diplomacy-oriented and revolutionary nationalists. Its argument that independence must come from popular revolution influenced later activists, including those who admired it without joining Uiyeoldan, and was also read by anarchists as supporting mass self-organization.

Shin Chae-ho's January 1923 Declaration of the Korean Revolution was written for Uiyeoldan and survives as a dated political manifesto. Its opening presents Japan's rule as a combined political, economic, and cultural assault, naming land, taxes, natural resources, and repressive institutions. The text then rejects external diplomacy, dependence on privileged elites, and reform projects that accept the continuity of colonial power. Its intended revolutionary subject was the Korean people, not merely a small circle of armed specialists. This breadth explains why the declaration matters in political intellectual history as well as the history of direct-action groups. Its language must be read as polemic, not a quantitative report on the colony. Claims about universal dispossession and national suffering were part of a program intended to mobilize resistance. It is also an ethically difficult source because it explicitly legitimized political violence. Students should analyze the author's premises, intended audience, and proposed social transformation without treating the manifesto as a practical model or endorsing its conclusions. Compare it with the Provisional Government's constitutional claims and cultural nationalist programs: all opposed the loss of sovereignty, but they assigned different roles to foreign recognition, institutions, social reform, and coercive change.

The manifesto's rhetoric is particularly useful for comparing the language of violence and that of rights. Shin presents colonial domination as denying the ordinary political freedoms required for peaceful change, and this claim becomes part of his argument for revolutionary action. The reader need not accept the moral conclusion to understand its historical logic. Comparing the Korean original to later modern-language transcriptions also shows how terms for nation, class and popular revolution could acquire different meanings in translation. A window should disclose that it offers paraphrase, rather than presenting a modern English rendering as a certified translation of the original text.

59.20 Revolutionary Attacks and Underground Networks during the 1920s

Activists associated with Uiyeoldan or sympathetic to it carried out attacks on police stations, colonial offices, and economic institutions in the 1920s. Kim Sang-ok attacked the Jongno police station in Seoul in January 1923 and was killed in a subsequent confrontation with police. Na Seok-ju bombed the Oriental Development Company and the Joseon Siksan Bank in 1926. The attributions of some other incidents, including those linked to Kim Ik-sang, are debated, and later commemorative accounts have sometimes enlarged the facts. The attacks drew heavy publicity and repression and commanded admiration among many Koreans; they did not threaten colonial control. This textbook discusses them as historical events and political choices, not as operational models.

The direct-action movement contained distinct episodes that should not be merged into a single uninterrupted campaign. The attempted assaults associated with Kim Ik-sang, Kim Sang-ok's conflict with police in Seoul in 1923, and Na Seok-ju's 1926 attack on institutions associated with colonial economic power illustrate differences in target, publicity, and political context. Later accounts sometimes assign organizational membership more confidently than contemporary evidence permits, so actions should be attributed only where the connection is documented. Korean newspapers, Japanese criminal investigations, and postwar commemorations have differing incentives for emphasizing a participant's role. The immediate outcomes were uneven. Some attacks failed to hit intended targets, some caused deaths or injuries, and the Japanese administration responded through expanded surveillance, raids, and punishment. Public symbolism mattered: participants could frame an act as proof that colonial control was not universally accepted even if it did little to alter military capabilities. This symbolic argument did not settle whether the violence advanced a realistic route to independence. The movement's own later shift toward formal training and party coalition suggests that at least some members concluded that politically meaningful change needed more than spectacular episodes. The chapter should neither romanticize nor flatten these historical choices.

The incidents associated with Kim Ik-sang, Kim Sang-ok and Na Seok-ju also differed in the legal evidence they generated. Some records come from arrested associates and police investigations; others rely more heavily on later narratives about participants killed during pursuit. A brief chronology should record the act, the location, the confirmed casualty or property consequence, and the source basis of organizational attribution. Praise or condemnation of the actors belongs to interpretation, not the establishment of these facts. This method prevents a history of direct action from becoming a collection of unexamined heroic legends.

59.21 From Small-Group Violence to Broader Revolutionary Organization

By the later 1920s Kim Won-bong and others concluded that isolated acts could not build a revolution. Some members studied at the Whampoa Military Academy under Chinese Nationalist sponsorship, and Uiyeoldan veterans took part in efforts to build coalitions and military training programs. In 1932 they established a revolutionary military and political training school near Nanjing, and in the following years joined broader alliances that culminated in the 1935 party described in §59.61.

Changing strategy can be traced through Uiyeoldan veterans' interaction with Chinese military and revolutionary institutions. Their experience of clandestine action made organizational security and political commitment important, but it could not compensate for the lack of sustained manpower, funding, or foreign backing. Contact with Chinese Nationalist circles and experience at Whampoa offered another way to imagine a revolutionary cadre: a political organizer with military training linked to a broader national movement. Such relationships were contingent on Chinese party politics and Japan's interventions, not unconditional sponsorship. The early-1930s training program near Nanjing and later coalition negotiations reflected attempts to connect the experience of Uiyeoldan to other nationalist and socialist forces. Participants debated whether building a party was more useful than maintaining separate armed cells and whether the future republic should include substantial social redistribution. In this shift, individual biography and institutional history should not be conflated: not every participant followed Kim Won-bong, and the political reputation of an earlier organization did not automatically give it command over all independent activists. The developments culminated in a 1935 unification effort whose program must be read on its own terms.

Military training in China should be tied to named institutions and years rather than described as if one academy trained every independence fighter. Members' exposure to Whampoa and later Nanjing-area programs took place under shifting Chinese Nationalist politics. Formal instruction could increase political cohesion but not guarantee equipment or protection for armed operations in Korea. The movement from cadre training to party formation also carried ideological changes: a future Korean state might be asked to redistribute land and broaden political participation, questions less explicit in accounts focused on early direct action. This makes the transition a major chapter in Korean revolutionary thought.

59.22 Kim Gu and the Formation of the Korean Patriotic Corps in 1931

April 26, 1932: Yun Bong-gil’s Korean Patriotic Corps oath

Identified record: Yun Bong-gil, handwritten oath to the Korean Patriotic Corps, April 26, 1932; held at National Museum of Korea and reproduced by NIKH.

What the record supports: The oath declares commitment to recovering Korean independence and freedom and addresses action against Japan’s military invasion of China. It reflects the writer’s intention before the April 29 Shanghai incident, not an assessment of the event’s subsequent consequences.

Provenance and limitation: Direct NIKH transcription and object history consulted; descriptive paraphrase rather than a reproduced translated quotation.

Research questions: How does the oath combine Korean national aims with the immediate situation in China?

Source: National Institute of Korean History — view the identified record or its scholarly reproduction.

Kim Gu, then a leading figure in the Provisional Government, founded the Korean Patriotic Corps in Shanghai in 1931 as a small secret organization for direct action. The Provisional Government was short of funds and influence, and the corps gave it a way to act after years of stagnation. Its operations were intended to draw attention and win Chinese sympathy, particularly as Japan’s seizure of Manchuria in 1931 heightened Chinese hostility to Japan.

Kim Gu's influence inside the weakened Provisional Government grew in a period when its diplomatic work had yielded little recognition and its finances were fragile. The Korean Patriotic Corps, organized around 1931, drew on the argument that an action with international political resonance could alter the balance of attention. Its relationship to the government was not simply that of an independent guerrilla army: it was a small organization closely associated with Kim Gu and the wider exile political leadership, and secrecy limited what contemporaries could know about its decisions. The surviving oath documents are unusually valuable because they establish a recruit's own stated purpose at a specific date. Yi Bong-chang's December 1931 oath and Yun Bong-gil's April 26, 1932 oath demonstrate commitments to independence, but neither document proves how foreign audiences would react to the intended action. Later memoirs contain dramatic episodes that should be compared with the dated texts, Japanese inquiries, and Chinese press. Political purpose, military result, civilian consequences, and later commemoration are separate analytical questions. The corps' history is thus a case study in the interaction between symbolic resistance and attempts to gain external support.

The oath preserved from April 1932 can be contrasted with Kim Gu's later autobiographical account. The oath is contemporary to Yun Bong-gil's decision and records his own stated allegiance; the memoir gives a retrospective story shaped by knowledge of what followed. The two sources answer different questions. One establishes the public language of commitment at the time; the other helps explain later interpretations of organizational strategy, although it demands corroboration. Using both without distinguishing their production dates would wrongly imply that the outcome was known before the attack. This is especially important for an event with such powerful commemorative significance.

59.23 Yi Bong-chang and Yun Bong-gil: The Actions of 1932

On January 8, 1932, Yi Bong-chang threw a grenade at the Japanese emperor’s carriage near the palace in Tokyo; it failed to harm him, and Yi was arrested and later executed. On April 29, 1932, at Hongkou Park in Shanghai, during a Japanese celebration of the emperor’s birthday, Yun Bong-gil threw a bomb that mortally wounded the Japanese commander of forces in Shanghai and injured other officials. Yun was arrested, taken to Japan, and executed in December 1932. The two incidents differed in outcome. The Tokyo attempt failed and had limited political effect beyond Japan. The Shanghai attack, coming soon after fighting in the city, was widely praised in China, and Chinese Nationalist leaders were reported to have expressed admiration, which increased Chinese support for Korean activists. It also led Japanese police to pursue the Provisional Government in Shanghai and forced its officials to flee the city. Whether such violence advanced independence, or invited costly reprisals, remains debated.

Yi Bong-chang's January 8, 1932 attack in Tokyo and Yun Bong-gil's April 29 action at Hongkou Park occurred in very different international settings. The Tokyo attack failed to kill the emperor; its effects included prosecution and publicity within Japan. The Shanghai action took place after the January 28 conflict between Japanese and Chinese forces and struck senior Japanese military and diplomatic figures at a public celebration. The commander Shirakawa Yoshinori was mortally wounded and died later, not immediately at the scene. A careful chronology should distinguish those wounded, those who died afterward, and later claims about political consequences. The Chinese Nationalist response was particularly important. Japanese military action in Shanghai had already transformed Chinese attitudes toward Japan, and Yun's attack contributed to more favorable responses to some Korean exile organizers. Kim Gu and associates subsequently faced intensified pursuit, prompting movement away from Shanghai. Yet claims that the action single-handedly rescued the entire Korean independence movement overstate one event's causal role. Chinese politics, money, prior networks, and the changing regional war also mattered. The 1932 incidents should be compared with Uiyeoldan's different political program and with diplomatic strategies; shared use of violent direct action does not make their organizational histories identical.

The precise aftermath of the Hongkou Park attack reinforces the need for chronological care. Shirakawa Yoshinori was fatally injured and died subsequently, while other officers and officials suffered different injuries. Chinese political sympathy for Korean resistance did not eliminate rivalries among Korean groups, and Japanese police pursuit constrained the Provisional Government's presence in Shanghai. The incident thus helped change the external opportunities available to Kim Gu's circle while increasing the immediate risks of exile organizing. It should not be presented either as a complete military victory or as an irrelevant symbolic act; its consequences were predominantly political and diplomatic.

Emperor Sunjong, the last ruler of the Korean Empire, died in April 1926, and his funeral procession on June 10 gave organizers a moment when large crowds would gather lawfully. Sunjong's death in April 1926 provided a public occasion shaped by mourning for the old empire, but participants used the June 10 funeral to express modern anti-colonial politics. Students, Cheondogyo networks, and socialist activists made partly separate preparations. Police arrests disrupted some plans before the day. Their language combined anti-imperial politics, calls for popular participation, and demands that reached beyond a simple wish for restoration of the dynasty. Police and school authorities responded with searches, interrogations, and punishments. The movement did not replicate the geographic scale of March First; equating them would erase important differences in organization and repression. Its larger significance lies in the emergence of politically educated student networks and their ability to coordinate public protest. This helps explain both the confidence with which activists later sought a nationalist–socialist united front and the authorities' growing suspicion of student associations.

The timing of Sunjong's funeral also reveals how national symbolism could be reinterpreted. Mourning for a deposed monarch brought crowds together, but the surviving leaflets made demands for modern political liberation and criticized imperial rule. Organizers used the ceremony's visibility without agreeing that restoring monarchy was the constitutional goal. One contemporary documentary collection describes student groups positioned along the procession route under extremely heavy security. The number of students gathered along the route is not equivalent to the number participating in demonstrations. This distinction protects the event from inflated accounts that treat every attendee as an active protester.

59.25 Student Associations, Reading Circles, and Political Education

Korean secondary-school and college students formed associations, reading circles, and underground study groups. Grievances over discriminatory treatment, curriculum, language, and unequal access to higher education (see Chapter 57) overlapped with the political theories circulating in those circles. School strikes could begin over a teacher’s dismissal or a discriminatory rule and turn into wider political protest, which is why colonial authorities treated student organizing as a security matter.

Student activism did not begin with a single street demonstration. Secondary-school associations, college study circles, Sunday lectures, political reading groups, and newspaper discussions accumulated political experience throughout the 1920s. Unequal access to schools and the use of Japanese language shaped grievances, while restrictions on students' right to assemble brought the wider colonial legal order into everyday educational life. The 1925 Joseon Student Scientific Research Society is especially useful: its declared interest in social science and student unity illustrates how intellectual activity could be joined to organization and political identity. School strikes were not identical to independence uprisings. Some began with questions of teaching quality, dismissal, disciplinary procedures, or access to representation. Under a colonial system, however, these issues could become arguments about national dignity, institutional self-government, and political freedoms. Students learned to assess newspaper censorship and police presence through direct experience. The historian should compare individual school records to the wider count of disputes, since a rising number of strikes does not prove every participant was a committed revolutionary. These networks provided continuity between June 1926 and Gwangju in late 1929 without making the latter an inevitable result of the former.

Student associations created habits of organization—electing officers, collecting dues, preparing lectures and maintaining contact across schools—that later supported political campaigns. These seemingly administrative acts were historical skills, but they did not make every association a clandestine revolutionary cell. The authorities' classification of a meeting might exaggerate the threat while the students' retrospective recollections might exaggerate its unity. The best evidence combines student publications, school discipline records and contemporary police reports. Such comparison also helps explain why a small educational dispute could spread: students recognized common constraints even when their immediate complaints differed.

59.26 The Jeonguhoe Declaration and the Search for Unity

By 1926, repression, factional rivalry, and the dispute over cooperation with colonial institutions had left nationalists and socialists weakened. The Jeonguhoe Declaration of November 1926, issued by socialist activists, called for cooperation with nationalists and a shift from purely economic struggle toward a broader political movement. Its significance lay in legitimizing joint action with noncompromise nationalists. For many socialists the shift was tactical and for some principled, and it was never accepted by all.

The Jeonguhoe Declaration of November 1926 responded to the fragmentation of socialist politics and the inability of separate circles to win mass influence. Its proposal for broader cooperation with noncompromise nationalists was not merely a change in rhetorical tone. It signaled that some socialists saw national political mobilization as an appropriate arena even when it involved temporary alliance with groups holding different class programs. Repression provided a practical reason to cooperate: separate organizations were vulnerable, and common public campaigns could reach audiences that clandestine communist cells could not. The change was contested. Socialists continued to disagree over whether such alliances postponed necessary class conflict, and nationalists worried that common bodies might be used to extend communist influence. Historians should also distinguish the political declaration from the later institution: Shinganhoe's membership, structure, and permissible activities were negotiated under colonial legal constraints rather than dictated by one document. The declaration is best used to ask how unity was defined, who was included, and what conflicts remained unresolved. Those questions make the founding of Shinganhoe understandable as a negotiated political achievement, not proof that ideological division had disappeared.

The Jeonguhoe shift can be examined in the sequence of late 1926 rather than through the later success or failure of Shinganhoe. Socialists were facing factional conflict and repeated arrests; nationalist organizers were divided over compromises with colonial administration. Both confronted a public that could be engaged through legal associations yet remained subject to police permission. Declaring a united front offered a way to concentrate political energy but left unresolved who would control its branches, finances and social policy. The resulting organization would inherit those disputes even if its founding program announced cooperation.

Shinganhoe was founded in Seoul on February 15, 1927, with the Christian nationalist Yi Sang-jae as its president and a leadership drawn from nationalist, Christian, Cheondogyo, and socialist circles. Because it operated under colonial law it could not declare independence openly, and its official platform spoke of political and economic awareness, unity, and rejection of opportunism. Historians often read the last term as a stand against cultural nationalists seen as ready to compromise with colonial authority. It was a mass association with provincial branches, not a parliamentary party.

Shinganhoe's founding on February 15, 1927, represented a rare experiment in cooperation among noncompromise nationalists, religious activists, and socialists inside Korea. Its platform emphasized political and economic awakening, solidarity, and opposition to opportunism. The January 20, 1927 Chosun Ilbo founding announcement preserved by the National Institute of Korean History identifies the public language through which promoters communicated their program before the inaugural meeting. Its phrasing must be read under censorship: the absence of an explicit independence declaration did not mean that every supporter accepted Japanese sovereignty. The association was organized with a national leadership and local branches rather than as an electoral party controlling state institutions. Yi Sang-jae's symbolic standing was valuable, but committees, district organizers, and affiliated social movements supplied practical activity. The structure contained built-in tensions: nationalist elites could favor cautious legal activity, while socialist organizers connected mass politics with labor and agrarian demands. Participation could therefore have different meanings locally. Students should ask how political legitimacy was produced in a colony where legal organizations existed only with continued administrative tolerance.

The original Shinganhoe principles should be compared with the political conditions under which they could be printed. An announcement issued by a newspaper before a founding meeting had to communicate meaningfully to prospective members and remain within censorship boundaries. Its opposition to opportunism is widely interpreted as a challenge to compromise, but it does not identify every individual later labeled a collaborator. Students should trace how the same three principles were interpreted by branch activists, nationalist elders and socialist organizers. The organization mattered precisely because its public language could accommodate participants who would later disagree sharply over methods.

59.28 Provincial Branches, Public Campaigns, and Social Base

Branches spread through the provinces and Korean communities abroad. Estimates of membership reach into the tens of thousands, with figures around forty thousand sometimes cited for its peak; the totals are approximate and depend on which branches are counted. Branches held lectures and night schools, campaigned against discrimination, supported tenant and labor disputes, and cooperated with youth and farmers’ associations. Local branches often acted more radically, or more cautiously, than the central leadership, and police approval of meetings could be withdrawn at will.

The reach of Shinganhoe was neither confined to Seoul nor evenly spread across Korea. Provincial branches held lectures, advocated against discrimination, supported student protests, and responded to local social conflicts. A contemporaneous or reconstructed membership figure must be dated. One National Institute of Korean History study reports approximately 37,000 members near the end of 1929 and 39,257 by November 1930; these should not be treated as an unchanging peak or as a count of unique protest participants. Branch lists can also conceal inactive chapters or differences in reporting. Relations between headquarters and branches were politically important. Local leaders faced the practical issues of rent disputes, school conditions, arbitrary police intervention, and access to meeting halls; national leaders often concentrated on survival and the association's legal standing. The Gwangju protests made these differences visible as branches and allied youth organizations took action while central organizers were arrested. A movement with a broad formal constituency might still lack a unified decision-making process. Its achievements included a network that could link local grievances, but that same network was open to surveillance and leadership crises.

Membership numbers also reveal the difference between national reach and direct mobilization. Approximately 39,000 members at a specified date constituted a significant legal association in colonial circumstances, but this number does not tell us how many attended each lecture, supported a student protest or paid regular dues. Local branches could be stronger where young organizers had existing contacts, while official repression could render a nominal branch inactive. A map should show the distribution of documented chapters and date their status. It should not use one annual total to suggest that organizational strength was uniform across all provinces.

Kunuhoe was founded in 1927 as a sister organization to Shinganhoe, bringing Christian, nationalist, and socialist women together, with leaders including Kim Hwal-lan, Yu Gak-gyeong, and Hwang Sin-deok. It ran educational programs, advocated women’s rights in marriage and education, and campaigned against colonial rule. Its significance lies in joining the case for women’s liberation to the national cause, though women’s issues were often treated as secondary by male-led organizations (see Chapter 57).

Kunuhoe, founded on May 27, 1927, followed wider calls for a national united front, but its participants also articulated demands that male-led associations did not consistently prioritize. Christian nationalist women and socialists cooperated around women's legal status, access to education, employment, and broader political participation. A founding record preserved in a 1927 colonial police report contains a concise program of Korean women's unity and improvement of their standing. Later, a July 1929 program set out more concrete economic and social demands, including equal pay concerns, marriage freedom, and opposition to exploitation. The contrast between founding principles and later action program shows a movement developing an independent agenda. Kunuhoe's local organization, educational work, support for students, and engagement with working women deserve analysis separately from Shinganhoe's record. Women's politics were not merely auxiliary logistics for men. Yet neither did the organization represent all Korean women: rural workers and domestic servants were less visible than educated urban organizers. The movement's eventual organizational difficulties reflected factional conflict and colonial restrictions, not simply the dissolution of its associated male-led organization.

The 1929 Kunuhoe program demonstrates how a women's united front could advocate changes that affected daily life directly. Demands about marriage, occupational safety, equal wages and maternity protections connected women working in factories or agriculture to the political language of liberty. This also challenged a tendency within nationalist movements to place women's rights after independence. The text's institutional provenance is complex: a report printed in a licensed newspaper under censorship might select language differently than a private meeting record. Comparing the 1927 founding program with the 1929 proposals offers a more grounded picture of change than a general assertion that women became politically active.

59.30 The Gwangju Student Independence Movement of November 1929

On October 30, 1929, on a train arriving at Naju station near Gwangju, Japanese male students harassed Korean female students, and fighting broke out between Korean and Japanese students. Police and school authorities were widely seen as favoring the Japanese side, and tension increased. Korean student groups, already organized in secret study circles, escalated into demonstrations on November 3, a day that coincided with a Japanese national holiday. Clashes in Gwangju led to arrests, expulsions, and school closures. The sequence shows how everyday discrimination in schools could be turned into anti-colonial political protest by existing student networks.

The Gwangju movement began in an environment of persistent educational inequality. The widely recounted Naju railway confrontation on October 30, 1929 followed harassment involving Korean female students and Japanese male students; subsequent school and police handling intensified perceptions of unequal treatment. Organized Korean students did not wait for an entirely new political consciousness to emerge. Reading circles, earlier school disputes, and connections with youth groups supplied a political language and a capacity to coordinate demonstrations. November 3, coinciding with a Japanese holiday and Korean commemorative date, became the first major turning point. The sequence continued beyond one day. Arrests and disciplinary measures stimulated further organizing, and on November 11 and 12 leaflets attributed to student organizer O Kwe-il made explicit demands concerning freedom of association, student self-government, the release of those arrested, and an educational system for Koreans. These texts are preserved in a 1930 Government-General education-bureau report, meaning that the words are accessible through a hostile administrative record. That provenance does not make the demands fictitious, but it requires attention to selection and the purpose of the report. Local anti-discrimination grievances had become overtly political demands.

The disparity in treatment after the Naju and Gwangju confrontations was a central mobilizing grievance. Colonial authorities handled Korean and Japanese student participation differently; one source collection details 72 Korean students arrested in the early local proceedings and only seven Japanese students arrested, none of the latter sent to prosecutors. Those figures should not be extended uncritically to the entire national movement. The leaflets' calls for freedoms of speech and association show that the movement reached beyond a quarrel between groups of boys. Student organizing addressed the relationship between schools, police power and the political status of Korean society.

59.31 The Nationwide Expansion of Student Resistance, 1929–1930

News of Gwangju and of police treatment of Korean students spread through Korea. Shinganhoe leaders planned a mass meeting in Seoul in December 1929 to protest, but police arrested key figures beforehand. Student strikes and demonstrations nevertheless spread to many schools into the first months of 1930. Commonly cited cumulative figures run to well over a hundred schools and tens of thousands of students, along with many arrests and expulsions. These are cumulative national estimates that combine different kinds of participation, and they should not be confused with the number of participants in any single demonstration.

Gwangju became a national student movement because teachers, students, reading circles, branch associations, and newspapers helped carry news beyond the city. The planned Shinganhoe-led public meeting in Seoul for December 13, 1929 was blocked by arrests, yet student strikes and demonstrations spread through the first months of 1930. The geography of the movement matters: participation extended to regional towns whose schools had different compositions and local grievances. The protest network was neither a single simultaneous nationwide march nor a centralized organization with perfect communication. Cumulative estimates of schools and participating students must be tied to an explicit timeframe. An established series refers to 149 schools and about 54,000 students through March 1930; such totals should not be used as the number arrested or the population at any particular rally. Colonial reports counted schools and disciplinary proceedings according to their own categories. Student statements expressed the movement's aspirations; police records reveal its suppression and the state's interpretation. Together they show the importance of public organization under repression, the way educational grievances widened into demands for civil rights, and the limits of legal united-front bodies facing mass arrests.

The Gwangju national movement also illustrates how authorities can inadvertently enlarge a protest by suppressing its mediators. The arrest of Shinganhoe personnel preparing a mass meeting prevented one specific gathering but contributed to the broader history of publicity and grievance. Newspapers operated under censorship, and information moved through student visits, correspondence and association networks as well as the formal press. The published count of participating schools describes cumulative institutional involvement; it cannot substitute for an event-by-event chronology. A map or timeline separating November 1929 from early 1930 would make that important distinction visible to students.

59.32 The Dissolution of Shinganhoe, May 1931

After the arrests surrounding the Gwangju protests, Shinganhoe’s leadership moved in a more moderate direction while socialists argued that class-based organizations, not a mixed coalition, were needed; international communist policy was shifting away from broad fronts with nationalists. In May 1931 a general meeting voted to dissolve the association. Critics on the left called this liquidation, defenders saw it as a response to unsustainable pressure. Shinganhoe’s achievement was a nationwide legal anti-colonial organization with a diverse base; its limits were its dependence on colonial permission and on an ideological coalition that was already splitting.

Shinganhoe's dissolution in May 1931 cannot be explained only by a Comintern instruction. The failure of its December 1929 mass-meeting strategy and arrests weakened the central leadership; competing nationalists and socialists differed over legalism and class-based mobilization; and local activists had varying experiences of collaboration. As socialists' international and domestic political priorities changed, advocates of dissolution argued that the united front could no longer serve their purposes. Others regarded its destruction as the surrender of an important nationwide public institution. The result reveals both political achievement and organizational weakness. For several years Shinganhoe linked disparate local movements and reached membership far beyond the small clandestine circles. Yet it could not solve the tension between legal activity licensed by the colonial state and the movement's stronger political goals. The decision to dissolve was taken by participants within their historical circumstances, not imposed by a single all-powerful external actor. Students should distinguish the formal end of the association from the persistence of members and local networks in other forms of activism. Its history provides an unusually clear example of unity as a contingent political accomplishment.

Even after the formal vote to dissolve Shinganhoe, members did not automatically cease political activity. Some returned to labor and rural associations, some remained in cultural organizations, and others joined new political networks. The collapse of a united-front body was a major political defeat, but its history continued through learned organizational practices and the memories of student campaigns. A balanced debate should distinguish responsibility for dissolution from explanations of why the organization became vulnerable. Police repression, party strategy, elite conflict and differences among branches can all be assessed without reducing the association to either a total success or a worthless compromise.

Division VI · §§59.33–59.36

Underground Organization, Colonial Repression, and Local Resistance

59.33 Political Surveillance, Arrests, Trials, and Colonial Prison Systems

The colonial state prosecuted resistance through political police, interrogation, trials, and imprisonment. After 1925 the Peace Preservation Law, extended to Korea, allowed prosecution for forming or joining organizations that aimed to alter the national polity or reject private property. Mass trials followed the breakup of communist networks and student protests. Historians have documented a pattern of lengthy pretrial detention and harsh interrogation, but the experience of individual defendants varied and generalizations should not be applied to every case.

The colonial political-police apparatus was not merely a distant legal threat. It monitored lectures, newspapers, associations, student circles, and cross-border contacts; detained suspects; and supplied investigations for prosecutors and courts. The Peace Preservation Law applied in Korea in May 1925 broadened the framework for prosecuting movements regarded as threatening the imperial national polity or private property. Judicial records reveal formal charges and sentence lengths, but the path from suspicion to conviction could include prolonged detention and pressure in interrogation. The nature and scale of coercion require evidence from particular cases rather than one generalized claim about all prosecutions. Political trials were also public contests over legitimacy. Prosecutors framed activists' actions as breaches of public order; defendants sometimes used proceedings to argue that colonial rule itself lacked legitimacy; censored press reports presented only selected parts of testimony. Prison experiences differed by time and institution, and survivors' recollections add important but retrospective evidence. The historian should avoid identifying every clandestine student group as communist simply because the police classified it that way. Investigative categories were tools of administration, not objective descriptions of the entire political spectrum.

Judicial records have a special evidentiary problem: they can be highly precise about dates of arrest and formal charges while incomplete about coercion during interrogation. A prison sentence is strong evidence that authorities convicted someone, but not conclusive evidence that the political narrative in the prosecution was accurate. Individual testimony, lawyers' records and later personal recollections are therefore indispensable when available. This lesson applies to nationalist as well as communist cases. There is no need to exaggerate every case of mistreatment to establish that political policing operated systematically under the colonial state.

Underground publications, leaflets, and study materials circulated among students and activists, passed from hand to hand and sometimes smuggled from Japan or China. Because police confiscated and catalogued such materials and recorded the testimony of those arrested, much of what historians know about underground networks comes from police evidence. This shapes the record: networks that were never discovered are largely invisible, while those that were are seen through hostile eyes.

The circulation of prohibited literature linked a seemingly minor educational choice to the larger independence struggle. Students copied passages from newspapers, discussed translated socialist writing, and circulated announcements of demonstrations. Newspapers sometimes omitted politically sensitive material after seizures, while underground writers could publish without editorial protection. Each form of communication had a different audience and risk profile: an openly sold newspaper reached broad readers but was easily confiscated, whereas a clandestine text could be more direct but depended on trust and limited reproduction. Evidence for these networks comes from confiscated documents, association minutes, police descriptions, and personal recollections. Because police records became most detailed when organizations were discovered, the surviving archive disproportionately describes compromised networks. A list of seized titles does not demonstrate that every recipient read or endorsed them. Conversely, the destruction of records should not be used as permission to invent unseen plans. The analytical question is how texts moved between legal and illegal political spaces, and how the resulting risk shaped rhetoric. The chapter's documentary methods workshop should teach students to reconstruct relationships cautiously from overlapping fragments.

Documents also traveled across linguistic borders. Korean activists encountered political works in Japanese or Chinese translation, and police translators later rendered those ideas into the language of official security reports. Concepts such as nation, class, republic and liberation can be flattened when a modern textbook uses only English equivalents. A documentary workshop should ask students to record the original title, language, translation date and responsible editor before inferring ideological meaning. Such care is especially necessary where a title survives only through a police inventory and the text itself is lost.

Police files list names, meetings, and texts but present activists as criminals; activist memoirs written later emphasize heroism and often omit factional quarrels. Neither is a neutral record. Students should ask what each source was made for and what it leaves out.

59.35 Religious, Rural, Youth, and Community Networks in Independence Politics

Protestant and Catholic churches, Buddhist temples, Cheondogyo, rural schools, youth associations, and farmers’ societies provided meeting places, communications, and members. Cheondogyo and Christian leaders had been prominent in 1919, and these networks continued to support nationalist activity. They were internally diverse, however, and membership in a church or a civic association was not in itself political resistance; many members avoided politics, and religious leaders also disagreed over accommodation with colonial authorities.

Religious communities supplied more than places to meet. Schools, publishing institutions, youth clubs, and networks of trust could help activists connect across localities. Cheondogyo played an important role in the 1919 independence movement, while Christian schools and associations later produced student organizers and educational campaigns. Buddhist activists and a variety of locally based associations also participated. But a denomination's institutional presence did not imply every believer resisted colonial rule. Some religious organizations prioritized spiritual work or negotiated with administrators; leaders themselves differed over the acceptable political risks. Rural tenant unions, labor associations, and youth groups likewise contained members whose immediate concern was rent, wages, or school treatment rather than a fully specified national political program. Chapter 58 explains the economic conflicts; Chapter 59 examines when and how political organizations used these associations to express wider anti-colonial goals. Activism could involve cooperation across boundaries without erasing social tensions. Police files may label a local movement 'political' because it threatened control, while participants might frame it as a demand for fairness. Both perspectives should be examined, and neither should automatically define every person's motivation.

A local community's support could be expressed through food, shelter, a lecture hall or a donation without creating formal membership in an independence organization. This matters for the interpretation of religious networks: a church congregation might contain committed activists, people opposed to politics and others concerned above all with avoiding police attention. Rural associations likewise could defend tenancy rights while disagreeing over national politics. In studying the movement's social base, students should look for specific individuals and institutions rather than assigning collective motives to every believer or worker in an area.

59.36 The Strengths and Vulnerabilities of Domestic Resistance

Domestic organizations balanced public participation, clandestine coordination, and the constant risk of repression. Legal recognition gave associations such as Shinganhoe public reach and legitimacy but also exposed their membership lists, meetings, and speeches to surveillance and permitted authorities to ban meetings or arrest leaders. Underground organizations were safer from public interference but small and fragile once penetrated. Survival often depended on movement between the two.

Domestic resistance faced a structural dilemma. A legal organization could distribute membership cards, rent halls, hold public lectures, and build regional networks, but this openness also made it easier for authorities to register its leaders and ban gatherings. Underground groups avoided some public exposure yet often relied on a small number of trusted individuals; arrests could sever communication and make leadership succession difficult. The two environments were not separate. Activists could move between an authorized reading society and a clandestine party, or use lawful education to develop relationships that later supported open protest. These strengths and vulnerabilities help explain the sequence from the June 10 movement to Shinganhoe and Gwangju. Political learning and mobilization expanded, but so did state surveillance and the cost of collective action. Success should not be measured only by immediate policy concessions; political ideas, experienced organizers, and memories of solidarity could survive organizational defeat. Nor should symbolic persistence be mistaken for a capacity to govern or to remove Japanese forces. The variety of domestic political life was itself evidence that colonial sovereignty never secured unanimous political acceptance.

The evolution of public associations after 1919 shows why repression and political development were not mutually exclusive. Publishing licenses and meeting halls made a certain form of organization possible, while the higher police apparatus kept that organization under constant threat. Students can compare one public Shinganhoe meeting, one prohibited student leaflet and one clandestine communist declaration to identify distinct levels of legal exposure. These distinctions explain how opposition movements could become more visible even as their organizational independence remained limited. The final evaluation should consider both political participation and the ability to preserve institutions over time.

Division VII · §§59.37–59.43

Armed Independence Warfare in Manchuria, 1919–1920

59.37 Korean Armed Independence Organizations after March 1919

After 1919 a cluster of armed organizations formed or reorganized in the Korean settlements of Manchuria, especially in Gando (Jiandao) and the Amnok (Yalu) borderland. Hong Beom-do’s Korean Independence Army, the Northern Military Administration Office associated with Kim Jwa-jin, and units tied to Choe Jin-dong were separate bodies with different origins. Some grew from earlier educational and religious networks, including Daejonggyo communities; others from veterans of the pre-1910 righteous armies and the Sinminhoe schools. They recruited from different villages, answered to different leaders, and often operated in different valleys. Describing them as one “independence army” is therefore misleading, even though Korean memory later fused them.

The armed movement in Manchuria combined older traditions of the anti-Japanese righteous armies with new organizations formed around exile settlements and military schools. Hong Beom-do's forces, Choe Jin-dong's units, and the Northern Military Administration Office associated with Kim Jwa-jin came from different experiences and maintained distinct command structures. A common cause did not give them a single national staff. The presence of Daejonggyo communities and earlier self-strengthening schools helps explain the relationship between cultural institutions, settlement politics, and armed mobilization. Recruits were often migrants whose farming villages provided both family support and the spaces in which military training became possible. The strategic setting mattered. Korean formations could launch border raids and engage Japanese detachments but could not match Japanese regular forces in sustained supplies, artillery, or transport. Chinese military authorities in the region had their own interests in avoiding Japanese pressure, and Russian civil-war conditions complicated arms markets and refuge. The differences among Korean commands became consequential when forces attempted to assemble larger formations. A proper order of battle identifies the units under each commander and distinguishes those present at Bongodong from those at Cheongsanri, rather than attributing every action to one unified 'independence army.'

The Northern Military Administration Office is especially important for distinguishing the 1920 campaigns from a single heroic narrative. Its school and command connections differed from those of Hong Beom-do's formations, and Kim Jwa-jin's leadership belonged to that institutional setting. Combined fighting did not make the commanders interchangeable. A historical table should identify who commanded a specific unit, where it operated and with whom it cooperated. Such precision avoids collapsing the long and complex history of the Manchurian independence forces into a procession of famous names without institutional explanation.

59.38 Training, Recruitment, Weapons, Finance, and Civilian Support

Armies of this kind could not exist apart from the farming communities around them. Villages supplied food, shelter, couriers, and intelligence on Japanese movements; households paid contributions that functioned as informal taxation; schools doubled as military academies. Rifles and ammunition were acquired through cross-border markets and upheavals connected to the Russian Civil War and Siberian intervention; the timing and suppliers varied by organization and should be documented case by case. Money was always short. Chinese local authorities tolerated Korean activity unevenly: warlord officials in Manchuria sought to avoid open conflict with Japan, and Japanese diplomats pressed them to restrain or expel Korean groups. This dependence meant that a military success could expose the supporting villages to retaliation.

The human resources of an independence unit were as important as its weapons. Farming households supplied food, bedding, temporary shelter, and information, and emigrant organizations collected contributions. Cadets learned political ideas alongside basic military instruction, but the quality of training varied and constant movement disrupted recruitment. The supply of ammunition could not easily be replaced after every engagement, making the choice to fight or withdraw a political and logistical decision. Individual recollections of acquiring rifles should be treated as accounts tied to particular units and periods, not evidence of a uniform international supply system. Civilian support created dangers. Contributions imposed burdens on poor farmers, and Japanese reprisals could target villages suspected of aiding fighters. Chinese officials might demand disarmament when Japanese diplomatic pressure intensified. Schools and religious organizations connected communities to military organizers, but could be attacked as alleged bases. These constraints explain why a force might win a local engagement yet be unable to retain its bases. Students should examine soldiers' memoirs and village accounts together, asking whether success is measured by enemy losses, survival of fighters, protection of civilians, or continued ability to recruit and supply a movement.

Civilian provisioning should not be treated as entirely voluntary or entirely coerced. Households could regard fighters as protectors and still struggle to meet contributions in food or money. Local leaders had to balance claims of national solidarity with the risks of Japanese military retaliation and demands from Chinese officials. The history of military supply is therefore also the history of migrant household budgets. A military account that praises supporters for generosity may omit the cost of supplying an armed unit during a poor harvest. This is one reason to read community testimony beside officers' memoirs.

59.43 Refugees, Disrupted Communities, and the Reorganization of Armed Resistance

The reprisals displaced families, destroyed crops and school buildings, and cut supply routes. Armed units moved north toward northern Manchuria and the Russian Far East, where they sought Soviet territory as a refuge and arms source. This retreat reshaped the movement: it removed fighters from the border they had hoped to contest and exposed them to a different political environment, with consequences examined in the next division.

Retreat after the campaigns was shaped by more than fear of a renewed attack. Military units required supplies, recruits, and a friendly jurisdiction in which to regroup; the Gando reprisal campaigns damaged all three. Korean households displaced by violence could not easily sustain soldiers. Units therefore moved north through Manchuria, considering the Russian Far East as a source of protection and political support. The movement was uneven, and commands sometimes disputed whether to preserve independent military organization or accept the authority of foreign revolutionary institutions. For civilians, flight carried a different meaning than military redeployment. A household that lost its house and harvest needed land, relatives, food, and school access; a military unit sought new commanders, arms, and a base. These intertwined problems made refugee communities politically important without making every refugee a combatant. The Korean Independence Corps emerged in this setting as an attempt to coordinate fragmented units. The subsequent Free City Incident demonstrated how badly plans for military unity could fail when multiple Korean factions and Russian authorities claimed control. This sequence prevents the false impression that the campaign of 1920 ended in a straightforward transition to stronger guerrilla armies.

The consequences of displacement are visible in the later reorganization of armed units. As settlement networks lost food, buildings and security, commanders looked to more distant territories and foreign patrons. But the Soviet Far East introduced political risks of its own, and the hope of supplies did not translate into freedom from external command. The distinction between a family's migration route and a military unit's retreat makes the human story intelligible: people moved for shelter and livelihoods as well as for political strategy. The transition is an institutional failure and a social crisis, not merely the end of one successful campaign.

59.47 Self-Government, Education, Finance, and Armed Protection

The three organizations governed Korean migrant communities as well as raising troops: they ran schools, collected contributions, appointed local officials, and provided security, while negotiating with Chinese authorities who controlled the territory. For many Korean farmers they were the closest thing to a state; for the Japanese they were a threat, and for local Chinese officials a complicated presence.

Self-government in Korean migrant communities required everyday decisions about schools, land, roads, dispute settlement, and contributions for security. The three Manchurian federations organized these activities in different degrees, and for some farmers they provided services unavailable from distant colonial institutions. Yet the authority of a federation was incomplete. Chinese administrations retained territorial sovereignty, rival armed groups operated nearby, and Japanese consular officials pressed for restrictions on Korean political activity. A local school might teach national history and supply new recruits, while the same institution exposed students and teachers to coercive intervention. Sources for these organizations are uneven. Official proclamations state what leaders hoped to do; taxation records or local correspondence reveal what residents actually paid; memoirs may credit a beloved commander with achievements that belonged to several groups. A successful account also considers minority voices: households may have supported the independence cause but objected to compulsory contributions or military demands. The analytical point is that armed organization and civilian governance were intertwined, not identical, and that a military group's ability to govern depended on legitimacy within communities whose daily needs remained urgent.

The question of how these federations governed also leads to difficult comparisons with the colonial state. Their leaders claimed to administer Korean communities for national purposes, but their authority operated in territory formally under Chinese rule, without the fiscal base of a recognized state. Decisions on education and contributions could be welcomed by some residents and resisted by others. Published proclamations are therefore evidence of goals, not proof of uniform local compliance. 59.48 Attempts to Unify Manchurian Independence Organizations

The northern Manchurian force of the 1930s was linked to the Korean Independence Party, while other armies, associations and political groups maintained separate leadership. A useful research table would identify Hong Jin's role, Ji Cheong-cheon's military command, the communities supplying recruits and the Chinese partner formations at each date. The distinction between party program and army operations prevents students from interpreting a political declaration as a factual report on battlefield strength.

59.52 The Korean Revolutionary Party and Korean Revolutionary Army in Southern Manchuria

In southern Manchuria the Korean Revolutionary Party and its Korean Revolutionary Army, led by Yang Se-bong, developed separately from the northern forces. They operated in different terrain and communities, near the Korean border, and had their own political connections and command structure. The northern and southern movements shared nationalism and cooperation with Chinese forces but were distinct organizations.

The Korean Revolutionary Party and Korean Revolutionary Army in southern Manchuria had their own political origins, commanders, and communities. Yang Se-bong's leadership was associated with the southern nationalist military tradition and armed activity nearer the Korean frontier. These formations should not be conflated with Ji Cheong-cheon's northern Korean Independence Army, despite common opposition to Japan and overlapping aspirations for a sovereign Korea. Differences in geography altered the practical possibilities of cross-border movement, cooperation with Chinese volunteer commanders, and protection of agricultural settlements. The political affiliation of an armed group also affects how historians read its actions. A military unit might claim responsibility for an operation that involved Chinese partners, and Chinese commanders might regard it as part of a wider anti-Japanese campaign rather than specifically a Korean national struggle. Police and intelligence reports introduced another perspective by identifying all such forces as threats to order. Comparing source origins reduces the danger of assigning a modern nationalist identity to every participant. The southern movement's performance must be assessed in connection with civilians, alliances, and a changing strategic environment, not merely with the name of its principal commander.

Southern Manchuria's geography made border movement and Chinese regional politics central to the Korean Revolutionary Army's prospects. Its commanders operated close to villages whose inhabitants could supply fighters but also suffer reprisals. The party and army organizations must be distinguished in both dates and membership, because political leadership did not automatically translate into battlefield command. Comparison with the northern army should specify allies, supply routes and the political relationships of commanders rather than collapsing both into a generic anti-Japanese army. These distinctions help students recognize why simultaneous nationalist campaigns could have different outcomes and levels of durability.

59.53 Yang Se-bong and Korean–Chinese Allied Resistance in Southern Manchuria

From 1932 the Korean Revolutionary Army fought alongside Chinese volunteer armies in southern Manchuria. Yang Se-bong’s leadership was central to its cohesion, and his death in 1934 was a heavy blow. Growing Japanese pressure, intelligence penetration, and reprisals against supporting villages further reduced the movement’s capacity to continue.

Yang Se-bong became an important commander in the Korean Revolutionary Army's cooperation with Chinese anti-Japanese volunteer forces after Japan's 1931 Manchurian advance. The alliance joined fighters with different political constituencies but a shared immediate adversary. Engagements depended on local geography, the readiness of Chinese partners, and the ability of Korean settlers to sustain supplies. Success could be temporary when the occupying administration replaced commanders, expanded punitive operations, or severed routes to friendly communities. Yang's death in 1934 severely affected the southern army's cohesion, but it should not be treated as the only cause of its decline. Japanese military expansion, information gathering, disruption of supply, changes in Chinese forces, and pressures on civilian communities also weakened the movement. Later patriotic biographies can overstate one commander's role while obscuring these structural conditions. The southern campaigns should therefore be compared with northern cooperative operations and later communist guerrilla units: all fought Japan, but their political institutions, foreign partnerships, social support, and patterns of survival differed substantially.

Yang Se-bong's death belongs to the history of military leadership, but the broader erosion of the southern movement requires attention to Japan's expanding police and military apparatus in Manchukuo. The replacement of local allies and the difficulty of shielding civilian supporters weakened independent campaigning. Chinese anti-Japanese volunteer forces themselves faced internal division and Japanese pressure, which limited the protection they could offer. A narrative focused only on one heroic commander would miss these external constraints. The history of the southern army shows the dependence of nationalist armed struggle on institutions and communities beyond its direct command.

59.54 Korean Communists in Chinese Revolutionary and Guerrilla Organizations

After the 1928 Comintern intervention and the breakup of the Korean Communist Party’s existing organization, Korean communists in Manchuria were instructed to join the Chinese Communist Party, and many did so in the early 1930s, serving in its guerrilla forces. In 1936 various units were organized into the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army. These Korean communists differed from the nationalist armies in ideology, organization, and command, though both opposed Japanese rule. A grim episode was the Minsaengdan affair of 1932–1936, in which Korean communists in the borderland were suspected of working for Japan and many were purged or killed within the Chinese communist movement. Later biographies of communist guerrillas, including that of Kim Il Sung, belong to Chapter 63.

Korean communists in northeastern China increasingly operated within the Chinese Communist Party's organizational framework after the failures of the Korean party in the late 1920s. These networks were shaped by Chinese revolutionary strategy, the Comintern, and local conditions in Korean migrant communities. The Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army that emerged in the mid-1930s included Koreans and Chinese under a wider anti-Japanese coalition, but the organization developed over time; it should not be projected unchanged backward into 1931. The later wartime record and Kim Il Sung's biography belong primarily to Chapter 63. A central interpretive difficulty is the Minsaengdan affair. The group known as Minsaengdan was created in 1932 in a setting of Japanese influence and political manipulation; accusations that Korean cadres belonged to it subsequently spread within communist organizations. Suspicion and purges harmed Korean communists and civilian communities. Not every victim was proven to have participated in pro-Japanese activities, and later political memory has often used the episode to assign collective blame. A sober account distinguishes the documented organization from the expanding accusations and recognizes that internal coercion, ethnic mistrust, and foreign occupation could interact destructively within an anti-Japanese movement.

The Minsaengdan affair requires a particularly explicit distinction between allegation and proof. The existence of the original organization and Japanese attempts at political influence do not establish that all Koreans later accused of membership were actual agents. Accusations proliferated within Chinese communist networks amid pressure, ethnic mistrust and internal factional struggles. The resulting purges harmed the very communities from which guerrillas drew recruits and supplies. The episode complicates celebratory accounts of anti-Japanese unity and demonstrates how a liberation movement could turn coercion inward. Later Korean state narratives must be read critically because each sought a political genealogy from this period.

Guerrillas depended on villagers for food, information, and concealment, and villagers paid the price. Recruitment and requisitions strained farm households, fighting displaced families and destroyed harvests, and Japanese and Manchukuo forces answered with reprisals, village relocation, and collective punishment. Korean settlers, Chinese farmers, nationalist fighters, communist units, and occupation authorities had different interests, and the same village could be pressed by several sides. These pressures help explain why guerrilla resistance, despite its courage, could not be sustained against a state able to cut off the surrounding population.

Guerrilla warfare depended on cooperation from civilians who were trying to preserve homes, farms, and local trade. Villagers might provide food and shelter to nationalist or communist units, but armed groups could also make demands on them without genuine consent. Japanese and Manchukuo forces regarded the same settlements as possible supply bases for insurgents and developed policies of surveillance, punitive raids, and population control. The degree of coercion differed by locality and period. Civilians could be caught among overlapping claims from Chinese troops, Japanese authorities, Korean political factions, and guerrilla commanders. The record frequently celebrates commanders while obscuring these choices. Evidence of village displacement, school closures, and harvest disruption helps connect armed history to the economic and migration developments in Chapter 58. A campaign that forced a Japanese unit to withdraw might still increase the vulnerability of supporting families. Students should compare different criteria for military effectiveness and remember that villagers did not necessarily identify themselves with one exclusive organization. The wider phenomenon of forced village relocation and expanding counterinsurgency foreshadows the harsher wartime environment after 1937, but its full regional military history belongs to Chapters 61 and 63.

Civilians also made choices that are often invisible in military narratives. A farmer might feed guerrillas because of political commitment, family obligation, fear or hope of future protection. The same person might cooperate with an occupying official to obtain a ration or keep a child in school. These actions do not fit neatly into the later categories of resistance and collaboration. Counterinsurgency campaigns made neutrality harder, and different governments could classify ordinary survival as political loyalty or betrayal. The history must preserve this ambiguity while clearly identifying documented atrocities and coercive state policy.

Diaspora Organizing and International Independence Networks

59.56 Korean Students, Workers, and Political Associations in Japan

By the 1920s tens of thousands of Koreans lived in Japan as students and laborers (Chapter 58). Student associations in Tokyo and Kyoto had already contributed to the 1919 independence declaration, and in the 1920s Koreans formed labor mutual-aid societies, socialist study groups, and, later, unions, often cooperating with Japanese leftists. Koreans in Japan faced wage discrimination, housing exclusion, and police surveillance; the violence against Koreans after the 1923 Kanto earthquake, whose death toll is estimated very differently by Japanese official and Korean sources, deepened political consciousness. Activism in Japan connected economic grievances with anti-colonial politics.

Korean students and wage earners in Japan were located inside the imperial state but were not socially or politically equal to Japanese residents. Student circles in Tokyo and Kyoto exchanged Korean political ideas through associations and publications; workers organized around wages, mutual assistance, and exclusion from housing or occupations. Some Korean activists cooperated with Japanese socialists, while others insisted on distinct Korean national leadership. Japanese police surveillance made the relationship between ordinary labor organizing and anti-colonial politics particularly contentious. The 1919 declaration by Korean students in Tokyo supplied an important antecedent, but interwar groups had new constituencies and different circumstances. The Kantō earthquake of September 1923 and the mass violence against Koreans in its aftermath marked an especially grave moment. Rumors that Koreans had poisoned wells or committed arson circulated amid disaster and contributed to killings by vigilantes and other actors. Estimates of the dead vary widely depending on source and scope, so a precise figure without provenance would be irresponsible. The violence deepened awareness of ethnic vulnerability but did not cause all Korean political organization in Japan. The experiences of students, manual workers, established families, and political exiles differed; the chapter should study the interaction between discrimination and activism without assuming that shared ethnicity produced a single ideology.

The violence following the Kantō earthquake must be treated as an episode of racialized persecution and rumor in a disaster zone, not simply as an unfortunate side effect of a natural catastrophe. Some Japanese officials, police, soldiers and civilian groups played different roles in creating, spreading or acting upon accusations against Koreans. The range of estimated deaths is a matter of source evidence and political memory. Students should not infer a reliable death total from a modern textbook without knowing the underlying investigation. The episode helps explain migrant fears, but the history of Korean students and workers in Japan encompasses many additional years of organization and negotiation.

59.57 Korean Exile Organizations in Shanghai, Beijing, Nanjing, and Other Chinese Cities

Korean exiles in China built publishing networks, schools, and political organizations, and competed for support from Chinese authorities and parties. The Provisional Government remained in Shanghai until Japanese pressure forced it out in 1932. Revolutionary and anarchist circles clustered in Beijing and later in Nanjing, and Chinese Nationalist leaders occasionally supported Korean activities, including a small military training program for Korean cadets at Luoyang in 1933–1934. Chinese support was shaped by Chinese politics and by relations with Japan, not by a commitment to Korean sovereignty as such.

Chinese cities offered Korean exiles an extraordinary variety of political relationships. Shanghai held the Provisional Government and several Korean-language publishing institutions, but its international concessions operated under foreign jurisdictions vulnerable to diplomatic pressure. Beijing attracted nationalists, anarchists, and writers; Nanjing became increasingly important for political training and coalition-building. The political location of Chinese partners mattered. Cooperation with Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government differed from association with local Chinese warlords or Communist Party networks in Manchuria. A change in Chinese policy could determine the fate of an exile school or training program more quickly than any formal Korean resolution. The 1933 planning and February 1934 opening of a Korean special training class associated with Luoyang illustrate the practical possibilities and limits of Chinese sponsorship. Some Korean activists acquired military education, but the program did not establish a single national army. Chinese officials pursued their own strategic priorities in confronting Japan and handling competing Korean groups. This environment helped produce the later 1935 National Revolutionary Party, while Kim Gu's circle followed a different path. Reconstructing the chronology requires distinguishing political negotiations, formal founding, student recruitment, and the later career of graduates; these are separate events, not one undifferentiated 'cadet program.'

The special Korean training program at Luoyang illustrates the difference between receiving military education and possessing an independent Korean military institution. Chinese support could provide facilities and instruction but was conditioned by Chinese Nationalist politics and military priorities. The date of a negotiated plan is not the same as the date classes opened: the February 1934 commencement must be distinguished from 1933 discussions. These details matter because later biographies frequently condense years of networking into a single training episode. A university chapter should map the separate roles of Korean organizers, Chinese patrons and the students themselves.

59.58 Korean Organizations in the Russian Far East and Soviet Political Networks

Koreans in the Russian Far East had one of the earliest and largest emigrant communities, and the region was a base for socialist and nationalist activism. After the Free City Incident and the consolidation of Soviet rule, Soviet authorities treated Korean activism as subordinate to Soviet foreign policy and security concerns, which restricted independence movements. In the mid-1930s Soviet suspicion of Koreans as possible Japanese agents culminated in the 1937 deportation to Central Asia (see Chapter 58).

Korean communities of the Russian Far East predated the Bolshevik Revolution and included farmers, traders, local educators, and independence-minded organizations. During the Russian Civil War, shifting power among Red, White, interventionist, and regional authorities made the security of Korean communities uncertain. Some activists saw the Soviet state as a potential ally against Japanese imperialism; others were wary of being absorbed into a foreign party and army. The Free City disaster showed that these concerns were not theoretical. After Soviet control consolidated, the room for autonomous armed Korean activity narrowed as foreign policy and domestic security priorities took precedence. In 1937 Soviet authorities forcibly removed the ethnic Korean population of the Soviet Far East to Central Asia. Chapter 58 provides the demographic and economic history, but Chapter 59 must explain the political significance: borderland Koreans were treated as a potential security risk amid Soviet-Japanese tensions, regardless of the political behavior of individuals. The deportation was carried out by a state whose revolutionary rhetoric had earlier attracted some Korean anti-imperial activists. This apparent contradiction demonstrates the dangers of treating foreign support as guaranteed by shared ideology. It also cautions against retrospectively identifying every Korean resident of Soviet territory with the communist independence movement.

Soviet policy toward Korean migrants changed from revolutionary internationalist possibilities to an increasingly restrictive security regime. The 1937 forced deportation was a mass action against an ethnic population, not a selective punishment of proven Japanese agents. This should be explicitly distinguished from the earlier Free City military confrontation, which involved armed formations and disputed command. Combining the two under a vague description of Soviet betrayal would obscure their different mechanisms and historical contexts. Both events nonetheless demonstrate how severely Korean communities could be affected by the strategic priorities of foreign states.

59.59 Korean Americans, Hawaii, Overseas Fundraising, and International Advocacy

Korean communities in Hawaii and the continental United States, many of them former plantation laborers, raised substantial funds through the Korean National Association and related bodies and supported schools and political organizations. Rhee Syng-man’s followers, Ahn Chang-ho’s associates, and Pak Yong-man’s military-minded supporters led rival camps, and disputes over money and leadership split Korean communities. Lobbying in Washington won sympathy from missionaries and some politicians, but the United States did not recognize the Provisional Government, and American support never translated into a policy change before the 1940s.

Korean migration to Hawaii and the continental United States generated durable associations whose contributions included money, publicity, education, and political lobbying. The Korean National Association and related institutions connected local membership organizations with a broad claim to represent the interests of a nation deprived of sovereignty. Churches, newspapers, and community halls enabled organizing; political statements circulated to American supporters and officials. In some circumstances these groups financed overseas political work more reliably than organizations based near the Japanese border. Leadership disagreements were significant. Rhee Syng-man, Ahn Chang-ho, and Pak Yong-man represented overlapping but often competing political projects emphasizing diplomacy, organizational preparation, or military initiatives. Disputes over representation, funds, and how to respond to the Provisional Government's crises reflected a problem seen in Shanghai: how could overseas associations speak for Koreans living under colonial rule who had not delegated authority to them? American sympathy did not translate into formal recognition of Korean independence during the interwar period. A full analysis therefore distinguishes philanthropic interest, missionary support, access to legislators, and actual governmental policy. Contributions sustained political activity but could not substitute for foreign recognition or territorial control.

Hawaii's plantation-era Korean migration produced community institutions with a different social base from the Shanghai exile circles. Fundraising could come from workers making relatively modest contributions over many years, while a political leader's public recognition often depended on churches, newspaper networks and personal relationships. This structure made disagreement over accounting and representation especially consequential. The American government's refusal to recognize the Provisional Government in the interwar period also demonstrates a central distinction: a politically active diaspora can influence public opinion without being able to determine foreign policy. The independence movement required both local community infrastructure and external political opportunities it did not control.

New Alignments, Strategic Evaluation, and the Transition toward 1937

59.60 Attempts to Reunify Overseas Nationalist and Revolutionary Movements, 1932–1935

After the Manchurian crisis and the Shanghai incident of 1932, exile groups pursued new coalitions. In late 1932 several Korean organizations in China formed the Korean United Front League against Japan, bringing together groups with differing positions on class, republican government, and revolutionary organization. The coalition was fragile, because its members disagreed on whether to subordinate social revolution to national liberation and on who should lead.

The emergence of new alliances after 1932 must be viewed against Japan's occupation of Manchuria and fighting in Shanghai. Chinese hostility to Japanese expansion created opportunities for Korean nationalists and revolutionaries to seek training, political protection, and financial help. Yet Korean organizations remained divided over recognition of the Provisional Government, the social program of an independent state, and who could represent overseas constituencies. The Korean United Front League against Japan, established in the early 1930s, attempted to join groups with different traditions and build a wider anti-imperialist coalition. Coalitions were fragile for practical as well as ideological reasons. Party leaders differed over leadership offices, the control of dues and contributions, the place of armed units, and the value of relations with Chinese sponsors. Uiyeoldan veterans, Provisional Government loyalists, northern Manchurian nationalists, and socialist groups brought unequal resources and reputations. Some participants viewed unity as the prerequisite for action; others feared that compromise would surrender principles. The changes of 1935 were therefore the outcome of several years of negotiation, not an abrupt discovery that Korea needed an independence movement. Students should compare coalition declarations with the actual groups that entered or withdrew.

The Korean United Front League's history is a useful test of organizational continuity. It brought together groups that agreed on anti-Japanese struggle but disagreed on the centrality of the Provisional Government and the economic program of the future state. Some coalition building led toward the 1935 National Revolutionary Party; other participants remained attached to different nationalist institutions. A source-based account should name the participating organizations at each stage and record departures or reorganizations rather than treating the league as a single stable party. The inability to settle constitutional leadership remained a recurring problem even as Japan's growing threat encouraged cooperation.

59.63 Korean Resistance on the Eve of the Full-Scale Sino-Japanese War

By 1936 nationalist and communist organizations remained divided, Korean guerrillas were involved in broader Chinese resistance, and overseas groups continued to seek alliances and resources. Within Korea, repression had reduced open organizing. In China, the move toward a Chinese united front against Japan in 1936–1937 changed the political landscape for Korean exiles, who now faced both opportunity and the likelihood of a larger war. Participants faced unresolved choices about alliance, strategy, and whether to bind their cause to Chinese or other powers. Detailed treatment of the wartime history of these organizations belongs to Chapter 63.

By the middle of the 1930s no independence movement exercised sovereign authority over Korea. Nationalist parties in China sought alliances and legitimacy, communists operated through changing international organizations, domestic associations had suffered repression, and armed formations in Manchuria depended on local support and Chinese partners. Yet political initiative had not ended. The recent founding of rival nationalist and revolutionary parties, the survival of diaspora organizations, and the continued mobilization of students and villagers testified to multiple forms of endurance. Their ability to act depended on the same economic and international conditions that placed limits on them. The escalation toward full-scale war in 1937 transformed these circumstances without resolving old disagreements. Japan's military campaigns would alter regional alignments; wartime mobilization would expose Koreans to new coercion; exile political leaders would seek new opportunities among Chinese authorities; and Korean communists in northeastern China would follow different military paths. These developments belong principally to Chapters 61–63. Chapter 59 should close by keeping history open: participants in 1936 did not know when or how Japanese rule would end, which organizations would survive, or whether they would agree on the future republic. Their different political projects remained real and consequential before the events of 1945.

The chronological boundary at 1937 protects two kinds of accuracy. First, the organizations of 1935 and 1936 must be described according to the possibilities they faced at that time, not as steps necessarily leading to their later wartime careers. Second, Chapter 63 must have space to examine the transformation of the Provisional Government, the Korean Liberation Army and communist guerrillas under the wider Sino-Japanese and Pacific wars. Students should finish Chapter 59 understanding that political division persisted alongside anti-colonial commitment. The future course of independence was unresolved, even though the memory of earlier resistance later became central to rival postwar political claims.

Historical Vocabulary

Guerrilla Warfare, Political Exile, and Sovereignty

Guerrilla warfare: irregular fighting by small mobile forces that depend on local populations for support. Political exile: residence abroad of activists who cannot operate openly at home; exiles depended on host governments’ tolerance. Sovereignty: supreme authority over a territory and people; the Provisional Government claimed it without exercising it. Potential misunderstanding: recognition by foreign governments and sheltering by them were not the same thing.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Student under Surveillance

A secondary-school student in the late 1920s might join a reading circle that met discreetly, read banned or borrowed texts, and discuss school discrimination. Teachers, police, and school authorities watched for such groups, and discovery could mean expulsion, arrest, and a record that closed off future careers. Families weighed the risk of their children’s involvement against hopes for their education. Evidence for such lives survives mainly in police files and later memoirs.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household near an Independence Army

A Korean farming family in Gando might provide food, lodging, or information to independence fighters, pay contributions to a federation, and send children to its school. The same household risked requisition by several armed groups and reprisals from Japanese forces, and might be forced to flee when villages were burned. Women ran households and farms through these disruptions, and often bore the work of feeding and sheltering fighters.

Multiple Perspectives

The Shinganhoe Moment

A nationalist organizer: sought a legal platform for mass awareness.
A socialist activist: saw an opening for class organization within national unity.
A colonial police official: monitored a large association as a potential threat.
A student or woman organizer: found in it space for education and rights campaigns.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Changed: from pre-1910 righteous armies to party, student, and union organizing, and ideologies of republicanism and socialism. Continued: reliance on rural communities, borderland refuge, and exile. Why it matters: resistance adapted to colonial modernity rather than repeating earlier forms.

Cause and Effect

Long-term: colonial rule and diaspora geography. Short-term: failure of 1919 diplomacy. Effect: proliferation of strategies and organizations. Consequence: fragmentation, repression, and repeated coalition attempts. Complexity: repression both radicalized and dismantled movements.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Korean resistance after 1919 was neither a single organization nor an unbroken sequence of battles. It was a fragmented but interconnected struggle in which nationalists, socialists, anarchists, students, women, religious communities, guerrillas, and overseas Koreans pursued different strategies and different visions of a future Korea.

The geography of resistance shaped its forms. The colonial capital permitted limited legal organizing under heavy surveillance; Manchuria offered borderland refuge and armed bases but exposed civilians to reprisal; Shanghai and other Chinese cities provided exile under foreign tolerance; the Russian Far East was shaped by Soviet policy; and Japan, Hawaii, and North America supplied students, workers, and funds.

The Provisional Government held symbolic republican legitimacy but little power. Its 1923 conference and the 1925 removal of Rhee Syng-man exposed disputes over authority, money, and strategy, and its leadership was repeatedly reorganized. Cultural nationalists and noncompromise nationalists divided over how to work with colonial institutions.

Socialism, communism, and anarchism entered Korean politics after 1917 and developed into distinct movements. The Korean Communist Party, founded in 1925, was repeatedly broken by arrests and factionalism and by the Comintern’s 1928 intervention; anarchists proposed stateless federations of mutual aid; and nationalists and socialists sometimes cooperated, as in Shinganhoe (1927–1931), which became the largest legal anti-colonial association before dissolving.

Revolutionary direct action, from Uiyeoldan to the Korean Patriotic Corps, aimed to inspire rather than overwhelm. Its 1932 actions won Chinese sympathy but also drew pursuit and reprisal, and their political effect remains debated. Student movements, notably the Gwangju protests of 1929–1930, showed how everyday discrimination could be turned into national protest.

Armed struggle in Manchuria produced the engagements of 1920 and the heavy civilian toll of the Gando massacres. Casualty claims remain disputed. The defeats and the Free City Incident of 1921 shattered the armies, and the later Manchurian federations, together with Korean Independence Army and Korean Revolutionary Army cooperation with Chinese forces in the 1930s, illustrated both the potential and the limits of guerrilla war.

By 1937 Korean resistance was persistent but divided. Two new parties formed in 1935 rather than one united front. No single strategy brought independence before a wider regional war changed the situation, and later outcomes, including liberation in 1945 and division afterward, were not predetermined.
