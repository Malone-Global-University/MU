Nationalism, Race, Antisemitism, and Modern Social Thought

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 13 January 1898, a Paris newspaper carried across its front page an open letter to the President of the Republic. Its author accused named officers of the French army of concealing a miscarriage of justice. The letter did not settle a debate about one prisoner. It forced a whole society to ask who belonged to the nation, what the state owed to an individual, and whether loyalty and justice could be set against one another.

The newspaper was L’Aurore, the author was the novelist Émile Zola, and the letter, headlined J’Accuse…!, was addressed to Félix Faure, President of the French Republic. Its subject was the conviction in December 1894 of Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish artillery officer from an Alsatian family, for passing military information to Germany. By January 1898 a growing body of evidence pointed to another officer, Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy, and a military court had just acquitted Esterhazy. Zola’s letter, which the editor Georges Clemenceau framed with a bold headline, accused the military and governmental authorities of shielding wrongdoing. Zola understood that he risked prosecution for libel. After his conviction and further legal proceedings, he fled to England in July 1898 to avoid imprisonment. The front page of L’Aurore is therefore a useful emblem for this chapter: a mass-circulation newspaper, a public intellectual, an appeal to universal principles, and a state institution defended in the name of national honor.

The Dreyfus Affair did not arise from nowhere. It grew out of the political world that the unification of Germany and Italy, the defeat of France in 1870 to 1871, the spread of mass politics, and the industrial transformations of the previous decades had created. Between roughly 1870 and 1914, European states built national school systems, conscript armies, and mass-circulation press industries. Governments and political movements asked citizens to identify with a nation, and the question of who counted as a member of that nation became more urgent and more bitterly contested. Chapter 97 traced the origins of nationalism as an ideology, and Chapter 100 examined the creation of Italy and Germany. This chapter turns to what nationalism became after those states existed.

The same decades saw a remarkable expansion of scientific and scholarly inquiry into human societies. Evolutionary biology, anthropology, statistics, sociology, and psychology all matured into recognizable fields, and some of that work was careful, empirical, and valuable. Yet influential writers also borrowed the prestige of science to assert that humanity was divided into biologically unequal races, that nations were engaged in natural struggles for survival, and that social problems could be traced to inherited deficiency. These claims were ideological and pseudoscientific, not established biological facts. They nonetheless shaped public debate, university teaching, colonial policy, and political programs, and a responsible historical account must explain both why they attracted followers and why they were unsound.

Antisemitism occupies a central place in this story, and it requires independent treatment, not merely an introduction to the Dreyfus Affair. Jewish communities in western and central Europe had gained legal equality across the nineteenth century, but formal emancipation did not end social prejudice. From the late 1870s, organized political movements in Germany, Austria, and France made hostility to Jews a public platform, while in the Russian Empire Jews lived under residence restrictions and endured waves of violence. Jewish communities were not passive. They built philanthropic networks, defended civil rights, organized labor movements, created new cultural institutions, emigrated in large numbers, and debated the future of Jewish life in forms that included integration, socialism, cultural autonomy, and Zionism.

The chapter also asks how contemporaries resisted exclusion. The Dreyfusards founded rights organizations, Black intellectuals gathered in London in 1900 to challenge the international color line, minority-language activists built schools and associations, and sociologists developed explanations of inequality that did not depend on inherited racial hierarchy. European modernity before 1914 was not a single road. It produced powerful arguments for equal human dignity and increasingly organized ideologies of exclusion, often in the same cities and sometimes in the same minds.

Historians draw on a wide range of evidence for this subject: newspapers and caricatures, parliamentary and court records, petitions, census and language statistics, school curricula, scientific treatises, ethnographic museum collections, private correspondence, and the publications of political and communal organizations. Much of the surviving propaganda is hostile to the groups it targets. Such material must be read as evidence of the prejudice and the political strategies of its authors, never as reliable testimony about the communities it describes. Within Part XVI, this chapter supplies the ideological and intellectual background to the imperial expansion examined in Chapters 104 and 105, the comparative domestic politics of Chapter 106, and the international crises of Chapter 107.

Students should keep one caution in mind throughout. It is tempting to read these decades only as a prelude to the catastrophes of the twentieth century. That approach flattens the period and erases the opposition, contingency, and variety that historians must recover. The Holocaust and the world wars, treated in Volume V, had causes that cannot be reduced to pre-1914 ideas, and many people in the period resisted the ideologies examined here. This chapter traces genuine continuities, but it does not treat later outcomes as predetermined.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how nationalism changed in character after the unifications of Germany and Italy, and why it could support demands for rights as well as policies of exclusion.
  2. Distinguish civic from ethnic conceptions of the nation while recognizing that historical movements usually combined elements of both.
  3. Compare the situation of national and linguistic minorities in the German Empire, Austria-Hungary, and the Russian Empire, and explain how minorities responded.
  4. Analyze the institutions, including schools, conscription, the press, and public ceremonies, through which national identity was cultivated.
  5. Assess why nineteenth-century racial classification, craniometry, and hierarchies of civilization were scientifically unsound, and how they were used politically.
  6. Distinguish Darwin’s biological theory from Social Darwinism, and trace the ideological uses of evolutionary language.
  7. Trace the emergence of eugenics before 1914 without attributing later coercive programs to every earlier writer.
  8. Explain why legal emancipation did not end antisemitism, and distinguish religious anti-Judaism from modern political and racial antisemitism.
  9. Evaluate the Dreyfus Affair as a case study in prejudice, institutional secrecy, press mobilization, and judicial correction, including the difference between the pardon of 1899 and the annulment of 1906.
  10. Interpret how sociology, psychology, and antiracist and anticolonial critics offered alternatives to biological explanations of society, and connect these tensions to the broader contradictions of European modernity.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did nationalism change after national unification, and why did it increasingly generate competition among states and conflict within them?
  2. Who belonged to a nation, and how did competing definitions of membership determine who was included or excluded from full citizenship?
  3. How did multinational empires govern linguistic and religious diversity, and why did minority communities contest those policies?
  4. What made racial classifications persuasive to many contemporaries, and what evidence shows that they were not scientifically valid?
  5. Why did evolutionary language migrate from biology into political and economic argument, and why was that transfer problematic?
  6. Why did formal legal equality for Jews coexist with organized political antisemitism, and how did Jewish communities respond?
  7. What did the Dreyfus Affair reveal about the relation between national loyalty, individual rights, and the independence of legal institutions?
  8. How did the new social sciences and movements for universal rights challenge hierarchical explanations of human difference, and what limits did they themselves have?

Historical Context

The unification of Italy (1861, with Rome added in 1870) and of Germany (proclaimed at Versailles in January 1871) transformed the map of Europe and the meaning of nationalism. Chapter 100 examined how Cavour, Garibaldi, and Bismarck, together with war and diplomacy, produced these states. Once national unification was achieved, its advocates faced a new task, which was to turn territorial states into communities of citizens who felt national loyalty. The Italian politician Massimo d’Azeglio is traditionally credited with the remark that Italy had been made and that Italians now had to be made, though the exact wording and occasion are uncertain. The sentiment, whatever its precise origin, captures the problem that every new or reorganized state faced.

Two other developments shaped the period. First, Chapter 101 described the Second Industrial Revolution, with its steel, chemicals, electricity, and expanding urban populations. Rapid urbanization, internal migration, and economic crises, particularly the long depression that began in 1873 and affected agriculture, small trade, and craft production unevenly, unsettled many social groups and made them receptive to political explanations of insecurity. Second, Chapter 102 traced the growth of mass politics, with widening suffrage, political parties, trade unions, and mass newspapers. Politicians increasingly sought popular followings, and nationalist and racial arguments proved effective in mobilizing audiences, though they were never the only appeals on offer.

Intellectual change was equally important. Chapter 81 described the Scientific Revolution and Chapter 93 and following the industrial transformations that gave natural science and statistics growing cultural authority. By the second half of the nineteenth century, scientific language carried exceptional prestige, and many writers sought to present political and social arguments as findings of science. Darwin’s work on evolution (1859) was a genuine scientific advance, but it was quickly interpreted in many directions, some of which had little to do with biology. Meanwhile, the colonial empires of Britain, France, and others, together with the Atlantic slave trade and slavery examined in Chapter 70, provided a legacy of racial categories that scholars and administrators reworked into supposedly systematic classifications.

The position of Jews in European societies formed a further background. The French Revolution had granted Jews citizenship in 1790 and 1791 (Chapter 88), and Napoleonic expansion spread some emancipatory measures (Chapter 90). Over the following century legal restrictions were gradually removed in much of western and central Europe, though unevenly and often with setbacks, while in the Russian Empire the majority of the world’s Jews lived under a regime of residence restrictions. The centuries-old tradition of Christian anti-Judaism, which had inspired persecution and expulsions in medieval Europe (Chapters 47 and 49), remained available as a cultural resource, now combined with new economic, nationalist, and pseudoscientific arguments.

Historians reconstruct this period from an exceptionally rich documentary record, and they must read it critically. Official statistics, such as language censuses, did not simply record identities but also shaped them, because the categories offered to respondents determined what could be counted. Newspapers reveal public controversies but also the commercial and political interests of their publishers. Scientific treatises display the prestige of science but also the assumptions of their authors. Court records, parliamentary debates, and the archives of political and communal associations, including Jewish organizations, minority cultural societies, and rights leagues, reveal how contemporaries argued, organized, and resisted. Historians also scrutinize later memory, because national and political communities after 1914 reinterpreted these events, sometimes simplifying them into stories of inevitable progress or inevitable catastrophe.

Nationalism, National Identity, and the Boundaries of Citizenship

Who belonged to the nation?

Section 103.1

Nationalism after 1870: From National Unification to National Competition

Before 1870, the most visible nationalist movements sought to create states. The Italian and German movements examined in Chapters 97 and 100 asked for unification, constitutions, and an end to dynastic fragmentation or foreign rule, and liberal nationalists across Europe associated national self-determination with representative government. After 1871 the situation changed. Italy and Germany existed, and the central question for many nationalists shifted from how to create a nation-state to how to define, strengthen, and defend it. Nationalism in this later period took several overlapping forms: continued movements for autonomy or independence among peoples without their own states, efforts by existing states to consolidate national loyalty, and a more aggressive nationalism that emphasized power, territory, prestige, and supposed cultural superiority.

Historians therefore caution against treating nationalism as a single political position. In the early nineteenth century it was commonly linked with liberal and revolutionary movements, but by the late nineteenth century nationalist arguments were employed by conservatives, liberals, socialists, Catholics, and radical right movements alike. Nationalism was not intrinsically conservative, liberal, democratic, or authoritarian. A Czech activist demanding school instruction in Czech, a German Liberal defending the new Empire, a French republican seeking to recover Alsace-Lorraine, a Hungarian official promoting the Magyar language, and a Pan-German agitator calling for the exclusion of Jews and Slavs all appealed to the nation, but their goals, social bases, and conceptions of political legitimacy were very different.

Several concepts must be kept distinct. Sovereignty refers to supreme political authority within a territory. Citizenship is a legal status carrying rights and duties, which might be defined by birth, residence, descent, or administrative decision. National identity is a sense of belonging to a community imagined as sharing a history, language, culture, or destiny, and it does not always coincide with citizenship. A person could be a legal citizen yet be treated by others as an outsider, and a person could feel national loyalty to a state in which he or she was legally disadvantaged. The relationship among sovereignty, citizenship, language, historical territory, and political legitimacy was consequently always contested, never a settled formula.

Scholars have emphasized in recent decades that national identity was historically constructed, shaped by schools, bureaucracies, armies, newspapers, literature, scholarship, and political organization. That does not mean that nations were mere inventions without popular roots, or that people simply accepted what they were told. Pre-existing languages, religious communities, local loyalties, and shared memories provided materials that activists selected, standardized, and reinterpreted, while ordinary people adopted, adapted, or resisted national messages. Historians of nationalism have disagreed about the weight of older ethnic ties compared with modern institutions, and this chapter treats nations as historical constructions rooted in real social experiences.

Nationalism also changed because of its relation to the state system and the economy. The depression that began in 1873 and the return to protective tariffs in many countries in the late 1870s and 1880s encouraged interest groups to present economic claims in national terms. Great-power rivalry, colonial expansion (Chapters 104 and 105), and military competition (Chapter 107) increasingly allowed politicians to portray national interest as a matter of struggle among states. A nationalism of competition coexisted with older movements of liberation, and sometimes the same people appealed to both: a movement that claimed the right of self-determination for its own people could deny it to others living in the same territory.

A final tension runs through the chapter. Nationalism could be a demand for collective political rights, including language rights, representation, and self-government, but it could also be a means of excluding populations judged insufficiently national. Chapter 102 examined how mass parties and associations organized popular participation. The present chapter takes up the racial, linguistic, religious, and intellectual content of that mobilization, and asks who was welcomed into the national community and who was told that they did not belong.

Section 103.2

Civic Nationalism, Ethnic Nationalism, and Competing Definitions of the Nation

European thinkers and politicians defined the nation in different ways. Some emphasized a political community: people who lived under common laws and institutions and took part in shared public life. Others emphasized shared history and memory, a common language, inherited culture, religion, or supposed common ancestry. Each of these criteria had implications for who could belong. A definition based on political participation could in principle admit newcomers, while one based on descent made membership a matter of birth that could not be acquired. A definition based on language might include anyone who spoke the national tongue, but exclude those who spoke a minority language even when they were loyal citizens.

Scholars have long distinguished civic from ethnic nationalism. The distinction is associated especially with the twentieth-century historian Hans Kohn, who contrasted a western, political nationalism with an eastern, cultural one. Later historians have criticized the contrast as too geographical and too tidy. The sociologist Rogers Brubaker’s comparative study of citizenship in France and Germany (1992) remains an influential qualification, showing that differing traditions of citizenship law were shaped by specific institutional histories and political struggles, not by timeless national characters. French republican nationalism, often called civic, demanded cultural assimilation and could be intolerant of regional and religious difference. German nationalism, often called ethnic, was also embedded in constitutional institutions and legal traditions. The categories are therefore analytical tools to be used with care.

Historical Vocabulary
Civic Nationalism, Ethnic Nationalism, and National Belonging

Civic nationalism defines the nation as a political community of citizens bound by common institutions, laws, and shared participation, and in theory open to anyone who accepts them. Ethnic nationalism defines the nation by inherited characteristics, usually language, culture, religion, or presumed descent, and makes membership depend on belonging to a particular ancestral community. National belonging is the broader and less formal question of whether a person is accepted as part of the nation by neighbors, institutions, and the state, regardless of legal status.

Historical movements rarely fit one category exactly. The terms matter in this chapter because they help explain why legal equality could coexist with exclusion: a Jewish, Polish, or colonial subject might hold citizenship or legal rights yet still be told, by influential voices, that national belonging required something that could not be changed.

The most famous late nineteenth-century statement of a political and voluntarist view is Ernest Renan’s lecture Qu’est-ce qu’une nation? (What Is a Nation?), delivered at the Sorbonne on 11 March 1882, in the shadow of the loss of Alsace-Lorraine. Renan argued that a nation could not be defined by race, language, religion, geography, or material interest alone. In his account, a nation was a spiritual principle based on a shared heritage of memories and sacrifices and on present consent, the will to continue living together. Renan also observed that forgetting was a necessary part of national formation, since all nations had histories of violence that their members preferred not to recall. His rejection of race as the basis of nationhood is significant, though he did not abandon the hierarchical assumptions about civilizations that were common among his contemporaries.

Other writers argued very differently. Nationalists in many countries relied primarily on language, folklore, historical territory, or ancestry. In the German-speaking world, the Romantic emphasis on language and culture examined in Chapter 97 could be developed into more exclusive claims, and some political thinkers shifted from cultural or linguistic nationalism toward theories presenting national identity as biologically inherited. In France, the writer Maurice Barrès developed a nationalism rooted in land, ancestors, and the dead, in which people of different origins were treated as less authentically French. Houston Stewart Chamberlain, a British-born writer who settled in Germany, published The Foundations of the Nineteenth Century in 1899, arguing that history was driven by racial struggle and elevating a supposed Germanic or Aryan race. Such works were influential in some circles, but they were ideological constructions whose biological and historical claims were disputed even at the time. Some contemporary scientists and scholars promoted racial hierarchies, while others rejected them; modern research does not substantiate claims of inherited racial superiority.

The consequences for those at the margins were considerable. Speakers of minority languages could be treated as unreliable, and members of minority religions, especially Jews and in some regions Muslims or Protestants in Catholic societies, could be characterized as having divided loyalties. Legal equality did not automatically produce social acceptance. A citizen could vote and hold property yet be denied commissions, professorships, or club memberships, or be presented in the press as an alien presence. Because this contrast between formal rights and social recognition is central to later sections, students should treat the definition of the nation as a practical question about everyday inclusion as well as an abstract question of theory.

Finally, definitions of the nation interacted with state interest. Governments used the language of national unity to discipline dissent, while opposition groups used it to challenge governments that they accused of betraying the nation. Socialists criticized nationalism as a distraction from class struggle but frequently operated within national political systems and could be drawn into patriotic sentiment. Catholic movements, liberal reformers, and conservatives likewise negotiated between universal and national loyalties. The question of belonging was thus embedded in every major political tradition of the period.

Section 103.3

National Minorities and Multinational States: Language, Autonomy, and Assimilation

Much of central and eastern Europe was governed by states that were not nation-states. Austria-Hungary, reorganized by the Compromise (Ausgleich) of 1867 into two partly autonomous halves under a single monarch, contained Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Ukrainians (often called Ruthenians in official usage), Slovaks, Romanians, Slovenes, Croats, Serbs, Italians, Jews, and others. The Russian Empire ruled Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Poles, Lithuanians, Latvians, Estonians, Finns, Jews, Tatars, and many other peoples. The German Empire, though much more linguistically unified, included Polish, Danish, French-speaking, and Lithuanian-speaking populations. Britain, though not a multinational empire in the same sense on the European mainland, faced Irish demands for Home Rule and cultural revival. The Ottoman Empire’s remaining European provinces included Albanians, Bulgarians, Greeks, Serbs, Muslims, Christians, Jews, and others, a diversity that Chapter 106 treats in comparative terms.

The Polish case illustrates how circumstances differed within a single national community divided among three states. In Prussia and, after 1871, the German Empire, Poles faced pressure in schools and administration. Bismarck’s government in the 1870s restricted the Polish language in education, and in 1886 a Prussian Settlement Commission was created to purchase land for German settlers in the eastern provinces. Resistance included private Polish schooling, cooperative banks, agricultural associations, and the school strike at Września (Wreschen) in 1901 against the use of German for religious instruction. In Russian Poland, repression intensified after the failed uprising of 1863, and Polish was increasingly displaced in official use and schooling. In Austrian Galicia, by contrast, a gradual political autonomy after the 1860s allowed Polish elites to control local administration, universities, and the use of Polish, even as Ukrainians within Galicia complained of Polish dominance. Poles thus experienced three very different regimes.

In the Habsburg lands, language was a daily political issue. Czech activists in Bohemia and Moravia demanded equal status for Czech in schools, courts, and the civil service. The language ordinances issued by the Austrian minister-president Kasimir Badeni in 1897 required officials in Bohemia and Moravia to use both German and Czech, provoking furious German nationalist protest, parliamentary obstruction, and street violence, and they were withdrawn in 1899. In the Hungarian half of the monarchy, authorities promoted Magyar as the language of administration and increasingly of education, a policy usually called Magyarization, which affected Slovaks, Romanians in Transylvania, Serbs, Croats within Hungary’s jurisdiction, and Ukrainians. The Hungarian education laws associated with Count Albert Apponyi in 1907 strengthened the requirement for Hungarian instruction. South Slavs debated whether their future lay in autonomy within the monarchy, in a federal arrangement, or in a separate state, themes that become central in Chapters 106 and 107.

Alsace-Lorraine, annexed by Germany in 1871 after the Franco-Prussian War, formed a distinctive borderland. Its population included German-speaking, French-speaking, and bilingual communities, and its political loyalties were varied and shifted over time. The territory was governed as an imperial land (Reichsland) directly under the Empire, without the full status of a federal state until a constitution in 1911. Some residents left for France, others accepted German rule, and many sought local autonomy, while the controversy over French memory and German policy gave the region exceptional symbolic importance. In late 1913 the Zabern incident, in which a German officer’s insulting remarks about Alsatians and the army’s heavy-handed response to protest provoked a parliamentary crisis, showed how fragile relations remained.

Policies directed at minority languages differed and should not be lumped together. Germanization in Prussia, Russification in the Russian Empire, Magyarization in Hungary, and language standardization in other states each had distinct motives, legal frameworks, and degrees of enforcement. Russification, intensified under Alexander III and Nicholas II, included restrictions on language in schools and administration in the western provinces and, from 1899, constraints on Finland’s autonomy. Local conditions also mattered. Officials frequently lacked the resources to enforce policies uniformly, and many rural populations kept their languages despite pressure. Moreover, language use and identity were fluid. In many regions people were bilingual, and census categories such as the Austrian question about the language of daily use (Umgangssprache) turned fluid practice into politically consequential statistics.

Minority communities responded in many ways. They founded private schools, reading rooms, gymnastic societies such as the Czech Sokol movement, cooperative banks, choirs, and newspapers; they petitioned authorities, contested elections, and sent deputies to imperial parliaments. In Ireland, the Gaelic League, founded in 1893, promoted the Irish language and culture within a wider debate over Home Rule. Among the Muslims and Christians of Ottoman Europe, such as Albanians, the League of Prizren of 1878 had begun a political and cultural movement that would develop through the following decades. These communities were internally diverse. Goals ranged from cultural rights, to administrative autonomy, to federal reform, to independence, and activists often disagreed over religion, class, and strategy.

Section 103.4

Schools, Conscription, Newspapers, Monuments, and the Making of National Identity

National identity was cultivated through institutions that touched ordinary lives. The most important was often the school. Across Europe, states expanded compulsory primary education in the later nineteenth century, motivated by economic, military, religious, and political concerns. France’s Third Republic, under laws associated with Jules Ferry in 1881 and 1882, established free, compulsory, and secular primary schooling. Standardized instruction in a national language, geography lessons using national maps, and history textbooks emphasizing national heroes and memorable defeats all taught children to see themselves as part of a continuous national story. Literacy rates rose sharply, though unevenly, and in many regions schools were a central battleground in conflicts between state authority, churches, and minority languages.

Military conscription was a second major institution. After the Prussian victories of the 1860s and 1870, many states adopted or strengthened systems of universal male military service. For many young men, service meant leaving the village, meeting men from other regions, learning the standard language, and being drilled in loyalty to the state. It could reinforce national identification, but it was also resented, and conscripts from minority communities or from the poor had very different experiences from privileged officers. Jewish conscripts in the Russian Empire, for example, served under particular suspicion and discrimination. In several states, the army became a prestigious emblem of national honor, a status that, as Section 103.13 shows, had major consequences in France.

The press and print culture were equally important. The expansion of literacy, cheaper newsprint, railways, and the telegraph supported mass-circulation newspapers, illustrated magazines, and political cartoons. Papers such as the Paris Le Petit Journal reached hundreds of thousands of readers, and illustrated weeklies visualized national events for audiences who could scarcely read long texts. Newspapers created shared moments of national attention, but they also served partisan interests, and they could spread rumors and stereotypes as readily as information. Popular literature, adventure novels, and school readers likewise offered models of national character.

Public ceremonies and monuments made the nation visible in space. Statues of national heroes, memorials to war dead, national holidays such as the French celebration of 14 July (established as a national holiday in 1880), museums, world’s fairs, and military parades turned abstract identity into ritual. The German Empire commemorated the victory at Sedan with Sedan Day celebrations, though these were never universally observed, and many Catholics and socialists kept their distance. Historians influenced by the concept of the “invention of tradition,” associated with the volume edited by Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger (1983), have shown that many apparently ancient national customs were created or elaborated in this period. Popular culture also mattered: choral societies, gymnastic clubs, football and other sports, theater, and festivals expressed local, regional, class, and national loyalties, often at once.

Official national narratives always competed with other identities. Catholic and Protestant loyalties, regional dialects, local customs, class solidarity, and the internationalism of the socialist movement could all outweigh or complicate identification with the nation. In France the Breton, Occitan, and Alsatian languages survived alongside the standard language, even as schools promoted French. In Germany, the Kulturkampf of the 1870s, Bismarck’s struggle with the Catholic Church, showed how the state could treat Catholics as questionable citizens, while Social Democrats, targeted by anti-socialist laws between 1878 and 1890, were likewise described as enemies of the nation. Historians therefore avoid assuming that national identity spread uniformly or that its growth meant the disappearance of other loyalties. Many people were simultaneously villagers, Catholics, workers, and citizens of a nation, and the balance among these shifted with circumstances.

Continuity and Change Over Time

National Identity before and after 1871

What Changed

  • New or reorganized states, notably Germany, Italy, and Austria-Hungary, had to build loyalty through schools, administration, and public ceremony
  • Compulsory schooling, universal conscription, and mass-circulation newspapers reached far larger proportions of the population than before
  • Nationalist movements shifted from emphasizing liberation and unification toward defending state power, cultural primacy, and boundaries of membership
  • Language became a mass political issue through censuses, school laws, and administrative regulations

What Remained Continuous

  • Religion, region, class, and local community remained powerful sources of identity
  • Dynastic loyalty persisted, particularly in the Habsburg and Russian empires and in monarchical Germany
  • Minorities continued to maintain their languages and institutions despite assimilationist pressure
  • Elite and intellectual definitions of the nation continued to compete, as they had since the Romantic movements

Who experienced change most directly? Children in compulsory schools, conscripted young men, urban readers of mass newspapers, and members of minority communities subject to language and administrative policy. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural populations, especially in remote or economically marginal regions, whose daily lives and local loyalties changed more slowly.

Why the pattern matters: The timing shows that national identity was not a natural condition that unification revealed but a continuing project, one that people accepted, modified, and resisted in diverse ways.

Scientific Racism, Social Darwinism, and the Biological Interpretation of Society

How were racial hierarchies constructed and justified?

Section 103.5

The Classification of Humanity: Racial Theories, Anthropology, and Hierarchies of Civilization

European attempts to sort humanity into groups have a long history. In the eighteenth century, naturalists such as Carl Linnaeus and Johann Friedrich Blumenbach proposed classifications of human varieties, using criteria including skin color, skull shape, geography, and temperament. Blumenbach, who is sometimes portrayed as a founder of racial science, insisted that the varieties of humanity shaded into one another and belonged to a single species, though his scheme still contributed to later ranking. Over the following century, writers in Britain, France, Germany, and the United States increasingly treated races as fixed types with inherited mental and moral qualities, and the classifications multiplied. They did not form a single consistent theory. Authors disagreed about how many races existed, whether they shared a common origin, which characteristics defined them, and how mixture should be understood.

These classifications were shaped by the world in which their authors lived. Atlantic slavery, discussed in Chapter 70, had long associated African descent with enslavement and supplied pseudo-justifications for its continuation. The expansion of colonial empires gave Europeans access to peoples they described as primitive, and scholars used a hierarchy of civilizations that placed European societies at the summit to explain, and excuse, colonial rule. Racial categories were therefore not neutral discoveries awaiting confirmation. They emerged from specific relationships of power, and they in turn supported those relationships by presenting inequality as natural.

Arthur de Gobineau’s Essay on the Inequality of the Human Races, published between 1853 and 1855, offered an influential and gloomy version. A French diplomat and writer, Gobineau argued that civilizations rose and fell according to the racial composition of their populations, that racial mixture caused decline, and that a supposed white or Aryan stock was superior. His book had little evidence to support these claims and drew on speculative history and literary assertion, not on tested science. It attracted limited attention in France at first but was later taken up by German racial writers, notably within the circle around the composer Richard Wagner, and its fame grew posthumously. Gobineau himself was pessimistic and did not propose the political programs later associated with racial nationalists, which illustrates how ideas could be adapted to purposes their originators had not intended.

Scientific methods were mobilized in the effort to rank populations. Anthropometry, the measurement of the human body, and craniometry, the measurement of skulls, were practiced by respected scholars such as the French physician Paul Broca, who founded the Anthropological Society of Paris in 1859, and by the Italian criminologist Cesare Lombroso, who argued that criminals were born with identifiable physical traits. The cephalic index, a ratio of skull length to breadth, was widely used to define types. Such research was often carried out with painstaking measurement and statistical technique, which gave it the appearance of rigor. Yet the conclusions depended on prior assumptions about hierarchy, on unrepresentative samples, on the selective reading of results, and on the unjustified leap from measurement of a physical feature to inference about intelligence or character. Later critics, including the anthropologist Franz Boas in studies of immigrant children’s head forms published around 1910 to 1912, showed that physical measurements varied with environment and could not support fixed racial types.

Language families were a further source of confusion. Comparative philologists in the nineteenth century identified relationships among Indo-European languages, and the label Aryan, originally applied to a branch of those languages, was converted by some writers into a name for a supposed biological race. Similarly, “Semitic” designates a family of related languages including Hebrew and Arabic, but it was turned into a supposed racial category and later helped provide the vocabulary of racial antisemitism, as Section 103.10 discusses. The philologist Max Müller himself, who had popularized the term Aryan, warned in the late 1880s that speaking of an Aryan race made as little sense as speaking of a long-headed dictionary, since language and physical type are different things. This warning shows that scholars at the time criticized the conflation, and that racial doctrines were contested within the academic world.

Modern research gives no support to the nineteenth-century hierarchies. Genetics shows that human beings are a single species with relatively little genetic variation overall, that most of the variation that exists occurs within rather than between conventionally defined populations, and that traits such as skin color vary gradually across geography without sharp boundaries or neat correlation with intelligence or character. Historians therefore treat race as a social and political category that has had powerful consequences, not as a biological reality that earlier scientists were gradually discovering. The history of racial classification is the history of how people used the authority of science to rank other human beings, and of why that authority proved so persuasive to so many for so long.

Section 103.6

Darwin, Spencer, and Social Darwinism: Evolutionary Theory and Political Misuse

Charles Darwin’s On the Origin of Species, published in 1859, was a major scientific achievement. Drawing on decades of observation and comparison, Darwin argued that species change over time through descent with modification, and that natural selection, the differential survival and reproduction of individuals with heritable variations that suit them to their environment, explains much of that change. The theory was debated vigorously, and some religious critics rejected it, but within a few decades evolution became the foundation of the biological sciences. The work did not, however, tell human societies how to organize themselves, and Darwin’s own relation to its social uses was more complicated than later slogans suggest.

In The Descent of Man (1871), Darwin extended evolutionary reasoning to human origins and discussed human variation, sexual selection, and morality. The book displays the assumptions of its time, including ranking of peoples as more or less civilized and conventional views of sex differences, yet it also insists on the unity of humankind and denounces slavery, and it emphasizes the evolutionary importance of sympathy and social instincts. Historians therefore avoid presenting Darwin either as an innocent scientist whose ideas were simply abused or as the author of later racial doctrine. His work contained the biases of a Victorian gentleman and also scientific arguments that undermined some racial claims, notably the view that human races were separate species.

Herbert Spencer, an English philosopher who published widely on evolution, ethics, and society, in fact preceded and influenced the popular understanding of evolution. In his Principles of Biology (1864) he introduced the phrase “survival of the fittest” as a description of what Darwin called natural selection, and Darwin adopted the phrase in later editions of the Origin. Spencer, who believed that societies evolved from simple to complex forms and that progress depended on individual competition with minimal state interference, became very influential in Britain, the United States, and elsewhere. His writings were used to argue against poor relief, factory regulation, and public education, although his own views were more nuanced than the slogans associated with them, and he opposed imperial aggression.

Historical Vocabulary
Evolutionary Theory, Social Darwinism, and Scientific Racism

Evolutionary theory is the scientific explanation, developed by Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace and refined by later biology, of how populations of living things change over generations. Social Darwinism is a label, applied mostly by later critics, for a range of arguments that treated competition among individuals, classes, nations, or supposed races as a natural struggle that justified inequality, laissez-faire policy, militarism, or imperial power. Scientific racism refers to the use of scientific vocabulary, measurement, and institutions to assert that human races are biologically unequal.

The terms matter because they distinguish a biological theory from political doctrines that borrowed its prestige. Few thinkers called themselves Social Darwinists, and the label covers diverse and sometimes contradictory positions, so historians use it cautiously as a description of a family of arguments, not as the name of a unified school.

The transfer of evolutionary language from biology into social argument took many forms. Some writers defended unregulated capitalism by describing economic competition as the market equivalent of natural selection. Others applied the language of struggle to nations and races, arguing that war and imperial expansion were natural expressions of national vitality. In Germany, the zoologist Ernst Haeckel promoted a popular evolutionary philosophy that incorporated racial hierarchy and nationalism. In Britain and elsewhere, imperial enthusiasts claimed that conquest of weaker peoples was a law of nature. These uses were not logical consequences of Darwin’s theory, and they varied. Evolutionary language was also used to justify very different political positions, including socialist ones, and was far from a monopoly of the right.

The central problem with these arguments is a logical and scientific one. Natural selection describes what happens in populations over generations. It does not establish what is morally right, nor what should be done in human politics, because facts about nature cannot by themselves determine values. To say that something occurs in nature does not show that it is good. Moreover, the metaphors were applied loosely. “Fitness” in biology means reproductive success in a given environment, not strength, intelligence, or moral worth, and societies, classes, and nations are not breeding populations competing for survival in the biological sense. Contemporaries made these objections. Thomas Henry Huxley, Darwin’s prominent defender, argued in his 1893 Romanes Lecture Evolution and Ethics that ethical progress meant resisting rather than imitating the competitive process of nature. The Russian naturalist and anarchist Peter Kropotkin, in Mutual Aid (1902), argued that cooperation was also an important factor in evolution, and that social solidarity had biological roots.

The social consequences were nonetheless real. Evolutionary rhetoric lent apparent scientific authority to arguments that poverty reflected inherent inferiority, that colonial subjects belonged to lower stages of development, and that national rivalry was a natural law. It gave militarists and imperialists a vocabulary for presenting aggression as inevitable. At the same time, evolutionary science itself developed in ways that undermined racial hierarchy. The rediscovery of Gregor Mendel’s work on heredity around 1900 led, over the following decades, to modern genetics, which would eventually show how limited the older racial typologies were. Students should therefore keep apart three things: Darwin’s biological theory, the continuing scientific development of evolutionary biology, and the political doctrines that appropriated evolutionary language.

Section 103.7

Heredity, Degeneration, and Eugenics: The Politics of Biological Improvement

Concern about heredity grew in the later nineteenth century alongside urbanization, public health campaigns, and new statistical techniques. Physicians and social commentators in several countries worried about degeneration, the idea that nations might be biologically weakened by alcoholism, disease, poverty, urban life, or mental illness, and that such weakness could be inherited. The theory had roots in French medicine and gained wide attention through works such as Max Nordau’s Degeneration (1892), which applied the concept to modern art and culture. Declining birth rates in France and anxiety about military manpower in several countries added to the sense that population quality and quantity were matters of national importance.

Francis Galton, an English scientist and cousin of Darwin, gave these concerns a program. In Hereditary Genius (1869) he analyzed the family relationships of eminent men and concluded that ability was largely inherited. His method overlooked the extent to which family wealth, education, and social connection explained the success of relatives. Galton was a pioneer of statistical methods, including early work on correlation and regression, and these tools were important and lasting contributions even though he used them to support a flawed conclusion. In his Inquiries into Human Faculty and Its Development (1883) he coined the term eugenics, from Greek roots meaning “well born,” for the study of how to improve the inborn qualities of a population by encouraging the reproduction of those judged desirable.

Eugenic arguments assumed that complex human characteristics, including intelligence, character, poverty, disability, and criminality, were primarily determined by heredity. Advocates frequently treated social circumstances as evidence of inherited biological deficiency, mistaking the effects of malnutrition, lack of schooling, bad housing, and discrimination for innate traits. Proposals ranged from encouraging reproduction among the supposedly fit (positive eugenics) to discouraging or preventing reproduction among those judged unfit (negative eugenics). The categories “fit” and “unfit” reflected class, ethnic, and cultural prejudices of the people who defined them. The moral and political consequences of proposals to sort people into biologically desirable and undesirable groups were serious, because they treated human beings as breeding stock and justified unequal treatment on supposedly scientific grounds.

Organizations and research networks appeared before 1914. Alfred Ploetz published a program of “racial hygiene” in Germany in 1895, and the German Society for Racial Hygiene was founded in 1905. In Britain, Galton’s followers, together with the statistician Karl Pearson, who directed a Eugenics Laboratory at University College London, helped create the Eugenics Education Society in 1907. Interest also appeared in Scandinavia, France, Italy, Russia, and the United States, where state laws permitting compulsory sterilization were adopted from 1907. Eugenics found supporters across the political spectrum, among conservatives worried about national decline, progressives and some socialists who favored scientific social planning, physicians, and feminists who supported maternal health. The movement was therefore not simply a creature of the political right, even though its assumptions were hierarchical.

Contemporary science and later research cut against the eugenicists’ assumptions. Mendelian genetics, rediscovered in 1900, showed that inheritance of many traits is far more complex than simple models assumed, and subsequent work demonstrated that environment and heredity interact in ways that make crude predictions about individuals or groups unreliable. Some geneticists, and critics at the time, rejected eugenic programs on scientific and ethical grounds. Because the term was later associated with coercive sterilization and the Nazi program of racial persecution and murder (Chapters 115 and 121), historians must be careful about chronology. No European state had adopted large-scale compulsory eugenic sterilization before 1914, and attributing the later programs to every earlier eugenic writer would erase differences among arguments, institutions, and policies. What the pre-1914 history does show is how respectable, institutionally supported, and politically flexible the underlying assumptions had become.

Section 103.8

Race, Empire, and the Production of Colonial Knowledge

European empires were not only systems of conquest and administration. They were also systems for producing knowledge about the peoples they ruled, and that knowledge, gathered by missionaries, officials, travelers, soldiers, and academics, shaped how Europeans understood both colonized societies and themselves. This section examines the intellectual and cultural side of that process. The territorial partition of Africa and the history of colonial violence belong to Chapter 104, and colonial administration and resistance in Asia and elsewhere to Chapter 105. Here the focus is on how racial theories organized knowledge and public representation.

Ethnographic museums were prominent institutions. The Berlin Museum of Ethnology opened in 1886 after earlier collections, the Trocadéro ethnographic museum in Paris opened in 1878, and the Pitt Rivers Museum at Oxford received its founding collection in 1884. Such institutions arranged objects collected from colonial territories, often through purchase, exchange, military expedition, or looting, in displays that emphasized supposed stages of cultural development. Anthropological expeditions measured, photographed, and sometimes removed human remains for study. These practices treated colonized people as objects of classification, and the collections remain subjects of debate today over restitution and ethical responsibility.

Public exhibitions brought racial hierarchy to a mass audience. At world’s fairs, such as the Paris Exposition of 1889, colonial exhibitions displayed constructed villages and performers, and the Berlin colonial exhibition of 1896 and the Congo section of the Brussels international exhibition of 1897 at Tervuren presented African peoples as spectacles. Commercial impresarios such as Carl Hagenbeck of Hamburg organized touring “ethnographic shows” in which people from Africa, Asia, and the Arctic were exhibited in zoos and fairgrounds, to large audiences, in settings that blurred the line between science and entertainment. These events reinforced the notion that Europeans were advanced and others primitive, and they gave millions a visual vocabulary of racial difference.

The political consequences were significant. Depicting societies as primitive or incapable of self-government supplied a justification for conquest, paternalistic administration, forced labor, and unequal legal treatment. Many colonial administrators and missionaries described their work as a civilizing mission, a rhetoric that coexisted with economic exploitation and coercion. The same categories shaped metropolitan debate, because ideas about racial inferiority overseas intersected with ideas about inferiority within Europe, including prejudice against the Irish, southern Italians, Slavs, Jews, and Roma. The Bavarian police, for instance, established a central office for the surveillance of Roma in Munich in 1899, an example of how racialized suspicion operated against a European minority with little protection.

Colonized people were not passive subjects of European knowledge. Intellectuals educated in European or missionary schools wrote critically about European claims, visitors and students in European cities observed and described their hosts, performers sometimes negotiated terms and exercised limited control over their circumstances, and activists contested representations in print and in public meetings. W. E. B. Du Bois helped organize an exhibit on African American life for the Paris Exposition of 1900, intended to present an alternative to prevailing racial images. African, Asian, and Caribbean writers challenged assumptions of civilizational hierarchy, as Section 103.19 discusses. They also drew on, adapted, or rejected European concepts, so the history of racial knowledge is one of interaction, not one-way imposition.

Section 103.9

Jewish Emancipation, Citizenship, and the Persistence of Social Exclusion

Jewish emancipation meant the removal of the legal disabilities that had limited where Jews could live, what occupations they could follow, whether they could attend universities, and whether they could hold public office or vote. It was not a single event but a process that unfolded across the nineteenth century, state by state, often in stages, and sometimes reversed. Emancipation also involved bargains and expectations. Many governments and reformers assumed that Jews would modernize their customs and become culturally integrated, while many Jews sought equal rights without surrendering their religious or communal identity. The distinction between formal legal equality and actual acceptance in society is the central theme of this section.

France led the way. The National Assembly granted citizenship to Sephardic Jews in 1790 and to Ashkenazi Jews in 1791, and the Napoleonic regime, while imposing restrictive regulations on Jewish moneylending in 1808, created consistories to organize Jewish religious life under state supervision (Chapter 90). In Britain, disabilities were removed gradually. Jews could practice law and hold municipal office by mid-century, and in 1858 the Jews Relief Act allowed Jews to sit in Parliament, with Lionel de Rothschild taking his seat that year. In 1885 Nathan Mayer Rothschild became the first Jewish member of the House of Lords. In the German states, emancipation advanced unevenly in the first half of the century, was endorsed during the revolutions of 1848, and was extended throughout the North German Confederation in 1869 and the German Empire in 1871. In the Austrian half of the Habsburg monarchy, the constitutional settlement of December 1867 guaranteed civil and political equality, and Hungary adopted equal rights in 1867 as well.

In the Russian Empire the situation was strikingly different. Most Jewish residents were confined to the Pale of Settlement, a region in the western provinces that included present-day Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, and parts of Poland and Latvia, with limited exceptions for certain categories such as wealthy merchants, university graduates, and some artisans. Residence restrictions, limits on rural landholding, special taxes, and later educational quotas constrained economic life, so that many Jews lived in small towns (shtetls) and in cities of the Pale and worked as artisans, small traders, and laborers, with a minority becoming prosperous. Reforms under Alexander II in the 1860s allowed some groups to settle beyond the Pale, but the broader system remained, and Section 103.11 examines its intensification after 1881. Romania also restricted Jewish citizenship in practice, despite commitments at the Congress of Berlin in 1878, which shows that legal declarations and enforcement could diverge widely.

Where emancipation had been achieved, Jewish participation in public life expanded rapidly. Jews entered universities, commercial and financial enterprises, the professions, journalism, politics, and the arts in numbers disproportionate to their share of the population in cities such as Vienna, Berlin, Budapest, Paris, and London. Many were active in liberal and some in socialist politics, and some were converts to Christianity, usually for social or professional reasons. These facts were later exploited by antisemitic propagandists who portrayed them as evidence of Jewish dominance. A more accurate reading notes that Jewish success was concentrated in particular urban sectors that had been open to them or that had been closed to others’ ambition, and that most Jews, particularly in eastern Europe, lived in modest or poor circumstances.

Even in states with full legal equality, prejudice constrained opportunity. Religious hostility inherited from centuries of Christian anti-Judaism persisted in sermons, popular culture, and local custom. Professional barriers were common: in imperial Germany Jews found it difficult to obtain commissions in the Prussian officer corps or professorships without conversion, and many student fraternities and social clubs excluded them. In Britain, social exclusion operated through clubs and schools, though Jews held prominent public positions. France admitted Jewish officers to the army and a number reached senior rank, which is part of why Dreyfus’s career was possible, but anti-Jewish feeling was strong in some conservative, clerical, and nationalist circles. The pattern was uneven. Jews experienced greater acceptance in some places and periods than in others, and formal legal equality did not guarantee security.

Jewish communities themselves were highly diverse. They differed in language, with German, Yiddish, Hebrew for religious and increasingly literary use, Ladino among Sephardic communities of the Ottoman Balkans, Russian, Polish, Hungarian, and French in different regions. They differed in religious practice, among Orthodox, Reform, and Conservative or Neolog traditions and the popular Hasidic movement of eastern Europe, and in economic circumstance, from wealthy bankers to impoverished peddlers. Political outlooks ranged from liberal patriotism to socialism and, later, Zionism. Any account that treats European Jews as a single homogeneous group repeats the stereotypes that antisemites used.

Everyday Life and Social History

Citizenship and Social Belonging in a Late-Nineteenth-Century European Jewish Community

The following is an editorial reconstruction, drawn from patterns documented in community records, memoirs, and scholarship. It does not describe a single named family.

Consider a mid-sized Jewish community in a German or Habsburg city in the 1880s. A merchant family might attend a synagogue that had adopted German-language sermons and an organ, or an Orthodox congregation that resisted such changes, and children might attend a state secondary school where they were taught alongside Christians and received separate religious instruction. Sons could attend university and enter medicine or law, but the same sons might find that a state judgeship or an army commission remained effectively closed to those who had not converted. Daughters were educated at home or in girls’ schools, and in some cities women organized charitable societies that supported poor families, hospitals, and schools. Marriage within the community remained the norm, though intermarriage and conversion rose in some cities.

Daily life involved many negotiations. Families observed the Sabbath and dietary laws to differing degrees, kept shops open or closed on Saturdays according to economic pressure, and adjusted to a calendar of Christian holidays that structured school and work. Newspapers might carry sympathetic reports of Jewish institutions or insults in the same week, and the same neighbor might trade with a Jewish shopkeeper and repeat anti-Jewish remarks in a tavern. In the Pale of Settlement, by contrast, communities were typically larger relative to local population, spoke Yiddish at home, ran their own religious schools, and faced police permission requirements for movement. Jewish mutual-aid societies, burial brotherhoods, and charity networks were crucial across Europe because they supplied support that neither the state nor neighbors always offered. These contrasts show why generalizations about the Jewish experience must always be qualified by region, class, gender, and religious orientation.

Section 103.10

Modern Political Antisemitism: Germany, Austria, France, and the Politics of Exclusion

Hostility to Jews had a long history in Europe. Medieval Christian anti-Judaism was rooted in religious teaching and accusations such as ritual murder and well poisoning, and it resulted in massacres, expulsions, and restrictions examined in earlier chapters. In the later nineteenth century, new forms appeared. Some antisemites still used religious arguments, but they increasingly added claims about economic power, cultural influence, nationalism, and supposed racial difference. The result was modern antisemitism, a political phenomenon in which anti-Jewish hostility became the organizing principle of parties, leagues, newspapers, and electoral campaigns. Historians stress overlap with older prejudice, because modern movements drew on religious stereotypes and many ordinary followers were motivated by inherited hostility, but they also emphasize novelty in organization, vocabulary, and political style.

Historical Vocabulary
Religious Anti-Judaism, Modern Antisemitism, and Racial Antisemitism

Religious anti-Judaism is hostility to Jews grounded in Christian theological claims and in the status of Jews as a non-Christian minority; in principle it could be ended by conversion. Modern antisemitism is the broader political hostility that emerged in the later nineteenth century, drawing on economic, nationalist, cultural, and racial arguments and organized in movements and parties. Racial antisemitism is the strand of modern antisemitism that defines Jews as a biologically distinct and harmful race, so that baptism or assimilation could not change their supposed nature.

The word “antisemitism” became common in German-speaking Europe in the late 1870s, and the agitator Wilhelm Marr is often associated with its popularization, but the precise origin of the term is contested and attributing it to one person is unsafe. The term is itself misleading, since it suggests hostility to all “Semites” while referring only to Jews, and it borrows the discredited language of racial science. Students should recognize it as the self-description of a political movement and as a historical term, not as an accurate description of any real group.

Germany was an early center. After the financial crash of 1873 and the depression that followed, many people looked for explanations, and agitators blamed liberal economics and Jewish bankers and journalists. In 1879 Wilhelm Marr published a pamphlet claiming that Jews were triumphing over Germans, and that year helped found an Anti-Semitic League. The court preacher Adolf Stoecker, a Protestant clergyman, founded the Christian Social Workers’ Party in 1878 to win workers away from socialism, and his speeches increasingly attacked Jews. The historian Heinrich von Treitschke, a prominent professor, lent respectability to the cause in 1879 with an essay criticizing Jewish influence, which provoked a public controversy among academics, the “Berlin Antisemitism Dispute,” in which prominent scholars such as Theodor Mommsen defended Jewish citizens. In 1880 and 1881 organizers circulated a petition calling for restrictions on Jewish rights, reportedly gathering more than 250,000 signatures, though the Reichstag and Bismarck’s government did not adopt its demands. Antisemitic parties won a handful of Reichstag seats in the 1890s, and their electoral strength remained modest, but their ideas influenced conservative organizations, agrarian leagues, student groups, and some professional associations.

In Austria, antisemitism was integrated into mass politics with greater success. Georg von Schönerer, a Pan-German nationalist, combined hostility to Habsburg Catholicism, to Slavs, and to Jews in a program that called for union with Germany and the removal of Jews from public life, and his movement adopted racial vocabulary. He was a vehement agitator but was constrained by legal penalties and by his extremism. Karl Lueger took a different path. Founder and leader of the Christian Social Party, he appealed to small tradesmen, artisans, lower clergy, and civil servants threatened by industrial and commercial change, and he employed antisemitic rhetoric along with municipal social programs. The emperor Franz Joseph refused to confirm Lueger as mayor of Vienna several times after electoral victories, but Lueger was confirmed in April 1897 and governed until 1910. His city administration expanded municipal services such as gas, electricity, and transport, and he used antisemitism opportunistically in politics, though he also had Jewish acquaintances. Historians treat his career as showing how antisemitic mobilization could be combined with populist social reform and constitutional politics, which made it especially durable.

In France, Édouard Drumont’s two-volume La France juive (1886) was an extraordinary publishing success, going through scores of editions. Drumont, a journalist with Catholic and anticapitalist sympathies, presented French history as a conspiracy of Jewish financiers against the nation. In 1892 he founded the newspaper La Libre Parole, which exploited scandals, including the Panama Canal company collapse of 1892 to 1893, in which several Jewish financial intermediaries were implicated, to argue that corruption was a Jewish product. Conspiracy narratives of this type were not descriptions of reality. They were false stories that attributed collective guilt, control, or disloyalty to a diverse minority, and they were designed to channel economic and political anxieties against a visible scapegoat. A later and notorious example, the forged Protocols of the Elders of Zion, appeared in the Russian Empire in 1903 and circulated more widely after 1905.

Why did antisemitism have appeal? Economic insecurity played a role, particularly among artisans, shopkeepers, farmers, and clerks who feared competition and credit. Political distrust of liberal parliaments, resentment of rapid change, nationalist anxieties about loyalty, and surviving religious hostility each contributed. The movement’s ability to present itself as modern, with scientific vocabulary, petitions, mass meetings, and newspapers, was equally important. Yet it did not command a majority anywhere in western or central Europe before 1914, and it provoked organized opposition from liberals, socialists, many Christians, scholars, and Jewish defense organizations such as the Central Association of German Citizens of Jewish Faith, founded in 1893. The historian’s task is to explain its significance without overstating its strength or presenting later events as already determined.

Section 103.11

Pogroms, Restrictions, and Jewish Migration in the Russian Empire

The Russian Empire contained the largest Jewish population in the world, most of it within the Pale of Settlement, and its experience differed sharply from that of Jews in western and central Europe. The Pale was a legal framework, not a ghetto in the narrow sense, and it contained cities with large Jewish populations such as Vilna, Warsaw, Odessa, and Kiev (which received special exceptions). Restrictions on residence, limits on rural landholding and on entry into some occupations, and later educational quotas combined to produce overcrowding and economic constraint. A minority of Jews gained permission to live elsewhere, including some merchants and graduates, but they did so under revocable conditions.

The assassination of Tsar Alexander II on 13 March 1881 (1 March in the Julian calendar then used in Russia) was followed within weeks by anti-Jewish violence, beginning in Elizavetgrad in April 1881 and spreading through towns and villages in southern Russia and Ukraine, with further outbreaks in 1882. The word pogrom, from a Russian term for devastation, came to describe such attacks, which involved the looting and destruction of Jewish property, beatings, and killings, and some sexual violence. The violence of 1881 to 1882 killed relatively few people compared to later outbreaks, but destroyed enormous property and shook Jewish confidence in the empire. Historians distinguish carefully among responsibilities. Local rioters, often including workers, railway employees, and townspeople, carried out the attacks, fueled by rumors that the Tsar’s murder had been a Jewish plot or that the authorities permitted violence. Local police and officials often failed to act promptly, and in some places were slow or sympathetic to the attackers. Evidence does not support the claim that the central government planned the pogroms of 1881 to 1882, although officials blamed Jewish economic behavior and then adopted restrictive policies.

The government’s response was to restrict Jews rather than protect them. The “Temporary Rules” of May 1882, known as the May Laws, forbade new Jewish settlement outside towns and cities within the Pale and restricted property transactions in rural areas. Scholars note that the rules’ effects were complex and that enforcement varied, but they intensified existing limits and were followed by further measures. In 1887 educational quotas limited Jewish enrollment in secondary schools and universities to small percentages, and in 1891 to 1892 thousands of Jews were expelled from Moscow. Such policies treated Jews as a collective problem, and they encouraged both emigration and political radicalization.

Violence recurred in the early twentieth century. The Kishinev pogrom of April 1903, in Bessarabia, killed some fifty Jews, injured many more, and destroyed hundreds of homes and businesses, and it became an international scandal through press coverage, protest meetings in Europe and the United States, and poetry by writers such as Hayim Nahman Bialik. Another and larger wave struck in 1905 during the revolutionary crisis in the empire, especially after the October Manifesto of that year, when conservative and monarchist groups, sometimes labelled the Black Hundreds, attacked Jews and revolutionaries in many cities, with major violence in Odessa. Historians continue to debate how far the authorities encouraged these attacks, and the evidence points to a mixture of local initiative, organized right-wing groups, official tolerance, and in some cases active complicity of individual officials.

Jewish communities responded in varied ways. They organized mutual aid and relief funds, appealed to Jewish organizations abroad, and held public protests. After Kishinev, self-defense groups formed in several towns, and in 1903 in Gomel Jewish defenders resisted attackers, while in 1905 the socialist Bund and other groups organized armed defense units. Jewish activists joined revolutionary movements in large numbers, a participation that antisemites used as a pretext to claim that Jews were responsible for revolution, although Jews were a minority within those movements and many others were conservative or religious. Political responses also included the Zionist and autonomist movements discussed in Section 103.12.

Migration was among the most important consequences. Between 1881 and 1914, more than two and a half million Jews emigrated from Eastern Europe, including the Russian Empire, Austrian Galicia, and Romania. More than two million traveled to the United States alone, while others settled in Britain, Germany, France, Argentina, Canada, South Africa, and Ottoman Palestine. Estimates vary with the geographical boundaries and passenger records used. Emigrants were not driven by identical motives. Violence and legal restriction were major factors, but so were poverty, overpopulation in the Pale, the lure of economic opportunity, family networks, and the cheapening of steamship travel. Many returned or moved on. The arrival of poor eastern European Jews also provoked antisemitic reactions in receiving countries, including Britain’s Aliens Act of 1905, which restricted immigration. Migration thus transformed Jewish demography, created new diasporas, and carried the European dispute over belonging across the Atlantic.

Section 103.12

Jewish Political and Cultural Responses: Integration, Socialism, Zionism, and National Autonomy

Jewish responses to antisemitism and to the changing European state system were diverse and often opposed to one another. The most widespread response in western and central Europe was a continued commitment to civic equality and integration. Many Jews regarded themselves as loyal citizens of France, Germany, Britain, Hungary, or Austria who differed from neighbors only in religion, and they defended their rights through the press, courts, and political parties. Organizations such as the Alliance Israélite Universelle, founded in Paris in 1860, the Anglo-Jewish Association (1871), and the Central Association of German Citizens of Jewish Faith (1893) campaigned for rights and legal protection, aided persecuted communities abroad, and provided education. Religious reform movements adapted worship and practice to modern life, while Orthodox leaders defended tradition, and Jewish scholarship, including the German “Science of Judaism,” developed new historical study of Jewish texts and history.

In the Russian Empire and eastern Europe, socialism offered another path. Jewish workers’ circles had formed in Vilna and elsewhere in the 1880s and 1890s, and in October 1897 the General Jewish Labour Bund of Lithuania, Poland, and Russia was founded in Vilna. The Bund organized strikes, trade unions, and self-defense, and published in Yiddish, which it promoted as a language of the Jewish working masses. Its program combined socialism with the demand for civil and political equality and, increasingly, for national cultural autonomy for Jews within a democratic multinational state. It helped found the Russian Social Democratic Labor Party in 1898 but later quarreled with it over autonomy. The Bund was hostile to Zionism, which it regarded as a diversion from the struggle for rights where Jews lived, and it argued for “doikayt,” the principle of “hereness.”

Zionism developed from several roots. After the pogroms of 1881 to 1882, Hibbat Zion (“Love of Zion”) groups arose in Russia, encouraged by Leon Pinsker’s pamphlet Auto-Emancipation (1882), which argued that Jews needed to achieve national self-determination because assimilation could not end hostility. Small numbers of settlers began to move to Ottoman Palestine, beginning what is called the First Aliyah. Theodor Herzl, a Vienna-based journalist and playwright, gave political Zionism its organizational form with his pamphlet Der Judenstaat (The Jews’ State, 1896), which argued that antisemitism was a permanent feature of European societies and that Jews required a state. The First Zionist Congress met in Basel in August 1897 and founded the World Zionist Organization. Herzl’s early thinking took shape before the Dreyfus Affair, though he later cited events in France, and historians caution against making the Affair the sole cause of political Zionism.

Zionism itself included multiple positions. Political Zionists emphasized diplomacy to obtain a charter or international agreement for a Jewish homeland. Cultural Zionists, among whom the essayist Ahad Ha’am was the most influential, wanted a Jewish spiritual and cultural center, not primarily a state. Practical Zionists stressed settlement. Religious Zionists, who organized the Mizrachi movement in 1902, combined Jewish tradition with nationalism, while many Orthodox leaders opposed the movement as secular, and many liberals and socialists rejected it. Most European Jews before 1914 were not Zionists, and the movement’s influence grew gradually.

Other thinkers proposed autonomist or diaspora nationalist programs. The historian Simon Dubnow argued that Jews were a nation with the right to communal self-government and cultural autonomy wherever they lived, and his ideas influenced political movements in eastern Europe. The flourishing of Yiddish and Hebrew literature, the press, theater, and scholarship in these years was also a response, a creative and communal one, to exclusion and to the pressure of assimilation. These movements show that Jewish political life engaged the same debates about nationalism, citizenship, cultural autonomy, and self-determination that affected other European communities.

Section 103.13

France after 1871: Republican Nationalism, the Army, and the Crisis of National Loyalty

The Third Republic was born in defeat. After the Second Empire collapsed at Sedan in September 1870, a republic was proclaimed in Paris, and the war with Prussia ended with the Treaty of Frankfurt in 1871, under which France lost Alsace and a large part of Lorraine and paid a heavy indemnity. The Paris Commune of 1871 and its bloody suppression deepened social division. The republic’s constitutional laws of 1875 were adopted by a narrow margin in an Assembly with a monarchist majority, and for years the regime’s survival was uncertain. Republicans identified the regime with the principles of 1789, secular education, and parliamentary government, while monarchists, many Catholics, and later radical nationalists regarded it as corrupt and weak.

The loss of Alsace-Lorraine became a powerful national symbol, and French politics after 1871 were heavily concerned with recovery and security. The army was reconstructed, universal military service was introduced in stages, and the military became a revered national institution, associated with sacrifice, discipline, and revanche, the aim of redressing the defeat. The army’s relationship with civil authority was nonetheless delicate. Officers were conscious of their institution’s prestige and sensitive to civilian interference, and the General Staff and its intelligence section operated with considerable autonomy and secrecy. Many republicans were proud of the army while also fearing that it harbored monarchist and Catholic sympathies.

French political life was fragmented among competing traditions. Republicans were divided between moderates and Radicals. Monarchists were split between legitimists and Orleanists, and Bonapartist sentiment persisted. The Catholic Church was largely hostile to the secular republic, especially after anticlerical legislation, though in the 1890s the pope encouraged a policy of ralliement (rallying) to the regime that many Catholics only partly accepted. Socialists, divided into several organizations, grew in strength. Nationalism in this period was not owned by any one camp. It began as a patriotic republican sentiment in the tradition of the Revolution and later, in the 1880s and 1890s, was increasingly reshaped by the political right into a movement that defined the nation by tradition, army, Church, and ancestry, and that denounced republican parliamentarism.

General Georges Boulanger exemplified the new politics. As minister of war from 1886, he was popular for army reforms and patriotic bravura, and he attracted a coalition of discontented monarchists, Bonapartists, nationalists, and radicals in a movement that demanded constitutional revision. After a series of by-election victories, he won a decisive election in Paris in January 1889, but hesitated to seize power, fled when the government threatened prosecution, and committed suicide in Brussels in 1891. Boulangism showed that a charismatic figure could mobilize mass support against the parliamentary republic and that nationalism could be a vehicle of anti-parliamentary politics. It also showed that the republic could defend itself.

The press and public spectacle intensified political conflict. Mass-circulation dailies, illustrated papers, and caricature turned scandals into national dramas. The Panama scandal of 1892 and 1893, involving bribery of legislators by a failed canal company, was used by La Libre Parole and other papers to claim that the republic was corrupt and Jewish-controlled. The fear of foreign espionage, particularly by Germany, reinforced a climate of suspicion, and accusations of treason were politically potent. A military intelligence service, the Statistical Section, existed to counter German spying, and its officers placed an informant in the German embassy in Paris, who collected material from waste baskets. The conjunction of defeat, nationalist agitation, antisemitism in the press, and an army protective of its prestige set the conditions in which an accusation against a Jewish officer could be quickly believed.

Section 103.14

Alfred Dreyfus: The 1894 Arrest, Military Trial, and Miscarriage of Justice

Alfred Dreyfus was born in 1859 in Mulhouse, in Alsace, to a prosperous Jewish textile family. After the annexation of 1871, his family chose to remain French, and Dreyfus later attended the École Polytechnique and the artillery school and became a captain. He was a committed patriot, and his decision to pursue a military career reflected the confidence of many French Jews in the republic that had granted them equality. In 1893 he was assigned as a probationer to the General Staff in Paris, one of the few Jewish officers to reach it, which later exposed him to resentment among some colleagues. He was reserved and wealthy, and some officers regarded him with suspicion.

In September 1894, French intelligence obtained from the German embassy a handwritten, unsigned, undated note, later known as the bordereau, listing documents on artillery and other military matters that its writer proposed to transmit to the German military attaché, Maximilian von Schwartzkoppen. Officers of the General Staff set out to identify the author among those with access to such information. Suspicion fell on Dreyfus, and the supposed similarity of his handwriting to that of the note was the principal evidence, along with the conviction of some officers that a Jew and an Alsatian could not be trusted. An initial handwriting expert for the Bank of France did not conclusively identify the writing, while a police expert, Alphonse Bertillon, who had no training in handwriting analysis, advanced a complicated theory that Dreyfus had disguised his own hand.

Dreyfus was arrested on 15 October 1894. The investigation was conducted in secrecy, and the Minister of War, General Auguste Mercier, allowed details to leak to the nationalist press, in particular La Libre Parole, which proclaimed the arrest of a Jewish traitor and made the case a public matter before any trial. The court-martial sat in December 1894 and convicted Dreyfus on 22 December, sentencing him to degradation and deportation for life. The decisive illegality was that, outside the courtroom and without informing the defense, the judges were shown a secret dossier of material that supposedly proved Dreyfus’s guilt. Withholding evidence from the accused violated basic principles of due process, and the contents of the file proved weak or irrelevant. Historians agree on this point: the conviction rested on the faulty bordereau analysis, on a prejudicial climate, and on evidence the defendant could not contest.

On 5 January 1895, Dreyfus was publicly degraded in the courtyard of the École Militaire in Paris, with his insignia torn from his uniform and his sword broken in front of assembled troops and a crowd that shouted abuse. He protested his innocence, and observers reported his steady bearing. He was then transported to Devil’s Island, a small prison island off French Guiana, where he was held in conditions of isolation and severe hardship for four years. The ceremony of degradation was intended to symbolize the expulsion of a traitor from the national community, and for many witnesses it confirmed the identification of Jewishness with treason, though some observers and later Dreyfusards recalled it as a spectacle of injustice.

The conviction was difficult to review because of institutional secrecy and appeals to national honor. The army’s leaders regarded acknowledgment of error as a danger to the institution’s authority, and military intelligence officers, in particular Major Hubert-Joseph Henry, were ready to protect the verdict. Officials stressed the security implications of publicity. In this climate, the accusation of espionage and the actual evidence were separate matters, and historians do not treat guilt and innocence as equally supported possibilities. The evidence available to scholars, including the investigations of Picquart and later judicial reviews, shows that Dreyfus was innocent and that Esterhazy wrote the bordereau.

Section 103.15

Émile Zola, J’Accuse…!, and the Mobilization of Intellectuals and the Press

In March 1896, Lieutenant Colonel Georges Picquart, who had become head of the Statistical Section in 1895, discovered a pneumatic telegram card (the petit bleu) that suggested that Major Esterhazy, an officer heavily in debt and apparently in contact with the German attaché, was passing information to Germany. Picquart compared Esterhazy’s handwriting with the bordereau and found them identical. When he raised his findings with superiors, he was sent on assignment away from Paris, eventually to Tunisia, and was later imprisoned. The family of Dreyfus, led by his brother Mathieu, meanwhile worked tirelessly to secure review. They were helped by the Jewish journalist Bernard Lazare, who published a pamphlet in 1896 arguing the case for revision, and by Auguste Scheurer-Kestner, a vice-president of the Senate who became convinced of Dreyfus’s innocence in 1897.

In November 1897, Mathieu Dreyfus publicly denounced Esterhazy, and the army was compelled to hold an inquiry and then a court-martial. The military court acquitted Esterhazy on 11 January 1898 after a very brief deliberation. For the army, this outcome seemed to close the matter. For supporters of Dreyfus, it was an outrage that exposed the closing of ranks against evidence. Two days later, on 13 January 1898, Émile Zola’s open letter to President Félix Faure appeared in L’Aurore, under the headline J’Accuse…!, chosen by the paper’s editor Georges Clemenceau.

Petitions calling for revision were circulated by writers, scholars, and professionals, and one in January 1898, later called the “Manifesto of the Intellectuals,” included signatures from prominent academics and artists. Section 103.16

The Dreyfusards, Anti-Dreyfusards, and the Defense of Republican Citizenship, 1898–1906

The Affair divided French society, but along lines that did not follow simple party labels. Dreyfusards included many liberal republicans and Radicals, writers and academics, Protestants, Jews, and some socialists. The socialist leader Jean Jaurès initially hesitated but became a vigorous advocate of revision, arguing that the case involved the rights of the individual and the working class as well as a bourgeois officer, while other socialists were reluctant to become involved in what they saw as a quarrel among the ruling classes. Anti-Dreyfusards included many army officers, conservative Catholics, monarchists, and nationalists, and they also included some republicans who believed that defending the army’s honor outweighed the fate of one man. The Assumptionist newspaper La Croix and other Catholic publications conducted harshly antisemitic campaigns, although not every Catholic took that line. Historians therefore warn against assuming that every Catholic, conservative, socialist, or republican held the same position.

Organizations crystallized the divide. The League for the Rights of Man (Ligue des droits de l’homme) was founded in 1898, with the former minister Ludovic Trarieux as its first president, to defend individual rights against arbitrary state action, and it grew into a lasting republican institution. On the other side, nationalist and anti-Dreyfusard groups such as the League of the French Fatherland (Ligue de la patrie française) formed in the same year, and the movement Action française took shape around Charles Maurras at the turn of the century, developing an integral nationalism that rejected parliamentary democracy and portrayed Jews, Protestants, and Freemasons as alien elements.

The prosecution’s case came apart in the summer of 1898. When a new Minister of War reviewed the files, officials discovered that a document used in the Affair, supposedly proving Dreyfus’s guilt, was a forgery fabricated by Colonel Henry. Henry confessed on 30 August 1898 and was found dead in his cell the next day, apparently by suicide. Esterhazy fled to England, where he later admitted authorship of the bordereau. The Court of Cassation, France’s highest appeals court, ordered a new court-martial in 1899, and Dreyfus was brought back from Devil’s Island, physically broken by his imprisonment.

The retrial at Rennes in August and September 1899 was held in an atmosphere of intense tension, and Dreyfus’s lawyer Fernand Labori was shot and wounded before it began. The court-martial nonetheless again found Dreyfus guilty, by a vote of five to two, of treason “with extenuating circumstances,” a phrase that suggested an incoherent compromise, and sentenced him to ten years. The verdict provoked widespread international outrage. The government of René Waldeck-Rousseau, formed in 1899 with the declared aim of defending the republic against its enemies, arranged a presidential pardon on 19 September 1899, which Dreyfus accepted to secure his freedom and health, while saying that he would continue to seek vindication. A pardon is an act of clemency that remits punishment. It is not a finding that the conviction was wrong, and Dreyfus remained legally guilty.

Full judicial correction came on 12 July 1906, when the Court of Cassation annulled the Rennes verdict without sending the case to another court, finding that nothing remained that could properly be charged against Dreyfus. Parliament passed laws reinstating him in the army with the rank of major, and he received the Legion of Honour in a ceremony in July 1906. Picquart was restored and became Minister of War later that year. These decisions followed years of campaigning, and they show that institutions could eventually correct themselves, though not before years of suffering and only because of persistent political and civic pressure.

The consequences extended well beyond the individual. Republicans emerged determined to subordinate the army to civilian control and to reduce clerical influence, leading to anticlerical legislation that culminated in the law separating Church and State in 1905. The League for the Rights of Man established a model of citizen organization for civil liberties, and the concept of intellectuals engaged in public debate entered political culture. Historians continue to discuss how much antisemitism, as opposed to institutional self-protection, explains the army’s conduct, and the evidence supports the view that both mattered and reinforced one another. Legal vindication also did not eliminate antisemitism or resolve conflicts over French national identity. Nationalist and Catholic antisemitism continued, and the debate about who belonged to the nation remained a central French question into the twentieth century. The Affair shows a conflict between national loyalty understood as deference to institutions and republican citizenship understood as universal rights, and its resolution showed both the strength and the limits of republican institutions.

Section 103.17

The Emergence of Sociology: Durkheim, Weber, and the Scientific Study of Society

Sociology as a university discipline took shape in the decades after 1870, though its roots lay in earlier thought. Auguste Comte, who coined the word and proposed a science of society based on observation, had influenced later thinkers, while Marx, Spencer, and others had offered large theories of social development. What changed after 1870 was the emergence of research institutions, journals, university posts, and more rigorous empirical methods, along with a widespread sense that industrial urban society raised problems that traditional explanations could not address. Poverty, migration, crime, suicide, religious change, and the weakening of old communal ties became subjects of systematic study.

Émile Durkheim, a French scholar of Jewish background who held the first French university post in sociology at Bordeaux, was a central figure. In The Division of Labour in Society (1893), he argued that modern societies were held together by an interdependence arising from specialization, which he called organic solidarity, in contrast with the solidarity of likeness in simpler societies. In The Rules of Sociological Method (1895) he urged that “social facts” be studied as realities external to individuals and explained by other social facts, not by psychology or biology. In Suicide (1897) he analyzed official statistics to show that suicide rates varied systematically with social conditions such as religion, family structure, and social integration, which demonstrated that apparently private acts had social patterns. Durkheim was a Dreyfusard, and his 1898 essay “Individualism and the Intellectuals” defended individual rights as the moral foundation of modern society. His approach explicitly rejected explanations of behavior by race or heredity.

Max Weber, a German scholar trained in law, history, and economics, developed a different approach. He emphasized the meanings that people attach to their actions and proposed an interpretive sociology concerned with understanding social action, together with historical comparison of religion, authority, and economic organization. In The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, published in two parts in 1904 and 1905, he argued that certain currents of ascetic Protestantism had encouraged a disciplined, rational approach to work that helped shape modern capitalism. The argument was one about cultural and religious factors and has been debated ever since, with critics questioning its evidence and its chronology. Weber’s early career also shows the complexity of the period, since in his 1895 inaugural lecture at Freiburg he expressed German nationalist views and hostility to Polish migrant labor in eastern Germany. The same scholar who later advanced a comparative sociology of religions thus began from assumptions that reflected the nationalism of his milieu.

Systematic social investigation took other forms in Britain. Charles Booth’s multi-volume Life and Labour of the People in London, begun in 1886 and published from 1889, mapped poverty street by street, using school board visitors’ information and surveys of trades and religion, and concluded that around thirty percent of Londoners lived in poverty. Seebohm Rowntree’s Poverty: A Study of Town Life (1901) used household investigation in York to define a poverty line and distinguished primary poverty, due to inadequate income, from secondary poverty. These studies found that poverty arose largely from low wages, irregular work, large families, illness, and old age, so they undermined arguments that poverty reflected inherited inferiority. They also influenced debates about welfare reform.

The early social sciences were not a single school. Durkheim stressed social structure, Weber action and meaning, the German sociologist Ferdinand Tönnies the contrast between community and society (1887), and Georg Simmel the forms of interaction in the metropolis. Some practitioners shared assumptions about progress and civilizational hierarchy, and some, including a few early sociologists, engaged with race and heredity, so the field was not free of the prejudices of the period. What distinguishes the leading figures discussed here is that they sought to explain social variation through institutions, culture, economic conditions, and social relations, a style of explanation that offered a systematic alternative to biological determinism.

Section 103.18

Psychology, Psychoanalysis, Anthropology, and the Reinterpretation of Human Nature

The study of the mind also became institutionalized. Wilhelm Wundt established what is usually regarded as the first experimental psychology laboratory at Leipzig in 1879, using controlled experiments on sensation and reaction time, and similar laboratories followed in Germany, France, Britain, and the United States. In France, Jean-Martin Charcot’s work at the Salpêtrière hospital on hysteria and hypnosis shaped medical psychology, and Alfred Binet, with Théodore Simon, developed an intelligence scale in 1905 to identify children needing special schooling. These developments show psychology emerging as a laboratory science, a clinical practice, and a tool of educational administration, and they also show how psychological testing could be used to classify children and later, in other hands, populations.

Sigmund Freud, a Viennese neurologist of Jewish background, developed psychoanalysis out of his clinical work with patients suffering from neuroses. His Interpretation of Dreams was published in November 1899 though its title page carries the date 1900. Freud proposed that dreams are disguised expressions of unconscious wishes, that mental life involves conflict between conscious and unconscious forces, that childhood experiences shape adult psychology, and that talking could help relieve symptoms. He worked in the Vienna of Karl Lueger, and his experience as a Jew in that environment is part of the context, though historians debate how far it shaped his ideas. Students should distinguish Freud’s historical importance from the status of his claims. His theories transformed twentieth-century culture and clinical practice, but many of his specific claims and methods have been criticized as untestable or unsupported, and modern psychological science accepts only some of his ideas in modified form.

Relations among psychology, psychiatry, medicine, anthropology, and social inquiry were complex. Some physicians used theories of degeneration to classify patients, as Section 103.7 noted, while others explored the environment and personal history. Anthropology contained sharp internal contradictions. Many practitioners in the late nineteenth century measured skulls and ranked races, and an evolutionary framework in which societies passed through stages from savagery to civilization, associated with Edward Burnett Tylor in Britain, was widely held, even though Tylor emphasized the psychic unity of humankind. A younger generation changed direction. Franz Boas, a German-born scholar who moved to the United States, argued from the 1890s that cultures should be studied in their historical context and on their own terms, and his studies of physical change in immigrants’ children, published from 1910 to 1912, undermined fixed racial types. In 1911 he published The Mind of Primitive Man, which criticized racial explanations of cultural difference. Such work contributed to a growing emphasis on culture and language in place of biological determinism, though that shift was gradual and incomplete before 1914.

The debate between heredity and environment was open. Scientists, physicians, educators, and reformers disagreed about how far inherited characteristics, upbringing, education, and cultural context shaped individuals. These debates had political stakes, because heredity could be used to defend inequality and environment to support reform. The history of the human sciences in this period therefore does not support a simple story in which science gradually replaced prejudice. New disciplines advanced knowledge and also reproduced the assumptions of their practitioners, and the same universities and journals that supported Boas and Durkheim also supported craniometry and racial hygiene. Intellectual modernization did not automatically eliminate prejudice or unsupported theory, and it supplied tools for criticizing them.

Section 103.19

Universal Rights, Antiracist Criticism, and Challenges to European Hierarchies

Opposition to racial exclusion drew on several traditions. Republican universalism, rooted in the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789, held that rights belonged to individuals as such. Liberal civil-rights arguments defended equality before the law, and socialist internationalism insisted on the solidarity of workers across national and religious lines. Christian and humanitarian movements, including antislavery societies and missionary critics of abuses, argued for the dignity of all human beings. These traditions overlapped but also diverged, and each had limits. Universalist principles coexisted with exclusions based on gender, since women could not vote in most European states, as well as on class, race, religion, nationality, and the legal status of colonial subjects.

The Dreyfus Affair helped crystallize institutional forms of rights advocacy. The League for the Rights of Man, as noted, was created in 1898 to defend individuals against arbitrary power and spread through French towns, eventually including thousands of members. Jewish organizations such as the Alliance Israélite Universelle and the Anglo-Jewish Association had campaigned for the rights of persecuted communities, and the Central Association of German Citizens of Jewish Faith offered legal defense against libel and discrimination. Such groups demonstrated that rights could be defended through organization, publicity, and litigation.

Black intellectuals and activists organized internationally. The first Pan-African Conference met in London in July 1900, organized chiefly by Henry Sylvester Williams, a Trinidadian barrister living in Britain, and brought together delegates from Britain, the Caribbean, Africa, and the United States, among them W. E. B. Du Bois. The conference issued an address “To the Nations of the World,” which Du Bois helped draft, criticizing racial discrimination, the treatment of colonized peoples, and the seizure of African land, and appealing to European powers and to the United States to respect the rights of Black people. Du Bois warned that the problem of the twentieth century would be the problem of the color line, a formulation he developed in The Souls of Black Folk (1903). The conference was small and its immediate political effects limited, but it created networks and a vocabulary for later anticolonial and antiracist movements.

Critics of empire also appeared within Europe. The English economist J. A. Hobson’s Imperialism: A Study (1902) argued that imperial expansion served the interests of financial and commercial groups rather than the nation as a whole and distorted domestic politics. The Congo Reform Association, founded in 1904 with E. D. Morel as its leading journalist, campaigned against the atrocities committed in King Leopold II’s Congo Free State. Socialist congresses debated colonialism, and some socialists denounced colonial violence while others accepted the idea of a civilizing mission. Students should distinguish economic or humanitarian criticism of empire from a principled commitment to racial equality. Hobson, for example, criticized imperial rule in economic terms while still accepting some paternalistic assumptions, and many campaigners for reform of colonial abuses did not question the legitimacy of colonial rule itself.

Minority activists contested discrimination in other ways. Irish, Polish, Czech, Jewish, and other national and religious minority organizations defended language and cultural rights, anticolonial intellectuals in Asia and Africa wrote critiques of colonial rule, and women’s movements, discussed in Chapter 102, demanded political rights. The international exchange of ideas between European and non-European critics, through travel, correspondence, and the press, was important. The overall pattern is one of vigorous and varied opposition that had limited power to change policy before 1914 but laid foundations for later movements.

Section 103.20

National Belonging, Racial Ideology, and the Contradictions of European Modernity before 1914

Between 1870 and 1914, expanding education, scientific research, democratic participation, and mass communication created unprecedented opportunities for intellectual exchange and political mobilization. Literacy increased, universities grew, newspapers reached millions, and associations organized people who had previously had little public voice. These same developments, however, made it possible to spread discriminatory ideologies. Newspapers carried antisemitic propaganda, political associations organized exclusionary campaigns, universities housed racial anthropologists and eugenicists, and public institutions, from schools to museums, conveyed racial hierarchies as established knowledge. The infrastructure of modernity was neutral in itself, and its effects depended on who used it and for what purpose.

A fundamental tension ran through the period. Many European states defined citizenship by equal legal status, yet important political and intellectual currents treated ancestry, religion, language, or supposed racial characteristics as grounds for exclusion. The tension was visible in the position of Jews in Germany, Austria, and France, of Poles in Prussia, of Czechs and Germans in Bohemia, of Roma subject to police registration, and of colonial subjects who lived under European rule without equal rights. It was also visible in the thought of individual intellectuals, who might defend human rights in one context and endorse hierarchy in another. Minority-rights movements, nationalist programs, and conflicts over sovereignty interacted in multinational Europe, and demands for national self-determination by one group could entail the subordination of another, a problem that would become acute in the Balkans and the successor states of Chapters 106, 107, and 113.

The Dreyfus Affair brought these tensions into focus. It showed how antisemitism could undermine justice, how institutional secrecy and appeals to national honor could sustain injustice, and how a free press and civic organizations could expose it. Its resolution, slow and incomplete, showed that rights-based institutions could eventually challenge wrongful state action. Sociology, psychology, anthropology, and empirical social inquiry meanwhile widened the range of explanations of human behavior beyond inherited biological traits, giving critics of racism intellectual resources even though practitioners did not agree among themselves.

European modernity before 1914 can therefore not be described as moving in a single direction. It produced powerful arguments for universal human equality and increasingly organized ideologies of exclusion at the same time, in the same cities, and sometimes in the same institutions. This chapter has also traced the creation of an intellectual climate that would later be exploited, and Chapters 104 and 105 show how racial ideas shaped imperial conquest and colonial governance, Chapter 106 how nationality conflicts affected the major European states, and Chapter 107 how nationalist tension combined with alliance politics to produce the crisis of 1914. Yet the First World War and later racial persecution, including the Holocaust examined in Chapter 121, were not the inevitable consequences of pre-1914 thought. They resulted from specific political decisions, wars, economic crises, institutional collapse, and the choices of identifiable actors, and many people and institutions in the earlier period opposed the doctrines that later regimes embraced. The historian’s task is to identify continuities without erasing contingency, opposition, or change.

Historical Debate

Was European Nationalism Primarily Integrative or Exclusionary?

The question: Between 1870 and 1914, did nationalism chiefly function to bind diverse populations into inclusive political communities, or chiefly to define and exclude outsiders?

Interpretation A: Nationalism was primarily integrative. Some historians emphasize that national schools, conscription, and public culture drew peasants and workers into common political life, expanded literacy and participation, and gave citizens a sense of shared obligation. Nationalism also underpinned demands for representation and autonomy, as in Czech, Polish, Irish, and other movements, and was compatible with liberal and democratic politics. On this view, exclusionary radical nationalism was a minority current, opposed by many liberals, socialists, and Christians, and was not the central dynamic of the period.
Interpretation B: Nationalism was increasingly exclusionary. Others argue that national integration necessarily involved drawing boundaries, and that policies of Germanization, Russification, and Magyarization, antisemitic movements, racial theories of nationhood, and imperial rivalry show a shift toward defining nations against internal and external enemies. In this reading, the extension of mass politics gave exclusionary nationalists new leverage, and even moderate nationalism fostered suspicion of minorities whose loyalty was questioned.

Overlapping interpretations: Much recent scholarship treats the two as simultaneous, not alternative: the same institutions that included many people also marked others as outsiders, and the balance varied by state, region, and political moment. Historians also disagree over how typical the most radical movements were.

Evidence: Both positions draw on school curricula, language laws, census practices, electoral results, party programs, press content, associational records, and the testimony of minorities. The measurement problem is real, since the strength of a movement’s ideas is difficult to infer from its electoral support alone.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians relate pre-1914 nationalism to the First World War and later events. A purely exclusionary reading risks teleology, while a purely integrative reading risks overlooking the real victims of discrimination.

Cause and Effect

Nationalism, Racial Classification, Mass Politics, and Contested Citizenship

Long-term causes

Revolutionary and Romantic ideas of the nation, Christian anti-Judaism, slavery and colonial hierarchies, the prestige of natural science, and uneven emancipation provided materials for later movements.

Short-term causes

The creation of new states in 1861 to 1871, the depression after 1873, rapid urbanization, widening suffrage, mass newspapers, and imperial rivalry created audiences and incentives for exclusionary politics.

Immediate effects

Minority-language disputes, antisemitic parties and petitions, racial and eugenic organizations, the Dreyfus Affair, pogroms in the Russian Empire, and organized Jewish, minority, and antiracist responses.

Longer-term consequences

A durable set of ideas and organizations about belonging and hierarchy, available for adoption or rejection after 1914, together with institutions of rights advocacy and critical social science.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains these developments. Antisemitism flourished in some states and not others with similar economic conditions, racial theory persuaded some scientists and was rejected by others, and the outcome in each place depended on institutions, political choices, and opposition. Later catastrophes cannot be derived from this chain of causes alone.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how nationalism, racial thought, antisemitism, and the new social sciences shaped European ideas of identity and citizenship between 1870 and 1914. After the unifications of Italy and Germany, nationalism changed from a movement that sought to create states into a force that states and movements used to build loyalty, compete for power, claim cultural primacy, or demand autonomy. It was never uniformly liberal, conservative, or authoritarian, and it could support both rights and exclusion.

Competing definitions of the nation carried practical consequences. Civic conceptions based on shared political institutions and consent, such as Renan’s, contrasted with ethnic conceptions based on language, culture, or supposed ancestry, though actual movements mixed them. In multinational states, minorities such as Poles, Czechs, Alsatians, Ukrainians, Romanians, and others faced different policies of assimilation, and they responded with schools, associations, newspapers, and electoral politics. National identity was cultivated through schools, conscription, mass newspapers, and public ceremonies, yet it competed with religious, regional, linguistic, and class loyalties.

Racial classification, which developed from earlier natural history and colonial experience, used measurement and the prestige of science to rank human populations. Its conclusions rested on assumptions and flawed methods, and modern genetics offers no support for racial hierarchies. Darwin’s biological theory should be distinguished from Social Darwinism, the family of political arguments that borrowed evolutionary language to defend competition, militarism, empire, and inequality. Eugenics, begun by Galton, turned disputed claims about heredity into programs of biological improvement and attracted supporters across the political spectrum, though large-scale coercive programs belong to a later period. Racial knowledge also supported imperial rule through museums, expeditions, and exhibitions, and colonized people contested these representations.

Jewish emancipation proceeded unevenly. Where legal equality existed, social prejudice and professional discrimination persisted, and in the Russian Empire the Pale of Settlement and later restrictions imposed severe disabilities. Modern political antisemitism, represented by Marr, Stoecker, Schönerer, Lueger, and Drumont, combined older religious hostility with economic, nationalist, and racial arguments and false conspiracy narratives. Although it never commanded a majority in western or central Europe before 1914, it shaped public debate and organized politics.

In the Russian Empire, the pogroms of 1881 to 1882, the May Laws, educational quotas, the Kishinev pogrom of 1903, and the violence of 1905 combined local violence, official failure, and discriminatory policy. Jewish communities responded through mutual aid, self-defense, political activism, and substantial emigration. More than two million Jews from Eastern Europe reached the United States alone between 1881 and 1914, while the overall movement to all destinations exceeded two and a half million. Their political responses were diverse. Integrationists defended civil rights, the Bund combined socialism with Yiddish culture and autonomy, Zionists of several kinds sought a homeland, and autonomists and Orthodox leaders offered further alternatives.

The Dreyfus Affair provided a sustained case study. Alfred Dreyfus was convicted in 1894 on weak evidence and a secret file in a climate of nationalism and antisemitism, degraded in 1895, and sent to Devil’s Island. Picquart’s discovery of Esterhazy, the family’s campaign, and Zola’s J’Accuse…! of 13 January 1898 turned the case into a national struggle. Henry’s forgery was exposed in 1898, the Rennes court-martial of 1899 reconvicted Dreyfus, and a pardon followed that did not establish his innocence. The Court of Cassation annulled the conviction on 12 July 1906. The Affair exposed the conflict between institutional loyalty and individual rights and produced durable civic organizations, while antisemitism and arguments over national identity continued.

Modern social thought provided alternative explanations of human behavior. Durkheim and Weber, with different methods, explained social variation through institutions, culture, and social action, and empirical surveys by Booth and Rowntree showed that poverty arose from social and economic conditions. Psychology and psychoanalysis changed ideas of the mind, though their claims were contested, and anthropology divided between racial typologists and critics such as Boas who stressed culture. The human sciences did not automatically eliminate prejudice, but they supplied intellectual tools for criticizing it.

Universal-rights advocates, including the League for the Rights of Man, Jewish defense organizations, the Pan-African Conference of 1900 with Du Bois, and critics of imperialism such as Hobson and Morel, challenged racial hierarchy and exclusion. Their influence was limited, and universalist principles coexisted with exclusions based on gender, class, religion, and colonial status. By 1914, European modernity had produced competing visions of national identity, scientific authority, and human membership.

Taken together, the evidence shows that the period was marked by simultaneous movements toward inclusion and exclusion. These developments connect to the imperial expansion of Chapters 104 and 105, the national conflicts of Chapter 106, and the crises of Chapter 107, but they did not predetermine the First World War or the catastrophes that followed. The chapter’s argument is that later outcomes depended on political choices, institutional breakdowns, and contingent events as well as on ideas inherited from these decades.

A movement, named by Galton in 1883, for controlling reproduction to change the supposed hereditary quality of a population.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how ideas about belonging were made, contested, and institutionalized in a period of rapid change. Nationalism, racial thought, antisemitism, and social science were not marginal curiosities. They were bound up with schools, armies, courts, newspapers, universities, and political parties, and they shaped the lives of ordinary people as well as the arguments of intellectuals.

The chapter reveals processes that recur across European history: the tension between universal principles and particular communities, the capacity of new media and institutions to serve opposed purposes, and the way minorities acted as historical agents in defending their rights. The Dreyfus Affair condensed these processes in a single drama, but its lessons must be read together with the experience of Poles, Czechs, Alsatians, Russian Jews, colonial subjects, and Roma, who faced exclusion in different ways.

Within Volume IV, the chapter links the revolutionary and industrial transformations of earlier parts to the imperial politics that follow. It prepares students for the racial justifications of empire in Chapters 104 and 105, the national conflicts of Chapter 106, and the outbreak of war in Chapter 107, and it supplies background to later discussions of fascism, Nazism, and genocide in Volume V. It also reminds students that the same period produced sociology, rights organizations, and anticolonial criticism.

Finally, the chapter models a method. It treats ideas as historical phenomena, distinguishes science from ideology, reads hostile propaganda critically, and resists both celebration and inevitability. Students should carry this method forward: later catastrophes drew on inherited ideas but were produced by decisions, institutions, and circumstances, and the existence of opposition in 1900 is part of the historical record.
