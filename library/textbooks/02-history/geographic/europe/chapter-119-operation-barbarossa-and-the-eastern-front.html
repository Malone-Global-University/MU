Chapter Opening

On June 22, 1941, Germany and its partners attacked a state that had been Germany's trading partner and diplomatic collaborator for nearly two years. The war that followed was the largest and most destructive land war in European history, and it was designed from the start as something more than a conventional campaign.

Along a front of roughly 1,800 kilometers, from the Baltic coast to the Black Sea, German, Romanian, Finnish, Hungarian, Slovak, and other Axis forces crossed the frontier or opened operations in the first days of the attack. Border fortifications such as the old fortress at Brest, on the Bug River, became symbols of resistance because some of their defenders continued to fight for days and in places for weeks after German columns had passed far to the east. Behind them, in the frontier districts, Soviet airfields, headquarters, and railway junctions were already under attack. The first day of Operation Barbarossa shows, in miniature, the problem this chapter must explain: an enormous initial German victory that nevertheless did not produce the collapse its planners expected.

The scale of the campaign is difficult to grasp. By the end of 1944 the war on the Eastern Front had involved millions of soldiers on each side, the occupation of territories that held tens of millions of people, the destruction of thousands of towns and villages, the starvation of besieged and occupied cities, and the deaths of a majority of all Soviet citizens killed in the Second World War. Modern estimates of total Soviet wartime deaths, military and civilian, cluster around 26 to 27 million, though the figure is a demographic reconstruction with real uncertainty, as later sections explain. The Eastern Front was also where the Wehrmacht, the German army, suffered most of its losses.

This chapter argues that the German defeat in the east cannot be explained by a single cause. The weather, the distances, Hitler's decisions, Stalin's decisions, Soviet industrial relocation, Allied supply, the endurance of Soviet soldiers and civilians, the strengths and weaknesses of the Wehrmacht, and the logic of Nazi occupation all contributed. Equally, the chapter insists that the invasion was never simply a military operation that "became" brutal. German planners prepared, before June 1941, special rules that removed legal restraints on violence against Soviet civilians and prisoners of war, plans to feed the army by starving large parts of the Soviet population, and arrangements for SS and police units to murder categories of people defined as enemies, including Jews. The military narrative and the history of mass violence are therefore treated together.

The Soviet side requires equal care. The Soviet Union was a multinational state ruled by a one-party dictatorship that had in the late 1930s executed or imprisoned large numbers of its own officers and citizens. Its leadership was unprepared for the form and timing of the attack, and the Red Army suffered catastrophic defeats in 1941. Yet the state survived, relocated much of its industry, mobilized its population by a combination of patriotic appeal and coercion, and learned, at terrible cost, to fight a modern war. Soviet citizens of many nationalities experienced the war differently as soldiers, factory workers, evacuees, besieged city residents, collective farmers, partisans, prisoners, deportees, and inhabitants of occupied territory. They cannot be treated as a single undifferentiated mass.

The evidence for the Eastern Front is unusually rich and unusually contested. German operational records, the diaries of staff officers such as Franz Halder, the orders and situation reports of the SS Einsatzgruppen, and captured documents were used at the Nuremberg trials and have been studied for generations. Soviet military archives, long restricted, became much more accessible after 1991 and have transformed understanding of Soviet casualties, planning, and decision making. Civilian diaries, letters, oral histories, memoirs, and photographs add other perspectives. Each kind of source has limits. German reports exaggerated successes and were written by perpetrators, Soviet wartime communiqués served propaganda, and postwar memoirs, German and Soviet alike, were often shaped by the politics of memory.

Within Part XVIII, this chapter is the major military and social account of the German–Soviet war. It builds on Chapter 117, which traced Germany's victories in Poland and the west, and Chapter 118, which examined the Mediterranean and Balkan campaigns that affected Axis deployments before the invasion; the extent of their effect on the timetable remains debated. It leaves the comparative history of occupation, collaboration, and resistance to Chapter 120, the Holocaust to Chapter 121, and the final offensives into Germany to Chapter 122. At the level of Volume V, it shows how the European catastrophe of the mid-twentieth century became a war in which the fate of eastern Europe, and ultimately of Germany, was settled by the scale and endurance of the struggle between the German and Soviet states.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the ideological, strategic, and economic motives behind Germany's decision to invade the Soviet Union and the assumptions embedded in Directive No. 21.
  2. Compare the Wehrmacht and the Red Army in June 1941 and explain how geography, logistics, and prewar Soviet policy shaped the early campaign.
  3. Trace the course of German operations from June to December 1941, including the border battles, Smolensk, Kyiv, and Operation Typhoon.
  4. Analyze why the German offensive against Moscow failed and why Germany did not defeat the Soviet Union in 1941.
  5. Describe civilian experience in the siege of Leningrad and distinguish the 1941 blockade, the 1943 breach, and the 1944 lifting of the siege.
  6. Assess the Soviet evacuation of industry and the reorganization of the home front, recognizing regional and social variation.
  7. Distinguish the criminal orders, the treatment of prisoners of war, and the mass shootings from conventional military operations, and explain their relationship to the Holocaust.
  8. Interpret varied local responses to occupation, including partisan warfare, collaboration, and survival, without generalizing about entire national groups.
  9. Evaluate the causes of the strategic turn at Stalingrad and Kursk, including Soviet military learning, industrial output, and Allied assistance.
  10. Evaluate competing explanations of why the Eastern Front became decisive in Germany's defeat, using evidence from German, Soviet, and civilian sources.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Nazi ideology shape not only the aims of the invasion but also its operational planning, logistics, and conduct?
  2. Why did enormous German tactical and operational victories in 1941 fail to produce strategic victory?
  3. How did the Soviet state survive losses that, by most pre-1941 assumptions, should have destroyed it?
  4. What does it mean to call the German–Soviet war a "war of annihilation," and what evidence supports that description?
  5. How did civilians, prisoners of war, and soldiers experience the war differently, and whose experiences are best documented?
  6. How should historians weigh Soviet domestic production, Soviet military learning, German errors, and Allied aid when explaining the turn of 1942 to 1943?
  7. How did conditions of occupation vary across Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic region, and Russia, and why do those differences matter for judging collaboration and resistance?
  8. How have Cold War politics and national memory shaped accounts of the Eastern Front, and how do historians correct for them?

Historical Context

The war on the Eastern Front grew out of developments traced in earlier chapters. As Chapter 115 explained, the Nazi regime combined a program of racial hierarchy and territorial expansion with a violent hostility to communism, and the Soviet regime under Stalin combined industrialization and collectivization with mass repression. The two states were ideological enemies, but in August 1939 they signed a non-aggression pact, whose secret protocol divided eastern Europe into spheres of influence. Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, and the Soviet Union entered eastern Poland on September 17. In 1940 the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states and took Bessarabia and northern Bukovina from Romania, after having fought a costly Winter War against Finland from November 1939 to March 1940. Soviet raw materials, especially grain and oil, continued to flow to Germany under trade agreements until June 1941.

For Germany, the pact was a tactical arrangement, not a settled strategy. Hitler had written in the 1920s that Germany's future lay in the acquisition of living space in the east, and after the fall of France in June 1940 he turned to the question of how to force Britain to end the war. Chapter 117 traced that failure in the Battle of Britain. In the summer of 1940, according to Halder's diary and later testimony, Hitler concluded that destroying the Soviet Union would remove Britain's last hope of a continental ally and would secure the land and resources he believed Germany needed. Planning began in the summer and autumn, and Hitler signed Directive No. 21 on December 18, 1940, ordering preparations for an attack in the spring. The Balkan campaigns of April 1941, examined in Chapter 118, affected deployments and timetables, but how much they independently delayed the invasion, rather than interacting with weather, transport, and preparation, remains debated.

The Soviet Union in 1941 was the product of two decades of forced industrialization, collectivization of agriculture, and political terror. Between 1937 and 1938 the purges removed tens of thousands of officers from the Red Army, including most senior commanders, although a portion were later reinstated. The Red Army had grown quickly, had been reorganized several times, and had performed poorly in the early phase of the Winter War before improving. Soviet intelligence repeatedly received warnings that Germany was preparing to attack, but Stalin and much of the leadership doubted that Hitler would open a second front before ending the war with Britain, and they feared that mobilizing visibly would provoke Germany. The older argument that Stalin was himself preparing a preventive attack on Germany has been rejected by most historians, who regard Soviet war plans as offensive in orientation but not as evidence of a planned attack in the summer of 1941.

The historical setting also included the multinational geography of the western Soviet Union and the territories annexed in 1939 and 1940. Western Ukraine, western Belarus, and the Baltic states had been under Polish, independent, or other rule until recently, and many of their inhabitants had experienced the Soviet takeover as coercive, including deportations in June 1941 from the Baltic states. Jews, Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Lithuanians, Latvians, Estonians, Russians, and other peoples lived in territory that the Germans were about to invade, and their responses to German rule were shaped by those recent experiences as well as by the nature of German policy. Because Russian history has its own dedicated MGU History Series volume, this chapter treats the Soviet Union where its history intersects the European war.

Finally, readers should note the terminology used here. "Soviet" refers to the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics and its institutions, not to Russians alone, since Ukrainians, Belarusians, Kazakhs, Georgians, Armenians, and many others fought and died in the Red Army. "Wehrmacht" refers to the German armed forces as a whole, including the army, air force, and navy, while "the Axis" includes Germany's allies and co-belligerents, whose aims and capabilities differed. Place names follow the usage appropriate to the period, with modern equivalents given at first mention where they differ, such as Kyiv (Kiev in the Russian form used in many wartime sources) and Kharkiv (Kharkov).

Operation Barbarossa: Planning, Invasion, and the Soviet Military Catastrophe

December 1940–September 1941
Section 119.1

Hitler's War against the Soviet Union: Ideology, Strategy, and the Origins of Barbarossa

The decision to invade the Soviet Union rested on a combination of ideology, strategy, and economic calculation that Hitler and many of his subordinates did not regard as separable. Since the 1920s Hitler had described Bolshevism as the political creation of Jews and as the principal enemy of Germany, and he had argued that Germany needed to acquire territory in the east, which he called Lebensraum, to feed its population and secure its power. These beliefs were not marginal. They were part of the regime's foundational program, and in 1941 they shaped what German leaders expected the campaign to achieve: the destruction of the Soviet state as a political system, the removal of those considered ideological or racial enemies, and the permanent subordination of the Slavic populations of eastern Europe.

Strategic reasoning reinforced the ideological. After the defeat of France, Britain refused to make peace, and German leaders assessed that Britain might hold out in the hope of Soviet or American assistance. Eliminating the Soviet Union, the argument ran, would deprive Britain of a potential continental ally and free Germany to confront the United States later. Economic reasoning added another layer. Germany depended on imported oil, grain, and raw materials, and the British blockade limited its access to the world market. The Ukrainian grain lands, the Caucasus oil, and the industrial regions of the Donbas and the Urals appeared to offer self-sufficiency. The Nazi–Soviet Pact of August 1939 had temporarily served German interests by securing Soviet neutrality during the campaign in Poland and Soviet supplies afterwards, but it did not alter Hitler's long-term aims. Soviet deliveries of grain and oil continued even in the weeks before the attack, a paradox that shows how far Stalin sought to avoid giving a pretext for war.

Directive No. 21, issued on December 18, 1940, ordered the armed forces to prepare to crush Soviet Russia in a rapid campaign, with the main forces destroyed by deep armored thrusts and the retreat of intact formations into the Soviet interior prevented. The directive set as the general objective a line running roughly from the Volga to Arkhangelsk, beyond which the Luftwaffe could destroy remaining industrial centers. Planners assumed the campaign would take a few months. These assumptions rested on the memory of the Soviet performance in Finland, on the purges, and on racial contempt for Slavic peoples, which Hitler and many officers believed would make the Soviet system brittle. German intelligence underestimated Soviet reserves and industrial capacity, and the plan provided little for a prolonged war.

Economic planning was inseparable from military planning. Göring's Economic Staff East prepared to seize agricultural and industrial resources, and in a meeting on May 2, 1941, state secretaries recorded that the army should be fed from Soviet territory and that millions of Soviet people, perhaps tens of millions, would starve as a result. This policy, later called the Hunger Plan, shaped the treatment of cities and prisoners of war. It sat beside longer-range SS planning for resettlement and extermination that later received the name Generalplan Ost, whose drafts contemplated removing or killing tens of millions of Slavic inhabitants over decades. The planning documents survive in captured archives and were used at Nuremberg. They establish that the projected destruction of the Soviet state was inseparable from plans for racial domination, dispossession, and mass starvation.

The planning timetable also reveals the limits of the supposed separation between military and political policy. The army's operational staffs worked on routes, concentrations, crossings, and timetables, while economic agencies determined which populations would receive food and which regions would have their production diverted to Germany. Military victory, in this conception, was the means for creating an imperial order, not the conclusion of the project. The assumption that a shattered Soviet government could not coordinate production or resistance encouraged planners to leave the problems of an extended campaign unresolved. German commanders differed about the relative importance of Moscow, Ukraine, and Leningrad, but very few challenged the basic expectation that the Red Army would be defeated in a succession of frontier battles.

It is important to distinguish Nazi expectations from the real capabilities of the two sides. The German leadership believed the campaign would be quick and decisive, but the Wehrmacht had limited transport, depended heavily on horses, and had planned for supplies to last only to a certain line. The Red Army was in the midst of reorganization but had large reserves of men and equipment. The discrepancy between expectation and reality, rather than any single mistake, is the starting point for the failure examined in the rest of this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Barbarossa, Lebensraum, War of Annihilation, and Racial Ideology

Barbarossa was the code name for the invasion, taken from the medieval Holy Roman Emperor Frederick I, called Barbarossa, a figure associated in German national memory with eastern expansion and imperial renewal. The choice shows how the regime invoked selected pieces of European history for its own purposes. Lebensraum, "living space," was the idea that Germany required agricultural territory in the east; it was a racial and imperial program, not a neutral geographical term. War of annihilation (Vernichtungskrieg) refers to a war in which the destruction of political systems, social groups, and in the Nazi case whole categories of people was an aim, and in which normal restraints on violence were suspended. Racial ideology refers to the Nazi belief in a hierarchy of peoples, with Jews as the principal enemy and Slavs as inferior; it should not be confused with a neutral scientific category, since modern science rejects it.

These terms matter because they identify the framework within which German commanders and officials planned the invasion. Historians use "war of annihilation" as an analytical description supported by the orders and plans of 1941, not as a metaphor.

Section 119.2

The Armies, Geography, and Strategic Balance before June 1941

In June 1941 the Wehrmacht committed roughly three million German soldiers to the invasion, supported by Romanian, Finnish, Hungarian, and other allied forces that brought the total Axis strength in the theater to about 3.8 million by most counts. The force included several thousand tanks and assault guns, many of them lighter models or Czech-built vehicles, around 2,700 aircraft, and several hundred thousand motor vehicles, though the army also relied on well over half a million horses for transport and artillery. Four panzer groups concentrated armored and motorized divisions, supported by infantry armies that marched. The Wehrmacht had recent combat experience, a flexible command system that gave junior officers initiative, and a doctrine that combined air power, armor, and infantry. It also had weaknesses that would matter: limited motorization across the force as a whole, thin reserves, and a logistics system designed for short campaigns.

The Red Army in the western districts numbered about 2.6 to 3 million men in a force that totaled over five million across the country. It possessed more tanks and aircraft on paper than the Germans, including modern T-34 and KV tanks that were superior to most German tanks, but many vehicles were obsolete or under repair, and the new equipment had not been fully distributed. Soviet doctrine, developed in the 1930s by theorists such as Mikhail Tukhachevsky and Vladimir Triandafillov, emphasized deep operations, in which successive echelons of armor, artillery, and aviation would penetrate the enemy defenses and exploit into the rear. Several of the doctrine's champions had been executed in the purges. The purges had removed tens of thousands of officers, rapid expansion had promoted inexperienced men, the poor showing in Finland had prompted reforms still unfinished, and the new mechanized corps lacked equipment and training. Communications relied heavily on landlines and were vulnerable to disruption.

The geography of the theater shaped both plans. The German forces were organized in three army groups. Army Group North, under Wilhelm Ritter von Leeb, was to advance through the Baltic states toward Leningrad. Army Group Centre, under Fedor von Bock and with two panzer groups under Hermann Hoth and Heinz Guderian, was to advance along the Białystok–Minsk–Smolensk axis toward Moscow. Army Group South, under Gerd von Rundstedt, was to advance into Ukraine toward Kyiv and the Dnieper. The Pripet Marshes, a large region of forest and swamp in southern Belarus and northern Ukraine, separated the central and southern groups and limited cooperation between them. The great rivers, the Dvina, the Dnieper, and the Berezina, formed obstacles that Soviet forces tried to use as defensive lines, and the road network was thin and mostly unpaved. The Soviet railways used a broader gauge than the German system, which meant that captured lines had to be converted before German trains could use them. These conditions meant that supply lines lengthened quickly and that motor vehicles broke down at high rates.

Counting divisions or tanks without examining readiness can be misleading. Soviet armored forces possessed many vehicles, but a substantial share required maintenance or lacked trained crews, spare parts, radios, and reliable fuel distribution. The newly organized mechanized corps were enormous formations on paper and difficult to coordinate in the field. Conversely, German panzer divisions were comparatively small components of an army in which most supply columns were horse drawn. A formation that advanced quickly could outrun its workshops, ammunition columns, and fuel tankers. Neither side could translate its total national inventory into usable combat power at the decisive hour; deployment patterns and the ability to repair and replace equipment mattered as much as the inventory itself.

The Baltic coast and the western Soviet borderlands were also inhabited landscapes, not empty corridors on a staff map. The Red Army's newly acquired frontier positions lay in territories annexed in 1939 and 1940, where communications, administration, and relations with local populations were unsettled. Fortifications on the older border had been partly abandoned or stripped of equipment while construction on the new border was unfinished. This geography complicated Soviet defense and supplied the invaders with potential local informants, but it did not imply that those communities held a single attitude toward Germany. Military planners simplified this complexity; historians must restore it.

The Axis partners joined for varied reasons. Romania, under Ion Antonescu, sought to regain Bessarabia and northern Bukovina, lost in 1940, and eventually participated far beyond those objectives. Finland, which had lost territory in the Winter War, entered the war after Soviet air attacks on June 25, 1941, as what its leaders called a separate war, the Continuation War, aimed at regaining lost land and in places at gaining more. Finland was a co-belligerent, not a formal ally, and its commander Marshal Mannerheim limited operations on the Karelian Isthmus to the old 1939 border. Hungary declared war on June 27, Slovakia joined, Italy sent an expeditionary corps, and volunteers from Spain, Croatia, France, and other countries later served in German or associated formations. These partners contributed manpower, but their forces were often poorly equipped, and in 1942 and 1943 their weakness would have consequences.

Section 119.3

June 22, 1941: The German Invasion and the Border Battles

The attack began in the early hours of June 22 with an artillery barrage and air assaults. The Luftwaffe struck dozens of Soviet airfields in the western districts, destroying aircraft on the ground and in the air. Soviet records acknowledge losses on the order of more than a thousand aircraft in the first day, and German claims were higher. The air strikes helped the German armor and infantry break through the frontier defenses along the entire front. Panzer groups drove forward in narrow columns to cut off Soviet formations, while infantry followed to form the pockets. In the center, Hoth and Guderian aimed to meet east of Minsk and enclose the Soviet armies near Białystok. The pocket was closed within days and, after the fall of Minsk on June 28, German forces claimed more than 300,000 prisoners, along with the destruction or capture of thousands of tanks and guns.

The Soviet response was disorganized but not passive. Moscow's first directives on June 22 ordered counterattacks that were unrealistic given the situation, and the officers at the front lacked reliable information because communications were cut and headquarters were attacked. Many units were not at full strength or in defensive positions, others were moving to their assigned places, and some formations were caught on the march. In the southwest, in the area of Dubno, Lutsk, and Brody, Soviet mechanized corps launched counterattacks between June 23 and June 29 that cost the Germans time and casualties, though they lost most of their tanks, largely to air attack, mechanical breakdown, and lack of fuel. The Brest fortress and other strongpoints continued to resist, and German reports describe stubborn fighting by Soviet units even as the front was being torn open. The idea that Soviet troops simply refused to fight is contradicted by German and Soviet records alike.

At the political level, the Soviet leadership took time to establish control. Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov addressed the nation by radio at midday on June 22, since Stalin did not speak. The high command, Stavka, was formed on June 23 and a State Defense Committee, headed by Stalin, on June 30. Stalin's first broadcast on July 3 called the war a patriotic struggle, urged citizens to destroy anything that could be useful to the enemy in the event of retreat, and called for the formation of partisan units. In the meantime the western front's commander, General Dmitry Pavlov, and several of his staff were arrested for the defeat and shot in July, a fate that exemplified the Soviet practice of assigning blame to individuals while the system's own failures of preparedness went unexamined. Pavlov was posthumously rehabilitated in 1957.

The opening air campaign demonstrated the importance of timing. A large number of Soviet aircraft were destroyed on or near airfields, but others were dispersed or attempted sorties, and claims made by both air forces require checking against operational records. Soviet pilots continued to fight even as ground commands lost telephone contact and higher headquarters struggled to establish a reliable picture. Army reports could describe a road as open when retreating troops, fleeing civilians, and new formations converged on it. This confusion was a military disadvantage, but it was also a product of the scale and speed of the surprise attack and of incompatible orders passed down through successive levels of command.

In the north, Army Group North moved quickly through Lithuania and Latvia, taking Kaunas and Riga, and in Estonia fighting continued through the summer. In the Baltic states, the retreating Soviet authorities carried out arrests and executions, and some local inhabitants initially welcomed German forces, while others fled or resisted. By July the German armies had advanced several hundred kilometers, occupying most of the territory annexed by the Soviet Union in 1939 and 1940. The German command believed that the Red Army's main forces had been destroyed. On July 3 Halder wrote in his diary that the campaign against Russia had been won within a fortnight, while adding that it was not yet over, and that the vastness of the country and the stubbornness of the resistance would still absorb time. The comment captures a mood in which astonishing victory coexisted with unease.

Section 119.4

Smolensk, Kyiv, and the Advance into the Soviet Interior

After the border battles the German armies pressed on toward Smolensk, Leningrad, and Kyiv. At Smolensk, an old city on the Dnieper and the gateway to Moscow, Soviet forces fought from July 10 to September 10 in a long series of battles. The Germans encircled a large Soviet force in late July and early August and took hundreds of thousands of prisoners, but Soviet counterattacks, including at Yelnya, kept German units engaged and inflicted heavy casualties. The Yelnya fighting led the Soviet command to create the first "Guards" divisions in September as a mark of distinction. The two months at Smolensk cost the Germans time and strength that would matter in the autumn.

By August, Halder's diary recorded that German intelligence had identified far more Soviet divisions than expected, a sign that Soviet reserves were larger than assumed. A fierce debate began within the German command about priorities. The army leadership, including Halder and Bock, wanted to continue toward Moscow, which they considered the political and rail center of the Soviet Union and the likely place to force a decisive battle. Hitler, in a directive of August 21, preferred to secure the flanks, taking Ukraine, with its grain and industry, and the Crimea in the south, and the northern theater, the better to starve out Leningrad and link up with the Finns. He ordered Guderian's panzer group to turn south, and in mid-September it met Kleist's First Panzer Group east of Kyiv, closing a huge pocket. Soviet commanders, including the Southwestern Front's commander Mikhail Kirponos, who died in the breakout attempt, had urged withdrawal, but Stalin refused. German communiqués claimed approximately 665,000 prisoners in the wider Kyiv encirclement, while Soviet-based reconstructions produce lower totals using different force boundaries and categories; the figures are not equivalent measures. German forces entered Kyiv on September 19. It was probably the largest encirclement in the history of warfare, and one of the greatest Soviet disasters.

The territorial advances strained the German war machine. Supply lines stretched across hundreds of kilometers of poor roads and railways of a different gauge. Vehicles broke down and spare parts were insufficient. The army had suffered hundreds of thousands of casualties by autumn, and replacements could not keep pace. Soviet resistance, even in defeat, forced the Germans to use up ammunition and fuel. Meanwhile, the Soviet state adjusted. Stalin became Supreme Commander in August, the Stavka sent reserves forward, and the Soviet Union continued mobilizing men and moving industry east. Soviet forces took further catastrophic losses, but the state's capacity to continue was not destroyed. The Dnieper hydroelectric station, a symbol of Soviet industrialization, was blown up by Soviet forces in August in line with the scorched-earth policy, and the same policy was applied to factories, railways, and food stocks, with severe consequences for the civilians left behind.

The dispute over Kyiv also illustrates the difference between operational success and strategic consequence. The German encirclement eliminated much of the Soviet Southwestern Front and opened access to industrial and agricultural areas, an achievement that could not be dismissed as a distraction. Yet the diversion of armored formations south delayed a renewed concentration toward Moscow, and those same mobile units needed repairs after long marches. Historians disagree over whether a direct advance on Moscow would have brought victory, since the city still had defenders and Germany's transport and manpower problems were growing. The useful question is not whether one decision alone lost the war, but how Germany's simultaneous objectives outran its resources.

Prisoner figures demand special caution. German wartime announcements claimed approximately 665,000 captured in the Kyiv encirclement; later Soviet-based studies reconstruct losses by different unit boundaries and categories and often yield substantially smaller totals. Some accounts distinguish troops killed, missing, captured, or separated from their formations, whereas German communiqués could count soldiers collected from the wider area. The numbers therefore cannot be combined into a single range and treated as interchangeable headcounts. A defensible narrative gives the source and counting rule alongside the estimate, while retaining the uncontested conclusion that the battle devastated Soviet formations.

Capturing territory and encircling armies thus did not necessarily destroy the Soviet state's ability to fight. The Germans had destroyed a large part of the Red Army's prewar formations but had not found the final collapse the plan required. The Soviet leadership could draw on a large population, a developed rail system, and industries beyond German reach. The German command could advance, but it had no strategy for what to do if the Soviet system did not break.

Moscow, Leningrad, and the Survival of the Soviet State

September 1941–spring 1942
Section 119.5

Operation Typhoon and the Battle for Moscow

Operation Typhoon began on October 2, 1941, with Army Group Centre reinforced by panzer units transferred from the north and south. German forces struck the Soviet lines west of Moscow and within days closed two large pockets, at Vyazma and Bryansk. German accounts claimed roughly 600,000 or more prisoners, and the defeat left a gap in the Soviet defenses on the approaches to the capital. Moscow was not only the seat of government but the hub of the Soviet rail network, and a major industrial center. In mid-October, as German units approached the Mozhaysk defensive line, officials began evacuating ministries and diplomats to Kuibyshev on the Volga, and on October 16 and in the days after, there was panic in parts of the city, with looting and flight. The authorities declared a state of siege on October 19. Stalin remained in Moscow, and on November 7, the anniversary of the revolution, a parade went from Red Square directly toward the front.

Command of the Western Front was given in mid-October to Georgy Zhukov, who had been sent from Leningrad and who organized a defense in depth around the capital with whatever reserves could be gathered. Troops from other fronts and from the Far East, newly formed divisions, militia units, and workers' battalions were thrown into the line. Hundreds of thousands of civilians, many of them women, dug trenches and antitank ditches around the city. The Soviet command also benefited from information, much debated by historians, that Japan would not attack the Soviet Union, which allowed some forces to be shifted west. The German advance slowed in October as the autumn rains (the rasputitsa) turned roads into mud, and then resumed when frost hardened the ground in November. By early December, German units had reached the outskirts of the city, some patrols within about thirty kilometers.

The Soviet counteroffensive began on December 5 and 6, when the Kalinin and Western Fronts attacked the exhausted German flanks, followed by a wider offensive in January. German forces were pushed back in places by 100 to 250 kilometers. The Germans suffered the loss of many vehicles and heavy casualties, with frostbite widespread because winter clothing had not been issued in quantity. Hitler on December 16 ordered that troops hold their positions, and in the following weeks he dismissed several senior commanders, including Brauchitsch, and took personal command of the army on December 19. The order averted a rout, but it also fixed the pattern of rigid defense that would characterize later operations. The Soviet offensive was too ambitious and did not destroy Army Group Centre, and attempts to extend it, including at Rzhev, were costly. But the German attempt to capture Moscow had failed, and that failure dispelled the expectation of a short war. On December 7 Japan attacked Pearl Harbor and on December 11 Germany declared war on the United States, so the war was now global.

Defense of the capital was also a political and social undertaking. Factories were moved or converted to military production, militia formations dug antitank obstacles, and residents worked on defensive lines under conditions of inadequate shelter and rapidly changing instructions. Some Soviet institutions evacuated while others remained. The November 7 parade in Moscow had military and symbolic purposes: it was a demonstration of continued state authority at a moment when German forces seemed dangerously close. Soviet propaganda emphasized firmness, yet the population also experienced fear, rumors, and uncertainty. These experiences help explain why the capital could remain an organizing center even while the line of battle approached it.

The operational balance changed before the deepest winter cold arrived. German units had lost experienced troops and vehicles during months of campaigning; many formations entered the final advance with reduced infantry strength and inadequate reserves. Soviet commanders meanwhile assembled newly raised and transferred units and learned to combine defense in depth with localized counterattacks. Not all these forces were the famed troops from Siberia, and their arrival did not make victory automatic. The counteroffensive itself suffered shortages and heavy losses. Its significance lay in demonstrating that Germany could be pushed back and that the Soviet high command could coordinate a response across a substantial front.

It is tempting to explain the German failure by winter alone. The cold was real, and its effects on men and machines were severe, but the German advance had already stalled in the mud and exhaustion of November, and the Germans had begun the offensive with divisions under strength. The Soviet side had also suffered the hard weather, and the counteroffensive depended on reserves that the Soviet state had managed to assemble. Winter should be treated as a condition that magnified existing weaknesses, not as a sufficient cause.

Cause and Effect

Why the Offensive against Moscow Failed

Long-term causes

German plans assumed a short war and provided limited supply for a long one; the army was only partly motorized; intelligence underestimated Soviet reserves; and the sheer distance from the frontier to Moscow exceeded the capacity of the transport system.

Short-term causes

The delay from the Smolensk fighting and the August diversion to Ukraine left the final offensive until October; autumn mud and then severe cold hit exhausted units; casualties had not been replaced; and Soviet reinforcements arrived during the battle.

Immediate effects

The Germans lost the initiative on the central front, suffered heavy losses of men and vehicles, and faced a Soviet counteroffensive; Hitler dismissed commanders and imposed a stand-fast order.

Long-term consequences

The expectation of a quick victory ended, forcing Germany to plan for a long war in the east just as the United States entered the conflict; Soviet confidence and the prestige of the leadership increased.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the outcome. Soviet defensive skill and mobilization, German overextension, weather, and command decisions interacted, and historians differ on the weight of each.

Section 119.6

The Siege of Leningrad: Starvation, Resistance, and the Road of Life

Leningrad, the former imperial capital of St. Petersburg, was the second city of the Soviet Union, an industrial and cultural center, and the symbolic cradle of the revolution. Army Group North advanced toward it in the summer of 1941, while Finnish forces attacked from the north on the Karelian Isthmus and east of Lake Ladoga. On September 8 German troops captured Shlisselburg on Lake Ladoga, cutting the last land route to the rest of the country and beginning the blockade. Hitler decided not to storm the city, preferring to invest it and let hunger and bombardment do the work, in part because occupying it would have meant feeding its population. Zhukov arrived in mid-September to organize its defense, and Soviet forces held the line. The Finns halted at the former border and did not advance into the city.

The besieged city held perhaps 2.5 to 3 million people, including refugees. Food stocks were limited, and in November 1941 the bread ration fell to its lowest level, 250 grams per day for workers and 125 grams for others, much of the bread containing fillers. The winter of 1941 to 1942 was among the coldest. Heating, water supply, and transport failed, and death rates rose sharply, reaching in the worst months tens of thousands a week. Supplies came across Lake Ladoga, first by boat and, after the ice thickened in late November, by truck across the frozen surface, the route known as the Road of Life. It was exposed to bombardment and weather, but it brought food and fuel in and carried out evacuees, over a million in all over the course of the siege. Industrial plants continued to produce weapons, and the city's defense relied on soldiers, workers, and civilians, including those who built fortifications under fire.

Diaries kept inside the city provide a different kind of evidence from military casualty returns. Residents described the lengthening queues, the disappearance of pets, furniture burned for heat, and the shrinking radius through which an exhausted person could walk to obtain water or bread. Institutions such as hospitals, schools, and factories struggled to maintain routines; children and elderly residents were especially vulnerable. Families sometimes had bread cards but lacked the strength to reach distribution points. These details should not be reduced to a single heroic portrait of endurance. Conditions differed by occupation, age, access to ration categories, health, and whether a household could retain contacts or resources outside the city.

The figures used to describe Leningrad's suffering also reflect archival practice. Deaths registered within city limits are not the same category as people who died during evacuation, in surrounding settlements, or while serving in the armed forces. Some wartime records undercounted deaths when administration broke down. The commission's figure near 630,000 is an important documentary benchmark, not a universally accepted total of all civilian losses associated with the siege. Researchers comparing estimates must specify territory, dates, and whether deaths from malnutrition-related disease have been counted. That methodological discipline conveys the magnitude of the catastrophe more accurately than an unexplained larger figure.

The three phases of the siege must be distinguished. The blockade began on September 8, 1941. On January 18, 1943, after Operation Spark, Soviet forces opened a narrow land corridor along the Ladoga shore, a few kilometers wide, enabling a rail link that eased supply but left the city within range of German artillery. The siege was fully lifted on January 27, 1944, after a Soviet offensive pushed German forces back. By the standard inclusive reckoning the blockade lasted 872 days. Estimates of civilian deaths vary with definition and method. An official Soviet commission counted roughly 630,000, many historians have argued for 800,000 or more, and some higher figures include deaths among evacuees and soldiers. The majority of civilian deaths were from starvation and its related diseases, which should be distinguished from deaths by bombardment and from military casualties in the wider campaign around the city.

Everyday Life and Social History

Civilian Survival during the Siege of Leningrad

Survival in the winter of 1941 to 1942 was a matter of daily routines under extreme scarcity. Ration cards determined who could obtain bread, and the loss or theft of a card could be fatal. Residents burned furniture and books in small improvised iron stoves, collected water from the Neva and from holes in the ice because pipes froze, and moved the dead on sledges when they were able to. Dependents and children received the lowest rations, and families who could not carry bodies sometimes left them where they lay. Hospitals and factories organized canteens and communal kitchens, and industrial workers received a larger ration than others. Authorities documented cases of crime, including cannibalism, in secret police reports, evidence that must be read with attention to its source, since the state also had reasons to record and punish breakdowns of order.

Diaries are among the most valuable sources, and Alexis Peri's study of siege diaries shows how individuals used writing to maintain a sense of self, to record the deaths of relatives, and to reflect on morality and survival. Cultural life continued in a reduced form: radio broadcasts maintained morale, and a performance of Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony on August 9, 1942, by a weakened orchestra was broadcast to the city and beyond. Experiences differed by class, location, and access to food: party and military officials, factory workers, and people in the countryside experienced the siege differently from elderly and unemployed residents. Women kept many households alive, and children were among the most vulnerable. The evidence is richer for the literate and urban than for others, a limitation to keep in mind.

Section 119.7

Soviet Evacuation, Industrial Mobilization, and the Wartime Home Front

The Soviet leadership responded to the invasion with an effort to move industry out of the path of the advance. A Council for Evacuation was established on June 24, 1941, and in the second half of 1941 more than a thousand large enterprises, according to official Soviet data about 1,500, were dismantled, loaded on trains, and rebuilt in the Urals, western Siberia, the Volga region, and Central Asia, along with their skilled workers and engineers. Older summaries counted roughly ten to sixteen million people under different evacuation definitions, but the broader archival reconstruction by Wendy Z. Goldman and Donald Filtzer identifies at least seventeen million people evacuated by late 1942, including workers, family members, and other displaced people. The trains were bombed, delayed, and overloaded, and the equipment was sometimes reassembled in open fields during winter, with workers building the factory walls around machines already working. Cities such as Chelyabinsk and Sverdlovsk became centers of armaments production, and Chelyabinsk acquired the nickname Tankograd.

The disruption of 1941 was severe. The Soviet Union lost territory that had produced a large share of its coal, steel, and grain, and total industrial output fell sharply in the second half of the year. Recovery came during 1942, when production of tanks, aircraft, artillery, and ammunition rose rapidly and, by the end of the year, outstripped German output in several categories. This was achieved by concentrating on a few standardized designs, such as the T-34 tank, and by mobilizing labor on a vast scale. A decree of December 1941 treated unauthorized departure from war industries as desertion. Workers lived in barracks and dugouts, worked long shifts, and endured shortages of food, housing, and clothing. Rationing was introduced in July 1941 and was tied to workplace and status.

The evacuation was neither a simple orderly removal nor a single centrally controlled movement. A government evacuation council assigned priorities, but railway workers and factory directors had to act while the front moved and electrical power, communications, and transport capacity failed. Skilled employees could be moved with equipment, yet members of their families sometimes traveled separately or lacked clear destinations. The trains carrying machinery competed with military transports and civilian flight for scarce rolling stock. Some installations were lost before dismantling could be completed, and others arrived without buildings, electricity, or housing. The achievement of reestablishing production should be read alongside these failures and the human dislocation that made it possible.

Counting evacuated people requires an explicit time boundary. Older Soviet-era summaries often gave lower totals or emphasized organized industrial evacuation in particular years. Wendy Z. Goldman and Donald Filtzer, using archival research in Fortress Dark and Stern, estimate that at least seventeen million people had been evacuated by late 1942, together with large quantities of food and machinery. Their estimate includes broader movements than a count limited to workers assigned to transferred plants. The older ten-to-sixteen-million shorthand therefore understates that documented total when it is presented as everyone evacuated over the whole war. The figures remain sensitive to whether spontaneous flight, repeated evacuation, children, and subsequent relocations are counted.

Industrial relocation transformed places far from the front. Receiving cities had to find sleeping space, heating fuel, transport links, and food for a sudden influx of workers. Workshops were fitted into incomplete structures; machinery sometimes operated before roofs were finished. Local residents and evacuees competed for ration allocations and rooms, and working conditions could be hazardous. Central Asian republics received people as well as industrial activity, but their own labor and housing systems came under pressure. To call the process a triumph of organization without considering these unequal burdens would obscure the social history of mobilization. Equally, describing it only as hardship would miss its extraordinary contribution to Soviet production.

Women, adolescents, and the elderly replaced men who had gone to the front, and by the later war years women made up roughly half of the labor force, and a large share of industrial and agricultural workers. On collective farms the loss of men, horses, and tractors to the army shifted heavy labor to women and children, while the state continued to demand deliveries of grain. Evacuees were not always welcomed, and tensions over housing and food were common. The experiences of the home front were not uniform: workers in the Urals, residents of besieged cities, collective farmers, evacuees to Central Asia, and populations in territories later retaken differed considerably, and nationality, gender, and social status shaped access to food and safety. Propaganda, including posters and radio, urged sacrifice, and the state combined appeals to patriotism with discipline and surveillance.

Section 119.8

Why Operation Barbarossa Failed to Defeat the Soviet Union

The failure of Barbarossa to achieve its aim in 1941 resulted from several interacting causes. The German plan depended on destroying the Red Army's main forces near the border and then advancing to a line beyond which the Soviet state could not recover. It assumed that victory would come in weeks or a few months. German intelligence had underestimated Soviet reserves, and commanders discovered during July and August that new divisions kept appearing. Operational overextension stretched supply lines, while competing objectives, Moscow versus Ukraine versus Leningrad, divided the effort. Logistics, in a campaign across thousands of kilometers of poor roads, were inadequate, and by late autumn the army was short of fuel, spare parts, winter clothing, and replacements.

On the Soviet side, the state did not collapse despite catastrophic losses of men, equipment, and territory. In the first six months, millions of Soviet soldiers were killed, wounded, or captured, yet the leadership continued to function, the Stavka replaced losses by mobilizing reserves, the evacuation preserved industrial capacity, and the Red Army learned to fight more effectively, though slowly. Soviet commanders fought stubborn delaying actions that wore down German units. The political system, for all its failures and cruelty, retained control over resources and population, and Stalin's call for resistance found a response among many soldiers and civilians, in part because German occupation policy gave them little reason to hope for a better life under German rule.

That policy was itself a cause of the German failure. A more conciliatory occupation might have attracted greater local cooperation, especially in territories where Soviet rule was resented, but Nazi ideology made such a policy impossible. The mistreatment of prisoners of war, the starvation policies, the shootings, and the brutality of reprisals led to growing resistance and the strengthening of Soviet patriotism. The occupation also required security forces and diverted manpower.

The German inability to win a short war had consequences beyond the front line. Production plans, manpower allocations, and transport priorities had been calibrated to an early victory, leaving commanders to request replacements and winter clothing after much equipment was already worn out. The occupation authorities could seize grain but could not make hostile populations reliably cooperate, and partisan activity and damaged railways increased the forces needed to protect communications. Some Soviet civilians initially hoped German rule might end coercive Soviet policies, but German requisitions, shootings, and racial hierarchy rapidly undermined many such expectations. Thus violence was not merely a separate moral catastrophe; it also limited the political options available to Germany's military leadership.

Weather should be treated as a significant condition, not an all-purpose explanation. Mud and cold affected both armies, but the German command had planned for a campaign that would be over before winter. It is also necessary to distinguish the failure of the original strategy in 1941 from Germany's eventual defeat in 1945. After December 1941 the Wehrmacht retained great strength and in 1942 mounted a new offensive that again reached the Volga and the Caucasus. The failure at Moscow made a long war likely, but the outcome of that long war still depended on the interaction of resources, learning, and decisions explored in the rest of this chapter.

The War of Annihilation, Occupation, and Soviet Society

1941–1944
Section 119.9

Nazi Ideological Warfare: Criminal Orders and the Destruction of Soviet Society

In the weeks before the invasion, the German high command issued directives that stripped away legal protections for Soviet civilians and prisoners. The Barbarossa Jurisdiction Order of May 13, 1941, removed the obligation to prosecute German soldiers for crimes against civilians, and authorized collective reprisals against villages where attacks occurred. Guidelines for the conduct of troops, issued on May 19, described Bolshevism as the mortal enemy of the German people and called for ruthless action against Bolshevik agitators, partisans, saboteurs, and Jews. The Commissar Order of June 6 directed that political commissars of the Red Army be separated and killed. These orders were not improvisations by local officers. They came from the High Command of the Armed Forces (OKW) and the army leadership, in collaboration with Hitler, and were distributed to the army groups and armies before the attack.

The institutions that carried out the violence were intertwined. The army was responsible for the front and the rear areas, and for the treatment of prisoners captured in its zones. In an agreement negotiated in spring 1941 between the army's quartermaster general and Reinhard Heydrich of the SS, special task forces, the Einsatzgruppen, were authorized to operate in the army's rear areas to carry out "security" tasks, which meant, in practice, the killing of Communist officials and Jews. The SS and Order Police provided battalions, and German civil administrators, subordinate to Alfred Rosenberg's Ministry for the Occupied Eastern Territories and later to the Reich commissars, ran the occupied regions. Göring's economic organization managed exploitation. The relationships varied by region and over time, and the armies and the SS did not always agree, but the army generally cooperated, assisting with logistics, identification, and cordon. Historians since the 1970s, among them Christian Streit and Omer Bartov, have shown the extent of the army's participation. This research challenged a postwar myth of a "clean Wehrmacht" that had fought a conventional war separate from SS crimes, a myth that was fostered by the memoirs of former generals and by Cold War politics, and that was publicly contested in Germany in the exhibitions of the 1990s and 2000s on the crimes of the Wehrmacht.

The documentary chain matters when assigning responsibility. The May 13 jurisdiction order, identified in the Nuremberg record as C-50, established exceptional military procedures; the June 6 Commissar Order, preserved as NOKW-1076, explicitly directed the murder of captured political officers. These were separate instruments serving different purposes, and their existence can be demonstrated without relying on later memoirs. Commanders' acknowledgment, transmission records, and field reports are then needed to show which units received or implemented them. The source window should accordingly distinguish the provisions of an order, the institutional pathway through which it was distributed, and evidence about the actions taken under it.

The exceptional directives undermined the protections of international law, including the Hague Conventions, which Germany had signed, and the Soviet Union's absence from the 1929 Geneva Convention on prisoners of war was used as a pretext by German authorities for neglecting Soviet captives. In practice the orders encouraged brutality. The Commissar Order was carried out widely by army units and by SS and police, though some commanders did not pass it on or ignored it, and it was formally suspended in mid-1942, in part because commanders noted that it increased Soviet resistance. Historians have estimated that thousands of commissars were killed under the order, though precise figures are uncertain. The wider assault on Soviet society included plans to destroy the political and intellectual leadership, to reduce the population by starvation, and to eliminate the Jewish population.

Primary Source Window

The Commissar Order, June 6, 1941

Context: The order was issued after Hitler told senior commanders in March 1941, according to notes by Halder, that the coming war would be a struggle between ideologies requiring the elimination of the Bolshevik leadership. Commissars were Communist Party representatives attached to Red Army units to supervise political loyalty. The directive was sent to the army commands for transmission, and its distribution below the level of army commander was to be oral.

Paraphrase of the content The directive describes the political commissars as the bearers of an ideology hostile to National Socialism and instructs that, in the coming war, they not be treated as ordinary prisoners of war under the rules of international law. Commissars taken in battle or while resisting were to be separated from other prisoners and killed; the directive separately ordered that commissars arrested in rear areas on suspicion be transferred to the SS Security Service Einsatzgruppen or Einsatzkommandos. The order presented these killings as a necessary response to a supposedly barbaric method of warfare. This is an editorial paraphrase, not an original-language collation or a verbatim translation. Students should compare the directive with the source-identified translated document, NOKW-1076.

Analysis: As evidence, the order shows what German commanders intended before the first shot was fired. Its language is the language of perpetrators, and it must be read critically: it presents political and ideological categories as military necessity and dehumanizes the target, but it also documents planning. Its limits are that it does not tell us how widely it was implemented, how Soviet soldiers perceived it, or how it affected Soviet decisions to surrender or fight. For those questions, historians turn to unit records, prisoner testimony, and Soviet sources. The order does not speak for the commissars themselves, nor for the civilians whose treatment was governed by parallel directives.

  3. What can historians learn from it about German planning and the relation of ideology to military orders?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Section 119.10

Soviet Prisoners of War, Mass Shootings, and the Occupation Economy

The German armies captured vast numbers of Soviet soldiers, about 3.3 million by the end of 1941 and roughly 5.7 million over the whole war. Germany made almost no preparation to feed or shelter them. Prisoners were held in open camps, marched long distances on little food, exposed to cold, and denied medical care, and many died of hunger, disease, and exposure, while others were shot. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum estimates that about 3.3 million Soviet prisoners died in German custody during the entire war, which must not be misread as a figure for 1941 or 1942 alone, though the worst mortality came in the winter of 1941 to 1942, when perhaps two million died. Prisoners were also used as forced labor, and some were selected for execution. In September 1941 the first experimental gassings at Auschwitz were carried out on Soviet prisoners. After 1941 the German economy's need for labor led to some improvement, but mortality remained high. For the Soviet state, captured soldiers were also a problem: Order No. 270 of August 1941 condemned surrender as treason, and many returning prisoners faced interrogation and punishment after liberation.

The Einsatzgruppen, four formations (A, B, C, and D) with subunits, operated behind the front and murdered Communist officials, Roma, patients in institutions, and above all Jews. At first they shot mainly men, but from August 1941 the killing extended to women and children, and by the end of the year they and police battalions had murdered hundreds of thousands of Jews. German army units assisted, and local auxiliary police recruited in occupied areas took part in many places. The shooting operations created the conditions and precedents for the systematic extermination that followed, a connection examined in Chapter 121.

The massacre at Babyn Yar, a ravine on the edge of Kyiv, on September 29 and 30, 1941, illustrates this. After Kyiv fell, mines left by the retreating Soviet forces exploded in the city center from September 24, burning large areas, and the Germans used the explosions as a pretext. Posters ordered the Jews of Kyiv to assemble. Sonderkommando 4a of Einsatzgruppe C, with police battalions and local auxiliaries and with logistic assistance from the army, shot the victims at the ravine. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum documents that the perpetrators reported 33,771 Jewish victims for those two days. That figure must not be confused with the larger number murdered at the site over the occupation, which included Roma, Soviet prisoners, Communists, and others, and which is estimated at around 100,000 or more by some accounts, though the total is uncertain. In 1943 the Germans tried to destroy evidence of the graves.

Captivity itself operated through a hierarchy of decisions. Front-line units often marched prisoners great distances before they reached improvised collecting points, where shelter and sanitation were minimal. Transport to camps could be delayed, and food allocations were frequently grossly inadequate. German officials classified Soviet captives through ideological and racial categories and subjected many to selection for execution or forced labor. Prisoners died during marches, in exposed enclosures, through epidemic disease, and from the cumulative consequences of starvation. The approximately 3.3 million Soviet POW deaths often cited by historians refer to the war as a whole, not solely to the disasters of autumn 1941. Every comparison with other POW groups must consider changes in policy and the period being measured.

The economic exploitation of occupied territory followed the logic of the Hunger Plan. Food and raw materials were requisitioned to supply the army and the German home front. In cities such as Kharkiv the winter of 1941 to 1942 brought famine, as the occupation authorities cut off the food supply of the urban population, and in many areas farm output was seized. Over two million Soviet civilians were deported to Germany as forced laborers, the so-called Ostarbeiter, from 1942 onwards, some by coercion, and they were treated as inferior in Germany. Historian Karel Berkhoff's study of Ukraine under Nazi rule documents how hunger, forced labor, and killing combined in the occupation economy. The result was that the "war of annihilation" meant, for millions of civilians, a daily struggle to obtain bread under armed rule.

Boundary note: The full narrative of the Holocaust belongs to Chapter 121, and the comparative study of occupation, collaboration, and resistance to Chapter 120. This chapter introduces the relation of the Eastern Front to genocide as part of the war's character.

Historical Vocabulary
Prisoners of War, Starvation Policy, Einsatzgruppen, and Occupation

Prisoners of war are captured combatants whom the laws of war require to be fed, housed, and treated humanely; the German treatment of Soviet captives violated those principles. Starvation policy refers to deliberate decisions to deny food, whether to prisoners, besieged cities, or occupied populations, and was both stated policy (the Hunger Plan) and the outcome of neglect. Einsatzgruppen ("task forces") were SS security police units that operated behind the front line and carried out mass shootings. Occupation here means the German administration of conquered Soviet territory, which combined military and civil authorities and varied by region.

These terms are not interchangeable with ordinary military operations. They describe practices of state violence whose scale and intent must be stated clearly and precisely.

Section 119.11

Occupation, Local Responses, Partisan Warfare, and Reprisals

German rule was organized differently across the occupied territories. The Baltic states and western Belarus were combined into the Reichskommissariat Ostland, most of Ukraine became the Reichskommissariat Ukraine, headed by Erich Koch, and eastern Galicia was added to the General Government. Romania administered Transnistria, between the Dniester and the Southern Bug, while territory closer to the front stayed under army control. Policies varied, but all were extractive and discriminatory, and they changed with the military situation.

Initial reactions to the invasion varied. In western Ukraine and the Baltic states, where Soviet rule had been recent and coercive, some people welcomed the Germans, hoping for independence or relief from Soviet repression, and nationalist organizations sought to establish their own states. In Lviv, the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists (Bandera faction) proclaimed a Ukrainian state on June 30, 1941, and the Germans suppressed it. Violence against Jews, including pogroms in Lviv, Kaunas, and other towns, broke out in the first weeks, sometimes instigated or encouraged by German forces, and sometimes involving local participants. In central and eastern Ukraine, in Russia, and in much of Belarus, many people were more ambivalent or hostile to the invaders, and in all regions the policies of exploitation and racial contempt quickly disillusioned those who had hoped for better treatment. Disillusionment was often rapid but it did not produce uniform resistance.

Partisan warfare developed from small groups of soldiers cut off in 1941, from local underground networks, and from units organized by the Communist Party and the NKVD, following Stalin's call of July 3. A Central Staff of the Partisan Movement was established in May 1942. The movement was strongest in Belarus, where forests and marshes provided cover, and in parts of Ukraine and western Russia. Partisans attacked railways, communications, and local collaborators, and later coordinated operations with Red Army offensives. German counterinsurgency operations targeted villages suspected of helping partisans, and security units killed civilians in large numbers in reprisal. In Belarus, hundreds of villages were destroyed, many with their inhabitants, including Khatyn, where 149 villagers were killed in March 1943. Partisans sometimes also requisitioned food and coerced villagers, so civilians were caught between two armed forces. Other organizations operated independently of the Soviet movement, including the Ukrainian Insurgent Army, which fought both Germans and Soviet forces and also carried out violence against Poles in Volhynia, issues examined further in Chapter 120.

Partisan activity also evolved over time. Early detachments sometimes consisted of soldiers separated from their units who lacked local contacts, supplies, or safe bases. Later formations benefited from better communication with Soviet authorities, parachuted personnel, and the availability of forests and marshes that hindered German pursuit. Railway sabotage could interfere with German operations, but its effects varied with the ability to replace tracks and guard important junctions. German security forces frequently treated communities suspected of helping partisans as collective enemies. In Belarus especially, punitive expeditions burned villages, killed inhabitants, and displaced survivors. Students should ask which armed groups operated in a particular district and when, rather than assigning every civilian attack or act of resistance to one national label.

In areas where Soviet occupation had followed the partition of Poland or the annexation of the Baltic states, loyalties could change more than once. A person might oppose Soviet deportations and also resist Nazi racial rule; another might serve in a German-sponsored police formation for ideological, material, or coercive reasons. Jewish residents often faced immediate persecution from German forces and local collaborators, while some non-Jewish communities were themselves divided by competing nationalist projects. Occupation therefore created overlapping choices under unequal power, rather than a straightforward binary between collaboration and resistance. Specific testimony and local administrative records are essential to establishing agency and responsibility.

Collaboration also took many forms. Some men served as local police or in auxiliary units, some of which took part in killing Jews and civilians, some joined as Hilfswillige, auxiliaries who performed support tasks for German units, and some were recruited from prisoner camps for survival. Later in the war, former Soviet soldiers and officers, including General Andrei Vlasov, formed units under German command. Municipal officials, translators, farm managers, and others cooperated in order to survive, to protect their families, or from ideological conviction. It is essential to distinguish military resistance, political nationalism, local survival strategies, coerced service, and ideological collaboration, and to avoid treating entire nations as uniformly pro-German or pro-Soviet. In every region there were people who collaborated, people who resisted, and many who tried to avoid both.

Multiple Perspectives

Occupation as Experienced by Different Groups

Jews and Roma faced targeted murder, and their experience differed fundamentally from that of the rest of the population.

Section 119.12

Soviet Society at War: Patriotism, Coercion, Women, and Nationalities

The Soviet state justified the war to its population as the Great Patriotic War, a term that appeared in the press within days of the invasion and recalled the war against Napoleon in 1812. Stalin's speech of July 3 addressed citizens as brothers and sisters, and wartime propaganda emphasized the defense of the homeland and the heroes of Russian history, though it continued to invoke the party and socialism. The regime relaxed its pressure on the Orthodox Church, and in 1943 permitted a patriarch to be elected. The ideological shift allowed many people to support the war for their own reasons: defense of family and home, hatred of the invader, and in many cases loyalty to the Soviet system.

Coercion was equally central. Stalin's authority as head of the State Defense Committee and Supreme Commander was extensive, but he increasingly relied on professional generals such as Zhukov and Vasilevsky for operational decisions. Discipline was harsh. Order No. 270 condemned surrender, and Order No. 227, issued on July 28, 1942, forbade retreat without orders, created penal units for offenders, and authorized detachments behind the lines to stop retreating troops; the blocking detachments were later disbanded in 1944. The NKVD and military counterintelligence (later called Smersh) monitored loyalty. Censorship continued, and some groups suffered repression and deportation. The war did not end the Stalinist system, but it altered it.

Women played significant roles. Roughly 800,000 women served in the armed forces, as nurses and doctors, as snipers, as pilots in regiments such as the 588th Night Bomber Regiment, as anti-aircraft gunners, and as partisans. Millions more worked in factories and farms. Svetlana Alexievich's oral history interviews, collected decades afterward, record the experiences of women veterans, though such testimony, collected long after the events and shaped by memory and by the interviewer's questions, is a source that must be read with care. Civilians also endured displacement, separation, hunger, and bereavement. Children worked in factories and fields, and many were orphaned.

Soviet wartime patriotism was articulated in several registers. The government invoked the revolutionary state, but also Russian literary, religious, and military traditions previously treated with suspicion. Orthodox institutions received greater official latitude as the war continued, while official ceremonies celebrated historical commanders and the defense of the homeland. This rhetorical adjustment did not eliminate the Communist Party's monopoly on political authority. It helped officials build a language of belonging that could reach people whose reasons for fighting included protection of family, hatred of the invader, attachment to a city, and fear of punishment. Historians should examine public appeals alongside private diaries, recognizing that compliance and conviction could coexist.

Mobilization had an uneven social geography. A collective-farm household lost both male labor and draft animals, and its children often worked before reaching adulthood. A factory worker might receive ration benefits yet be prohibited from leaving the workplace, while an evacuee struggled to obtain registration and shelter. Women in uniform took part in air defense, communications, aviation, medicine, and combat, but official commemorations often privileged male infantry and tank crews. Soviet wartime correspondence and later interviews show that women faced both military danger and unequal expectations about gender. Repatriated prisoners, people who had lived under occupation, and minorities collectively accused of disloyalty could encounter further investigation or deportation by Soviet authorities.

The North Caucasian deportations of 1943–1944 provide an especially stark example of collective punishment. Entire populations, including the Karachays, Kalmyks, Chechens, and Ingush, were removed under Stalin's orders on accusations of collaboration that did not establish individual guilt. Many people died during transport or in forced settlement. These policies were enacted while the state was fighting a genocidal invader, a combination that makes any uncomplicated story of universal patriotic unity inadequate. The different legal and political purposes of Soviet repression and Nazi racial extermination must nevertheless be investigated precisely rather than treated as equivalent merely because both inflicted severe suffering.

The Soviet nationalities experienced the war differently. Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Jews, Kazakhs, Georgians, Armenians, and many other peoples served in the Red Army and worked in the rear, and Central Asian republics received evacuees and industry. Jewish citizens were targeted by German policy and also served in large numbers in the Red Army, and a Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee was established in 1942 to mobilize international support. At the same time the state deported entire peoples, accusing them collectively of disloyalty. Soviet Germans of the Volga region were deported after a decree of August 28, 1941. Karachays (November 1943), Kalmyks (December 1943), Chechens and Ingush (February 1944), Balkars (March 1944), Crimean Tatars (May 1944), and in November 1944 Meskhetian Turks and other groups were deported to Central Asia and Siberia, with heavy mortality among the deportees. The responsibility for these deportations lay with the Soviet state and its security organs, and their scale and character were different from German mass murder, though they were also acts of collective punishment. Soviet society was neither wholly unified by patriotism nor wholly governed by fear. It was both, and in varying proportions for different groups.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Soviet Institutions and Civilian Society before and during the War

What changed? Industry moved east and shifted to armaments, women entered the workforce and the armed forces in greater numbers, the state appealed to patriotism and relaxed some pressure on the church, and the military gained prestige and professional influence. The countryside lost men, animals, and machines. Millions were displaced.

What remained continuous? One-party rule, the secret police, censorship, collective farming, and the personal authority of Stalin remained in place, and the state continued deporting groups it considered unreliable, as it had done in the 1930s and in 1939 to 1941.

Who experienced change most directly? Evacuees, women workers and soldiers, deported peoples, and residents of besieged and occupied areas experienced the greatest upheaval.

Who experienced greater continuity? Party and security officials, collective farmers in unoccupied regions, and the political elite experienced more continuity, though many also faced danger and loss.

Why does the pattern matter? The war strengthened the Soviet state's legitimacy among many citizens, while leaving its coercive structure intact. The combination shaped the postwar Soviet Union and its influence in eastern Europe, subjects examined in Volume VI.

Stalingrad and the Strategic Turning of the War

Summer 1942–spring 1943
Section 119.13

Case Blue: Germany's Offensive toward the Caucasus and the Volga

By the spring of 1942 the Wehrmacht had recovered enough to plan a new offensive, but it could no longer attack along the entire front. The Soviet winter counteroffensives had been costly, and in May a Soviet attempt to retake Kharkiv ended in a German encirclement, while the Germans took Kerch and, on July 4, Sevastopol, after a long siege. The new plan, Case Blue (Fall Blau), began on June 28, 1942, in the southern sector. Its aim was to seize the Caucasus oilfields, the agricultural lands of the Don and Kuban, and the Volga, which would cut a major transport artery of the Soviet economy. Hitler stressed oil, since German supplies were limited and Soviet oil from Baku was crucial to the Red Army.

The operation began well. German forces broke through near Voronezh, advanced across the Don steppe, and took Rostov on July 23. Army Group South was divided in July into Army Group A, directed toward the Caucasus, and Army Group B, directed toward the Volga and Stalingrad, with Hitler's Directive 45 on July 23 setting two simultaneous objectives rather than sequential ones. The Fourth Panzer Army was shifted between the two groups, creating delays. Army Group A reached the Maikop oilfields in August, but the Soviet forces had wrecked the wells, and German forces never obtained significant oil. German mountain troops raised a flag on Mount Elbrus on August 21, a gesture of little strategic value. The flanks of Army Group B along the Don were held largely by Romanian, Italian, and Hungarian armies, less well equipped than German formations.

The planning problem was sharpened by competing demands on an army already stretched across the continent. German forces had to defend the long northern and central fronts while concentrating armor and aircraft in the south. Directive No. 45, issued in July 1942, further divided objectives between the Caucasus and Stalingrad, imposing tasks that needed overlapping supplies and mobile reserves. The Don and Volga rivers offered important lines of communication, but advancing toward them did not automatically secure fuel. Oilfields had to be captured intact, connected to transport, and operated with skilled labor and equipment. Soviet withdrawals and demolition threatened each part of that process.

German planning also gave limited consideration to the protection of the extended flanks. Romanian, Hungarian, and Italian armies held large stretches of the line with uneven antitank weapons and reserves, while German mobile formations were committed to separate advances. The issue was not that allied soldiers were inherently inferior; equipment allocations, the breadth of defended sectors, command coordination, and available artillery shaped their vulnerability. As the offensive continued, the distances between army groups increased, vehicles deteriorated, and the possibility of a Soviet counterstroke against the shoulders of the advance became more serious. These structural problems explain why initial operational progress could disguise growing strategic danger.

The divergence of objectives, the extended distances, and the shortage of fuel and reserves exposed the plan's flaws. The Germans' planned gains differed sharply from their actual achievements: they occupied vast territory and cut part of the Volga, but they neither reached Baku nor destroyed Soviet armies. The Soviet command, having learned from 1941, often withdrew rather than accept encirclement, preserving forces. Stalin's Order No. 227 of July 28 sought to stiffen resistance with the slogan that not one step back was permitted, and the Soviet command prepared defenses at Stalingrad while collecting reserves for a counterstroke.

Section 119.14

The Battle of Stalingrad: Urban Warfare and Civilian Destruction

Stalingrad, formerly Tsaritsyn and now Volgograd, was a long, narrow industrial city on the west bank of the Volga, with huge factories, including the Tractor Factory, Barrikady, and Red October, which produced tractors, tanks, and guns. It had a prewar population of about half a million. Sixth Army under Friedrich Paulus advanced across the Don bend, and the conventional opening of the battle is dated July 17, when forward units clashed on the approaches. On August 23 the Luftwaffe carried out a massive bombing of the city, with estimates of the number of civilian dead ranging widely from thousands to tens of thousands, and on the same day German armored units reached the Volga north of the city. Civilians had not been evacuated in time, in part because the authorities had been reluctant to signal that the city might fall, and many were trapped.

In September the fighting moved into the streets. General Vasily Chuikov took command of the Soviet 62nd Army on September 12 and held the city's western bank and ruins in a narrow strip. Soviet soldiers used factory halls, apartment blocks, grain elevators, and cellars, fighting in small groups over individual buildings, as in the defense of a building later associated with Sergeant Yakov Pavlov. Soldiers fought for the Mamayev Kurgan, a commanding hill. Supplies and reinforcements crossed the Volga under fire at night by boat, and the wounded were evacuated across the river. German forces, supported by aircraft and artillery, took most of the city by November but never destroyed the Soviet bridgeheads. Casualties on both sides were severe. The battle consumed German infantry and engineers in tactics that neutralized their advantages in mobility and combined arms.

The battle's geography changed the meaning of mobility. German forces had used speed and concentration to break open fronts in 1941; among Stalingrad's rail yards, apartment buildings, ravines, and factory compounds, individual units fought for rooms or stairways. Defenders could hold positions close to German lines, complicating the use of artillery and aircraft. The Volga was both a lifeline for Soviet reinforcement and an obstacle under German fire. Crossing boats carried ammunition and fresh troops east-to-west and wounded soldiers in the opposite direction, sometimes under bombardment. At night, the city looked very different from the operational maps used by headquarters staffs.

Contemporary testimony from Soviet correspondents such as Vasily Grossman and later interviews gathered by historians including Jochen Hellbeck help reconstruct the mental and physical conditions of battle. Soldiers feared isolation, exhaustion, and loss of communication as much as spectacular assaults. Many civilians were evacuated, but others remained because of orders, lack of transport, caring responsibilities, or the speed of the advance. Shelter in damaged cellars did not guarantee safety from collapse, fire, or starvation. Such accounts must be distinguished from commemorative narratives produced after victory, yet they make clear that a battle usually remembered through operational arrows was lived in extremely small and dangerous spaces.

The experience of civilians and ordinary soldiers is as central as the generals. Thousands of civilians lived in cellars, trenches, and ravines under bombardment, some working in the factories until they were destroyed, and some were evacuated across the Volga. The German Sixth Army's infantry endured relentless close combat. Soviet soldiers from many nationalities, including large numbers drawn from the Volga region, Siberia, and Central Asia, fought in the city, and Soviet snipers and assault groups developed skills in urban combat. Jochen Hellbeck's use of interviews recorded in the city soon after the battle has added testimony from Soviet soldiers and officers, a source that, because it was gathered by Soviet historians in wartime conditions, reflects the language of the time. The campaign should not be reduced to a duel between Chuikov and Paulus. It was a battle fought by hundreds of thousands of soldiers, supported by engineers, workers, and civilians on both sides of the Volga.

Section 119.15

Operation Uranus, Encirclement, and the Defeat of the German Sixth Army

While the Germans concentrated on the city, the Soviet leadership gathered reserves behind the flanks. On November 19, 1942, the Southwestern and Don Fronts attacked the Romanian Third Army to the northwest of the city, and on November 20 the Stalingrad Front struck to the south, against Romanian formations. The Romanians, short of antitank weapons and with few reserves, were overwhelmed. The Soviet pincers met near Kalach on November 23, trapping about 250,000 German and Axis soldiers, including Sixth Army and part of Fourth Panzer Army, along with Romanian and other units. The plan for the operation had been developed by Zhukov and Vasilevsky, and carried out by commanders including Nikolai Vatutin, Rokossovsky, and Andrei Yeryomenko. A parallel offensive, Operation Mars, at Rzhev, failed with heavy losses, a reminder that Soviet success was not automatic.

Hitler refused to authorize a breakout, and Göring promised that the Luftwaffe could supply the pocket, which required several hundred tons a day, far more than the aircraft could deliver in winter conditions. Deliveries averaged a fraction of the need. Manstein launched a relief operation, Winter Storm, on December 12, which reached to within about fifty kilometers of the pocket but halted, while Soviet Operation Little Saturn threatened the larger German position and forced him to turn back. Paulus did not attempt a breakout without Hitler's permission. The encircled troops suffered from cold, hunger, typhus, and shortages of ammunition and fuel, and the Soviet command launched Operation Ring on January 10, 1943, to compress the pocket. Paulus, promoted to field marshal on January 30, surrendered the southern pocket on January 31, and the northern group ended organized resistance on February 2. About 91,000 prisoners were taken, many sick, and only a small fraction, perhaps a few thousand, ever returned to Germany.

Soviet planners did not merely rely on the Germans remaining fixed inside the city. They assembled forces to the north and south, concealed movements where possible, and selected sectors where the Axis flanks were thinly held. The northern breakthrough crossed areas defended largely by Romanian formations; the southern attack joined it near Kalach, cutting the German Sixth Army and attached units off from secure land communications. German commanders had warning of growing Soviet concentrations, but the available reserves were insufficient and the scale and direction of the counteroffensive were misread. The encirclement was an operational achievement whose consequences depended on the subsequent failure to open a corridor or supply the pocket.

The promised airlift was beyond the capacity of the Luftwaffe in the circumstances. Aircraft were needed elsewhere, weather and Soviet interception reduced flights, and suitable airfields within the pocket were eventually lost. The relief operation associated with Manstein and the failure of the enclosed army to break out together illustrate the conflicts between political orders and military assessments. Inside the pocket, starvation, frostbite, ammunition shortages, and medical breakdown progressively destroyed fighting capacity. Different pockets surrendered on different dates, so the January 31 capitulation of Paulus did not itself end the battle. The final organized resistance ceased on February 2, 1943; many surviving prisoners subsequently died in captivity.

Stalingrad became a turning point, both strategic and psychological. It destroyed an army, cost Germany's allies heavily, and broke the German offensive capacity in the south. It also affected German morale and public opinion, as Goebbels proclaimed three days of mourning. For the Soviet Union, it demonstrated that the Red Army could encircle and destroy a large German force. But it did not mean that German defeat in the entire war was inevitable. Manstein stabilized the southern front in February and March, recapturing Kharkiv, and Germany retained the ability to mount further offensives. The significance of Stalingrad lies in the combination of the loss it inflicted, the confidence it created, and the shift in the initiative that followed, not in an inexorable sequence of consequences.

Section 119.16

The Red Army's Recovery: Command, Industry, Logistics, and Allied Assistance

The Soviet success at Stalingrad reflected changes in the Red Army during 1942. Commanders promoted on the basis of performance replaced many of the officers of 1941. Stalin gradually allowed the General Staff and the front commanders more latitude, and in October 1942 the system of dual command by commissars was abolished, restoring unity of command. Operational planning improved. The Soviet command created tank corps and mechanized corps and then tank armies, formed artillery divisions and concentrated guns in massed barrages, and improved coordination between infantry, armor, and air power, with Stavka representatives sent to coordinate operations on the fronts. Training of reserves and the use of intelligence improved. The January 1943 regulations restored shoulder-board insignia and reinforced the formal appearance of military authority; personal military ranks had already been introduced in 1935 and senior ranks revised in 1940.

Industry supplied the weapons. After the dislocation of 1941, Soviet factories in the Urals and Siberia produced rising numbers of tanks, aircraft, guns, and shells, and in 1942 the Soviet Union produced more tanks and aircraft than Germany. Mark Harrison's quantitative studies show the scale of the Soviet defense burden. Production depended on standardization, on the mobilization of labor, and on the control of materials. The railway system, including new lines and repaired track, carried troops and supplies. Food, fuel, and ammunition were still scarce at times, but the supply system functioned well enough to sustain offensives.

Reorganization took place at several levels. The Stavka improved the practice of coordinating operations by multiple fronts, and artillery preparation became more closely connected to infantry attacks and mobile exploitation. Commanders learned to assemble reserves rather than commit every formation immediately after a breakthrough. Soviet planning still produced costly failures, and rapid advances sometimes outran supplies; the important development was an increased capacity to correct errors and to carry out successive operations. Central direction existed alongside the judgment of front and army commanders, whose performance differed considerably. A narrative that substitutes one exceptional marshal for the entire Soviet command system misses the institutional learning involved.

Changes in military dress symbolized the recovery of formal command authority but require accurate dates. Personal military ranks had been introduced in the Red Army in 1935 and senior general-officer ranks reorganized in 1940. January 1943 regulations restored shoulder-board rank insignia and altered uniforms; they did not introduce officers' personal ranks for the first time. These changes occurred while Soviet armies were gaining organizational experience, but the insignia themselves were not a cause of success. Their significance lies in how a revolutionary state drew selectively upon older military traditions to reinforce discipline and professional status.

Lend-Lease assistance should be assessed by function and chronology rather than by treating every imported ton as equivalent. In 1941 Soviet defenses depended overwhelmingly on domestic resources and existing stocks, since the program was only beginning to deliver at scale. Later shipments of trucks, locomotives, railway equipment, food, radio gear, explosives, and industrial materials relieved important bottlenecks. Increased transport capacity made it easier to exploit breakthroughs and sustain armies over long distances, even where most tanks and artillery pieces were Soviet-built. Aid moved through Arctic convoys, the Persian Corridor, and the Pacific route under different risks and diplomatic conditions. Its contribution was substantial without displacing Soviet production or the principal Soviet burden of combat.

Allied assistance contributed to this. Lend-Lease supplies from the United States and Britain reached the Soviet Union by the Arctic convoys to Murmansk and Arkhangelsk, through Iran, and across the Pacific to Vladivostok. By the end of the war they included thousands of aircraft and tanks, hundreds of thousands of trucks and jeeps, locomotives and rails, aluminum, explosives, aviation fuel, and food. In 1941 and early 1942 the quantities were modest and the contribution mainly symbolic and political, but by 1943 and 1944 deliveries of trucks and railway equipment significantly improved Soviet mobility and logistics, as the National WWII Museum and other studies show. Historians disagree on the proportion of Soviet needs met, but most agree that aid was most important for transport, certain raw materials, and food, rather than for the bulk of tanks and guns. Neither Soviet domestic production and sacrifice nor Allied supply should be dismissed. Soviet leaders also complained about the delay in opening a second front in western Europe, a matter of continuing tension within the coalition. The development of these capabilities changed the battlefield balance after Stalingrad: the Red Army could now plan large offensives, sustain them, and exploit success.

Kursk, Soviet Offensives, and the Reversal of German Power

July 1943–December 1944
Section 119.17

Operation Citadel and the Battle of Kursk

After the spring thaw of 1943, the front line left a salient around the city of Kursk, about 200 kilometers wide and 150 deep, which projected into the German lines. Hitler's Operational Order No. 6 of April 15 set out Operation Citadel, a pincer attack to cut off the salient, with Walter Model's Ninth Army attacking from the north and Hermann Hoth's Fourth Panzer Army and Army Detachment Kempf, under Army Group South, from the south. The offensive was postponed several times to allow the delivery of new Panther and Tiger tanks, which gave the Soviet side time to prepare.

The Soviet command, informed by intelligence and its own assessment, decided to meet the attack with a deliberate defense and then counterattack. Hundreds of thousands of civilians helped to dig trenches, and the defenders built several belts of fortifications with extensive minefields, antitank guns, artillery, and strong armored reserves, with the Steppe Front held back as an operational reserve. Soviet forces in the salient and behind it numbered well over a million men, outnumbering the German force, although estimates differ depending on what is counted. The northern German attack made limited progress and had stalled within days. In the south the Germans penetrated deeper, and on July 12 the II SS Panzer Corps met the Soviet Fifth Guards Tank Army near Prokhorovka in one of the day's most intense armored engagements. Hitler halted the offensive on July 13, in part because of the Allied landing in Sicily on July 10 and because Soviet counterattacks had begun in the Orel sector.

The Soviet defensive system at Kursk was built in successive belts rather than a single trench line. Infantry strongpoints, minefields, antitank guns, artillery concentrations, and reserves were positioned to absorb the German attack and slow armored exploitation. Engineers and civilians participated in preparation. German formations possessed new equipment, including Panther tanks and Ferdinand assault guns, but mechanical problems, defensive mines, and determined Soviet resistance limited the benefits of technical sophistication. The offensive was delayed more than once as equipment and preparations were assembled, giving Soviet commanders additional time to strengthen the salient. Soviet intelligence helped confirm German intentions, but it did not remove the uncertainty of when and where the attack would reach its maximum strength.

Prokhorovka is an especially useful source-critical case. German and Soviet wartime reports, later memoirs, battlefield records, and post-Soviet archival studies count tanks and losses differently. Popular portrayals of vast, symmetrical armored collisions have not survived close comparison with surviving German unit records and Soviet loss returns. The battle was fiercely contested, but its meaning cannot be established from a single dramatic photograph or a commemorative total. The broader failure of Citadel resulted from defenses, attrition, operational choices, and Soviet offensive activity elsewhere, not the overnight destruction of every German tank in one encounter.

The popular account describes Prokhorovka as the greatest tank battle in history, in which the German armor was destroyed. Archival research, including studies of German and Soviet records, has challenged this account. The fighting was heavy and Soviet losses, in particular, were severe, but the number of tanks involved was smaller than traditionally claimed, German losses on the day were much lower than Soviet accounts suggested, and the German offensive was halted for a combination of reasons rather than annihilated in one encounter. The battle is better understood as part of a larger operation in which Soviet defenses held and then Soviet counteroffensives, beginning at Orel and then toward Belgorod and Kharkiv, took the initiative. By August 23 Kharkiv was retaken, and Kursk became the last major German strategic offensive in the east.

Historical Vocabulary
Defense in Depth, Operational Reserve, Encirclement, and Offensive Planning

Defense in depth is the organization of defenses in successive belts so that an attacker who penetrates the first line meets further obstacles and the defenders can absorb the blow. Operational reserve refers to forces held back, beyond the immediate battle, to be committed at a decisive moment or to launch a counteroffensive. Encirclement means surrounding an enemy force so that it is cut off from supply and retreat; it was the characteristic German success of 1941 and became a Soviet technique from 1942. Offensive planning in the Soviet usage involved coordinating fronts, artillery, aviation, and deception for a single operational aim.

These terms matter because Kursk was a contest between German offensive methods and a defense designed to wear them down before counterattacking. They do not describe automatic victory. The defense still cost Soviet forces heavily.

Section 119.18

The Soviet Offensive in 1943: Ukraine, the Dnieper, and the Return to Kyiv

After Kursk, Soviet forces mounted offensives on a broad front. Orel fell in early August and Kharkiv on August 23, and Smolensk was recaptured in September. Across eastern Ukraine, the Red Army pushed the Germans back toward the Dnieper, and during the autumn of 1943 the Battle of the Dnieper took place, in which Soviet armies crossed the river at many points, often using improvised means, under fire. The Germans had planned a defensive line along the river, but it was incomplete. Soviet bridgeheads at Bukrin and Lyutezh, north of Kyiv, allowed Nikolai Vatutin's First Ukrainian Front to launch the attack that recaptured the city on November 6, 1943. The crossings were costly, with heavy casualties among the assault troops, and an airborne landing failed.

The strategic value of the region was high. Ukraine's grain, the industrial centers of the Donbas and Dnipro, and the rail network were important to both sides. The Germans carried out a scorched-earth retreat, destroying infrastructure, taking livestock, and deporting people to Germany. Behind them, the Germans left devastated towns, and in Kyiv much of the center had been destroyed in 1941. Soviet forces and civilians also paid a heavy price in the reconquest.

Crossing the Dnieper posed a formidable operational problem. Bridges and ferries had been destroyed, German troops contested the western bank, and Soviet commanders needed to move infantry, guns, supplies, and vehicles over a wide river while establishing bridgeheads under fire. Improvised rafts and local craft were used before heavier crossings could be organized. Some assaults were mounted with inadequate engineering equipment, and the pressure to maintain momentum imposed severe casualties. The contest was not resolved in one uninterrupted rush: bridgeheads expanded or failed, and the front shifted as Soviet forces concentrated strength at selected points. Operational maps should be read alongside accounts of the soldiers who had to cross the water.

The retaking of Kyiv in November 1943 was followed by further German counterattacks, evidence that the campaign remained contested after the symbolic recovery of the Ukrainian capital. In the countryside, advancing armies encountered destroyed bridges, mined roads, burned farms, and populations whose resources had been seized by successive authorities. Soviet officials investigated residents who had remained under occupation, sometimes without distinguishing voluntary collaboration from survival under coercion. The Red Army also mobilized men from recently recovered areas, at times with inadequate preparation or equipment. For many residents German rule ended and family members could reunite; for others the return of Soviet authority brought immediate scrutiny. These different outcomes belong together in an account of military reconquest.

The return of Soviet authority meant different things to different people. For many it meant the end of German occupation, forced labor, and the shootings. It also meant the restoration of Stalinist state institutions: security organs investigated those suspected of collaboration, men of military age were conscripted, often with little training, and punishments for suspected collaborators, deportations, and the reassertion of collective farming followed. "Liberation" is therefore an incomplete description of the experience of every community. It correctly describes the expulsion of German forces and the end of the mass killing of Jews, Roma, and others, but it does not capture the coercion that came with the Soviet return, nor the fact that very few Jews remained alive in many of the recaptured areas.

Section 119.19

The Lifting of the Leningrad Siege and Soviet Advances in 1944

The year 1944 began with a Soviet offensive in the north. Beginning on January 14, the Leningrad and Volkhov Fronts attacked the German Eighteenth Army, and by January 27 the Germans had been driven back far enough that the city was no longer under blockade or in range of German artillery. This completed what had begun with the 1943 corridor, and the day is commemorated as the end of the 872-day siege. The city had survived at terrible cost, and the victory was a powerful symbol for the Soviet Union.

In the south, the Soviet advance continued through the winter and spring. In the Dnieper–Carpathian offensive of December 1943 to April 1944, Soviet armies drove the Germans out of most of Ukraine west of the Dnieper, trapping German units in the Korsun–Cherkassy pocket in February. In Crimea the offensive began on April 8, and Sevastopol was retaken on May 9. Soon after, in May 1944, the Soviet state deported the Crimean Tatars from the peninsula, accusing them collectively of collaboration, a policy that illustrates how military reconquest and state coercion proceeded together. In June the Red Army attacked Finland on the Karelian Isthmus, and Finland sought an armistice in September.

The January 1944 Leningrad-Novgorod offensive depended on the cumulative rebuilding of the northern fronts. Soviet artillery and ground forces launched attacks from multiple sectors, and German units withdrew under sustained pressure. The lifting of the siege permitted the city to reestablish land communication, but it did not restore the dead, the damaged housing, or the disrupted lives of evacuees. Factories and public institutions continued working amid shortages; many survivors returned to a city where property, employment, and family structures had changed irreversibly. The distinction between military relief and social recovery is essential to understanding the aftermath of the blockade.

In the south the Dnieper-Carpathian offensives involved repeated river crossings, armored penetrations, and operations in difficult winter and spring terrain. Soviet armies advanced across western Ukraine toward the Romanian border, while German commanders attempted to preserve mobile forces by counterattack and withdrawal. Fighting for the Korsun-Cherkasy pocket showed both the Soviet capacity for encirclement and the continued ability of some German formations to escape, albeit with heavy losses and the abandonment of equipment. The Crimean campaign in April and May recovered Sevastopol and other positions but involved the evacuation and destruction of Axis units and severe consequences for local communities. These campaigns weakened the German position before the summer offensive in Belarus.

The changing front also altered the situations of peoples accused of collaboration or targeted for collective punishment. In May 1944 Soviet authorities deported the Crimean Tatars, accusing an entire population of disloyalty despite the varied wartime experiences of individuals. Deportation was carried out amid conditions of force, dislocation, and mortality. Meanwhile, the Soviet advance toward eastern and central Europe raised political questions about which governments would return and how Soviet security interests would be exercised. An operational history ending with arrows across a map would miss these consequences, even though the detailed postwar settlement belongs in later chapters.

German forces were by now exhausted. They suffered from heavy losses, a shortage of reserves and fuel, and Hitler's insistence on holding positions, which made orderly withdrawals difficult. Germany's allies were weakening. In March 1944 Germany occupied Hungary to prevent its defection, with consequences examined in Chapter 121. Soviet commanders had learned to coordinate their operations more effectively. The civilian consequences continued to be severe: retreating forces destroyed buildings and deported civilians, and the fighting devastated towns and villages. Renewed Soviet authority again brought both relief and coercion. These advances prepared the way for the larger offensives of summer 1944.

Section 119.20

Operation Bagration and the Eastern Front Assessed

Operation Bagration, launched in June 1944, was the largest Soviet operation of the war to that point and destroyed much of Army Group Centre in Belarus. The Stavka planned it as a series of coordinated attacks by four fronts, the First Baltic, Third Belorussian, Second Belorussian, and First Belorussian, under commanders including Ivan Bagramyan, Ivan Chernyakhovsky, Georgy Zakharov, and Rokossovsky. Preliminary actions took place on June 22, three years after the German invasion, and the principal assault followed on June 23. The Soviet command used maskirovka, deception, to make the Germans expect the main blow in Ukraine, and concentrated forces in secret. Partisans carried out large-scale attacks on the German rail network just before the offensive. Soviet artillery, air power, and mobile groups broke through German defenses at Vitebsk, Orsha, Mogilev, and Bobruisk, and encircled large forces.

Army Group Centre, led first by Ernst Busch and then by Walter Model, was poorly positioned, with Hitler's insistence on holding fortified towns as "fortresses" hindering withdrawals, and with its reserves drawn off to other fronts. Minsk was retaken on July 3, trapping over 100,000 German troops east of the city. For the commonly defined June–August 1944 Belarusian strategic offensive, estimates of German losses vary with whether killed, wounded, missing, and captured are combined; figures around 350,000 to 400,000 appear in some studies. Soviet casualty totals are often reported above 700,000, predominantly wounded, but boundaries and reporting periods are not always identical, so these numbers should not be treated as directly comparable. On July 17, tens of thousands of German prisoners were marched through Moscow. The offensive carried the Red Army into Lithuania and Poland, to the Vistula by August, and the strategic pressure on the whole German eastern position increased. The coincidence in time with the Normandy landings of June 6 gave the Western Allies and the Soviet Union a shared military interest, but the coalition was politically complex. The Polish Home Army's uprising in Warsaw, which began on August 1, took place as Soviet forces halted at the Vistula, and became an enduring source of controversy between the Soviet Union, the Polish government in exile, and the Western Allies. The National WWII Museum emphasizes both the scale of Bagration and its political consequences for Poland.

The Soviet advances in the summer and autumn of 1944 also included a Soviet offensive into Romania, launched on August 20, followed by the Romanian coup of August 23, which brought Romania over to the Soviet side, and offensives in the Baltic region later in the year. The final campaigns into Germany and Berlin belong to Chapter 122.

The geography of Bagration explains the scale of the German collapse. Soviet breakthroughs at Vitebsk, Orsha, Bobruisk, and Mogilev undermined neighboring sectors, while mobile units exploited the gaps faster than German reserves could restore a continuous line. Rail junctions and road crossings became decisive objectives because their loss turned local withdrawals into general retreats. German orders to hold isolated strongpoints made individual formations easier to encircle; by the time command changes occurred, Army Group Centre no longer possessed the reserves needed to reconstitute its earlier position. Soviet operations were costly as well, and the losses should be described for a clearly specified June-to-August operational period with killed, wounded, and missing identified separately from prisoners.

The strategic reversal did not settle the political future of the territories the Red Army entered. In Poland the Soviet government supported political structures different from those of the London-based Polish government-in-exile, creating disputes over sovereignty and wartime legitimacy. The Warsaw Uprising must be situated within this conflict and the German response, without asserting that a single operational pause provides a complete explanation of Soviet political intent. Farther south, Romania's change of sides affected German oil supplies and opened routes toward the Balkans. By December 1944 military advances had created enormous new possibilities for Soviet power, but the final fighting and the political order constructed after victory remained unresolved.

By the end of 1944 the Red Army had recovered most of the territory lost in 1941 and had destroyed the bulk of the German forces that invaded. Several factors explain the transformation. Soviet industry had produced vast quantities of weapons, and military learning had improved command and coordination. The Soviet population had sacrificed enormously, and the human cost, in the millions of soldiers and civilians killed, is part of the explanation, not an incidental feature of it. German strategy was hampered by ideological commitments, overextension, and Hitler's rigid control, while occupation violence strengthened resistance. Allied aid improved mobility and supply, and the Allied campaigns in the Mediterranean, in the air, and at sea, and later in France, diverted German resources. The Eastern Front became central to Germany's defeat because it was where most German military power was committed and destroyed, a pattern visible in the fact that most German divisions remained in the east even after the Normandy landings.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Eastern Front Become Decisive?

The question: Historians agree that most German military losses occurred in the east. They differ on how to explain why the Eastern Front proved decisive and how much weight to give Soviet and Allied contributions.

Interpretation A: Soviet-centered explanations. Scholars such as David Glantz and Jonathan House, and Richard Overy, stress Soviet military learning, industrial mobilization, and the capacity of the Soviet state to absorb losses and recover. On this view, the Red Army defeated the main forces of the Wehrmacht, and Allied aid, though helpful, played a secondary role.
Interpretation B: Coalition and structural explanations. Others, including Adam Tooze, emphasize Germany's limited economic base, the effects of the blockade and Allied air and naval war, and the role of Lend-Lease in Soviet mobility, arguing that German defeat was the outcome of a global coalition war in which the Eastern Front was the largest, but not the only, decisive element.

Evidence: Both interpretations use German and Soviet military records, economic data on production, and studies of supply, with differences in how they weigh Soviet industrial output against Allied supply and the diversion of German air power and resources to the west and the Mediterranean. A further area of discussion concerns memory: the postwar "Hitler's mistakes" narrative promoted by German generals, and the Cold War tendency in the West to minimize the Soviet contribution, were both shaped by politics, whereas Soviet and later Russian accounts have often emphasized the Soviet role and downplayed Allied aid and Soviet repression.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how the Second World War is remembered in Europe and how the postwar settlement in eastern Europe is explained. Most current scholarship combines the two interpretations rather than treating them as opposed camps, with disagreement over relative weight.

Chapter Summary

The German invasion of the Soviet Union on June 22, 1941, was the product of Nazi ideology, strategic calculation, and economic ambition. Hitler's hostility to Bolshevism, the pursuit of Lebensraum, the belief that destroying the Soviet Union would end Britain's hopes, and the need for grain and oil combined in Directive No. 21 of December 18, 1940. The Nazi–Soviet Pact had been a tactical arrangement, and the invasion was planned within a racial and ideological framework that anticipated the destruction of the Soviet state, the starvation of millions, and the murder of Jews and other designated enemies.

The opening campaign brought enormous German victories. The Luftwaffe destroyed much of the Soviet air strength in the west, and panzer groups encircled armies at Białystok and Minsk, Smolensk, Kyiv, and Vyazma and Bryansk, capturing millions of prisoners. Yet the Soviet state did not collapse. The Red Army, though badly damaged by the purges, unfinished reforms, and surprise, fought stubbornly, the Stavka and State Defense Committee reasserted control, and industry began moving east within days. German forces were overextended, short of supplies, and divided over priorities, and the delay at Smolensk and the diversion to Ukraine left the drive on Moscow to October.

The battle for Moscow, the siege of Leningrad, and the evacuation of industry determined whether the Soviet state could endure. The Soviet counteroffensive of December 1941 drove back Army Group Centre, ended the expectation of a short war, and coincided with the entry of the United States into the conflict. Weather magnified existing German weaknesses but did not alone explain the outcome. At Leningrad, civilians endured an 872-day siege, which began on September 8, 1941, saw a narrow corridor opened on January 18, 1943, and was fully lifted on January 27, 1944, and which killed several hundred thousand people, most by starvation.

The war was also a war of annihilation. The Barbarossa Jurisdiction Order and the Commissar Order removed legal restraints, the Wehrmacht, SS, police, and civil administrators cooperated in violence, and the Hunger Plan guided economic exploitation. About 3.3 million Soviet prisoners died in German custody during the war, and Einsatzgruppen, police, and auxiliaries murdered Jews and others, as at Babyn Yar, where 33,771 victims were reported by the perpetrators on September 29 and 30, 1941. Occupation brought hunger, forced labor, and reprisals, and civilians responded with resistance, collaboration, and survival strategies shaped by local circumstances, not by uniform national loyalties.

Soviet society at war combined patriotism and coercion. The state appealed to the Great Patriotic War, relaxed some pressure on the church, and relied on women's labor and military service, while enforcing harsh discipline through Orders No. 270 and No. 227, and deporting entire peoples accused of disloyalty. The multinational character of the Soviet Union meant that Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Jews, Central Asians, Caucasians, and others experienced the war differently. The war strengthened the state's legitimacy among many citizens without dismantling its coercive structure.

In the summer of 1942, Case Blue sent German forces toward the Caucasus oilfields and the Volga, but divided objectives and extended distances left them exposed. Stalingrad became a battle of attrition in a ruined city, and Operation Uranus, launched on November 19, encircled the Sixth Army. Hitler's refusal to withdraw, the failure of the airlift and relief attempt, and Soviet pressure led to surrender on January 31 and February 2, 1943. Stalingrad was a major strategic and psychological turning point, but German recovery in the following months shows that defeat was not yet inevitable.

Soviet military capacity grew through command reform, new mechanized and artillery formations, industrial output, rail transport, and Allied assistance. Lend-Lease was most important for trucks, rail equipment, materials, and food, and its importance grew in 1943 and 1944. Neither Soviet production and sacrifice nor Allied supply can be dismissed. At Kursk, the Soviet defense in depth absorbed Operation Citadel, which began on July 5, 1943, and counteroffensives retook Orel, Kharkiv, Smolensk, and Kyiv. The traditional account of Prokhorovka as the largest tank battle in history has been substantially challenged by archival research.

In 1944 the siege of Leningrad ended, Ukraine and Crimea were cleared, and Operation Bagration destroyed much of Army Group Centre. Soviet reconquest meant the end of German occupation and mass killing, and also the return of Stalinist institutions, so that "liberation" must be used with care. By the end of the year the Red Army had recovered much of the territory lost in 1941 and prepared the final offensives treated in Chapter 122.

The central argument of the chapter is that German failure resulted from the interaction of ideological overreach, logistical limits, strategic errors, Soviet recovery, the endurance and sacrifice of Soviet populations, Allied assistance, and the cumulative destruction of German forces. The Eastern Front became decisive because it was where most German strength was committed and destroyed, at an immense human cost that fell above all on the peoples of the Soviet Union and eastern Europe.

Glossary: Thirty Key Terms

Operation Barbarossa
The code name for the German-led invasion of the Soviet Union beginning June 22, 1941.
Directive No. 21
Hitler's order of December 18, 1940, instructing the armed forces to prepare a rapid campaign to crush the Soviet Union.
War of annihilation
A war aimed at destroying political systems and designated groups, in which normal legal restraints on violence were suspended; applied to the German war in the east.
Army Group
A German command grouping several armies; Army Groups North, Centre, and South carried out the invasion.
Deep operations
Soviet doctrine of successive echelons of armor, artillery, and aircraft penetrating enemy defenses and exploiting into the rear.
Encirclement
Surrounding an enemy force to cut off its supply and retreat, producing large captures in 1941 and later used by the Red Army.
Soviet Front
The largest Soviet field formation, roughly comparable to a German army group, such as the Western or Southwestern Front.
Stavka
The Soviet supreme high command, created on June 23, 1941, which directed military operations.
Scorched-earth policy
The deliberate destruction of resources, infrastructure, and food in territory to be abandoned, practiced by Soviet forces in 1941 and by German forces in retreat.
Operation Typhoon
The German offensive toward Moscow that began October 2, 1941.
Siege of Leningrad
The German blockade of the city from September 8, 1941, to January 27, 1944, lasting 872 days.
Road of Life
The supply and evacuation route across Lake Ladoga, by boat and later over ice, that connected besieged Leningrad to the rest of the country.
Great Patriotic War
The Soviet name for the German–Soviet war, evoking national defense and the war of 1812.
Generalplan Ost
SS planning for the resettlement, removal, and death of tens of millions of people in eastern Europe and the German colonization of the region.
Commissar Order
The directive of June 6, 1941, instructing that captured Soviet political commissars be shot.
Einsatzgruppen
SS security police task forces that followed the army into Soviet territory and carried out mass shootings.
Soviet prisoners of war
Red Army soldiers captured by Germany, about 5.7 million in total, of whom about 3.3 million died in custody.
Hunger Plan
German economic planning to feed the army and Germany from Soviet resources while accepting the starvation of large parts of the Soviet population.
Partisan warfare
Irregular armed resistance behind enemy lines, strongest in Belarus and parts of Ukraine and Russia.
Collaboration
Cooperation with an occupying power, ranging from coerced labor and survival to ideological participation in violence; the term covers very different acts.
Case Blue (Fall Blau)
The German summer offensive of 1942 toward the Caucasus and the Volga.
Caucasus oilfields
The oil-producing region around Maikop, Grozny, and Baku, a principal objective of Case Blue.
Order No. 227
Stalin's order of July 28, 1942, forbidding retreat without orders and creating penal units and blocking detachments.
Operation Uranus
The Soviet counteroffensive beginning November 19, 1942, that encircled the German Sixth Army.
Stalingrad
The Volga industrial city (now Volgograd) where the German Sixth Army was destroyed in 1942 to 1943.
Operation Citadel
The German offensive against the Kursk salient beginning July 5, 1943, the last major German strategic offensive in the east.
Defense in depth
Layered defenses that absorb and exhaust an attack, used by the Red Army at Kursk.
Lend-Lease
The American program, extended to the Soviet Union in 1941, supplying war materials and goods to Allied states.
Operation Bagration
The Soviet offensive in Belarus launched in June 1944 that destroyed much of Army Group Centre.
Maskirovka
Soviet military deception, including concealment, feints, and disinformation, used notably before Bagration.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the German–Soviet war was the central military struggle of the Second World War in Europe and the setting for the largest part of the Nazi regime's mass violence. The invasion of June 1941 turned a European war into a contest over the fate of eastern Europe, and its outcome determined who would control the continent's east in 1945.

The chapter shows how military operations, economic mobilization, ideology, and civilian experience are inseparable. German strategy rested on assumptions about racial hierarchy and a quick victory, and those assumptions shaped both its early success and its failure. The Soviet state, with its coercion, its multinational population, and its capacity to learn from catastrophe, survived and eventually prevailed, at a cost that fell most heavily on soldiers, prisoners, and civilians.

The chapter also connects regions normally studied separately. Leningrad, Kyiv, Belarus, the Baltic states, Poland, Romania, and the Volga lands were bound together by one war, and each experienced it differently. These experiences shaped postwar borders, memories, and political orders, which later chapters examine, including the occupation and resistance of Chapter 120, the Holocaust of Chapter 121, the German defeat of Chapter 122, and the division of Europe in Volume VI.

Within Volume V, the chapter contributes to the argument that the mid-twentieth century catastrophe was the outcome of choices, ideologies, and capabilities, not of inevitability. Germany's defeat was not certain in 1941, nor even after Stalingrad, and Soviet victory was not free of repression. Students should carry forward the habit of separating what happened, what contemporaries believed, and how later memory has told the story.
