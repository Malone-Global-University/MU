Chapter Opening

Between 1871 and 1914 the great powers of Europe built a diplomatic order meant to prevent catastrophe. By 1914 that same order, made of secret treaties, informal understandings, rival fleets, mobilization plans, and the ambitions of small states, had become far harder to steer toward peace.

In October 1912, a photograph was taken in New York City of Greek emigrants setting out to return to their country and fight in the First Balkan War. The Library of Congress catalogs the image among its archival holdings, and students are encouraged to consult the record. The scene compresses several of this chapter's themes into a single moment. The men in the photograph were migrants who had crossed the Atlantic in search of work, yet they answered a national call issued by a small state that sought territory from a much larger empire. Steamship lines, telegraph news, diaspora communities, and mass national feeling made it possible for a Balkan war to draw volunteers from another continent. That war, in turn, unfolded under the watchful and anxious eyes of governments in Vienna, St. Petersburg, Berlin, Paris, London, Rome, and Constantinople.

This chapter asks how a European diplomatic system that was designed to preserve security and manage great-power competition became increasingly vulnerable to a general war. The question has to be asked with care. Between 1871 and 1914 Europe's great powers did not fight one another in a continental war, and they resolved a series of serious crises through negotiation, conference, and compromise. The history of these decades is therefore not a simple countdown. It is the story of how repeated crises, imperial rivalries, military preparations, and nationalist movements made peaceful settlement more difficult, while leaving real choices open to governments until the summer of 1914.

Three distinctions govern the chapter. First, alliances did not make war automatic. The Dual Alliance, the Triple Alliance, and the Franco-Russian alliance contained conditional and sometimes narrow commitments, while the Anglo-French and Anglo-Russian understandings were ententes that settled disputes and created habits of cooperation without binding Britain to fight. Second, the peoples of the Balkans were historical actors with their own goals, resources, and rivalries. Serbian, Bulgarian, Greek, Montenegrin, Romanian, Albanian, and Ottoman leaders and populations pursued interests that the great powers could influence but never fully control. Third, the First World War was not predetermined. Statesmen, generals, parliaments, newspapers, and ordinary citizens made choices before 1914, and the chapter explains why the range of acceptable choices narrowed without treating the narrowing as destiny.

The evidence for these developments is unusually rich and unusually contested. Treaty texts, some secret for decades, show what governments formally promised. Diplomatic correspondence, ministerial memoranda, and parliamentary debates reveal how officials understood those promises. Military plans and staff records show how armies prepared. Newspapers, pamphlets, memoirs, and the reports of foreign observers in the Balkans illuminate public opinion and civilian experience. Yet many of the most accessible document collections were assembled after 1919 in the shadow of the war guilt controversy, and memoirs were written by men who had reasons to defend their conduct. The chapter therefore reads its sources critically and notes whose perspectives are missing.

Chapter 107 is the final numbered chapter of Volume IV and of Part XVI. It builds on the unification of Germany and Italy examined in Chapter 100, on the imperial expansion traced in Chapters 104 and 105, and on the comparison of the major political systems in Chapter 106. It concludes with the condition of European diplomacy in June 1914. The assassination at Sarajevo, the July Crisis, the mobilizations, and the declarations of war belong to Chapter 108, which opens Volume V. Readers should not expect this chapter to narrate the outbreak of war. Its task is to explain the situation that Europe's leaders inherited when the crisis came.

The chapter is organized in five divisions and twenty sections. Division I examines the formation of the alliance blocs from 1871 to the 1890s. Division II follows Britain's diplomatic reorientation, the naval race, and continental military planning. Division III treats imperial crises in Africa and the Mediterranean that repeatedly rearranged relationships within Europe. Division IV turns to the Balkans, where national movements and the weakening of Ottoman authority produced a sequence of crises and two wars. Division V examines the Balkan settlement, its human costs, and the diplomatic and military condition of Europe on the eve of the July Crisis.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the principles of balance-of-power diplomacy shaped European security after German unification in 1871.
  2. Trace the formation of the Dual Alliance, the Triple Alliance, and the Franco-Russian alliance, and distinguish their conditional commitments.
  3. Distinguish formal military alliances from ententes and explain why the Triple Entente was not a single binding mutual-defense treaty.
  4. Analyze the Anglo-German naval rivalry and assess its effects on British diplomatic choices and German security.
  5. Compare continental armies, conscription systems, and general-staff plans, and evaluate the claim that mobilization made war unavoidable.
  6. Interpret the Fashoda, Moroccan, and Italo-Ottoman crises as episodes in which imperial rivalry reshaped alignments within Europe.
  7. Assess the roles of Serbian, Bulgarian, Greek, Montenegrin, Romanian, Albanian, and Ottoman actors in the Balkan crises as independent historical agents.
  8. Distinguish Austria-Hungary's occupation of Bosnia-Herzegovina in 1878 from its annexation in 1908 and explain the consequences of the annexation crisis.
  9. Describe the First and Second Balkan Wars as distinct conflicts and evaluate their civilian and territorial consequences.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of why Europe approached war in 1914, distinguishing structural pressures from political decisions.

Historical Context

The diplomatic order examined in this chapter grew out of earlier arrangements. The Congress of Vienna of 1814 and 1815, examined in Chapter 92, had established the expectation that the major powers would consult one another to prevent any single state from dominating the continent, an expectation often called the Concert of Europe. That expectation was strained by the revolutions of 1848 and the wars of the 1850s and 1860s, examined in Chapters 98 and 100, which redrew the map of central Europe. The creation of the German Empire in January 1871, after Prussia's defeat of France, ended the older arrangement in which a divided central Europe had acted as a buffer among the larger powers. A new and economically dynamic state now stood at the continent's center.

Two other long-term developments framed the period. The first was the continuing weakness of the Ottoman Empire in southeastern Europe, a problem that contemporaries called the Eastern Question and that Chapter 79 introduced in its treatment of the Ottoman European order. Ottoman decline was not steady or uniform, and Ottoman reformers repeatedly tried to strengthen the state, but nationalist movements, foreign pressure, and fiscal difficulty steadily reduced Ottoman control of the Balkans. The second was the global imperial expansion described in Chapters 104 and 105. Competition for territory and influence in Africa, Asia, and the Mediterranean linked European diplomacy to events far from the continent, and the industrial and financial transformations examined in Chapter 101 supplied states with the means to build modern fleets, railways, and armies.

The chapter also depends on the political systems compared in Chapter 106. Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Russia were monarchies in which rulers and a small circle of advisers shaped foreign policy, though parliaments, press, and public opinion increasingly mattered. Britain and France were parliamentary systems in which cabinets answered to legislatures, yet foreign policy remained largely in the hands of a few ministers and officials. Italy, Romania, Serbia, Bulgaria, and Greece were constitutional monarchies of varying stability. The Ottoman Empire experienced a constitutional revolution in 1908. Understanding who decided what, and who could be overruled, is essential for judging how alliances and plans actually worked.

Historians reconstruct this period from several kinds of evidence. Treaties and conventions show formal obligations, though secret agreements were sometimes unknown even to members of governments. Diplomatic dispatches, marginal comments, and private letters reveal perceptions, fears, and tactical calculations. Military staff memoranda and war plans show contingency planning but must not be mistaken for political decisions. Parliamentary debates and newspapers indicate public discourse, while photographs, travel writing, and the reports of international observers document conditions in the Balkans. Each type has limits. Official publications after 1919 selected documents to support national narratives, and many figures wrote memoirs to shape their reputations. The chapter treats such sources as evidence to be weighed, not as neutral windows onto the past.

Division I: The European Diplomatic Order and the Formation of Alliances · Section 107.1

Europe after 1871: Great Powers, Security, and the Balance of Power

The proclamation of the German Empire in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on January 18, 1871 changed the strategic geography of Europe. The Treaty of Frankfurt of May 10, 1871 required France to pay a large indemnity and to cede Alsace and part of Lorraine, territories that Germans regarded as historically and strategically important and that many of their inhabitants, whose dialects and loyalties were mixed, experienced in varied ways. For French governments the loss became a durable grievance and a security problem, though French leaders differed sharply over whether recovering the provinces by force was realistic. Chapter 100 has examined the wars of unification. The point here is their diplomatic consequence: a united Germany with the continent's strongest army and a rapidly industrializing economy now stood between a resentful France and an anxious Russia.

Each great power defined security differently. Germany's leaders, above all Bismarck, wished to keep the new empire intact and to prevent France from finding allies. Bismarck repeatedly described Germany as a satisfied power with no further territorial ambitions in Europe, though his critics at home and abroad doubted that such restraint could last. France sought to escape isolation and to preserve its colonial and financial position. Austria-Hungary, reorganized as the Dual Monarchy by the Compromise of 1867, was a multinational state whose Slavic, Romanian, Italian, and other populations made it acutely sensitive to nationalist movements beyond its frontiers, and it saw the Balkans as its natural zone of influence. Russia pursued security on its western and southern frontiers, influence among Orthodox Slavs, and access through the Straits from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. Britain, with the world's largest navy and empire, sought to protect sea lanes, India, Egypt and the Suez route, and a continental balance in which no single power controlled the Channel ports.

These states shared a set of diplomatic conventions. The Concert of Europe, though weakened, survived as a practice of conferences and consultations on crises such as the London Conference of 1871 on the Black Sea and the Congress of Berlin in 1878. Diplomats thought in terms of balance of power, compensation for gains by a rival, spheres of influence, and the prestige of great-power status. Such language could restrain governments by encouraging bargaining, but it could also make concessions seem humiliating and treat smaller states as objects of exchange. The ruling elites, including monarchs, foreign ministers, ambassadors, and military leaders, were small, often related by marriage, and formed a transnational social world, though one increasingly watched by mass-circulation newspapers and nationalist associations.

The settlement of 1871 also created competing narratives of justice. French politicians disagreed over the future of Alsace-Lorraine, and inhabitants of the annexed territories cannot be reduced to a single national preference. In central and southeastern Europe, the boundary between protection of a state and intervention on behalf of a national group was especially contested. A government that defended its own sovereignty might support another population's claim to revise borders; its opponent might describe the same intervention as destabilizing aggression. Students should therefore read diplomatic claims as arguments made by historical actors under pressure, not as neutral measurements of legitimate interest. This interpretive problem becomes increasingly important as the chapter turns from great-power treaties to Balkan communities whose aspirations did not fit neatly into those treaties.

Power was not measured by population alone. The ability to raise taxes, borrow, educate officers, transport troops, and supply artillery could convert population into usable military strength, yet uneven railway development and differences in administrative capacity made straight comparisons misleading. Britain's imperial fleets gave it a reach unmatched by land powers, while the defense of shipping and overseas bases demanded resources that a continental comparison might overlook. Germany's central position offered rapid movement between fronts but also exposure to simultaneous threats. Russia possessed vast territory and manpower, yet long distances and incomplete transport networks complicated concentration. French financial resources helped it maintain alliances and investment abroad despite demographic concerns. These advantages and vulnerabilities conditioned diplomatic language about danger long before any war began.

Balance-of-power reasoning could mean different things to different participants. For a diplomat, it might involve keeping several governments sufficiently strong to deter aggression. For an army officer, it could mean estimating how rapidly a potential enemy could mobilize. For a finance minister, it meant asking whether armaments and allies could be afforded. These considerations were related but never identical. German security after unification appeared to Bismarck to depend less on further conquest than on avoiding a hostile coalition. French security required recovering diplomatic partners despite the shadow of the defeat of 1870 and 1871. Austria-Hungary feared that changes in the Balkans could encourage challenges to its authority over nationalities within its own borders. Russia faced pressures to protect Balkan prestige while addressing its own distant borders, infrastructure, and reform difficulties. No government could resolve all these problems by signing a single treaty.

Power, legitimacy, and changing expectations

Historical Vocabulary
Alliances and Balance of Power

A balance of power is a condition, and also a policy, in which states try to prevent any one power from becoming strong enough to dominate the others, usually by joining or opposing coalitions. An alliance is a formal agreement committing signatories to specified action, often military support, under defined circumstances. Alliances differ greatly in scope. Some bind members to fight, some promise only neutrality, and some apply only if a stated power is the aggressor. Modern readers often imagine twentieth-century blocs and assume that nineteenth-century alliances were automatic and universal. In fact, their terms were conditional, their texts often secret, and their application dependent on political judgment. The distinction matters because this chapter argues that alliances increased risk without making war inevitable.

Section 107.2

Bismarck's Alliance System and the Dual Alliance of 1879

Bismarck's first major effort to secure Germany was the Three Emperors' League of 1873, an understanding among the monarchs of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Russia that rested on common conservative interests and a wish to avoid mutual conflict. It proved fragile because Austrian and Russian aims in the Balkans clashed. The Great Eastern Crisis of 1875 to 1878 exposed the problem. Uprisings in Herzegovina and Bulgaria against Ottoman rule, followed by Russia's war with the Ottoman Empire in 1877 and 1878, led to the Treaty of San Stefano in March 1878, which created a large autonomous Bulgaria. Austria-Hungary and Britain regarded that settlement as an unacceptable extension of Russian influence, and Bismarck, presenting himself as an honest broker, hosted the Congress of Berlin from June to July 1878.

The Congress revised San Stefano. It divided the proposed Bulgaria into a smaller autonomous principality north of the Balkan range and an autonomous province of Eastern Rumelia to the south, returned Macedonia to direct Ottoman rule, recognized the independence of Serbia, Montenegro, and Romania, and authorized Austria-Hungary to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina while garrisoning the Sanjak of Novi Pazar. Britain separately gained the administration of Cyprus, and Russia retained territory in Bessarabia and the Caucasus. Russians widely felt that they had been denied the fruits of victory, and some blamed Germany. The settlement also left many national claims unfulfilled, a theme developed in Section 107.13.

Anger in Russia, along with a harsh letter from Tsar Alexander II to Emperor Wilhelm I in the summer of 1879, convinced Bismarck that Germany needed a firmer arrangement with Vienna. The Dual Alliance, signed on October 7, 1879, committed Germany and Austria-Hungary to mutual assistance if either was attacked by Russia, and to benevolent neutrality if either was attacked by another power, unless that power was supported by Russia. It was a defensive treaty that remained in force, through periodic renewal, until 1918. Wilhelm I had accepted it reluctantly, and Bismarck pressed hard to obtain his consent. The treaty did not end Bismarck's diplomacy with Russia. In 1881 he renewed the Three Emperors' League in a secret form, and later negotiated a separate arrangement discussed in Section 107.4.

Bismarck's answer to uncertainty was overlapping cooperation. He hoped that a German-Austrian defensive treaty could deter Russia from applying force while renewed conservative-monarchy consultation might prevent an open break with St. Petersburg. This combination was not necessarily contradictory to him: the treaties applied to different scenarios and were instruments to influence expectations. Its weakness was that other governments could interpret a precaution taken in Berlin as evidence of a threatening design. The 1879 treaty was secret, and its precise language was not immediately available to foreign publics. Later readers who know the eventual alignment of the First World War must resist reading that outcome backward into every clause written in the late 1870s.

Article 25 illustrates the precision required when reading these arrangements. It authorized Austria-Hungary to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina, yet explicitly preserved Ottoman administration in the Sanjak of Novi Pazar while allowing Austro-Hungarian garrisons and military or commercial roads. Ottoman formal authority, Habsburg military access, and the ambitions of Serbia and Montenegro therefore overlapped in a narrow strategic corridor. Describing this entire area simply as an annexed Habsburg province would erase the legal distinctions that made later negotiations difficult. The opportunity to garrison a territory was not the same thing as civil administration, and neither was identical to formal sovereignty.

The Congress of Berlin worked through a hierarchy of participants. Representatives of the great powers could bargain over territorial settlements with a standing not shared by every people affected, and arrangements made on paper did not settle how communities would be governed. In Bosnia-Herzegovina, Austro-Hungarian occupation encountered resistance and subsequently required policing, investment, taxation, and an elaborate provincial administration. In Bulgaria, the distinction between the autonomous principality and Eastern Rumelia initially preserved a boundary important to the powers but contested by Bulgarian political movements. In Macedonia, restoration of direct Ottoman authority did not eliminate rival claims articulated through churches, schools, language, and armed groups. Rather than completing a final partition, the settlement fixed provisional terms that later actors tried to change.

Primary Source Window

The Dual Alliance Treaty between Germany and Austria-Hungary, October 7, 1879

Creator: Negotiated by Bismarck for Germany and Count Gyula Andrássy, then Austro-Hungarian foreign minister, for the Dual Monarchy · Date: signed October 7, 1879 · Type: A secret bilateral treaty of defensive alliance. Its text was kept confidential until 1888, when the two governments published it to signal solidarity to Russia. Several English translations are available through reputable document collections and online legal archives. Read a published English transcription of the October 7, 1879 treaty (Avalon Project, Yale Law School). The extract below is an independently prepared instructional paraphrase, not a verbatim reproduction of this transcription.

Context: After the Congress of Berlin, relations between Germany and Russia deteriorated, and Bismarck feared Russian pressure on Germany's eastern frontier and a Franco-Russian combination. Austria-Hungary, newly entrusted with Bosnia-Herzegovina, wanted German backing against Russia. The treaty was negotiated rapidly in the autumn of 1879.

Paraphrase of the treaty's main provisions The first article provided that if either empire were attacked by Russia, the other would support it with the whole of its military strength, and the two would make peace only jointly. The second article provided that if either were attacked by a different power, the other would at least observe benevolent neutrality toward its partner, but that if the attacker were supported by Russia, whether by active cooperation or by threatening military measures, the first article's obligation of full mutual assistance would apply. A further provision made the treaty secret, to be disclosed to a third power only by joint agreement. This is a paraphrase prepared for study, not a verbatim translation, and students should consult a published translation for exact wording.

Analysis: The treaty is revealing for what it says and what it omits. It is explicitly defensive and directed at Russia. It does not promise German support to Austria-Hungary in every conflict, and it distinguishes sharply between a Russian attack and an attack by another power. Yet its second article created a mechanism by which a conflict with another state could become a general one if Russia supported that state, which is why historians study the wording carefully. The source reflects the perspectives of two governments, not of the Czech, Polish, Hungarian, or other populations of the Habsburg lands, or of Russia, against which it was aimed. It reveals diplomatic intentions at the time of signing but cannot establish how the alliance would work in a later crisis, since later events depended on political decisions.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the circumstances in which each empire would support the other?
  2. Who created it, and for what purposes might the signatories have kept it secret?
  3. What can historians learn from it about German and Austro-Hungarian threat perceptions in 1879?
  4. What can historians not establish from the treaty alone about how the alliance operated in later crises?
Section 107.3

The Triple Alliance of 1882: Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy

Italy joined the Dual Alliance for reasons rooted in its own insecurity. The Kingdom of Italy, unified between 1859 and 1870, was the weakest of the great powers, and in May 1881 France established a protectorate over Tunisia through the Treaty of Bardo, a territory across the Mediterranean from Sicily in which many Italians had settled and Italian governments had hoped for influence. Isolated and angered, Italian leaders turned to Berlin, and Germany insisted that any understanding include Austria-Hungary. The Triple Alliance was signed on May 20, 1882.

The treaty's obligations were conditional and uneven. Italy and Germany promised each other support if either was attacked by France without provocation. If one or two of the signatories were attacked by two or more great powers, all three would act together. Italy was not committed to fight Russia on Austria-Hungary's behalf, and the treaty contained assurances of benevolent neutrality in some circumstances. Italy added a declaration that the treaty could not be directed against Britain. The alliance was revised through new treaty texts in 1887, 1891, 1902, and 1912 and tacitly continued in 1896 and 1908, with provisions and accompanying agreements changing over time.

Nominal allies did not always agree. Italian nationalists known as irredentists claimed that Italian-speaking lands under Habsburg rule, including Trento and Trieste, should belong to Italy, and Italian and Austro-Hungarian interests also clashed in the Adriatic and Albania. At the same time Italy sought a colonial position in North Africa and the Mediterranean, and in 1902 it concluded a secret understanding with France regarding Morocco and Tripolitania, which promised neutrality if either were attacked. A student of the alliance system should therefore note that the Triple Alliance was real and durable, but that Italy retained wide room for maneuver and that observers in Vienna and Berlin were often unsure how Rome would act in a crisis.

An alliance repeatedly adapted to changing interests

The Triple Alliance needs to be studied as a sequence of diplomatic instruments rather than one unchanging pledge. In 1887 supplementary Italian-Austrian and Italian-German agreements addressed compensation in the Balkans and the Mediterranean. During the 1891 renewal, important provisions were incorporated into the main treaty, including understandings about adjustments if the Balkan status quo changed and about Italy's North African interests. Renewals or extensions occurred again in 1896, 1902, and 1908, before the treaty was renewed in 1912. Such changes mattered because a new colonial acquisition or a new Balkan frontier could have a different significance to Italy than to Germany or Austria-Hungary. Preserving an alliance on paper did not require its members to share a consistent imperial program.

Italy's leaders sought security and diplomatic recognition in a position partly defined by the recent creation of the kingdom. Many wanted a larger presence in the Mediterranean; others argued that investment in schools, agriculture, or the southern regions should take precedence over colonial expenditure. The electorate and parliament were not always able to scrutinize secret treaty commitments. Irredentist agitation concerning Trentino and Trieste made the alliance with Vienna particularly paradoxical, because a state that promised military cooperation with Austria-Hungary also contained popular movements claiming Habsburg territory. Italy's separate understandings with France were not simply an act of deception; they reflected a policy of seeking alternatives before a crisis determined the price of choosing sides.

The result illustrates the difference between the name of an alliance and the calculation of its members. German ministers might wish Italy to deter France; Italian officials might hope the treaty discouraged French expansion or strengthened bargaining over the Mediterranean. Austrian ministers could value Italian neutrality more than assistance in particular Balkan disputes. Each party could publicly advertise solidarity while privately disputing contingencies and compensation. These tensions were visible before 1914 and make it inaccurate to portray Europe's states as units locked into fixed opposing camps. The alliance created obligations, but it also contained exceptions, reservations, and opportunities for strategic choice.

Section 107.4

The Reinsurance Treaty, Bismarck's Dismissal, and the Franco-Russian Alliance

In the mid 1880s a crisis in Bulgaria revived Austro-Russian rivalry. The union of the principality with Eastern Rumelia in 1885 and the subsequent Serbo-Bulgarian War of that year, in which Bulgaria defeated Serbia, divided Russia from Austria-Hungary and ended the Three Emperors' League when it expired in 1887. Bismarck responded with a layered system. In 1887 Britain, Italy, and Austria-Hungary exchanged Mediterranean agreements aimed at preserving the status quo. In the same year, on June 18, Germany concluded the secret Reinsurance Treaty with Russia, in which each promised neutrality if the other fought a third power, except if Germany attacked France or Russia attacked Austria-Hungary. A confidential protocol addressed Bulgaria and the Straits. The arrangement showed Bismarck's wish to keep Russia from joining France, even while Germany was allied with Russia's rival.

The system was personal and delicate. Emperor Wilhelm II, who succeeded in 1888, and his advisers dismissed Bismarck in March 1890, and the new chancellor, Leo von Caprivi, supported by officials who thought the treaty inconsistent with the Austrian alliance, declined to renew the Reinsurance Treaty even though Russian representatives wished to continue it. German decision makers believed they were simplifying commitments. Russia, however, now lacked any treaty tie with Germany, and French leaders were eager for a partner.

Franco-Russian rapprochement advanced through finance, visits, and military talks. French investors lent large sums to Russia, and the visit of a French fleet to Kronstadt in July 1891 was a public demonstration. A military convention was negotiated in 1892 and confirmed in the winter of 1893 to 1894. It provided that if France were attacked by Germany, or by Italy supported by Germany, Russia would attack Germany, and that if Russia were attacked by Germany, or by Austria-Hungary supported by Germany, France would attack Germany. It also provided that mobilization by any member of the Triple Alliance would trigger the mobilization of both France and Russia. This was a formal alliance with military commitments, and it ended France's isolation. By the 1890s Europe was divided between two groupings, though the groupings were looser than later memory suggests and Britain remained outside both.

The political meaning of a diplomatic realignment

France and Russia did not become partners solely because Germany refused to renew a treaty. French diplomacy had to reconcile its republican institutions with Russian autocracy, persuade investors that Russian securities were attractive, and develop military procedures across considerable distances. Russian elites had their own disagreements about depending on France; some continued to value understandings with Germany, while others feared German economic predominance and the Austro-German connection. The attraction of an alliance was partly that both sides confronted the possibility of German power in a future conflict. Public ceremonies involving ships, flags, and anthems gave the relationship a symbolic life beyond private negotiations.

There was also an important difference between diplomatic agreement and military application. The military convention envisaged reciprocal offensive action in specified cases of attack, and its mobilization provisions were meant to prevent either partner from leaving the other to face an opponent alone. However, armies with different railway networks, force structures, and mobilization times would not necessarily arrive at the battlefield simultaneously. Staff officers had to exchange information, estimate the location of German forces, and decide how operations in the east and west might interact. The convention encouraged military coordination but did not remove uncertainty about whether political decision-makers would judge a particular crisis to trigger its promises.

The breakup of Bismarck's layered agreements is sometimes narrated as a single disastrous decision that divided Europe. That interpretation overlooks surviving flexibility and later attempts at rapprochement. Germany continued to maintain economic relations with Russia, and other powers repeatedly negotiated arrangements with partners outside their immediate groupings. British foreign policy, examined next, remained especially open. Nevertheless, the Franco-Russian alliance changed the problem facing German strategists. Even when diplomacy was peaceful, a contingency in either east or west now had to be evaluated in light of an organized partner on the other side of Germany. How that problem influenced military planning is a separate historical question from whether the alliance itself caused war.

Britain Reconsiders Its Diplomatic Position: From Isolation to the Entente Cordiale

For much of the nineteenth century Britain avoided permanent continental commitments, a posture later called splendid isolation, though it was never complete. British leaders relied on the navy and on the flexibility to shift between partners, and they regarded France and Russia as imperial rivals in Africa, the Mediterranean, and Asia. By the end of the 1890s this posture looked costly. The Second South African War of 1899 to 1902 revealed military strain and international hostility, and British officials worried about overstretch. Overtures to Germany in the late 1890s and early 1900s, including conversations associated with Joseph Chamberlain, produced no agreement, partly because each side wanted terms the other considered too high.

Britain therefore turned elsewhere. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance of January 30, 1902 committed each to neutrality if the other fought one power in East Asia, and to assistance if the other faced two. It protected British interests in the Far East without a continental treaty. Meanwhile, the settlement of old quarrels with France, aided by the shock of Fashoda examined in Section 107.9, led to the Entente Cordiale of April 8, 1904. The agreements recognized British primacy in Egypt and French primacy in Morocco and resolved disputes over Newfoundland fishing rights, West Africa, Siam, Madagascar, and the New Hebrides. The entente was a package of colonial settlements, not a military alliance. Yet it changed expectations, and the Russo-Japanese War of 1904 to 1905, which tied Russia down and weakened it, complicated the picture by temporarily reducing Russia's weight in European diplomacy.

The empire-wide calculation behind British policy

British diplomacy cannot be understood solely as a choice between Paris and Berlin. The government also faced concerns about Russia along India's approaches, French rivalry in Egypt and North Africa, commerce in East Asia, and the protection of maritime communications. A treaty that freed naval resources in the Pacific or settled an African colonial dispute could therefore have consequences for European security without having been negotiated as an anti-German alliance. British ministers varied in their views of Germany: commercial competition and naval anxiety coexisted with substantial trade, dynastic ties, and advocates of cooperation. The language of splendid isolation can obscure this continuous search for practical partners.

The South African War influenced opinion by revealing how difficult it was to conduct an expensive imperial campaign while remaining diplomatically unpopular. In Parliament and the press, some observers saw continental powers as hostile, while critics of the war questioned the costs and conduct of empire. This climate made an agreement with Japan attractive: it strengthened the security of British interests in East Asia without demanding the same permanent naval commitment there. Japan's own objectives were not identical to Britain's, however. Its interests in Korea and regional security developed rapidly, and the alliance brought Britain into a relationship with a rising Asian imperial power. Such a connection illustrates that Europe's prewar alignments cannot be mapped exclusively on European territory.

The Entente Cordiale of 1904 resolved several disputes, including those concerning Egypt and Morocco, by acknowledging different spheres of influence and setting related colonial questions. This was diplomacy through compromise, but it also affected people whose consent was not requested. Moroccan rulers and populations, and inhabitants of Egypt, experienced arrangements concluded primarily by London and Paris as constraints on their political autonomy. The agreement did not create a military treaty requiring Britain to support France in a war against Germany. Its later importance arose because negotiations established confidence, enabled staff discussions, and survived international crises. The distinction between the original legal text and the expectations built upon it is essential to understanding the next decade.

Section 107.6

The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 and the Triple Entente

After defeat by Japan and the revolution of 1905, Russia sought to settle its disputes with Britain, and Britain sought to reduce the burden of defending India. The Anglo-Russian Convention of August 31, 1907 addressed Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet, regions where the two empires had competed for decades. Persia was divided into a Russian zone in the north, a British zone in the southeast, and a neutral zone between them, though the Persian government was not a party and the Persian constitutional movement resented the arrangement. Afghanistan was recognized as lying within the British sphere of influence, and both powers pledged to respect Tibet's territorial integrity and to deal with it through the Chinese government. The convention reduced imperial friction, but it was also unpopular among some British liberals who disliked association with autocratic Russia.

Observers soon spoke of a Triple Entente linking Britain, France, and Russia, and German leaders interpreted it as a hostile coalition, a perception often called encirclement. The term is useful but must be handled carefully. There was no single treaty uniting the three. France and Russia were bound by an alliance. Britain and France had an entente supplemented, after 1906, by confidential staff talks, and in November 1912 they exchanged letters stating that consultation would follow if either feared an unprovoked attack, while declaring that neither was committed to act. Britain and Russia had a convention on Asian affairs. British ministers, led by Foreign Secretary Edward Grey, insisted publicly and privately that Britain retained freedom of action, though French and Russian officials hoped for firmer support.

German fears of encirclement were not wholly invented, since France and Russia were formal military partners and Britain was becoming more closely associated with both. But using the phrase without qualification adopts the interpretation of German policymakers as a general law of European politics. Germany retained ties to Austria-Hungary, Italy, the Ottoman Empire, and other states, and still had avenues for negotiating with Britain. The question is not whether Berlin had genuine reasons to worry, but how leaders assessed alternatives and whether their decisions alleviated or intensified those fears. An objective account must preserve the gap between a nation's perception of insecurity and the actual obligations of other governments.

British and French conversations about possible military cooperation developed unevenly and were influenced by the Moroccan crises. Officials could consider naval deployments and continental operations without committing an elected government to war. Private staff exchanges had practical consequences, because planners began to imagine how different navies or armies might divide tasks in an emergency; they were not equivalent to ratified collective-defense obligations. Some members of Parliament feared that unofficial understandings would limit future freedom, while supporters argued that consultation improved deterrence. The actual diplomatic commitment remained more ambiguous than the term Triple Entente often suggests.

The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 was an imperial accommodation with European implications. In Persia it defined northern and southern areas in which the two powers would limit interference with each other's interests, leaving a central zone without the same designated predominance. It recognized Afghanistan as outside Russia's sphere of influence and arranged how Russian relations with its government would be conducted, while provisions concerning Tibet addressed noninterference and relations with Chinese authority. These arrangements did not amount to an equal treaty negotiated with the Persian, Afghan, and Tibetan populations. Political movements in Persia in particular had to contend with restrictions imposed during a constitutional struggle already underway. The agreement reduced direct British-Russian competition, but it could not settle domestic conflicts in the affected regions.

Historical Vocabulary
The Triple Entente and Diplomatic Commitments

An entente is an understanding between governments that settles disputes or aligns policies without necessarily binding them to military action. The Triple Entente is the label historians give to the combination of the Franco-Russian alliance, the Anglo-French entente, and the Anglo-Russian convention. It was never a three-power treaty of mutual defense. Commitments existed at several levels: a formal military convention between France and Russia, diplomatic understandings between Britain and each partner, and informal staff conversations whose political meaning was disputed. Distinguishing these levels is essential, because assuming that all three states were automatically bound together would hide the political decisions that governments had to make in 1914, which Chapter 108 examines.

Section 107.7

Britain, Germany, and the Naval Arms Race

Anglo-German antagonism was driven by commerce, colonial rivalry, press campaigns, and above all by naval competition. Germany under Wilhelm II pursued what is often called Weltpolitik, a policy of seeking global standing alongside the other great powers. Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, naval secretary from 1897, persuaded the Reichstag to pass Navy Laws in 1898 and 1900 that authorized a large battle fleet. Tirpitz argued that a fleet strong enough to threaten Britain's navy would deter British hostility, a reasoning sometimes called the risk theory. The navy also served domestic purposes, winning support among industrialists, nationalists, and the middle classes. British leaders, who depended on sea power for the survival of the empire and the food supply of an island dependent on imports, interpreted the German fleet as a challenge aimed at them.

The launch of HMS Dreadnought in 1906, an all-big-gun battleship championed by Admiral Sir John Fisher, rendered older battleships obsolete and restarted the race on new terms, since Germany could now build comparable ships. Further German Navy Laws in 1906, 1908, and 1912 maintained the pace. A British naval scare in 1909, over whether Germany was building dreadnoughts faster than announced, produced public demands for rapid construction, and the competition strained public finances, helping to shape the budget politics of Liberal Britain. Diplomatic attempts to limit it, notably the Haldane mission of February 1912 and Winston Churchill's proposal in 1913 for a naval holiday, failed, in part because Britain would not give a neutrality pledge and Germany would not accept a fixed ratio.

The race did not make war inevitable, and by 1912 Germany was in fact slowing its naval growth as army needs rose. But it had diplomatic consequences. It encouraged Britain to cooperate more closely with France and Russia, and in 1912 the British and French agreed to concentrate their fleets in different waters, with France guarding the Mediterranean and Britain the North Sea, an arrangement that left Britain morally exposed if France were attacked. The naval race thus transformed British perceptions of Germany from a competitor into a potential danger, while many German leaders concluded that Britain was determined to block German growth. Both interpretations contained misjudgments.

Building a fleet and paying for it

Sea power was a political as well as a technological question. The German Navy Laws used long-term construction schedules to make battle-fleet expansion harder to reverse through annual budget debate. Naval enthusiasts argued that a powerful fleet could protect trade and colonies, improve Germany's international status, and deter Britain from diplomatic pressure. Yet a fleet concentrated in European waters could look threatening to British planners even if its sponsors described it as defensive. Britain's maritime position differed sharply from Germany's: British governments had to protect not only the North Sea approaches but also routes linking dominions, colonies, trade, and the food imports upon which urban populations depended. They therefore evaluated a growing German battle fleet as a strategic risk out of proportion to its stated colonial purpose.

Construction depended on dockyards, industrial suppliers, steel, guns, skilled labor, finance, and trained crews. The introduction of the all-big-gun battleship altered designs and rendered earlier ships relatively less useful, but it did not remove differences in geographic position, overseas bases, personnel, or the availability of naval reserves. The contest also involved smaller vessels, submarines, scouting forces, and coastal defenses that mattered for actual operations. Political slogans about numbers of dreadnoughts simplified a complicated strategic balance. A ship authorized by a parliament was not yet a completed unit in a fleet; a ship launched was not necessarily ready for operations; and maintaining crews and repairs imposed recurring costs.

Naval expansion generated competing interpretations within both states. The Admiralty's arguments were scrutinized by those concerned about social expenditure and taxation, while German naval policy had to compete with demands from the army. Britain's geographic advantages and shipbuilding capacity enabled it to preserve superiority, and officials reorganized deployments to concentrate on more immediate European risks. Negotiators explored arms limitation, including during British contacts with Berlin in 1912, but mutual distrust and disagreement about recognition of naval ratios hindered agreement. The failure to stop the race was consequential, although it cannot be isolated from wider diplomatic choices or described as a direct mechanical route to war.

Section 107.8

Continental Armies, Conscription, Mobilization, and Strategic Planning

On the continent, power rested on large conscript armies. France, Germany, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and Italy relied on compulsory service and trained reserves who could be called to the colors in an emergency, which allowed small peacetime forces to expand into vast armies. The systems differed. Germany conscripted only a portion of each age cohort, partly because of cost and the size of its population, while France drew a larger share of its young men. France lengthened service from two years to three in August 1913, in response to German increases. Russia had the largest population and, after reforms following its 1905 defeat, launched ambitious expansion in 1913 and 1914. Austria-Hungary's army was limited by the budgetary politics of its two parliaments and by the multilingual composition of its ranks. Military spending rose across the continent, and the German army laws of 1912 and 1913 added substantial numbers.

General staffs prepared detailed plans. Railways had made it possible to move huge forces quickly, and planners drew up timetables for concentrating troops on frontiers. Germany, which expected a two-front war against France and Russia, adapted a plan associated with Alfred von Schlieffen, chief of the general staff until 1906, and modified by his successor Helmuth von Moltke, calling for a rapid offensive against France through Belgium before turning east. France adopted Plan XVII in 1913, concentrating on offensive action toward Alsace and Lorraine. Russia revised its plans to deploy against both Germany and Austria-Hungary. The Austro-Hungarian chief of staff, Franz Conrad von Hötzendorf, planned for contingencies against Serbia and Russia and repeatedly urged preventive war against Serbia and Italy.

Historians disagree about how far these plans constrained politicians. Plans were contingency documents, not decisions, and civilian ministers had authority to approve or refuse mobilization, but the plans made some options faster and others slower, and military leaders warned that delay would be disastrous. The German plan especially depended on speed, which meant that a political pause after mobilization could appear militarily dangerous. The relationship between civil and military authority varied across states. In Germany the army enjoyed wide autonomy, in France the government held firmer control, and in Russia the tsar's decisions were influenced by competing ministers and generals. Chapter 108 examines how these arrangements worked in July 1914. Here the important point is that armaments, railways, and planning made a conflict more destructive and fast, while political leaders retained responsibility for deciding whether to use them.

Staff planning, civilian authority, and the problem of alternatives

A mobilization plan converted a state's peacetime resources into wartime formations. It identified reservists who would report to particular depots, rolling stock and railway junctions needed for concentration, ammunition and food stocks, and the units assigned to prospective frontiers. Because delays could leave a force exposed, planners often sought precise timetables. Those timetables created incentives to act quickly when ministers considered mobilization, but they did not make a railway schedule legally superior to government. Political leaders could postpone, revise, or reject military recommendations, although doing so might appear to accept strategic disadvantage. The tension between technical preparation and political judgment was a characteristic feature of Europe's modern states.

German discussion of an attack through Belgium illustrates the need to separate several kinds of source. Alfred von Schlieffen's memorandum associated with 1905 is not identical to every earlier staff exercise or to the deployment orders later developed under Helmuth von Moltke the Younger. Historians disagree over whether the memorandum was a feasible operational blueprint or partly a proposal to demonstrate the need for greater manpower, and newer research has complicated older stories about a single rigid plan handed down unchanged. Nonetheless, German planning increasingly considered concentrating force against France before shifting attention east, an approach shaped by uncertainty about Russian mobilization and fears of a two-front struggle. Planning to pass through neutral Belgium created legal and diplomatic issues that could not be solved through arithmetic alone.

French planning also evolved from defensive concentration to Plan XVII, which provided for mobilization and offensive possibilities in response to German actions, while Russian planners debated how to distribute strength against Austria-Hungary and Germany. Austria-Hungary faced the additional difficulty of contemplating a limited Balkan conflict while preparing for the possibility of Russian intervention. No staff possessed reliable knowledge of an adversary's intentions, and estimates of enemy strength could become self-reinforcing. The analytical task is to show how plans expanded or restricted available choices without pretending that the plans themselves decided whether political leaders wanted war. The events in Chapter 108 must therefore be understood as decisions under pressure rather than the automatic execution of a script.

Fashoda and the Transformation of Anglo-French Relations

The imperial histories of Africa and Asia belong to Chapters 104 and 105. This division examines only how imperial disputes altered diplomacy within Europe. The first example is Fashoda. In July 1898 a small French expedition under Jean-Baptiste Marchand, having crossed central Africa, reached Fashoda on the upper Nile in what is now South Sudan, hoping to establish a French claim that might force a wider negotiation over Egypt. In September, after the Anglo-Egyptian victory at Omdurman on September 2, a British force under General Herbert Kitchener arrived at Fashoda and insisted on British and Egyptian rights. For some weeks, the two governments exchanged firm statements, and the press in both countries was bellicose.

The crisis ended in early November 1898 when France withdrew. French leaders recognized British naval superiority and the weakness of their local position, and they did not wish to confront Britain while the Dreyfus Affair divided France. A declaration of March 1899 then delineated spheres in the Nile and Congo watersheds. Fashoda is significant because it shows that imperial rivalry could bring great powers close to confrontation, and because its resolution helped make later cooperation possible. Foreign Minister Théophile Delcassé, who drew the lesson that France should settle colonial disputes with Britain and concentrate on the continental threat, became a key architect of the Entente Cordiale. Rivalry and rapprochement were not opposites. The settlement of one crisis could begin a new relationship.

Confrontation, settlement, and changing diplomatic calculation

The French expedition to the Upper Nile was the product of more than a distant geographic ambition. French policymakers wanted an east-west imperial connection, while British authorities viewed the Nile's waters and approaches as indispensable to their position in Egypt and Sudan. These aspirations intersected at an outpost where neither side could assume that local possession would settle the matter. The movement of the British-led force under Horatio Kitchener followed military victory over the Mahdist state at Omdurman, itself part of a history of Sudanese sovereignty, resistance, and British-Egyptian intervention treated more fully in Chapter 104. The clash between Marchand's French mission and the British expedition therefore arose from overlapping imperial projects rather than a dispute over an empty dot on a map.

Decision-making in Paris involved the French army, foreign ministry, parliamentary politics, and concern that a confrontation with Britain could expose France to Germany. Britain also had reasons to avoid unnecessary war: a standoff in Sudan might threaten wider imperial administration, shipping, and relations with other powers. The crisis was serious, but negotiation and a calculated French withdrawal provided an exit. This result did not automatically produce Anglo-French friendship. French public opinion included bitterness, and the Dreyfus Affair sharpened divisions over military authority and national prestige. Britain likewise had no predetermined policy of alliance with France.

The eventual accommodation shows how diplomatic rivals could revalue their interests. After the crisis, governments could conclude that continuing competition for particular African territories was less useful than an agreement securing larger priorities. The 1899 arrangements about African spheres were stepping stones toward the 1904 entente, though each settlement had its own terms. Students should compare this case with Agadir: in both instances, governments tried to convert imperial disagreement into a negotiated distribution of influence. The settlements prevented a wider European war, but colonial populations had little capacity to shape the bargain. Diplomatic success for great powers and political justice for affected societies were not equivalent outcomes.

Section 107.10

The First Moroccan Crisis, 1905 to 1906

Morocco was an independent sultanate whose rulers faced financial pressure, internal revolt, and European interference. The Anglo-French agreement of 1904 recognized French predominance there, and France sought to extend influence over Moroccan administration. German leaders, who had commercial interests and objected to being excluded, saw an opportunity to test the new entente and perhaps to weaken it. On March 31, 1905, Emperor Wilhelm II landed at Tangier and declared support for the sultan's independence, a gesture that publicly challenged France.

The consequences were the opposite of what Berlin hoped. Pressure from Germany contributed to the resignation of Delcassé in June 1905, but France did not abandon its position, and Britain moved closer to France. At the Algeciras Conference, held from January to April 1906, delegates of thirteen states, including Morocco, adopted an act affirming Moroccan sovereignty and the principle of open commercial access, while assigning France and Spain a central role in policing the ports and creating a state bank. Germany found itself isolated, with only Austria-Hungary reliably supporting it, and Italy and others sided partly with France. The crisis encouraged Anglo-French staff conversations, strengthened ties within the entente, and fed German suspicions of encirclement. It also showed that conferences could still resolve disputes. Moroccan voices, though formally present, had little power to shape the outcome, a reminder that African sovereignty was subordinated to European bargaining.

Moroccan sovereignty and the competing claims of European powers

Morocco had its own ruler, court, political constituencies, and commercial relationships. European governments were deeply involved in Moroccan customs revenues and finance, but the sultanate was not yet a French protectorate when the crisis began. Sultan Abd al-Aziz confronted difficult questions of taxation, reform, and regional authority, and European pressure complicated his ability to negotiate with domestic opponents. It is therefore inadequate to treat the episode as merely a confrontation between Germany and France: Moroccan sovereignty was the object of rival external claims. German officials invoked the principle of the open door and equal commercial opportunity; French representatives sought administrative and security advantages that could translate informal predominance into direct intervention.

The Kaiser’s visit to Tangier in March 1905 deliberately raised the political stakes, yet German expectations about support from other powers proved too optimistic. The Algeciras Conference included a wider group of states whose positions did not correspond simply to the two later wartime alliances. Its General Act established arrangements for policing and the State Bank of Morocco while recognizing the formal sovereignty of the sultan. At the same time, the organization of police supervision by French and Spanish officers made the outcome an instrument of continuing European involvement. The apparent legal recognition of Moroccan independence therefore coexisted with new external controls, a combination familiar from other cases of informal empire.

The crisis also tested diplomacy inside the European governments. The resignation of French foreign minister Delcassé demonstrated that pressure could produce a political concession without accomplishing Germany's broader diplomatic purpose. British sympathy for France grew, but London still had to evaluate its obligations carefully. German officials could claim that the conference defended international procedure, while critics argued that public threats had isolated Berlin. These conflicting interpretations matter because governments often assessed a crisis not only by its immediate territorial results but by what it revealed about other states' reliability. A peaceful settlement could thus become the basis for sharper distrust even as it demonstrated that compromise remained possible.

Section 107.11

The Second Moroccan Crisis of 1911: Agadir and Great-Power Confrontation

In spring 1911, unrest around Fez led France to send troops to the Moroccan capital, effectively moving toward a protectorate. Germany's foreign secretary, Alfred von Kiderlen-Wächter, decided to demand compensation and on July 1, 1911 dispatched the gunboat Panther to the port of Agadir, claiming to protect German commercial interests. The move was intended as a bargaining signal. It alarmed Britain, which feared a German naval base on the Atlantic coast, and on July 21 Chancellor of the Exchequer David Lloyd George, speaking at the Mansion House, warned that Britain would not accept being treated as if it did not matter in matters affecting its interests.

Franco-German negotiations proceeded under this pressure, and a treaty of November 4, 1911 resolved the crisis. Germany accepted a French protectorate over Morocco, formalized in the Treaty of Fez in March 1912, while France ceded to Germany a large area of the French Congo to be added to Cameroon, with a smaller cession in the opposite direction. The settlement avoided war, but it left a bitter residue. German nationalists regarded the outcome as humiliating and criticized the government for appearing weak, while British leaders concluded that Germany had been willing to use force to achieve its goals. Anglo-French military and naval cooperation deepened. The crisis also demonstrated that nationalist public opinion in several states was now an active participant in diplomacy and could punish leaders who seemed to concede too much.

Gunboat diplomacy and negotiated compensation

Agadir revealed how colonial disputes could acquire a symbolic weight greater than their immediate economic value. Germany's decision to send a gunboat to a Moroccan Atlantic port was described officially as a measure to protect German interests, but officials also hoped it would generate leverage for territorial compensation elsewhere in Africa. The vessel did not constitute a force capable of conquering Morocco; its significance lay in signaling that Berlin would not accept French gains without negotiation. Such a signal could be misread in London, where policymakers feared that Germany might seek a permanent Atlantic base and thereby alter naval calculations. The crisis was therefore partly about competing predictions of intentions rather than only about territory on the ground.

Britain's reaction, including the widely discussed Mansion House speech by Chancellor of the Exchequer David Lloyd George in July 1911, signaled that major diplomatic humiliation of a partner would have consequences. Yet public speeches did not bind the British cabinet to particular military action. Within Germany, politicians and newspapers disagreed about how much compensation was required to preserve prestige, and some critics condemned their government for accepting too little. France likewise had constituencies concerned about the cost of a protectorate and about the danger of provoking rivals while deepening control over Moroccan institutions. Newspapers amplified perceived insults, but ministers retained opportunities to compromise.

The territorial bargain added land to German Kamerun through French concessions in Equatorial Africa, commonly described as Neukamerun, while German claims in Morocco were abandoned. This arrangement mattered to the peoples living in the transferred territories, whose political futures were discussed as if an exchange among governments could settle their authority. The agreement reduced the immediate risk of war and established the setting for the French protectorate, but relations between Britain and Germany did not immediately recover. Agadir is therefore a useful comparative exercise: a crisis could be defused through conventional negotiation and nonetheless teach every participant to prepare more seriously for future confrontation.

Section 107.12

Italy, the Ottoman Empire, and Mediterranean Competition

Italy had long hoped for colonial gains in North Africa, and the French position in Tunisia and the settlement over Morocco made Libya, the Ottoman provinces of Tripolitania and Cyrenaica, an attractive target. After agreements with France, Russia, and others that eased diplomatic risk, Italy issued an ultimatum and declared war on the Ottoman Empire on September 29, 1911. Italian forces occupied coastal towns, faced determined resistance from Ottoman officers and local fighters including the Sanusi order, and used aircraft for reconnaissance and bombing, among the first such uses in warfare. In 1912 Italy also occupied the Dodecanese islands in the Aegean and attacked the Dardanelles, which disrupted trade.

The Treaty of Ouchy, signed on October 18, 1912, ended the war through Ottoman troop withdrawal and related arrangements, while Italy asserted sovereignty over Libya; the public peace treaty did not itself contain a simple explicit Ottoman cession clause, and the sultan retained specified religious prerogatives. The Italian government promised to return the Dodecanese, but it did not, and Italy retained them until after the Second World War. The war's European consequences were large. It showed the weakness of Ottoman defenses and the unwillingness of the great powers to intervene, which encouraged the Balkan states to act while the empire was distracted. It also reminded Austria-Hungary that its Italian ally had Adriatic and Mediterranean ambitions that could conflict with Habsburg interests. The Libyan population, which resisted Italian rule for years, was not consulted.

The legal limits of the peace settlement

Libya's political geography complicated Italian expectations. Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and the territories to their south possessed local religious institutions, tribal networks, commercial routes, and leadership structures that did not disappear when Italian forces occupied coastal towns. Ottoman military withdrawal did not signify the surrender of every community's authority. Resistance inland survived beyond the diplomatic settlement, and Italian officers confronted formidable logistical difficulties in projecting power away from ports. The occupation thus illustrates a distinction central to Chapter 104 as well: a declaration of sovereignty, a treaty concerning a territory, and effective administration over its inhabitants are different developments.

The Treaty of Ouchy, also called the First Treaty of Lausanne, ended formal hostilities in October 1912 through deliberately indirect language. It did not provide a straightforward public treaty clause explicitly ceding Libya to Italian sovereignty. The associated arrangements facilitated Ottoman military withdrawal and Italian control while allowing the Ottoman sultan to retain a religious role connected with the caliphate. Italian authority was asserted through unilateral measures and actual military occupation, leaving room for controversy about the settlement's legal form. The parties also addressed islands seized by Italy in the Aegean; contemplated withdrawal was tied to implementation of the Libyan settlement, and Italy ultimately retained control rather than carrying out a prompt evacuation. Compressing this complex agreement into an uncomplicated territorial sale would misrepresent what negotiators signed.

The war had repercussions beyond North Africa. Ottoman naval weakness and competing claims in the Dodecanese affected how Balkan governments assessed the empire's capacity to defend its European provinces. Italy's membership in the Triple Alliance did not prevent friction with Austria-Hungary over Mediterranean ambitions, and British and French governments watched the fighting with concern for maritime routes. The Italian campaign therefore belonged simultaneously to an imperial history of conquest and a European diplomatic history of opportunities and risks. Its importance for this chapter is that it changed the strategic expectations of Balkan leaders just as they were discussing cooperation against the Ottoman Empire.

The Balkans after the Congress of Berlin: States, Frontiers, and National Claims

The Balkan Peninsula in 1878 was a mosaic of states, provinces, and populations rather than a set of ethnically uniform nations. Serbia, Montenegro, and Romania were independent, Bulgaria was an autonomous principality under Ottoman suzerainty, Eastern Rumelia was an autonomous Ottoman province until its union with Bulgaria in 1885, and Greece, independent since the 1830s, gained Thessaly in 1881. Bosnia-Herzegovina was nominally Ottoman but administered by Austria-Hungary, and much of Macedonia, Thrace, Albania, and Kosovo remained under direct Ottoman rule. Populations were mixed in religion and language. Orthodox Christians, Muslims, Catholics, and Jews lived beside Serbs, Bulgarians, Greeks, Albanians, Turks, Vlachs, Roma, and others. Villages and families often did not fit the neat categories that nationalist movements and foreign mapmakers imposed.

National movements competed over territories whose inhabitants were disputed. Serbian leaders looked toward Bosnia, Kosovo, and parts of Macedonia, and the idea of South Slav unity had varied forms, including Serbian-led and Croatian-led versions. Bulgarian nationalists remembered the large Bulgaria promised at San Stefano and sought Macedonia and Thrace. Greek leaders pursued the union of Greek-speaking and Orthodox populations in Macedonia, Epirus, the Aegean islands, and Crete, which sought union with Greece. Montenegro wished to expand. Romanian opinion sought Transylvania, then under Hungarian rule. Albanian activists, who began organizing in the League of Prizren in 1878, aimed first at defending Albanian lands from partition and later at autonomy and independence. Churches, schools, and armed bands, particularly in Macedonia, competed to shape identities, and the Ottoman state tried to maintain control through taxation, administration, and reform.

Great-power policies also mattered. Austria-Hungary sought to prevent Serbian expansion and kept influence in Belgrade through economic and political pressure, especially after the Obrenović dynasty fell in a military coup in 1903 and a Karađorđević king more friendly to Russia and France took the throne. Russia presented itself as protector of Orthodox Slavs, though its policy was cautious and shaped by Straits concerns. Britain, France, and Germany had financial and strategic interests, including railway concessions. Yet small states often pursued their own agendas, using great-power rivalry for support while sometimes defying their patrons.

Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, and Montenegro were independent or autonomous political actors, but their freedom of action was shaped by great-power policy and fiscal limits. Rulers and ministries had to consider their armies, public sentiment, and the possibility that diplomatic patrons would not support a new war. Albanians articulated claims that did not fit the objectives of neighboring states, while Ottoman reformers attempted to preserve imperial citizenship and administrative authority across religious communities. The fact that multiple movements described the same land as part of their national homeland helps explain why the withdrawal of Ottoman rule could create additional conflicts rather than completing a peaceful settlement. Political geography, not ethnic destiny, must organize the section's analysis.

In Macedonia, rival national movements pursued schools, churches, and access to village communities. A family might use one language at home and a different language in church or commerce; identities could be situational, and claims reported by nationalist organizations were not necessarily disinterested demographic counts. The Bulgarian Exarchate, Greek patriarchal institutions, Serbian educational networks, and Ottoman administration all shaped local political life. Armed bands entered these contests, sometimes demanding affiliation under threat. Such activity was not simply the awakening of fixed ethnic groups waiting to establish states. It involved persuasion, financing, violence, administrative opportunities, and the pressures of a region contested by surrounding governments.

A historical map of the Balkans requires several layers. One layer shows international frontiers established by treaty; another shows the institutions exercising civil administration; a third might represent military occupation, ecclesiastical jurisdictions, or spheres of diplomatic pressure. The separation is especially important in 1878, when Ottoman suzerainty over Bulgaria, provincial autonomy in Eastern Rumelia, and Austro-Hungarian administration in Bosnia-Herzegovina were not equivalent forms of authority. Later maps that color entire areas by twentieth-century national states risk giving students the false impression that the 1878 legal settlement already anticipated later borders. Even accurate outlines of states cannot disclose the mixed linguistic, religious, and local loyalties within them.

The Bosnian Annexation Crisis of 1908 to 1909

Since 1878 Austria-Hungary had administered Bosnia-Herzegovina under the authority of the Congress of Berlin, while the sultan remained the nominal sovereign. The occupation had met armed resistance in its first months, and Habsburg officials then governed the provinces through a joint ministry, introduced schools, roads, and railways, and tried to manage tensions among the Orthodox, Muslim, and Catholic communities, which included Serbs, Bosniaks, and Croats in roughly comparable but unequal numbers. Many Serbs in the provinces and in Serbia regarded Habsburg rule as foreign and obstructive.

The Young Turk Revolution of July 1908, discussed in Section 107.15, raised the possibility that the Ottoman government would reassert claims over the provinces, and Austro-Hungarian Foreign Minister Alois Lexa von Aehrenthal decided to annex them. He met the Russian foreign minister Alexander Izvolsky at Buchlau in mid September 1908, and the two discussed a possible exchange involving Russian interests in the Straits, though their understandings about timing and conditions were unclear and later disputed. On October 5, Bulgaria proclaimed full independence under Prince Ferdinand, now tsar, and on October 6 Austria-Hungary announced the annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina, while withdrawing from the Sanjak of Novi Pazar.

The reactions were strong. Serbia, which had hoped for eventual union with the provinces, mobilized part of its forces and organized protests, and nationalist associations such as the Narodna Odbrana were formed. The Ottomans declared a boycott of Austrian goods and eventually accepted financial compensation in an agreement of early 1909. Izvolsky, who had not obtained support for Straits revision from the other powers and who was criticized at home, protested against the violation of the Treaty of Berlin. Britain and France supported a conference, but Germany backed its ally. In March 1909, Berlin pressed Russia to accept the annexation, and Russia, still recovering from war and revolution, yielded. Serbia declared on March 31 that it would accept the new situation and live in good relations with Austria-Hungary.

The crisis ended peacefully but poisoned relations. Serbia and Russia felt humiliated, and Russian leaders concluded that Russia could not again retreat. Austria-Hungary gained territory but not Serbian acceptance, and some Habsburg officials, notably chief of staff Conrad, concluded that Serbia needed to be defeated. German support for Vienna during the crisis made Berlin appear to be tied to Habsburg Balkan policy, and the Anglo-Russian relationship was strengthened. Habsburg authorities in Bosnia later granted a constitution and provincial diet in 1910, yet the annexation left the provinces' political status and loyalties a continuing source of tension.

The crisis exposed how reputations could become strategic resources. Serbia's leaders opposed annexation but lacked the capacity to defeat Austria-Hungary alone. Russia had suffered military defeat by Japan and internal upheaval, and its leadership judged that an immediate confrontation would be dangerous. Germany's support strengthened Vienna's bargaining position, helping bring the crisis to an end without a general war. The settlement left grievances and sharpened suspicions, yet the existence of a negotiated end remains historically important: the powers did not simply march from one crisis toward the next without agency. Students should ask how humiliation affected later judgments without assuming that every participant subsequently became committed to war.

Foreign Minister Aehrenthal's diplomatic calculations included exchanges with Russian Foreign Minister Alexander Izvolsky, whose wish for favorable treatment of Russian interests in the Straits became entangled with the Bosnian question. Accounts of their discussions at Buchlau differ in important details, and the absence of a jointly agreed written understanding limited what either side could credibly claim. Bulgaria's declaration of independence in October 1908 formed part of the same period of rapid changes to the Berlin settlement. Austria-Hungary also withdrew its troops from the Sanjak of Novi Pazar, an area that had remained under Ottoman civil administration. It would be inaccurate to describe the Sanjak as territory that Vienna had annexed or administered like Bosnia-Herzegovina.

For three decades the Habsburg state invested in Bosnia-Herzegovina's roads, towns, policing, and public institutions while the Ottoman sultan retained nominal sovereignty. The region's Muslim, Orthodox Christian, Catholic, and Jewish communities did not respond to the administration as a single collective actor. Some individuals benefited from schools or government employment; others resisted taxation, land relations, or the ambitions of officials. Political associations increasingly developed around confessional and national identities, but they operated within a legal order that differed from either Austrian or Hungarian parliamentary government. This situation made the act of annexation consequential beyond a change of label: it sought to transform a temporary international authorization into a permanent dynastic claim.

Occupation, formal title, and international legitimacy

Historical Vocabulary
Occupation, Annexation, and Sovereignty

Occupation is the military and administrative control of territory without the transfer of formal ownership. Annexation is the formal act by which a state declares a territory to be part of itself. Sovereignty is supreme legal authority over a territory. After 1878 Austria-Hungary occupied and administered Bosnia-Herzegovina, but the Ottoman sultan remained the sovereign. In 1908 Austria-Hungary annexed the provinces, changing the legal position without the consent of the signatories of the Treaty of Berlin. The difference mattered because the occupation had been authorized by an international congress and could be defended as temporary, whereas annexation was a unilateral change that appeared to defy the settlement and to close the door on Serbian hopes. Confusing the two obscures why the crisis of 1908 was so serious.

Section 107.15

Macedonia, Albania, the Young Turks, and the Formation of the Balkan League

Macedonia, still Ottoman, was the most contested region in the Balkans. Its Bulgarian, Greek, Serbian, Albanian, Turkish, Vlach, and Jewish inhabitants lived in a patchwork, and rival churches, schools, and armed bands sought to win loyalties. The Internal Macedonian Revolutionary Organization, founded in 1893, aimed at autonomy and staged the Ilinden uprising in August 1903, which Ottoman forces crushed with heavy civilian suffering. Austria-Hungary and Russia then sponsored the Mürzsteg reform program, which placed foreign officers in the police but did not solve the underlying conflict. A meeting between Edward VII and Nicholas II at Reval in June 1908 raised fears of further great-power intervention.

In July 1908 officers and officials associated with the Committee of Union and Progress, the Young Turks, forced Sultan Abdülhamid II to restore the constitution of 1876. They promised equality, representative government, and modernization, and the revolution was initially welcomed by many Christians and Muslims. But the new government also pursued centralization and a more assertive Ottoman identity, and a counterrevolutionary attempt in April 1909 led to the sultan's deposition. Policies on taxation, disarmament, schooling, and language alienated many Albanians, who rose in revolts between 1910 and 1912, and Balkan Christians who had hoped for autonomy. The new regime strengthened the Ottoman state, but it did not satisfy the neighbors' ambitions, and it feared that reform could succeed only if Balkan rivals acted soon.

The Balkan League emerged in 1912, aided by Russian diplomats who preferred a defensive coalition but who underestimated the offensive aims of its members. A Serbian-Bulgarian treaty of March 1912 included a secret annex dividing Macedonian territory, with a contested zone to be arbitrated by Russia. A Greek-Bulgarian agreement followed in May, and Montenegro reached understandings with the others. The League members shared the goal of expelling the Ottomans from Europe, but they disagreed deeply about how to divide the spoils, and that disagreement was built into the alliance from the start. The Italo-Ottoman War, the Albanian revolts, and Ottoman political turmoil offered an opportunity, and the great powers urged restraint without effective power to impose it.

Competing programs within a changing Ottoman order

The Young Turk Revolution began with officers, officials, and civilian networks who opposed autocratic government and demanded restoration of constitutional rule. Many welcomed the initial promise of equal citizenship, but the question of what equality meant in a multinational empire proved contentious. Advocates of centralization feared that extensive local autonomy would encourage disintegration; Albanian, Armenian, Arab, Greek, and other activists had different expectations of provincial rights, language policy, and representation. The Committee of Union and Progress was itself not an unchanging, unanimous organization. Ottoman domestic politics after 1908 should therefore be understood as a struggle over how the empire might be renewed, not merely as a prelude to its Balkan defeat.

In Macedonia, consular agents and national committees from neighboring states monitored the work of schools and churches. Violence by armed bands had roots before 1908, and temporary hopes for a constitutional settlement did not eliminate rival territorial objectives. Albanian politics also cannot be treated as simply an auxiliary to the Serbian or Greek national movements. Leaders from different religious and regional backgrounds sought recognition, schools, language rights, and forms of self-government, sometimes working through Ottoman constitutional institutions and sometimes resisting central policies. Their political decisions mattered directly when neighboring states prepared to divide the remaining Ottoman provinces.

The Balkan League was neither a single coherent federation nor a simple implementation of Russia's wishes. Serbia and Bulgaria negotiated secret provisions about Macedonia that left disputed areas to possible arbitration, while Bulgaria and Greece found cooperation useful despite unresolved claims. Montenegro shared opposition to Ottoman authority but had separate territorial goals and political dynamics. Diplomatic sponsors hoped the arrangement would stabilize matters or strengthen leverage, while member governments increasingly saw the possibility of taking territory by force. It is significant that the league could win a war together yet collapse when participants had to decide who would govern the conquests. The tension between common military interests and incompatible political settlements is central to the two subsequent sections.

Section 107.16

The First Balkan War, 1912 to 1913: Conquest and the Ottoman Retreat

Montenegro declared war on the Ottoman Empire on October 8, 1912, and Serbia, Bulgaria, and Greece followed within days. Many Balkan sources use the Julian calendar, which in this period ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian, so dates in Balkan records often appear earlier than the dates used here, and students should check which system a source uses. The allied armies, mobilized quickly and well supplied by their standards, faced Ottoman forces weakened by the war with Italy, internal unrest, and poor mobilization of reserves. Serbian forces defeated the Ottomans at Kumanovo in late October and later at Monastir, while Bulgarian forces won at Kirk Kilisse and Lule Burgas and pressed toward the Çatalca lines protecting Constantinople. Greek forces took Salonica in early November, entering the city only hours before Bulgarian troops arrived, and Greece also besieged Ioannina. Montenegrin forces besieged Scutari.

An armistice in December opened a conference of belligerents in London, but a coup in Constantinople in January 1913 led by Enver and other Young Turk officers resumed the war in February. Adrianople (Edirne) fell to Bulgarian and Serbian forces in March, Ioannina fell to Greece, and Scutari surrendered to Montenegro in April, though great-power pressure later forced Montenegro to leave it. Disease, especially cholera, swept through armies and refugee columns. Meanwhile the Conference of Ambassadors in London, chaired by Edward Grey, negotiated on Albania and borders. Austria-Hungary and Italy insisted on an independent Albania, which would deny Serbia access to the Adriatic. Albanian leaders, led by Ismail Qemali, had declared independence at Vlorë on November 28, 1912, and the powers eventually established a principality.

The Treaty of London of May 30, 1913 ended the war. The Ottomans surrendered nearly all of their European territory west of a line from Enos to Midia and ceded Crete, leaving the future of Albania and the Aegean islands to the great powers. The treaty did not divide the conquered land among the allies, and that omission proved decisive. The war showed that the Balkan states could defeat the Ottoman army and that great powers, though they influenced outcomes, could not simply dictate them. It also tested great-power relations, since Austria-Hungary and Russia undertook partial military measures during the winter of 1912 to 1913, yet their governments, with German and British encouragement, avoided a general conflict. Cooperation at the London conference showed that diplomatic management was still possible.

Military success and the limits of coalition unity

The opening operations of the First Balkan War unfolded over several fronts. Bulgarian forces advanced in Thrace toward the approaches to Constantinople, while Serbian troops moved through Kosovo and Macedonia and Greek forces fought toward Thessaloniki. Montenegro's campaigns included operations around Scutari, known today as Shkodër, where diplomatic controversies developed over the future Albanian state. The outcome depended on logistics, the condition of the Ottoman army, coordination among the allies, and the ability to hold captured towns, not simply on national enthusiasm. Ottoman commanders faced organizational problems and uneven readiness after years of political upheaval, yet Ottoman defense at Çatalca and in fortified positions demonstrated continued military capacity.

Naval operations also affected the war. Greece's fleet limited Ottoman movement across the Aegean and supported its campaign to secure islands and coastal routes. The fighting around Edirne and the deployment of artillery and siege forces showed how modern armies combined rail transport, trenches, heavy guns, and supply systems. The war thus connected Balkan national ambitions to the industrial military capacities analyzed earlier in the chapter. It also placed heavy demands on soldiers and civilians, including requisitioning, disrupted harvests, disease, and the care of the wounded. Victory in a field battle did not mean that a government could immediately establish accepted administration over its new territory.

In London, diplomatic negotiations took place alongside decisions by armies still in the field. Great-power representatives sought to manage the future of Albania and the Aegean and to prevent a local war from drawing their states into direct conflict. The declaration of Albanian independence in November 1912 did not settle the country's boundaries, which remained vulnerable to neighboring claims and external supervision. The Treaty of London ended the war between the Ottoman Empire and its opponents, but its dispositions and exclusions did not allocate every contested Macedonian district among the victors. That unresolved problem was fundamental. The military coalition had achieved its shared objective of reducing Ottoman territorial control, but it lacked a universally accepted plan for distributing the benefits.

The Second Balkan War of 1913 and the Territorial Settlement

The Second Balkan War was a separate conflict with different combatants and aims, and it should not be treated as the continuation of the first. Its origin lay in the division of Macedonia. Bulgaria, which had borne the heaviest fighting at Çatalca and Adrianople, felt entitled to most of the region, while Serbia, denied an Adriatic outlet by the creation of Albania, demanded revision of the 1912 treaty, and Greece held Salonica and a large part of southern Macedonia. In May and June 1913 Serbia and Greece formed an alliance against Bulgarian claims, and Russian attempts to arbitrate failed. Bulgarian leaders, confident in their army and hoping to settle matters by force, ordered attacks on Serbian and Greek positions in the night of June 29 to 30, 1913.

The gamble failed. Serbian and Greek forces held and counterattacked, Romania, which wanted compensation in Southern Dobruja for Bulgaria's growth, invaded Bulgaria in July, and the Ottomans reoccupied Adrianople. Montenegro joined the anti-Bulgarian side. Bulgaria sued for peace. The Treaty of Bucharest of August 10, 1913 gave most of Vardar Macedonia to Serbia, southern Macedonia, including Salonica and Kavala, to Greece, and Southern Dobruja to Romania, leaving Bulgaria a smaller portion of Macedonia and a narrow Aegean coastline. The Treaty of Constantinople of September 29, 1913 confirmed the Ottoman recovery of Adrianople and eastern Thrace, and a treaty with Greece followed in November. Serbia and Greece grew substantially, by estimates of roughly four fifths and two thirds in area respectively, though figures vary by source. Bulgaria, humiliated, bore a lasting sense of national catastrophe.

The settlement mattered for great-power relations. Austria-Hungary considered action against a stronger Serbia in the summer of 1913, but Germany and Italy did not support it, and Vienna backed down. In October 1913 Austria-Hungary issued an ultimatum demanding Serbian withdrawal from Albanian territory, and Serbia complied. Russian leaders, seeing Serbia as a client that must be sustained, committed further to its defense in diplomatic terms. The wars therefore strengthened Serbia while hardening Habsburg fears of it.

Changes in territory had different meanings for governments and inhabitants. A new boundary could alter taxes, conscription, schools, property claims, religious administration, and the language of official communication. Some residents found their national preferences better represented; others became minorities or refugees under a new state. The importance of these consequences explains why later narratives that list only the gains of Serbia, Greece, Bulgaria, and Romania are incomplete. The settlement ended fighting among governments but did not end every political grievance. Yet it also showed that Balkan states and great powers could still agree to territorial terms after war, a reminder that the outcome of future crises remained dependent on negotiations and decisions.

Romania was not a member of the original Balkan League, and its entry into the second war demonstrated that the conflict was not merely a dispute among former allies. Its government sought changes to its southern frontier, while Ottoman forces reoccupied territory in eastern Thrace, including Edirne. Bulgaria therefore confronted opponents along several fronts. The Treaty of Bucharest of August 10, 1913 assigned most of Vardar Macedonia to Serbia, southern Macedonia including Thessaloniki and Kavala to Greece, and Southern Dobruja to Romania. Bulgaria retained a smaller portion of Macedonia and access to the Aegean through western Thrace. A separate settlement at Constantinople addressed the renewed Ottoman-Bulgarian frontier. These treaties must be distinguished from one another in chronology and on historical maps.

The disagreements after the First Balkan War arose partly because wartime occupation differed from the allocations anticipated in secret prewar understandings. Serbia argued that the creation of an Albanian state deprived it of desired Adriatic access and therefore justified compensation in Macedonia. Bulgaria regarded previous agreements and the scale of its own sacrifices as evidence of a stronger claim. Greece emphasized control of Thessaloniki, communication routes, and districts its army had occupied. The same region could be described as essential for economic access, a national homeland, or a reward for military expenditure by different governments. Arbitration was discussed, but the political cost of compromise rose while armies still occupied the disputed areas.

Who experienced greater continuity? Great-power diplomats and many rural families whose livelihoods continued in much the same form. Section 107.18

Civilians, Displacement, Violence, and the Human Consequences of the Balkan Wars

The Balkan Wars were fought by mass conscript armies in regions inhabited by civilians of many faiths, and the civilian cost was heavy. Sources include reports by war correspondents, relief workers, and diplomats, and a commission of inquiry established by the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, whose report appeared in 1914. The commission documented atrocities attributed to armies and irregular bands of all the belligerents, though its findings were criticized and defended by partisans. Such sources must be read critically, since national governments and newspapers had incentives to emphasize the crimes of enemies and conceal their own.

Many Muslim civilians fled or were expelled from districts conquered by the Balkan states, and Christians and Jews also suffered depending on where armies passed. Villages were burned, populations were coerced to convert or leave, and local authority changed hands several times. Cholera and other diseases killed many soldiers and civilians, and refugee camps around Constantinople and Salonica were overcrowded. Estimates of the number of displaced people run to several hundred thousand, and estimates of total deaths from combat, disease, and violence against civilians vary widely and remain uncertain. Historians should avoid treating any single figure as settled. In some places, new states promised legal equality but practiced discrimination, and later population exchanges and minority treaties, examined in Chapter 113, grew out of attempts to manage these legacies.

The wars also changed the lives of those who stayed. Landholding, schooling, church authority, and language policy shifted when new administrations took control, and in Macedonia and Thrace families faced demands for new loyalties. Women managed farms and households while men served, and many served as nurses or relief workers. The wars influenced public opinion across Europe, since newspapers, photographs, and early film brought images of war and refugees to wide audiences. They also left a memory of violence that later Balkan and European conflicts, including those discussed in Chapter 135, would revisit.

Women, children, and older people often had to manage household survival during mobilization and displacement. Their experiences are harder to reconstruct from official military correspondence than the activities of generals. Relief records, letters, oral histories, local property claims, and population registries can partially rebalance the picture, although documents may survive unevenly across languages and archives. A classroom composite household can help students connect taxation, safety, labor, language policy, and migration, provided it is explicitly identified as editorial reconstruction rather than an authentic witness. The broader point is that maps of territorial gains are also maps of people whose lives were reorganized by political decisions they rarely controlled.

Experiences varied widely. Some households fled when armed forces entered a district; others remained but faced requisitions, confiscation of animals, interrupted trade, or separation of family members. The destruction of fields or a market could produce hardship long after military operations moved away. People could become displaced by fear, by forced removal, or by the loss of means of survival, and such experiences should not automatically be combined into one statistical category. Muslim inhabitants of territories leaving Ottoman control were especially affected by migration and expulsion, but Christian populations and other groups also experienced violence in particular localities. Historians should not use the suffering of one community to deny that of another.

Contemporary observers recorded destruction, flight, and attacks on civilians, but they did not have unrestricted access to all theaters. The Carnegie Endowment's international commission published its report in Washington in 1914 after collecting testimony and examining the conduct of several armies and communities. Its chapter on war and the noncombatant population provides important evidence for patterns of intimidation, burning, and coercion, while its methods and political context require critical reading. Diplomats, military officers, journalists, and relief workers generated other records with different audiences and incentives. Students should compare a claimed incident across sources before using a general report to make a precise claim about an individual village or a total number of deaths.

From the perspective of Muslim villagers in Macedonia and Thrace: The arrival of armies of Christian states meant the loss of security and often of homes. Many fled toward Ottoman lines, and those who remained faced pressure to leave, convert, or accept subordinate status, while the Ottoman defeat ended a political order they had known.
From the perspective of Albanian communities: The war threatened partition among neighboring states, and independence in 1912 was both a victory and a source of anxiety, since the new frontiers left large Albanian populations outside the state and the powers decided the principality's borders.
From the perspective of the Jewish community of Salonica: Jews formed a large community, by many estimates the largest single group in the city, and had prospered under Ottoman rule. Greek administration brought uncertainty about commerce, language, and religious life, and some residents emigrated.
From the perspective of great-power diplomats: The wars were a dangerous challenge to the territorial status quo and to their influence, and they worked to contain the conflict while protecting their own interests, showing both the possibilities and limits of great-power management.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Displaced Balkan Household

Consider a composite household, assembled from patterns reported in contemporary accounts and not from a single named family. A farming family in a Macedonian village might include grandparents, a married couple, and several children, working fields of wheat, tobacco, or vines and keeping sheep or goats. When fighting approached, men of military age were conscripted or fled, and women, children, and elders faced the choice of leaving with what they could carry or staying under the new authority. Carts, oxen, bedding, tools, and food were loaded in haste, and families traveled along muddy roads toward towns, ports, or camps, often through cold autumn weather and with outbreaks of disease nearby.

Arrival brought other burdens. Refugees depended on relatives, religious charities, municipal authorities, and foreign relief workers for shelter and food, and they struggled to prove land claims left behind. Children missed schooling, and widows faced poverty. Language, religion, and local custom shaped how communities received them, and some were treated with sympathy while others met suspicion. Whether a family could ever return depended on which army controlled the village and on policies of the new government. The wider point is that national frontiers redrawn on a map became lived experience in the movement of ordinary households.

Section 107.19

Diplomacy, Armaments, and the Remaining Choices, 1913 to June 1914

The Balkan Wars altered every capital's calculations. In Vienna, officials concluded that a larger Serbia, backed by Russia, threatened the cohesion of a multinational monarchy, and some, including Conrad, advocated a decisive reckoning. Others, including the Hungarian prime minister István Tisza, feared that war would add more Slavs to the monarchy and upset its internal balance. Romania, which had joined the Triple Alliance secretly in 1883 but whose public opinion sympathized with Romanians in Transylvania, drifted away from Vienna. In Belgrade, leaders celebrated expansion but also faced instability in newly acquired territories and a powerful nationalist press. In St. Petersburg, military leaders and ministers believed Russia must support Serbia and prepare for war, supported by the Great Military Programme, while others warned that Russia was not ready.

In Germany, army increases of 1913 reflected fear of a Franco-Russian military buildup, and some leaders believed that Russia's strength would grow so rapidly that time favored Germany's enemies, an argument sometimes called preventive reasoning. Contemporary evidence for German intentions is contested, and a meeting held by Wilhelm II with military leaders in December 1912, often called a war council, has been interpreted in very different ways. France passed the Three-Year Law, and Raymond Poincaré, president from 1913, pursued firm cooperation with Russia. Britain focused on its fleet and its commitments to France while seeking to avoid being drawn into continental conflict.

Yet diplomacy also continued. The Liman von Sanders affair of December 1913 to January 1914, in which a German general was appointed to command Ottoman troops near Constantinople, alarmed Russia, which feared German influence over the Straits, and was resolved by a compromise on his rank. Britain and Germany negotiated over the Baghdad Railway and the future of Portuguese colonies, and an agreement was close by the summer of 1914. The Conference of Ambassadors in London had shown how great-power consultation could work. Some contemporaries, including diplomats and financiers, believed that commercial interdependence and shared interests made a major war unlikely. The system therefore contained both pressures toward conflict and habits of compromise, and the balance between them depended on decisions made by particular officials. The diplomatic condition of Europe in June 1914 was tense but not yet determined.

Even amid suspicion, diplomatic channels remained open. The Liman von Sanders dispute over German military advisers in the Ottoman Empire was addressed through adjustments of status and command arrangements, avoiding an immediate confrontation with Russia. Britain and Germany continued negotiations on colonial questions and the Baghdad Railway, illustrating practical cooperation despite the naval rivalry. The ambassadorial conference process associated with the Balkan settlements likewise demonstrated that governments could convene to settle disputes when they judged compromise necessary. These examples do not erase aggressive policies or unresolved grievances; they prevent an account in which every year mechanically removed the possibility of peace. The condition of Europe in June 1914 was dangerous precisely because political alternatives still existed but could be rejected under pressure.

Some ministers and officers reasoned that a war postponed might be harder to win if an adversary's military modernization continued. Others feared that rash action would expose their own state to defeat, revolution, or unwanted imperial losses. Preventive reasoning is an important historical concept, but it should be demonstrated through specific memoranda and statements rather than attributed automatically to every government. Press rhetoric and nationalist mobilization sometimes raised the political cost of concession. Yet the capacity to persuade cabinets, impose taxes, and obtain allies remained variable. A general could urge action without obtaining an immediate governmental decision, and an armament program could be a signal of deterrence as well as of possible offensive capability.

Military expenditure after 1912 increased in several states, but appropriations and planned formations should not be confused with completed fighting strength. The French three-year service law of 1913 aimed to increase available trained manpower, provoking political argument over cost, liberty, and national defense. Germany's army legislation of 1913 enlarged the intended peacetime army and required financial arrangements that generated parliamentary controversy. Russian officials discussed further improvements in forces and railway capacity, yet projects under consideration were not the same as troops and trains ready for use by June 1914. Austrian and Italian planners likewise had to reconcile military wishes with finances and parliamentary or dynastic constraints. These differences altered perceptions about the balance of strength in the future, not just the present.

Section 107.20

Europe on the Eve of War: Alliances, Nationalism, and the Limits of Diplomacy

By June 1914 Europe was divided into two loosely connected groupings, the Triple Alliance and the Franco-Russian alliance supplemented by British understandings, with extensive armies and navies, rival plans, and numerous sources of friction. Nationalism, examined in Chapter 103, shaped press campaigns, popular expectations, and the politics of the Balkans and the Habsburg lands. Economic interdependence remained strong, and international cooperation in fields such as postal services, law, and science continued. Few governments sought a general European war, yet several were prepared to risk one over interests they considered vital, and misjudgments about what rivals would tolerate, what allies would support, and how quickly events would move were widespread.

A careful account distinguishes structural pressures from political decisions. Structural pressures included alliance commitments with ambiguous obligations, military plans that rewarded speed, naval and land arms competition, fiscal strain, nationalist politics, and the weakness of the Ottoman and Habsburg states. Political decisions included the choices of ministers, monarchs, and generals about whether to threaten, bargain, or fight. Pressures narrowed options but did not dictate choices, which is why Chapter 108 must examine the decisions of July 1914 in detail.

This chapter's endpoint is analytically important. It has established a diplomatic world of overlapping commitments, unequal power, nationalist aspirations, contested imperial boundaries, mass armies, and functioning, if strained, procedures for negotiation. It has not established what governments would decide when confronted with an assassination, an ultimatum, or a perceived deadline. Those decisions must be reconstructed from the events and evidence of the July Crisis, not inserted into the years before it as though officials already knew the outcome. Volume IV closes its numbered chapter sequence with the European order still intact and still subject to human choices. Volume V begins with an examination of how that order was tested and ultimately broken.

Historians disagree about the weight of German decision-making, the objectives of Austria-Hungary, Russian ambitions, the instability of Balkan settlements, the significance of British policy, and the effects of military planning. These are not merely disputes about whose actions were most dramatic. They involve assessing secret documents, determining when officials acquired information, distinguishing public rhetoric from private judgment, and allowing for the possibility that actors changed their views. An interpretation based on the inevitability of war cannot readily explain why serious international crises in 1898, 1905–1906, 1908–1909, 1911, and 1912–1913 ended without a continental war. Conversely, successful past settlements do not prove that every subsequent crisis would have been resolved in the same way.

A convincing explanation of Europe's vulnerability requires attention to interaction rather than a ranking that automatically places one cause above every other. Alliances could deter aggression when their members seemed reliable, but they could also produce fear that a limited quarrel might involve a partner. Armaments could reflect defensive preparation but encourage an adversary to increase its own forces. National claims could provide genuine representation for some communities while threatening the security of populations who rejected a proposed border. Diplomats could settle a crisis and later remember that settlement as humiliating, making them more reluctant to concede again. The same development therefore had different possible consequences depending on subsequent choices.

Causes, evidence, and the problem of hindsight

Historical Debate

Why Did Europe Approach War?

The question: Historians agree on many facts about alliances, armaments, and crises, but disagree about which factors best explain why Europe became so vulnerable to a general war, and how far particular governments bore responsibility.

Interpretation A: Systemic and shared explanations. Some historians stress the structure of the international system, including alliance entanglements, arms races, imperial rivalry, and miscalculation. In this view, no single government fully controlled events, and leaders in several capitals pursued risky policies. Christopher Clark's 2012 study, The Sleepwalkers, is a well-known example, emphasizing the multipolar character of the system and the autonomy of actors in the Balkans.
Interpretation B: Greater weight to specific German and Austro-Hungarian policies. Other historians, following the controversy launched by Fritz Fischer in the 1960s, argue that German leaders pursued expansionist aims and accepted the risk of war, and that Austro-Hungarian determination to crush Serbia was decisive. Later scholars, including Annika Mombauer, have refined and defended versions of this argument using evidence about German decision making.

Evidence: Both rely on diplomatic dispatches, military plans, memoranda, and memoirs, but they weigh them differently and disagree about the reliability of postwar publications. Many historians now blend the approaches, recognizing both systemic pressures and the particular responsibility of decision makers. This chapter concentrates on the period before the crisis, so students should return to the debate after reading Chapter 108.

Why it matters: How historians weigh structure and decision changes how one understands responsibility, the lessons drawn from 1914, and the later memory of the war, including the war guilt clause discussed in Chapter 113.

Student task: Identify evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, explain what additional evidence about decisions in 1914 would help, and evaluate which combination of explanations you find most persuasive.

Cause and Effect

The Diplomatic Road to War

Long-term causes

German unification, French grievance over Alsace-Lorraine, Austro-Russian rivalry in the Balkans, imperial competition, Ottoman weakness, and the growth of nationalism.

Short-term causes

The Moroccan crises, Anglo-German naval rivalry, the Bosnian annexation, the Italo-Ottoman War, and the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913, each increasing distrust.

Transformation

By 1914 two armed groupings, accelerating military programs, and unresolved Balkan disputes had made European diplomacy more brittle.

Immediate effects

Military expansion, a more assertive Serbia, a more anxious Austria-Hungary, Russian determination not to retreat again, and sharper Anglo-German suspicion.

Long-term consequences

The conditions examined here formed the setting for the crisis of July 1914, which Chapter 108 explains, and they shaped later debate about responsibility.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the result. Alliances were conditional, plans were contingent, crises were resolved, and decisions in 1914 still had to be made.

The chapter therefore ends before the assassination at Sarajevo on June 28, 1914. The treatment of that event, the ultimatum to Serbia, the mobilizations, and the declarations of war is the work of Chapter 108, which begins Volume V. What students should carry forward is a precise picture of the European order at that moment: dangerous, armed, and anxious, but not yet at war.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the European diplomatic order that emerged after 1871 became more vulnerable to war by 1914 without making war inevitable. The unification of Germany created a powerful central state, left France aggrieved over Alsace and Lorraine, and prompted Bismarck to build a network of agreements meant to protect the new empire and isolate France. The Dual Alliance of 1879 and the Triple Alliance of 1882 were defensive and conditional, and the Reinsurance Treaty of 1887 showed Bismarck's effort to keep Russia close even while Germany was bound to Austria-Hungary.

After Bismarck's departure in 1890, the failure to renew the Reinsurance Treaty helped open the way to the Franco-Russian alliance, a formal agreement with military commitments. Britain, long outside permanent continental ties, reconsidered its position after the South African War, concluded an alliance with Japan in 1902, and settled colonial disputes with France in 1904 and with Russia in 1907. The resulting Triple Entente was not a single treaty of mutual defense, and the difference between formal alliance, entente, and staff conversation is essential to understanding 1914.

Naval and military competition intensified distrust. Tirpitz's fleet legislation and the Dreadnought launched an Anglo-German naval race that pushed Britain toward France, while continental armies, conscription systems, railways, and general-staff plans increased the speed and scale of potential war. Historians disagree about how far plans constrained civilian leaders, but they agree that planning made delay seem dangerous and increased the destructiveness of any conflict.

Imperial rivalry repeatedly shaped European diplomacy. Fashoda led to Anglo-French settlement, the Moroccan crises of 1905 to 1906 and 1911 strengthened the entente and deepened German fears of encirclement, and the Italo-Ottoman War of 1911 and 1912 revealed Ottoman weakness. Each crisis ended in negotiation, yet each left residues of resentment, and public opinion increasingly shaped what leaders felt able to concede.

The Balkans were the most dangerous arena. After the Congress of Berlin, the region contained new states, unfinished national claims, mixed populations, and rival great-power interests. The Bosnian annexation of 1908 turned a long administration into a formal annexation, embittered Serbia and Russia, and tied Berlin more closely to Vienna. The Young Turk revolution, Albanian revolts, and the Macedonian question prepared the ground for the Balkan League, whose members acted on their own ambitions as much as on any patron's wishes.

The First Balkan War of 1912 and 1913 drove the Ottoman Empire out of nearly all its European territory, and the Second Balkan War of 1913 pitted Bulgaria against its former allies and Romania. The Treaties of London and Bucharest redrew the map, enlarged Serbia and Greece, created Albania, and left Bulgaria aggrieved. The wars were distinct conflicts with different aims, and they caused substantial civilian suffering, displacement, and violence, the scale of which remains uncertain in surviving estimates.

Between 1913 and June 1914, army expansion, fears about Russian strength, Austro-Hungarian anxiety about Serbia, and Russian determination to support Serbia increased tension. Yet the great powers also cooperated through the Conference of Ambassadors, and Britain and Germany came close to agreements on colonial and railway issues. European diplomacy was thus both brittle and capable of compromise.

The chapter's central argument is that structural pressures narrowed the range of peaceful options but did not dictate outcomes. Alliance obligations were conditional, plans were contingency documents, and earlier crises had been resolved. The assassination at Sarajevo, the July Crisis, and the decisions that produced a general war are the subject of Chapter 108, which begins Volume V.

Preparation of hypothetical operations and deployments, not itself proof that a government has decided to fight.

Overlapping demands for political control or territorial inclusion in areas where identities and loyalties are diverse.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains the situation Europe's leaders inherited in the summer of 1914. It shows that the diplomatic order of the preceding decades was neither a simple machine for war nor a stable system of peace. It combined real mechanisms for restraint, such as conferences, bargaining, and economic interdependence, with accumulating pressures, such as rival blocs, arms races, nationalist movements, and unresolved territorial claims.

The chapter also reveals how Europe's history linked regions. Crises in Morocco, Libya, Bosnia, and Macedonia connected Africa, the Mediterranean, and the Balkans to Berlin, Paris, London, Vienna, and St. Petersburg. The Balkan peoples were not merely objects of great-power policy, and their ambitions, alliances, and wars changed the map and the expectations of every capital. The suffering of civilians in 1912 and 1913 is a reminder that national projects had human costs well before 1914.

Within Volume IV, this chapter completes the movement from the revolutions and industrial transformations of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to a continent of mass politics, imperial rivalry, and armed alliances. Within Part XVI, it draws together the imperial, social, and political developments traced in Chapters 101 to 106 and shows their diplomatic consequences. It does not conclude the story of 1914, since the processes it describes continue into Volume V.

Students who move on to Chapter 108 should carry forward three habits of mind. They should distinguish the commitments of treaties from the decisions of governments, treat Balkan actors as historical agents, and resist both inevitability and the opposite assumption that nothing could have mattered. The chapters that follow examine how decisions made in July 1914 turned a dangerous order into a continental war.
