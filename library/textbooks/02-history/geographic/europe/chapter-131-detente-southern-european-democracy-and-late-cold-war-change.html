Chapter Opening

Between 1969 and 1983 Europe experienced treaties that eased old confrontations, dictatorships that gave way to elected governments, an economic shock that ended an age of confidence, and a renewed fear of nuclear war, all at the same time.

On August 1, 1975, representatives of thirty five states signed the Helsinki Final Act. Less than a year earlier, on April 25, 1974, Portuguese officers had overthrown a dictatorship that had lasted for decades. Between those two events lay the Greek junta's collapse over Cyprus, the oil embargo of 1973, and a wave of inflation that unsettled western European governments. Historians cannot tell this decade as a single story, because its developments pulled in different directions.

This chapter follows Chapter 130's account of 1968 and its social movements by asking how Europe's political and international order changed in the following years. It argues that the 1970s were neither simply the end of postwar prosperity nor the beginning of the Cold War's conclusion. Diplomatic accommodation, democratic expansion, economic insecurity, and renewed strategic competition developed together. Later outcomes, examined in Chapter 132, were not inevitable.

The evidence is unusually rich: treaty texts, constitutions, parliamentary records, central bank and statistical series, memoirs, press coverage, photographs, and the records of monitoring groups. Each reflects the purposes of its authors, and the chapter notes those limits. The chapter belongs to Part XIX and closes the Cold War narrative before the revolutions of 1989.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define détente and distinguish it from reconciliation between the blocs.
  2. Explain Ostpolitik and the treaties that changed relations between the two German states.
  3. Analyze the Helsinki Final Act as a politically binding, not legally enforceable, agreement.
  4. Compare the collapse of the dictatorships in Portugal, Greece, and Spain.
  5. Trace the stages of Portugal's revolutionary transition and Spain's constitutional reform.
  6. Assess the role of Cyprus in the fall of the Greek junta.
  7. Explain the causes and consequences of the oil shocks, stagflation, and industrial restructuring.
  8. Distinguish the political responses of Britain, West Germany, France, and Italy.
  9. Evaluate why détente eroded after 1979 and how the nuclear confrontation resumed.
  10. Connect democratization to European Community membership without treating membership as its cause.

Historical Context

By the late 1960s, the division of Germany (Chapter 124), the bloc alliances (Chapter 125), and the long boom in western Europe (Chapter 126) had become settled facts of life. The Berlin and Cuban crises had shown the risks of confrontation. The Prague Spring's suppression in 1968 (Chapter 129) had reminded Europeans of the limits of reform in the Soviet bloc, while the movements of 1968 (Chapter 130) had shown generational dissatisfaction in the West.

In the south, Portugal, Spain, and Greece remained under authoritarian rule while their economies modernized through emigration, tourism, and industrial growth. In the West, governments had built welfare states on the assumption of steady growth and full employment. All these assumptions were tested after 1969.

Division I · 1969–1975 · Sections 131.1–131.4

Détente and the Changing European Security Order

Détente reorganized diplomatic practice without removing the alliance division of Europe.

Section 131.1

From Confrontation to Détente

The Berlin and Cuban crises, the growth of nuclear arsenals, the Sino Soviet split, and economic costs persuaded leaders in Washington and Moscow that managing rivalry was prudent. Western European governments had their own interests in trade, in the German division, and in borders. Détente limited risk but did not end ideological competition, military modernization, or conflicts outside Europe.

The language of détente was born of danger rather than trust. After the Berlin crises and the Cuban missile confrontation of October 1962, leaders recognized that competitive displays of resolve could move more quickly than the mechanisms intended to control them. Nuclear arsenals were larger and more diversified by the end of the decade, and neither superpower could expect to escape catastrophic retaliation. The challenge was to reduce the likelihood of war without surrendering alliances, political principles, or overseas influence. European governments had particular reasons for seeking arrangements that would make crises less frequent. A military encounter in central Europe would put their territories and populations on the front line, whether or not the original dispute had begun in Europe.

The Soviet Union approached accommodation from a position of both military strength and economic constraint. Its leaders sought recognition of postwar European frontiers and the legitimacy of their security system. They also wished to reduce costly strategic uncertainty, secure access to technology and trade, and prevent their relationship with the United States from being dominated by the Sino-Soviet split. Washington pursued arms-control arrangements, a more manageable European balance, and diplomatic flexibility in an era when the Vietnam War consumed resources and public support. Neither government abandoned competition in Asia, Africa, or the Middle East. Détente operated alongside proxy conflicts, intelligence activity, military exercises, and sharply opposed public ideologies.

The interests of the western European allies were not identical to American policy. Paris guarded its strategic independence and its separate nuclear force, while remaining within the Western political community. Bonn needed a workable relationship with Moscow because German division and the Berlin question could not be addressed without Soviet agreement. Smaller European countries wanted stable borders, reduced military tension, and more opportunities for trade. Within NATO some officials feared that American–Soviet agreements might leave the continent exposed to conventional forces or intermediate-range missiles not covered by strategic limitations. Even an apparent improvement in superpower relations could therefore create new arguments over whose security was being negotiated and by whom.

Nor did détente remove uncertainty from the eastern European states. Communist governments could welcome cross-border trade and diplomacy while fearing the free movement of information and citizens. Greater contact with western Europe provided consumer goods, cultural influence, loans, and technologies, but it also made comparison easier and expanded discussion of rights and standards of government. The Soviet authorities wanted international acknowledgment of existing arrangements without allowing the political pluralism that some inhabitants associated with improved relations. Thus the same process could stabilize the division of Europe internationally and bring fresh ideological pressures to the governments maintaining that division internally.

Historians sometimes describe détente as a strategic bargain between two states; others give greater importance to European diplomacy, economic interdependence, and the political changes of the 1960s. These explanations need not be exclusive. Strategic fear created strong incentives to negotiate, but European initiatives supplied channels through which specific problems could be addressed. The period also illustrates the difference between intentions and outcomes. Agreements designed to stabilize frontiers or regulate communication could later become resources for activists who challenged the governments that had signed them. The essential historical question is not whether détente ended competition, which it did not, but what kinds of competitive behavior it constrained and what new possibilities it unintentionally opened.

Historical Vocabulary

Détente

The French term for relaxation, used for the negotiated management of East–West rivalry. It did not imply the disappearance of nuclear weapons, ideological conflict, or contested frontiers. Distinguish diplomacy between states from political freedom within them.

Section 131.2

Ostpolitik and German Relations

Brandt and Scheel sought practical improvements by accepting existing realities. The Moscow Treaty (August 12, 1970) and Warsaw Treaty (December 7, 1970) renounced force and addressed the postwar frontiers, the Four Power Agreement on Berlin (September 3, 1971) eased access and travel, and the Basic Treaty (December 21, 1972) established relations between the two German states. Both joined the United Nations in September 1973. Reunification and the Berlin Wall were untouched. At Warsaw in December 1970, Brandt knelt at the Ghetto Uprising memorial, linking memory to diplomacy.

Before Willy Brandt became chancellor in October 1969, West German governments had generally refused formal recognition of the German Democratic Republic and claimed a continuing responsibility for Germans on both sides of the border. Yet the Berlin Wall, the survival of the eastern state, and the entrenched Soviet military presence showed that diplomatic refusal could not change conditions on the ground. Brandt and Foreign Minister Walter Scheel approached the problem through practical agreements. Their strategy, often associated with the phrase change through rapprochement, sought better contacts among divided families and societies while maintaining the Federal Republic's long-term constitutional commitment to German unity. Recognition of existing political arrangements did not require endorsement of the political system governing East Germany.

The Moscow Treaty of August 12, 1970, committed West Germany and the Soviet Union to renounce force and respect existing European frontiers. The Warsaw Treaty of December 7 similarly addressed the Oder–Neisse frontier and the relationship with Poland. These measures were politically difficult in West Germany because the borders affected the histories and claims of Germans expelled or displaced after the Second World War. Opponents feared that recognizing frontiers would abandon German interests. Supporters argued that reconciliation with eastern neighbors and meaningful humanitarian improvements were impossible without confronting the territorial settlement of 1945. The treaties used careful diplomatic language in a situation where final German peace-settlement questions had not been extinguished by bilateral declarations.

Brandt's kneeling at the memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising during his December 1970 visit came to symbolize a second dimension of Ostpolitik: the willingness to confront Nazi crimes as part of restoring trust with neighbors. The gesture was not a substitute for treaty obligations, reparations, or political debate, and contemporaries interpreted it differently. Some saw it as necessary humility, others as excessive symbolism. Its lasting significance is that international relations were here conducted not only through strategic calculations but through contested public memories of wartime violence. West German diplomacy carried an ethical dimension shaped by the Holocaust and the occupation of Poland, even as negotiators dealt with concrete security and border problems.

The Four Power Agreement on Berlin was signed on September 3, 1971, and entered into force on June 3, 1972, after the relevant implementing arrangements. It sought to improve civilian transit to West Berlin and reduce recurring disputes over access, without treating West Berlin simply as another part of the Federal Republic. The Basic Treaty signed by the two German states on December 21, 1972, established practical relations and permanent representations, making cooperation possible across a border still reinforced by barriers and guards. In September 1973 both the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic entered the United Nations. These changes made division more manageable, not less real: political imprisonment, restrictions on emigration, and the Wall remained.

Ostpolitik affected ordinary people unevenly. More reliable transportation routes and negotiated visiting arrangements could mean an enormous difference to elderly parents, separated siblings, and people whose jobs or studies had been interrupted by German division. Other families still faced restrictions they could not overcome. East German leaders gained international recognition but worried about the political consequences of opening contacts. West German leaders gained a vocabulary for negotiation without conceding the legitimacy of repression. Students should distinguish three levels: the legal language of treaties, the decisions of administrations applying them, and the human experiences produced by those decisions. A treaty can establish a framework without guaranteeing every right that its supporters hope to advance.

There was also a domestic political struggle over who had authority to define the national interest. Critics of the treaties feared the abandonment of expelled Germans' claims and a permanent accommodation of division. Brandt's coalition survived sharp parliamentary controversy, including a constructive vote of no confidence in 1972 that failed to remove him. Later elections and treaty ratifications showed that Ostpolitik was not simply a diplomatic initiative imposed by a small circle of officials. It required democratic authorization amid genuine disagreement. The experience also demonstrates why policy continuity cannot be measured only by whether borders visibly move: new travel rules, representations, and procedures can change citizens' lives without redrawing a map.

Section 131.3

Arms Control and European Security

SALT I and the ABM Treaty (1972) limited specific systems, not nuclear competition itself. European NATO members welcomed reduced tension but feared that European concerns would be neglected. Talks on Mutual and Balanced Force Reductions began in 1973 to address conventional balances in central Europe, without a decisive result. Diplomacy managed rivalry, and did not end it.

Arms control became a visible sign of détente because it addressed the military systems that made confrontation particularly dangerous. The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks began in 1969 and produced agreements in 1972. The Interim Agreement placed ceilings on certain strategic ballistic-missile launchers, while the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty restricted defenses that might otherwise undermine the logic of mutual vulnerability. Neither eliminated nuclear weapons. The agreements excluded important categories of weapons and permitted modernization within or around the rules. Nevertheless, limitations, inspection-related expectations, and continuing negotiations encouraged governments to treat military predictability as a legitimate objective rather than a sign of weakness.

European security complicated these calculations. American and Soviet strategic weapons could strike across oceans, but European cities and military installations were vulnerable to shorter-range systems and large conventional forces. NATO's flexible-response doctrine left room for different levels of military escalation. Soviet military planners also considered the European theatre central to their alliance commitments. An agreement between Washington and Moscow over intercontinental launchers might therefore reassure some leaders while unsettling others. Western European governments asked whether an American president would risk the destruction of an American city to defend one in Europe. These concerns were not entirely new, but the appearance of strategic parity made them more prominent in alliance discussions.

Negotiations on Mutual and Balanced Force Reductions began in Vienna in 1973. Their objective was to address conventional military balances in central Europe, but participants disputed how forces should be counted, which territories were included, what reductions would be equitable, and how compliance could be verified. NATO and Warsaw Pact members had different organizational structures and geographical advantages, making apparently simple numerical parity difficult to establish. The talks failed to produce an early decisive agreement. Their existence still mattered: they created recurring exchanges of military information and kept questions of force reduction on the diplomatic agenda during periods when other aspects of détente weakened.

Superpower arms-control documents reveal a narrow but significant kind of cooperation. They record categories, ceilings, signatures, and institutional procedures; they do not tell students whether governments trusted one another or whether ordinary Europeans felt safer. Nor do they capture the politics of governments outside the principal negotiation. France retained its own nuclear force and had withdrawn from NATO's integrated military command in 1966, while continuing as a member of the alliance. Britain had a separate deterrent tied to American technology. Different strategic positions could lead allied leaders to endorse the principle of negotiation while disagreeing about particular weapons or the future balance of military risk.

The central paradox was that détente could encourage both restraint and modernization. If states agreed to limit a certain launcher category, they might direct resources toward other systems. If talks lowered the immediate risk of war, governments might feel freer to compete elsewhere. Historians therefore evaluate arms control by specific achievements rather than by an all-or-nothing test of whether the Cold War disappeared. For European society, the political significance was similarly conditional: treaties offered hope and diplomatic routine, but continued arsenals and exercises meant that nuclear conflict remained a serious possibility throughout the 1970s.

Section 131.4

The Helsinki Final Act of 1975

The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe, proposed by the Soviet side and accepted by the West on condition of wider topics, produced the Final Act, signed August 1, 1975, by 35 states. It addressed security and the inviolability of frontiers, economic, scientific, and environmental cooperation, and humanitarian contacts and human rights. Moscow valued recognition of borders, Western governments and activists stressed human rights and freedom of contact. It was politically binding but not a ratified treaty.

The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe grew from proposals to consolidate European security arrangements through a multilateral forum. The Soviet leadership had long sought acknowledgment of the European territorial and political settlement established after World War II. Western governments agreed to broad negotiations on condition that the agenda include more than frontiers and military principles. The conference opened in Helsinki in 1973, continued through negotiations in Geneva, and culminated in a summit that adopted the Final Act on August 1, 1975. Thirty-five states participated, including the United States and Canada as well as European countries. Albania was an important European exception. The range of signatories reflected a shared interest in rules for relations among states that otherwise sharply disagreed about political legitimacy.

The Final Act grouped commitments around related subjects: security in Europe; economic, scientific, technological, and environmental cooperation; and humanitarian contacts and information. It affirmed sovereign equality, territorial integrity, non-use of force, respect for frontiers, and human rights and fundamental freedoms. These principles could pull in different directions. Governments seeking protection from external interference emphasized sovereignty; activists seeking redress for arrests, censorship, or restrictions on movement emphasized human rights. The agreement was not a conventional treaty requiring ratification, and it established no international court empowered to punish each violation. Its commitments were political, not empty: repeated review meetings and public scrutiny made it harder to dismiss the text once signed.

Western governments did not all expect the human-rights provisions to transform eastern Europe immediately. Some officials considered the security clauses the principal achievement, and the East European governments anticipated political advantages from recognition of existing frontiers. Yet an agreement publicly endorsed by rulers could be quoted by their opponents. Dissidents could compare the words of state representatives in Helsinki with the practices of police and censorship agencies at home. The difference between an unenforceable promise and a politically useful standard became increasingly important. Many social movements have relied on public declarations that provide no immediate remedy but help organize pressure over time.

The documents also contributed to institutional habits. Review meetings required participating states to defend their records in diplomatic settings. Information campaigns and independent monitoring placed questions of individual liberty before international audiences. Contact provisions mattered for families, religious organizations, journalists, academics, and cultural exchanges, although the degree of access varied sharply among countries. Students should not mistake diplomatic signature for automatic implementation. A citizen seeking a passport, a writer facing censorship, or a family divided by an international border could continue to encounter the full force of a state's bureaucracy despite its published promises.

The historical effect of Helsinki must be assessed over a longer period without importing the outcome of 1989 into the intentions of 1975. The participating governments entered negotiations to manage a divided continent, not to schedule the end of communist rule. Nevertheless, the Final Act supplied language and procedures later used by the Moscow Helsinki Group and Charter 77, developments introduced in Section 131.17. The same agreement that offered recognition to existing security arrangements provided critics with a shared vocabulary for contesting abuses. That dual legacy explains why Helsinki belongs at the center of the chapter's account of late Cold War change.

Division II · 1967–1976 · Sections 131.5–131.8

The Collapse of Authoritarian Government in Portugal and Greece

Military and civilian action challenged old regimes through distinct pathways.

Section 131.5

Southern Europe under Dictatorship

Portugal's Estado Novo, founded under Salazar and continued by Caetano, the Greek junta (April 1967), and Franco's Spain shared censorship, political imprisonment, and restrictions on parties and unions, but differed in ideology, church relations, and alignment. Economic modernization, emigration, education, and labor organization pressed on all three. Their endings were not predetermined.

The dictatorships of southern Europe survived the defeat of fascism in 1945 for different reasons. Portugal's Estado Novo had roots in the interwar order and was sustained by a corporatist political system, security policing, censorship, and a far-reaching colonial empire. Spain's Franco regime emerged from the civil war of 1936–1939 and combined military authority, conservative nationalism, anti-communism, and historically close relations with the Catholic hierarchy. Greece's junta was newer, having seized power in April 1967, but it drew on a history of military intervention and deep conflict over communism and the monarchy. These regimes were not constitutionally or ideologically identical, and each related differently to NATO, the United States, and the European Communities.

Authoritarian government did not prevent economic and demographic change. Tourism brought foreign visitors and new forms of cultural contact; emigrants sent remittances from northern European workplaces; expanding education produced professionals, students, and teachers whose expectations did not always match official controls. Spain's economic growth after the 1959 stabilization program transformed cities and industries. Greece developed shipping, tourism, manufacturing, and migration connections despite the junta's repression. Portugal's colonial war expenditures and weaker productivity created distinct burdens. Growth also accentuated regional inequalities and exposed the tension between a modernizing economy and institutions that restricted independent unions, political competition, and freedom of expression.

Opposition was persistent but fragmented. Communists, socialists, liberal reformers, Christian democrats, trade unionists, students, intellectuals, regional nationalists, and religious figures could all challenge dictatorship while disagreeing about the political order that should replace it. Clandestine organization was dangerous. Police infiltrated groups, censored newspapers, detained organizers, and used torture or intimidation in different settings. Movements could nonetheless build support through workplaces, universities, neighborhood networks, cultural organizations, and relationships with exiles abroad. The history of transition begins before a ruler falls, in these attempts to sustain political alternatives despite the risks.

Military institutions were crucial, but their roles differed. Portuguese officers became a central vehicle of overthrow because colonial warfare changed their professional interests and political judgments. The Greek dictatorship was itself a military government, which lost credibility after its intervention in Cyprus produced a grave security crisis. In Spain, the armed forces contained elements determined to defend the Francoist legacy, and their resistance shaped the pace and tactics of reform. Civilian politicians in all three countries had to negotiate some relationship with soldiers whose acceptance could not be assumed. Democratization therefore involved both institutional innovation and the gradual reconstruction of authority over coercive forces.

These contrasts explain why there was no single southern European democratic transition. Portugal experienced a revolutionary struggle over economic power and the place of the military. Greece moved rapidly back to competitive elections under an established civilian leader but faced the unresolved Cyprus question and a fragile regional security environment. Spain pursued a reform process operating partly through institutions inherited from dictatorship, under pressure from citizens and opponents of change. None followed a guaranteed path. Their differences provide the evidence needed later in the chapter to evaluate theories of negotiated compromise, mass mobilization, and military defeat as causes of political transformation.

Section 131.6

Portugal's Carnation Revolution

Wars in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea Bissau exhausted the regime and its officers, whose grievances included careers as well as colonial war. The Armed Forces Movement acted on April 25, 1974, toppling Caetano's government, and crowds joined the soldiers. Political activity was legalized and the colonial wars ended, though decolonization itself belongs to Chapter 128.

The Portuguese revolution began inside an army that had been asked to sustain wars across several African territories without a credible political settlement. In Angola, Guinea-Bissau, and Mozambique, Portuguese forces fought independence movements whose organization and strategies differed. Men conscripted into the armed forces, officers serving repeated tours, and families awaiting their return experienced the conflict as an enduring social burden. The professional grievances of captains became politically explosive when they merged with doubts about the regime's ability to win or end the wars. The movement's immediate origins included disputes over career arrangements, but many members came to see a change of government as necessary to make a new colonial policy possible.

The Armed Forces Movement, initially organized among junior and middle-ranking officers, developed plans that combined a military operation with a political program. It did not speak for every officer or every opponent of the dictatorship. Leaders differed over the future of the armed forces, relations with left-wing parties, the economy, and the speed of decolonization. On April 25, 1974, units moved to seize strategic positions in Lisbon and other places. Radio broadcasts and military signals coordinated the operation; the regime's capacity to resist collapsed as forces declined to defend Marcello Caetano's government. The surrender did not follow a conventional contest between two armies of comparable strength. It reflected a decisive breakdown of loyalty inside institutions on which the dictatorship depended.

Civilian participation quickly gave the event a political character broader than the officers' original plan. People crowded streets and squares, greeted soldiers, and called for the release of political prisoners and an end to censorship. Carnations became associated with the revolution, but the symbol should not obscure the organized resistance that preceded it or the political disputes that followed. Political detainees were freed, censorship institutions dismantled, opposition parties and unions emerged into public life, and the secret police apparatus was discredited. What counted as democratic freedom was no longer a question decided solely by authoritarian officials. Workers, students, and neighborhood groups began to test the new possibilities immediately.

The MFA program offered a framework for a transition but did not prescribe its later outcome. It committed the movement to ending repression and establishing political freedoms and representative institutions, while recognizing that the colonial wars required a political solution. The program emerged through drafting and revision and survived in more than one contemporary version, a useful reminder that revolutionary documents themselves have histories. General António de Spínola initially occupied a prominent position, yet his understanding of decolonization and the new order did not coincide with that of all MFA officers or civilian activists. The fall of dictatorship created opportunities, but it also made previously suppressed differences visible.

The immediate transformation is best understood as an interaction between military initiative and popular mobilization. The officers could remove an unpopular government; they could not determine unilaterally the direction of a population newly free to organize. The public could press for rapid reform but faced powerful institutions, economic difficulties, and an uncertain international setting. The end of colonial rule, examined in Chapter 128, accelerated the political struggle in Portugal itself, including the return of people from former African territories and questions about economic resources. The challenge after April 25 was to replace arbitrary government with institutions whose authority could survive electoral disagreement and divisions within the armed forces.

An event celebrated for relatively limited bloodshed should still be examined without assuming that all participants faced the same risks. Police officials, soldiers loyal to the regime, opposition prisoners, and citizens in different cities experienced the day differently, and deaths did occur. The designation Carnation Revolution emerged as a powerful public symbol of a regime's swift collapse, but historical explanation requires attention to preparations within the military, civilian movements that had sustained opposition, and uncertainty about which commanders would comply with orders. The contrast between the rapid overthrow and the prolonged struggles of the next two years is among the most important lessons of the Portuguese case.

Section 131.7

Portugal's Revolutionary Transition, 1974–1976

Provisional governments, military factions, strikes, land occupations, and nationalizations accompanied disputes over socialism and parliamentary democracy. The Constituent Assembly was elected on April 25, 1975, with the Socialist Party first. The events of November 25, 1975, shifted the military balance, and the Constitution was adopted on April 2, 1976, and entered into force on April 25.

The period from April 1974 to the Constitution of 1976 is often called the Portuguese revolutionary process, or PREC. Its complicated sequence resists a simple story of democracy arriving immediately after the fall of a dictator. Provisional governments changed, the armed forces divided, and parties disputed whether Portugal should become a parliamentary pluralist state, a socialist democracy, or a more centralized revolutionary system. Workers occupied workplaces, agricultural laborers organized land occupations, and residents formed local committees to address housing and services. Some changes responded to longstanding inequalities; others intensified questions about property rights, economic management, and the limits of military political influence.

The political landscape included the Socialist Party led by Mário Soares, the Communist Party led by Álvaro Cunhal, emerging center-right formations, and various smaller revolutionary groups. Each maintained different links to the MFA and to organized labor. Some participants regarded the armed forces as the continuing guarantor of revolutionary gains. Others feared that soldiers claiming a permanent political role would undermine the representative institutions for which they had opposed dictatorship. Within the officer corps, differences over speed of economic change and control of state institutions shaped successive crises. The terms left and right are useful only if students also recognize the variety of programs within each camp.

The Constituent Assembly election of April 25, 1975, showed the strength of electoral pluralism. Participation was exceptionally high, and the Socialist Party emerged ahead of its rivals. That vote did not instantly settle who would govern the revolution, because political authority remained divided between elected representatives, provisional executives, and military institutions. Nationalizations of banks and major enterprises, agricultural reforms, and confrontations over control of the media continued. The election nevertheless supplied an important measure of public preferences at a time when groups with competing claims to revolutionary authenticity were attempting to define the future of the country.

The confrontation of November 25, 1975, marked a decisive shift within the military. Attempts by radical military elements to alter the balance were defeated by forces loyal to a more restrained constitutional transition. Historians dispute the intentions of every participant and the degree to which there was a coherent revolutionary plan, so the day should not be reduced to an uncomplicated victory of one party over another. Its broader consequence is clearer: the balance of coercive power moved away from military factions able to obstruct parliamentary consolidation. This reduced, without eliminating, the tension between the revolution's social program and the authority of representative institutions.

The Constituent Assembly adopted Portugal's Constitution on April 2, 1976; its text was published that month and took effect on April 25. The original constitution reflected the transformative ambitions of the revolutionary era, including references to socialism and the institutional role of the armed forces. It also established electoral institutions and guaranteed fundamental rights. Later constitutional revisions altered important aspects of that original settlement, making it essential to use the 1976 text when explaining the initial compromise. Parliamentary elections followed in April 1976, and competitive politics became increasingly central, although consolidation took time. The Portuguese case demonstrates that democratization can involve conflict over the meaning of democracy itself, not simply consensus about replacing dictatorship.

Section 131.8

Greece, Cyprus, and July 1974

The junta's repression provoked the Athens Polytechnic uprising of November 1973. On July 15, 1974, forces linked to Athens overthrew Archbishop Makarios in Cyprus, and Turkey intervened militarily from July 20. The military failure, displacement of civilians, and collapse of the junta followed, and Karamanlis returned in July 1974. The Cyprus problem was not settled by this restoration.

Greece's military junta seized power in April 1967 and justified its intervention through claims that it was protecting the nation against communist subversion and political disorder. The officers restricted parties, censored newspapers, detained opponents, and used security agencies to intimidate critics. The dictatorship also relied on public ceremonies and claims of discipline and national regeneration. Such rhetoric never eliminated domestic opposition. Students, political exiles, trade unionists, lawyers, and others continued to challenge military rule, while international criticism damaged the government's reputation. The Athens Polytechnic uprising in November 1973 revealed the capacity of student protest to attract broader social support even under harsh surveillance.

The Polytechnic confrontation did not directly restore democracy. Security forces violently suppressed the protests, and internal struggles produced a still harder regime under Dimitrios Ioannidis. The government faced a crisis of legitimacy but also tried to strengthen its position through nationalism, particularly over Cyprus. The island had been independent since 1960 under arrangements intended to protect Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot communities. Conflicts over constitutional authority, intercommunal violence, and the stationing of foreign troops had already complicated its independence. Athens' military rulers were therefore intervening in a dispute with a long history and multiple parties, not in a simple bilateral struggle between Greece and Turkey.

On July 15, 1974, a coup backed by the Greek junta and carried out through Cypriot military structures overthrew President Archbishop Makarios. Turkish forces landed on July 20, citing treaty rights and the danger to Turkish Cypriots. The intervention developed into wider military operations and was followed by extensive population displacement. The legal justifications, conduct of combatants, and consequences are contested matters of international history, and the chapter must differentiate those questions from the political result in Athens. Greek military leaders had undertaken a risky intervention and proved unable to reverse the ensuing crisis. Their authority deteriorated rapidly as fears of regional war intensified.

Konstantinos Karamanlis was recalled from exile and returned on July 24 to lead a civilian government. His immediate problem was not only to announce the end of the dictatorship but to restore public confidence and maintain command over armed forces associated with the failed regime. The government legalized political activity, released or rehabilitated prisoners, and took steps toward elections. The Cyprus conflict remained unresolved and profoundly shaped Greek relations with Turkey. Many Greeks interpreted the restoration of civilian government through the lens of both the hope of political freedom and the humiliation and insecurity created by the island's division.

The fall of the junta shows how an external crisis can accelerate domestic regime collapse, but it does not mean that foreign intervention alone created Greek democracy. Opposition networks, memories of earlier democratic politics, divisions within the armed forces, and Karamanlis's reputation were important to what followed. The case also warns against treating a democratic transition as the automatic solution to every problem inherited from dictatorship. Civil liberties could be restored and elections held while displaced Cypriots, military families, and Greek and Turkish communities continued to live with the consequences of 1974. The transition succeeded institutionally in Greece without settling the wider geopolitical conflict that had precipitated it.

Cyprus also illustrates the dangers of compressing legal and humanitarian history into a single date. The July landings were followed by ceasefire negotiations, renewed Turkish military operations in August, and enduring population movements, property claims, and contested political institutions. Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot experiences were not interchangeable, nor should civilians be treated as extensions of the governments acting in their name. United Nations resolutions and peacekeeping records provide evidence about international efforts to manage the crisis, but they must be read alongside sources produced by the island's communities. A military government's fall in Athens did not repair the loss of homes or resolve questions of sovereignty and security on Cyprus.

Division III · 1974–1982 · Sections 131.9–131.12

Spain, Greek Democracy, and Constitutional Consolidation

Democracy required elections, constitutional authority, accountability, and acceptance of political opposition.

Section 131.9

Greece's Metapolitefsi

Karamanlis legalized parties, restored liberties, and asserted civilian control over the army. After the elections of November 17, 1974, the referendum of December 8 rejected the monarchy by about 69 percent. The 1975 Constitution created a parliamentary republic, and Andreas Papandreou's PASOK won in 1981 through ordinary competition.

The Greek transition after July 1974 is commonly called Metapolitefsi, a term pointing to a change of political regime. Karamanlis led a government that attempted to bring competitive politics back into operation while demonstrating that civilian leaders, not military officers, would make the basic decisions of the state. The government restored rights of political organization, permitted the Communist Party to operate legally, and addressed restrictions left by the junta. The return of political exiles and public discussion of repression gave the transition a social dimension. It also reopened conflicts that had roots in the civil war and the years of polarizing anticommunist politics that preceded the dictatorship.

The election of November 17, 1974, established a new parliamentary mandate. Karamanlis's New Democracy achieved a substantial victory, but the significance of the election extended beyond the winning party: it demonstrated that political competition could take place without the military claiming the right to invalidate its outcome. On December 8 voters were asked whether to restore the monarchy or establish a republic. Approximately 69.18 percent rejected restoration of the king. The result closed an immediate constitutional uncertainty, although debates about national memory and the monarchy's previous political role continued. The distinction between elections and referendum matters: one selected representatives; the other determined the form of the state.

The Constitution of 1975 consolidated a parliamentary republican framework and set out rights, institutions, and procedures for lawful government. Its drafters drew on earlier Greek constitutions and contemporary western European examples. A constitution can create a legal framework, but it cannot by itself ensure that military units obey civilian authority, courts act independently, or parties accept electoral defeat. The restoration government therefore also addressed the military hierarchy, the security institutions associated with repression, and legal proceedings against members of the junta. Trials raised difficult questions about accountability, evidence, punishment, and the relationship between national reconciliation and justice.

International relations made democratic consolidation more complicated. Turkey's military position in Cyprus and the continuing division of the island shaped Greek defense policy and national politics. Greece's relationship with NATO became a subject of controversy, and the country's place in European institutions was reconsidered. These choices could intensify domestic disagreements without threatening the principle that such disagreements should be resolved through elected civilian government. The emergence of PASOK under Andreas Papandreou as a major opposition party showed that the new political order would not be limited to the conservative forces that managed the initial transition.

PASOK's election victory in October 1981 provided another test of institutional durability. A peaceful change of government to a party that criticized aspects of the existing economic and foreign-policy establishment demonstrated the capacity of elections to alter power without a coup. Yet democratic stability did not erase disputes over education, the public sector, rural and urban inequality, or Cyprus. The Greek experience therefore shows consolidation as a sequence: removal of military rulers, restoration of liberties, electoral authorization, constitutional settlement, accountability, and acceptance of alternation in office. Each stage had its own risks and could not be inferred simply from the first successful election.

Historical Vocabulary

Democratic Transition versus Democratic Consolidation

Transition refers to the process of replacing authoritarian government with representative institutions and civil liberties. Consolidation refers to their durable acceptance, including repeated competitive elections, peaceful transfers of power, civilian authority over armed forces, and workable protection of rights. Neither requires a society without political disagreement.

Section 131.10

Spain after Franco

Franco died on November 20, 1975, and Juan Carlos I became king. The monarchy did not guarantee either continuity or democracy. Labor conflict, regional nationalism, and clandestine opposition pressed for change, and Suárez became prime minister in July 1976.

Spain's political transformation began before Franco's death because the society governed by his dictatorship had changed profoundly since the civil war. Industrialization, tourism, urban expansion, and migration altered the distribution of employment and exposed citizens to influences from neighboring European countries. University enrollments grew and workers organized strikes despite restrictions on independent unions. The Catholic Church was no longer uniformly aligned with the political regime, and regional cultural and national movements challenged the centralized conception of Spain that Franco had defended. These developments did not make democracy inevitable, but they made indefinite continuation of the existing political order difficult.

Franco's regime had institutionalized authority through the military, the administration, the security services, the single official political movement, and the system of Fundamental Laws. Opposition included socialists, communists, trade unionists, liberals, democrats, regional activists, and others whose historical disagreements were substantial. Some sought an immediate rupture with dictatorship; some favored negotiated changes; others wanted to preserve many existing institutions. Political violence, including the activities of ETA, contributed to insecurity and supplied authoritarian factions with arguments against liberalization. The transition emerged within this complex setting, not from a simple consensus among all Spaniards.

Franco died on November 20, 1975, and Juan Carlos I became king under a succession framework designed to preserve the regime. This created a central contradiction: the monarchy's initial legal authority derived from institutions that many citizens wanted to dismantle. Juan Carlos's willingness to support political change proved important, but his influence should not be detached from the initiatives of reforming officials, organized opposition, and social protest. Early changes were limited and hesitant. The question was whether the system could transform its legal basis without producing another military intervention or leaving the opposition permanently excluded.

Adolfo Suárez became prime minister in July 1976 and pursued reform through institutions inherited from the dictatorship. A central measure was the Political Reform Law, approved by the old Cortes in November and endorsed by voters in a December 1976 referendum. It established a route to elected representative institutions. Using existing procedures to dismantle authoritarian legal arrangements was strategically important because it made reform less easy to dismiss as a coup or constitutional vacuum. It was also controversial: opponents feared that those who had governed under Franco would control the terms of democratization and prevent a full reckoning with repression.

The Spanish case illustrates how change from within can depend on pressure from outside. Demonstrations and strikes showed that political participation could not remain narrowly controlled, while government negotiations sought to keep the armed forces and conservative institutions from blocking reform. Regional demands in Catalonia and the Basque Country added questions of language, autonomy, memory, and political belonging that could not be solved by restoring elections alone. The transition thus involved a continuing negotiation over who would define the new polity and which aspects of the old regime could be retained, dismantled, or prosecuted. The outcome remained uncertain even as political freedoms gradually expanded.

Section 131.11

Spain's Democratic Transition

The Political Reform Law of 1976 opened the way to legal parties and the election of June 15, 1977. The Moncloa Pacts (October 1977) joined economic stabilization to political agreement, and the Constitution was approved by referendum on December 6, 1978. It created a parliamentary monarchy and autonomous communities, leaving regional tensions unresolved. The transition was shaped by workers, neighborhood groups, women's movements, and regional mobilization as well as elites.

The democratic election of June 15, 1977, returned an elected legislature after decades without genuine competition. Suárez's Union of the Democratic Centre emerged as the largest party, while the Socialists, Communists, and regional political organizations won significant representation. The legalization of the Communist Party earlier that year was especially controversial among sections of the military and authoritarian right, making the election a test of whether institutions would accept a pluralist outcome. The elected Cortes had to construct a constitutional settlement while the country confronted inflation, unemployment, violence, and unequal regional development. It was not possible to separate political reform completely from the material pressures affecting households and workplaces.

The Moncloa Pacts of October 1977 arose from an attempt to stabilize the economy and sustain support for constitutional negotiation. Governments, parties, unions, and other actors faced inflation that threatened wages and public finances. Agreement on broad economic objectives and certain reforms created space for continued bargaining, but the pacts did not eliminate conflicts over distribution or guarantee that every organization accepted the same sacrifices. Their importance lies in showing political forces with sharply different visions using negotiation to avoid a breakdown in the new institutions. Students should also consider those who were represented indirectly or not at all when agreements were drawn up at national level.

The constitutional drafting process required compromises over monarchy, parliamentary power, religion, civil rights, and Spain's territorial organization. The Constitution was approved by referendum on December 6, 1978, sanctioned by the king on December 27, and published on December 29, when it entered into force. It established a parliamentary monarchy, parliamentary accountability of government, and extensive rights protections. Its language recognized the autonomy of nationalities and regions within the unity of the Spanish state. That settlement made room for self-government while leaving unresolved disputes about the limits of autonomy, relations among regions, and the interpretation of national identity.

The political participation that made reform possible was broader than the negotiating circle of party leaders. Neighborhood associations demanded housing and services, workers organized for rights and representation, women's movements challenged discriminatory family law, and Catalan and Basque organizations advanced linguistic and political claims. People who had experienced repression also disagreed about the role of amnesty, memory, and accountability. The 1977 Amnesty Law helped release prisoners and remove legal penalties associated with political opposition, but it later became central to controversy over the absence of prosecution for abuses committed under dictatorship. It is misleading to treat Spain's passage to democracy as either a completely consensual reconciliation or an exclusively elite bargain.

A constitution's significance is tested by implementation. Elections had to become routine; courts and public administrators had to apply the new rules; security agencies had to accept civilian oversight; and the political opposition had to retain the freedom to criticize. Regional autonomy required statutes, elections, budgets, and negotiations, not just constitutional language. Economic uncertainty and continuing violence made each of these steps contentious. The constitutional settlement established rules for competition without resolving every substantive social dispute. That distinction is important because it explains how a state can become democratic while continuing to face intense political disagreements and conflicts over historical injustice.

The 1978 constitutional referendum produced broad approval, yet turnout and regional voting patterns are important for understanding the limits of any claim to unanimity. Some voters viewed the constitution as a necessary democratic foundation, some endorsed it despite reservations, and others rejected it for sharply different reasons. In the Basque provinces, abstention and opposition were significant, reflecting political demands that the national compromise did not satisfy. The settlement was a workable framework for institutions and rights rather than a document that reconciled every national identity or resolved the memory of the civil war. Later disputes over autonomy and historical justice therefore cannot be dismissed as merely post-constitutional developments.

Section 131.12

The Failed Coup of 1981

On February 23, 1981, Lieutenant Colonel Antonio Tejero and Civil Guards interrupted the investiture of Leopoldo Calvo Sotelo. The coup failed after the king and most commanders refused support. PSOE's victory in October 1982 and Felipe González's government in December showed a peaceful transfer of power.

The attempted coup of February 23, 1981, exposed the fragility that remained beneath Spain's new constitutional order. Suárez had resigned amid political tensions and challenges to his leadership. Parliament was voting on the investiture of Leopoldo Calvo-Sotelo when armed Civil Guards led by Lieutenant Colonel Antonio Tejero entered the chamber and interrupted proceedings. Deputies and members of the government were held inside the parliament building, and the assault appeared in contemporary broadcasts and photographs that became enduring symbols of the attempted overthrow. The event was not merely an episode of theatrical disorder: it directly threatened the sovereignty of the elected legislature.

The plot involved different officers and plans, and historians continue to examine the relationships among those involved. General Jaime Milans del Bosch deployed military forces in Valencia, while other commanders considered what action to take. These distinctions matter because the coup was not a single coordinated movement supported uniformly by the armed forces. Its success depended on wider military backing that failed to materialize. Civilian officials, political parties, and military commanders had to act under uncertainty about who held authority and whether orders issued by the insurgents would be followed elsewhere. The situation could not be resolved simply by declaring that democracy was popular.

King Juan Carlos addressed the country in military uniform and publicly supported the constitutional order, an intervention that became central to public accounts of the crisis. Yet the failure of the coup also depended on the absence of the hoped-for general military uprising, the resistance of constitutional institutions, and the inability of the plotters to construct a legitimate alternative government. Students should distinguish contemporary knowledge from later investigations and memories: broadcasts recorded dramatic moments but did not reveal every private conversation or calculation. The survival of the elected institutions was a political outcome involving multiple actors, not an act of one person alone.

The failed coup did not end the problems that had contributed to instability. The armed forces required further restructuring and the boundaries of civilian control remained a concern. Political violence connected to ETA and other groups continued, while economic pressures and disputes over regional autonomy strained government coalitions. Critics of the transition debated whether reform had compromised too much with institutions inherited from Francoism. Supporters argued that the peaceful defeat of a coup showed the new political rules were taking root. Both perspectives gain meaning when placed against the actual uncertainties of February 1981 and the broader history of Spanish civil-military relations.

The election of October 1982 gave the Socialist Workers' Party a large parliamentary majority, and Felipe González formed a government in December. A peaceful transfer of power from centrist leaders to the Socialists marked a different form of validation from the survival of the coup attempt. It demonstrated that the constitutional order could produce a major change in policy direction through ballots rather than military intervention. The case also illustrates why historians distinguish transition from consolidation. The end of a dictatorship is an event or sequence of events; acceptance of repeated elections, competing governments, and civilian authority is a longer process that must be continuously maintained.

Division IV · 1971–1983 · Sections 131.13–131.16

Economic Disruption and Political Realignment

Monetary uncertainty, energy shocks, and deindustrialization tested the postwar settlement.

Section 131.13

The End of the Postwar Boom

The United States suspended dollar gold convertibility in August 1971, ending Bretton Woods fixed rates. The October 1973 war and the oil embargo raised energy prices sharply, and the Iranian Revolution brought another shock in 1979 and 1980. Energy costs hit industry, transport, and households, but inflation, wage bargaining, and monetary policy also mattered.

The postwar economic expansion had been supported by arrangements that made currencies relatively predictable and encouraged trade. The Bretton Woods system linked many exchange rates to the American dollar, while the dollar was convertible into gold for foreign monetary authorities at an agreed rate. This order came under strain as American overseas commitments, domestic inflation, and international financial imbalances increased. On August 15, 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold. The Smithsonian Agreement later in 1971 attempted to restore an adjusted exchange-rate structure, but by 1973 major currencies were moving toward generalized floating rates. The breakdown was a process, not a single instantaneous end to all fixed exchange arrangements.

Monetary instability altered the choices available to European governments. Exchange-rate movements could make imports more expensive, affect export competitiveness, and complicate wage bargaining. European leaders attempted forms of currency cooperation in response, but different inflation rates and economic structures made stable arrangements difficult to maintain. Governments managing welfare programs, industrial investment, and unemployment increasingly faced both domestic and international constraints. Economic turbulence therefore preceded the first great oil shock; it cannot be explained solely by the events that followed the Arab-Israeli war of October 1973.

The October war was followed by production cuts and an embargo imposed by Arab oil-exporting states against selected countries. Prices rose sharply across world markets, and supply uncertainty generated panic, conservation measures, and political conflict. Western European economies depended heavily on imported petroleum, making them especially exposed to higher costs for heating, transportation, electricity generation, chemical production, and manufacturing. The effects were transmitted through supply chains as firms paid more for fuel and raw materials. Some consumers faced restricted driving or heating practices, while governments pursued energy conservation and stockpile policies. The shock also redistributed income toward oil producers and encouraged the international recycling of petroleum revenues through banks and investment markets.

The second oil shock developed around the Iranian Revolution of 1978–1979 and disruptions to output, and was intensified by further geopolitical instability into 1980. The term shock suggests an external blow, but the economic outcomes depended on domestic policies and institutions. Wage indexation, currency depreciation, monetary expansion or restraint, and the structure of industrial production all influenced inflation and employment. Some countries had stronger collective bargaining institutions or different energy sources and were better placed to limit particular effects. The same world price increase could therefore have unequal consequences across Britain, France, West Germany, Italy, and smaller economies.

An appropriate statistical comparison must use the same price series over time. OPEC's historical annual bulletin reports a nominal crude-price series rising from approximately $3.05 per barrel in 1973 to $10.73 in 1974, then $17.25 in 1979. These values are annual observations in nominal dollars and should not be mixed without adjustment with spot-price peaks, domestic regulated prices, or inflation-adjusted comparisons. The numbers show the scale of the external price shifts; they do not, by themselves, measure unemployment or explain governmental decisions. The end of the postwar boom thus involved interconnected monetary, energy, structural, and political changes.

Historical comparison also requires attention to who paid the bills. Energy-intensive industries could pass costs to consumers only when competition and demand permitted; households on fixed incomes could not negotiate fuel prices; governments subsidizing essentials had to find revenue or borrow. The effect of oil prices on a particular community could therefore be mediated by tax policy, welfare provision, housing construction, and public transportation. Countries with domestic oil resources, such as Britain as North Sea production expanded, had different longer-run prospects from countries heavily dependent on imports. These distinctions help explain why a common international shock contributed to divergent national political arguments rather than producing one European response.

Section 131.14

Stagflation and Industrial Restructuring

Coal, steel, shipbuilding, and automobiles shrank unevenly across Britain, West Germany, France, Italy, and smaller economies. Unemployment fell hard on young workers, women, and migrant workers, and industrial disputes multiplied.

Stagflation described a combination that challenged confidence in the economic assumptions of the long boom: high inflation coinciding with weak growth and rising unemployment. Inflation could reduce the purchasing power of wages and savings; unemployment could reduce income altogether. Policies that restrained demand might help limit inflation while worsening joblessness in the short term. Policies designed to stimulate employment could encounter new price pressures and external deficits. This dilemma was not an absolute law, because institutions and conditions varied, but it became central to debates among governments, unions, employers, and economists during the 1970s.

Traditional industries experienced particular difficulty. Steelmaking, coal mining, shipbuilding, textiles, and some branches of automobile production faced increased international competition, changes in technology, shifts in demand, and the costs of energy and environmental protection. Firms closed unprofitable plants, modernized production, or moved operations. Industrial regions whose communities were built around one employer or industry could suffer concentrated losses. Schools, shops, housing markets, and local tax revenues were affected along with the workforce. Deindustrialization should therefore be understood as a transformation of regional societies as well as a change in national output statistics.

The burden was unequally distributed. Young workers entering labor markets after education could not assume the secure careers their parents had anticipated during the 1950s and 1960s. Women were concentrated in some sectors with lower pay and less secure employment, although changes in female labor participation complicated any uniform picture. Migrant workers could face unemployment and discriminatory access to housing and social services. Older industrial workers sometimes possessed skills tied to sectors in decline and found retraining difficult. Governments had to decide whether to subsidize particular workplaces, fund regional development, expand training, or permit market changes to proceed more rapidly. Each option carried costs and different distributions of risk.

Countries responded differently because their institutions and economies differed. West Germany's vocational training and export industries offered some mechanisms for adjustment, but the economy still faced slowing growth and unemployment. Britain experienced sharp industrial conflict and pressure on both public finances and wages. Italy combined powerful labor mobilization with inflation and structural divisions between regions. France used planning institutions and state enterprises while also confronting the limits of policy under changed international conditions. Scandinavian and smaller European economies tried different mixtures of collective bargaining, public expenditure, exchange-rate policy, and industrial support. A European average can conceal these contrasting paths and should not be treated as the experience of every household.

The transformation was accompanied by intellectual and political disputes over the role of the state. Some economists argued that governments had underestimated inflationary expectations and that monetary discipline should be given priority. Others insisted that austerity and the abandonment of industrial communities would produce needless unemployment and inequality. Policymakers often attempted combinations rather than applying one pure theory. A family deciding whether it could afford fuel, a factory manager considering closure, and a minister facing an external payments problem encountered the same crisis from very different positions. That is why the chapter's everyday-life study should accompany macroeconomic statistics rather than leaving them as abstract lines on a graph.

Section 131.15

Political Realignment

Margaret Thatcher took office in 1979 promising monetary restraint. West Germany moved from Helmut Schmidt's coalition to Helmut Kohl in 1982. François Mitterrand won in 1981 and soon faced limits to his initial program. Italy sustained unstable coalitions alongside a strong Communist Party. These were different responses, not a single inevitable solution.

The economic disruptions of the 1970s reshaped political competition without producing one uniform ideological outcome. Parties of the center-left and center-right had often accepted parts of the postwar settlement: welfare provision, public investment, a role for labor representation, and the goal of full employment. Persistent inflation, fiscal pressure, and industrial restructuring forced them to reconsider what governments could promise and how economic stability should be achieved. Electoral debates shifted toward taxation, public borrowing, the ownership of industry, trade union power, and monetary policy. The meaning of efficient government became contested as voters evaluated both living standards and the protection of social services.

Britain's experience showed the pressure that inflation and industrial conflict placed on governing coalitions. Labour governments sought incomes policies, negotiated with trade unions, and obtained international financial support during the 1976 sterling crisis. Disputes over public-sector pay and services contributed to the political climate surrounding the winter of 1978–1979. Margaret Thatcher's Conservative election victory in May 1979 did not immediately solve the economic problems, but it signaled an approach giving greater priority to monetary restraint, market competition, and limits on organized labor. The social costs and political consequences of these policies remained intensely disputed and developed across the following decade.

West Germany followed a different route. Helmut Schmidt's Social Democratic–Free Democratic coalition sought fiscal and monetary stability while maintaining important features of the social-market economy. The Bundesbank's anti-inflation priorities helped shape policy, but export performance and industrial bargaining were equally important. Coalition disagreements eventually brought Helmut Kohl to office in October 1982. This change of government was a parliamentary realignment, not a repudiation of constitutional democracy. It illustrates how a stable political system could adapt its policy priorities through shifting alliances while continuing commitments to social insurance, Western security institutions, and European cooperation.

In France, François Mitterrand's 1981 victory brought a Socialist president to power after the long dominance of the right and center-right under the Fifth Republic. His government pursued nationalizations, social reforms, and expansionary measures, but faced inflation, capital movements, and foreign-exchange pressures. The policy shift of 1983 toward greater monetary restraint reflected the constraints of European currency arrangements and changing economic conditions. The choice should not be portrayed as proof that every socialist program was impossible, nor should the early measures be assumed to have produced every subsequent difficulty. What matters is the interaction between electoral mandates, international monetary commitments, and national policy discretion.

Italy's political parties faced a fragmented electorate, coalition bargaining, and the continuing strength of the Communist Party. Inflation and industrial disputes interacted with the legacy of political violence and uneven development between north and south. Governments attempted combinations of austerity, negotiated wage arrangements, industrial support, and monetary adjustment, without eliminating uncertainty. Across Europe, the crisis unsettled but did not erase the institutions built after 1945. Voters and parties argued over how much of the welfare state should be preserved, how it should be financed, and which groups should bear the costs of restructuring. Political realignment was therefore a contest over the future of an existing social order, not simply its replacement.

Section 131.16

Political Violence and the State

Italy's Years of Lead included the kidnapping and murder of Aldo Moro in 1978, and West Germany faced the Red Army Faction and the German Autumn of 1977. Conflicts in Northern Ireland and Spain had separate origins. These groups did not represent the wider movements of 1968. The question was how states balanced security with legal protections.

Political violence confronted western European democracies with difficult questions about security and constitutional authority. In Italy, the years commonly called the Years of Lead involved attacks by organizations of the radical left and the far right, as well as state failures and controversies over investigations. The Red Brigades targeted officials and employers and kidnapped former prime minister Aldo Moro in March 1978; he was murdered in May. The event shocked political institutions already strained by coalition negotiations and public distrust. Far-right attacks, including bombings, formed another part of Italy's violent landscape. Treating these different perpetrators as one movement obscures their goals, organizations, and relationships to wider society.

In West Germany, the Red Army Faction carried out kidnappings, robberies, and killings in the 1970s. The German Autumn of 1977 included the kidnapping and murder of industrial leader Hanns Martin Schleyer and the hijacking of a Lufthansa aircraft by militants demanding the release of imprisoned RAF members. The state sought to protect citizens and institutions while preserving legal process. New security measures and police operations provoked debates about surveillance, detention, the press, and the limits of executive authority. Most participants in the student and antiwar movements of the late 1960s did not endorse the RAF's violence, and many publicly opposed it. Chronological connection must not be mistaken for collective responsibility.

Northern Ireland involved a conflict with a distinct history of partition, civil rights, competing national identities, and the role of British security forces. Spain confronted violence associated with ETA, a Basque separatist organization that continued attacks after the return of democratic elections. These conflicts cannot be made interchangeable with Italian or West German militant politics. Their social constituencies, political goals, and historical claims differed. The relevance to this chapter is comparative: democratic institutions confronted threats under circumstances in which a security response might itself alter citizens' trust in the state. The success of democracy cannot be measured only by the number of attacks prevented.

Victims and their families experienced bereavement, intimidation, and the sense that ordinary civic life had become unsafe. Security officials emphasized the responsibility to prevent further killings. Civil liberties advocates warned that broad emergency powers could outlast the threat invoked to justify them. Politicians faced electoral pressure to appear decisive, sometimes alongside concerns about the independence of courts and the reliability of intelligence. These positions are not morally equivalent in every respect, but each helps explain a part of the historical record. Official investigations and judicial findings should be distinguished from allegations circulated during periods of panic and conspiracy speculation.

Political violence illustrates a more general tension in the democratic transitions of the decade. A state was expected to protect representative government while continuing to recognize opposition as legitimate. If dissent itself was equated with violence, ordinary unions, student groups, and unpopular parties could be unfairly stigmatized. If violence was ignored, citizens could lose confidence in the capacity of public institutions. The response required policing, courts, political leadership, and public accountability. Democratic resilience meant sustaining lawful authority amid conflict rather than proving that democratic societies would never face severe divisions.

The relationship between public fear and governmental legitimacy deserves particular attention. Emergency measures may win initial support after a spectacular attack, but confidence can deteriorate if investigations appear selective, victims are neglected, or coercive powers are used against people unconnected to violence. Parliamentary oversight, access to legal representation, independent courts, and accurate public information become especially important precisely when violence makes political debate more emotional. The cases of Italy and West Germany offer contrasting records of institutional response, while Spain's efforts to manage terrorism alongside democratic transition reveal another context. Students should compare evidence before treating every security expansion as either unavoidable protection or disguised authoritarianism.

Section 131.17

Human Rights and Helsinki Monitoring

After 1975, the Moscow Helsinki Group (1976) and Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia used Helsinki's language against their governments, which signed but still restricted freedoms. Chapter 132 treats Solidarity and later opposition.

The Helsinki commitments gained importance when organizations outside government began using them as standards against which to judge official conduct. The Moscow Helsinki Group, formed in 1976, collected information about violations of rights that Soviet representatives had publicly agreed to respect. Activists circulated reports concerning detention, restrictions on emigration, religious activity, and political expression. Their work was dangerous, because Soviet authorities did not consider international signatures a license for independent domestic monitoring. Some members were arrested, tried, or pressured into exile. Their efforts nevertheless linked personal cases to a European diplomatic framework that governments could not readily deny having endorsed.

Charter 77 emerged in Czechoslovakia in January 1977 after the authorities' repression of cultural and intellectual activity. Its signatories invoked human-rights obligations and criticized the contrast between the state's legal promises and the realities of censorship and police supervision. The group was not a conventional political party offering an immediate program to seize government. Its central method was to document and publicize violations, thereby insisting that political legitimacy also required the state to follow its stated commitments. This mode of dissent differed from the mass uprisings discussed in Chapter 129, but it grew within a political landscape shaped by the defeat of the Prague Spring.

Western governments and international organizations responded unevenly. Some officials pressed rights questions vigorously at review meetings; others favored quieter diplomacy to preserve wider security cooperation. Journalists, religious communities, lawyers, and campaign organizations helped carry information across borders. Public attention could protect a detainee in one case and make repression more severe in another. International monitoring should not be romanticized as a mechanism that automatically constrained police power. It created political costs, diplomatic embarrassment, and wider networks of solidarity, but it lacked direct control over prisons, courts, or security ministries within the states it criticized.

The Helsinki process also illustrates a changing understanding of European security. Diplomatic stability had often been treated primarily as a question of frontiers, military forces, and relations among capitals. Human-rights activists argued that the conduct of governments toward their own populations was relevant to a genuinely secure order. This proposition challenged the claim that repression was an exclusively domestic matter. It also raised difficult issues about consistency: western governments were required to consider their own human-rights records and their treatment of minorities, migrants, and dissidents. The Helsinki language did not erase political double standards, but it created a common vocabulary in which they could be examined.

The longer consequences belong partly to Chapter 132, which considers Solidarity and the final decades of communist government. Within Chapter 131, the important point is chronological and causal. In 1975 many signatories hoped to stabilize the existing political division; by the later 1970s some citizens were using the same agreement to question how the divided order functioned. A diplomatic document acquired new users and meanings beyond its negotiators' initial intentions. Its influence was neither a guarantee of reform nor an insignificant rhetorical gesture. It became one of several channels through which the legitimacy of authoritarian rule could be challenged.

Section 131.18

The Breakdown of Détente

Disputes over regional conflicts and Soviet intermediate range missiles eroded détente. SALT II was signed in June 1979 but not ratified by the United States. NATO's dual track decision of December 12, 1979, paired negotiations with planned missile deployments, and the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan began that month. Large peace movements formed, and deployments began in 1983. Diplomatic and economic contacts continued.

The erosion of détente did not have a single cause or a universally agreed starting date. American and Soviet leaders disagreed about the meaning of strategic parity, competition outside Europe, and the military systems not covered by earlier agreements. Soviet modernization of intermediate-range forces, especially the SS-20 missile, alarmed NATO governments concerned about weapons capable of striking European targets. Moscow, in turn, portrayed NATO modernization as evidence that the West intended to undermine the balance of power. Each side presented its actions as necessary responses to the other. The competing narratives mattered politically, but historians should examine actual deployments, alliance decisions, and negotiations rather than accept either description uncritically.

SALT II was signed by Jimmy Carter and Leonid Brezhnev in Vienna in June 1979. It was not ratified by the United States Senate, and growing American political opposition made its future uncertain even before the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in December. An unratified treaty is historically different from one that had completed the constitutional steps required for entry into force. Nevertheless, its negotiation shows that arms-control diplomacy and heightened rivalry could operate simultaneously. The late 1970s were not divided into a period of perfect cooperation followed by a moment when every diplomatic channel suddenly closed.

NATO's decision of December 12, 1979, was described as dual-track because it combined plans to deploy American ground-launched cruise missiles and Pershing II systems in Europe with an offer to negotiate limits on intermediate-range nuclear forces. The official communiqué specified 108 Pershing II launchers and 464 cruise missiles in the planned force. Supporters argued that modernization would make NATO's deterrence credible while negotiations might limit deployments. Opponents warned that introducing or replacing weapons would increase the danger of nuclear escalation on European soil and normalize the idea of a limited nuclear war. France did not participate in the special ministerial meeting in the same way as integrated-command members, a reminder that the alliance was not institutionally uniform.

Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in December 1979 further damaged relations with the West, even though the conflict was outside Europe. The episode influenced American domestic debates, sanctions and sporting boycotts, military policy, and perceptions of Soviet intentions. In Europe, it added to fears already generated by missiles and international crises. During the early 1980s, large peace demonstrations opposed NATO deployments and called for disarmament. Participants included religious organizations, trade unions, environmentalists, students, social democrats, and independents, many of whom also criticized Soviet arsenals. Antinuclear activism should not be treated as simply aligned with one bloc or as uniform in its diplomatic priorities.

NATO began the planned deployments in 1983 after negotiations failed to deliver an agreed alternative. European citizens confronted a strategic paradox: their governments claimed the weapons would prevent war, while protesters argued that they made the continent a more dangerous battlefield. Neither argument can be evaluated only through the number of missiles, because credibility, command systems, perceptions, and public legitimacy were also at stake. Diplomatic relations and some economic ties continued despite intensified tension. The chapter's final Cold War sections should therefore describe deterioration, not total diplomatic rupture, and should reserve the later negotiations and transformations under Gorbachev for Chapter 132.

The debate over intermediate-range weapons also exposed differences in democratic process. NATO decisions were negotiated among governments, but deployment sites were located in particular towns and regions whose residents did not all share the strategic calculations. Demonstrators argued about consent, the environmental and social costs of military installations, and whether nuclear deterrence depended on threatening civilian populations. Government supporters countered that deterrence prevented a conventional conflict or coercive Soviet advantage. The debate reached parliaments, churches, universities, and households. Its social history belongs beside the diplomatic history because public willingness to sustain alliance policy influenced what governments could implement in practice.

Section 131.19

Southern Europe and the Changing Political Map

Greece joined the European Communities in 1981, and Portugal and Spain joined in 1986. The first direct elections to the European Parliament took place in June 1979. Membership was supported by and connected to democratization but did not cause it. Chapter 133 treats institutions.

The return of constitutional democracy to Greece, Portugal, and Spain altered the political map of western Europe. For much of the postwar period, European institutions had developed alongside authoritarian regimes on the continent's southern edge. By the early 1980s all three countries possessed elected governments and constitutional frameworks that could support participation in broader European cooperation. Their routes to that position were markedly different. Greece emerged from a military crisis and rapidly restored parliamentary government; Portugal experienced prolonged revolutionary contention; and Spain pursued reform through a mixture of institutional bargaining, civic mobilization, and pressure from inherited authoritarian forces.

The relationships between democracy and European institutions had two directions. Democratic governments regarded membership and association as ways to secure international recognition, support investment, and reinforce the expectation that constitutional rules would be respected. European partners in turn saw the consolidation of southern democracies as part of a more stable continent. Greece joined the European Communities in January 1981, while Portugal and Spain entered in January 1986 after negotiations. The difference between the date of application, the accession treaty, and actual membership matters when tracing the sequence. Community institutions created incentives and support, but they did not themselves overthrow dictatorships or write national constitutions.

The first direct elections to the European Parliament in June 1979 provided another marker of changing political participation. Before then, members of the European Parliamentary Assembly had been delegated from national parliaments. Direct election introduced a distinct representative process, although the European Parliament's powers at the time were far more limited than those it later acquired. These events belong to the wider history of integration examined in Chapter 133. Here they demonstrate that the institutional setting in which Europe's democracies operated was changing at the same time as domestic constitutional settlements and economic policies.

Comparisons should avoid ranking Portugal, Greece, and Spain as simple success stories. Portuguese constitutional politics retained arguments over revolutionary social change; Greek parties struggled over military legacy, Cyprus, and their relationship to NATO; and Spain faced regional autonomy disputes, political violence, and economic instability. The presence of elections did not eliminate conflict. The decisive difference from dictatorship was the possibility of contesting those issues through representative institutions, independent organizations, and law. Democratic consolidation can be measured through repeated peaceful transfers of power, civilian control of armed forces, and the capacity to protect legal rights during crisis, rather than through the absence of social tension.

These transformations also affected foreign-policy choices. Southern European governments reconsidered relations with the United States, NATO, neighboring states, and the European Communities. Popular memories of dictatorship shaped debates about military bases, political independence, and membership. Migrants who had worked in northern Europe brought back ideas and expectations, while new investment and tourism linked the region more closely to wider markets. Such connections had begun under dictatorship but now took place in a different political context. The early 1980s therefore represented an expansion of Europe's democratic space without an end to the continent's larger Cold War division.

Section 131.20

Historical Synthesis

Détente reduced some confrontation while leaving alliances and systems intact. The three democratic transitions differed in timing, process, and outcome. Oil shocks and inflation challenged the postwar boom's assumptions. By the early 1980s, southern dictatorships had become democracies, economic policy was being reconsidered, human rights had entered East West diplomacy, and nuclear confrontation had intensified. Chapter 132 follows what came next, without making it inevitable.

The decade between 1969 and the early 1980s did not yield a single trajectory toward peace, democracy, prosperity, or crisis. Diplomatic negotiations reduced some immediate risks and established rules for contact between states that remained ideological rivals. Southern European citizens and political leaders dismantled three dictatorships, but their methods differed and consolidation remained contested. Economic changes undermined confidence that postwar growth would continue indefinitely, and political parties responded with competing programs rather than one universal solution. The same continent experienced more democratic participation and renewed anxiety about nuclear confrontation. This coexistence, rather than a neat succession of eras, is the central historical lesson of Chapter 131.

Ostpolitik demonstrated how accepting existing borders and authorities for diplomatic purposes could improve contact across a divided country without endorsing every feature of its political system. The Helsinki Final Act similarly allowed governments to pursue territorial stability while recognizing principles that dissidents would later invoke. These agreements illustrate a distinction between what leaders intend when they negotiate and what later political actors make of the text. They were neither meaningless nor sufficient to guarantee peace or freedom. Their historical significance developed through implementation, interpretation, and controversy over time.

The transitions in Portugal, Greece, and Spain reveal several routes to the reestablishment of democracy. Military officers initiated the Portuguese overthrow, but popular mobilization and constitutional bargaining shaped the new order. Greece's junta collapsed amid the Cyprus crisis, and a civilian leadership rapidly restored parties and elections while the regional conflict endured. Spain's leaders combined reform from within with demands from workers, neighborhood groups, political organizations, and regional movements, then survived the armed challenge of 1981. Across the three cases, ending dictatorship was only the beginning; institutions had to sustain pluralism and the possibility of lawful opposition.

Economic disruption complicated these achievements. Inflation reduced real incomes, industrial restructuring threatened familiar occupations, and unemployment placed new burdens on households. Governments could not simply reproduce the fiscal and employment policies of the previous boom. Conservative, socialist, social democratic, Christian democratic, and liberal parties adopted different approaches, each distributing costs and opportunities unevenly. Political violence placed additional pressure on institutions expected to protect citizens without abandoning legal principles. The quality of democracy was thus tested not only at the moment of constitutional founding but during economic hardship and public insecurity.

By 1983 the Soviet and Western alliances still confronted one another, and a new missile confrontation had energized peace movements across Europe. Greece had joined the European Communities, Portugal and Spain were negotiating entry, and direct elections to the European Parliament had begun. The language of Helsinki circulated among human-rights activists whose governments sometimes punished them for invoking it. It would be a mistake to describe this landscape as already moving inevitably toward 1989. Contemporary actors faced genuine uncertainty about nuclear war, the future of communist rule, and the durability of democratic settlements. Chapter 132 follows the later developments that changed those calculations; the present chapter ends with a Europe transformed, but not resolved.

The term late Cold War can obscure the absence of a common clock. A Portuguese citizen in 1974 might have understood the central political problem as democracy after dictatorship and war, while a West German confronting the missiles of 1983 might have regarded nuclear survival as paramount. For a British steelworker, employment and inflation could dominate political choices; for a Soviet dissident, the gap between Helsinki commitments and police repression was decisive. These experiences belonged to the same European decade but were not felt in the same way. A convincing synthesis compares simultaneous processes without claiming that every society traveled the same path or that one event supplied the explanation for all the others.

Chapter Summary

The European political order of the 1970s developed in several directions at once. Diplomatic negotiations moderated some forms of confrontation while preserving rival alliances. The governments that signed agreements often sought stability, but citizens and activists put some of those documents to unforeseen uses.

Détente grew from strategic danger, nuclear parity, economic calculation, and European demands for a manageable continental order. It was not a declaration of shared ideology. Soviet and Western leaders continued military modernization and competition in regions beyond Europe.

Ostpolitik showed how West German diplomacy could accept existing realities without abandoning a longer-term sense of German unity. The Moscow and Warsaw treaties, the Berlin arrangements, and the Basic Treaty improved practical contacts while leaving the Wall in place.

The Helsinki Final Act joined security principles to commitments about human rights and humanitarian contacts. Though politically rather than legally binding, its words later supplied monitoring groups with standards against which to judge state repression.

Portugal’s dictatorship collapsed when organized officers confronted a regime exhausted by colonial wars. The revolution expanded far beyond military action through popular participation, party organization, and disputes over economic ownership and the future of the state.

Greece’s junta fell during the Cyprus crisis. Constitutional restoration under Karamanlis advanced through elections, a referendum, accountability for repression, and the 1975 Constitution, even while Cyprus remained a source of diplomatic and human suffering.

Spain’s transformation after Franco depended on political reform through inherited institutions, organized social pressure, constitutional compromise, and the ability of elected leaders to survive a coup attempt. The PSOE victory of 1982 demonstrated peaceful electoral alternation.

The collapse of dollar–gold convertibility and the oil-price shocks exposed weaknesses in the monetary and economic structure of the postwar boom. Stagflation, unemployment, and industrial restructuring affected countries and social groups unevenly.

Western European parties debated inflation, public expenditure, monetary restraint, welfare protection, and industrial restructuring. Different outcomes in Britain, France, West Germany, and Italy show why economic crisis did not dictate one political ideology.

Political violence tested democratic institutions and drew attention to the competing obligations to protect citizens, enforce law, and preserve civil liberties. The actions of militant minorities should not be confused with the wider movements of the preceding decade.

After 1979, arms-control disappointments, regional crises, and intermediate-range missiles sharpened the Cold War. NATO’s dual-track decision combined an offer to negotiate with planned deployments, prompting intense disagreement and large peace movements.

By the early 1980s southern European dictatorship had given way to constitutional government, but Europe remained divided strategically and affected by economic uncertainty. The chapter closes without claiming that the later events of 1989 were inevitable.

Chapter Reflection

Between 1969 and 1983 European history did not move along one track. Diplomats negotiated security frameworks, citizens defeated dictatorships, workers and households faced new economic insecurity, and campaigners struggled over the risks of nuclear war. These processes were simultaneous but not identical. To understand the decade, historians must attend to the different institutions and experiences through which change occurred.

The central question is what makes a political order legitimate when stability is uncertain. Treaties can lower danger but cannot guarantee compliance; a constitution can set rules but cannot make officers obey them; elections can create a mandate but cannot eliminate unemployment or violence. The durability of European democracy depended on institutions capable of managing conflict while permitting genuine opposition.

As Chapter 132 turns to Solidarity, Soviet reform, and 1989, retain the contingency of the early 1980s. Nobody negotiating the 1979 missile dispute or preserving Spain’s parliament in February 1981 could know the subsequent outcome. The historian’s task is to explain choices within the constraints and uncertainties of the time.
