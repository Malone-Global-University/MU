Chapter Opening

In 1945 Germany had no independent central government or army and could not exercise sovereignty under occupation. Four foreign powers assumed supreme governmental authority without annexing the country. Within four years there were two German states, competing currencies, a new Western security alliance, and a divided Berlin at the most exposed point of contact between rival European systems.

On June 5, 1945, four generals representing the United States, the Soviet Union, Great Britain, and France signed a declaration in Berlin announcing that their governments had assumed supreme authority over Germany. The document did not annex the country, and it did not divide it into permanent pieces. It created a temporary, joint, military form of government for a society whose cities lay in ruins, whose population included millions of displaced people, and whose conquerors had very different histories, interests, and political systems. This chapter asks how that arrangement of shared authority became, by October 1949, the separation of Germany into the Federal Republic of Germany in the west and the German Democratic Republic in the east.

The answer cannot be found in a single decision. No conference in 1945 divided Germany into two states, and most Allied leaders, in public at least, continued to speak of a unified German future. Division emerged instead from a chain of disagreements and responses: over reparations and the administration of the German economy, over what democracy and political reconstruction should mean, over security against a revived Germany and against one another, and over the future of eastern and southeastern Europe. Each measure taken in one zone altered the options available in the others. Each side tended to read the other's defensive measures as proof of aggressive intent, a dynamic that students will meet repeatedly in this chapter.

The chapter belongs to Volume VI, which follows Europe from the ruins of 1945 through division, integration, and the end of communist rule. It opens Part XIX, Cold War Europe, immediately after Chapter 123, which examined destroyed cities, displaced populations, war crimes trials, and the first phase of reconstruction. Chapter 124 is concerned with the political and diplomatic structure that formed on those ruins. Chapter 125 will turn to the consolidation of Soviet-dominated political systems in eastern Europe and to the later development of military alliances, and Chapter 126 will examine the Marshall Plan and the western European boom in depth. The present chapter therefore treats the Marshall Plan, the communist takeover in Czechoslovakia, and the creation of NATO only as far as they explain the division of Germany and the beginning of the European Cold War.

Germany stood at the center of the story for reasons both material and symbolic. Its coal and steel region, the Ruhr, had been the industrial foundation of two German attempts to dominate Europe by force. Its population, even after the war, was larger than that of any other western or central European state. Its capital, Berlin, lay roughly a hundred and ten miles inside the Soviet occupation zone yet was jointly occupied by all four powers. And its political future touched the central fears of every victor: the Soviet fear of a third invasion from the west, the French fear of a revived neighbor, the British fear of an economic burden they could not afford, and the American fear of a Europe too impoverished and unstable to resist communist political pressure.

Historians reconstruct this story from a wide range of evidence. The formal record includes the Berlin Declaration, the Potsdam Protocol, Allied Control Council proclamations, treaties, and constitutional texts. Diplomatic telegrams, such as those of George Kennan and Nikolai Novikov, reveal how officials interpreted one another. Military government records, airlift operational reports, and rationing and municipal files document administration on the ground. Newspapers, radio broadcasts, posters, speeches, memoirs, and diaries show how ordinary Berliners and German politicians experienced events. Since the early 1990s, partial opening of Soviet and east German archives has allowed historians to test older Western accounts against evidence from the other side, though access remains uneven and some crucial records are still closed.

Students should keep several cautions in mind. First, the Cold War was not predetermined in May 1945, and the participants did not know how events would unfold. Second, Germans were not only objects of Allied policy. Politicians such as Konrad Adenauer, Kurt Schumacher, Ernst Reuter, Walter Ulbricht, and Wilhelm Pieck, together with voters, trade unionists, officials, and ordinary Berliners, shaped the outcome, usually under severe constraints. Third, the Berlin Blockade of 1948 to 1949 and the airlift that answered it should be understood as a political and logistical confrontation rather than a simple morality tale. Finally, a divided Germany in 1949 was not yet the Germany of the fortified frontier or the Berlin Wall; those belong to later decades and later chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the legal and political basis of Allied occupation, distinguishing occupation, sovereignty, and annexation, and identify the borders of the four zones, the Berlin sectors, and the territories under Polish administration.
  2. Compare Soviet, American, British, and French objectives for Germany and assess where documented policy ends and historians' inference about motives begins.
  3. Trace the gradual breakdown of the Allied Control Council and four-power administration between 1945 and March 1948.
  4. Analyze the Kennan and Novikov telegrams as contemporary perceptions rather than neutral accounts of the other side's intentions.
  5. Describe the origins of containment in the Greek and Turkish crises and the Marshall proposal, and explain how economic recovery, security, and ideology became intertwined.
  6. Distinguish the stages of economic and political separation: Bizonia, the creation of the SED, the western currency reform, the Frankfurt Documents, and the formation of the two German states.
  7. Evaluate Soviet and Western objectives in the Berlin Blockade, and explain why the airlift succeeded as a logistical and political response.
  8. Reconstruct everyday life in blockaded Berlin and assess the roles of Berlin civilians as historical actors.
  9. Compare the institutions, legitimacy, and international positions of the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic at the end of 1949.
  10. Assess traditionalist, revisionist, and post-revisionist explanations of the origins of the Cold War, and evaluate them against the evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Was four-power occupation ever a workable mechanism for governing Germany as a whole, or did incompatible objectives undermine it from the beginning?
  2. How did decisions taken within single occupation zones come to shape the future of Germany as a whole?
  3. To what extent did each side's defensive measures appear threatening to the other, and how did that dynamic make cooperation harder?
  4. What can historians learn from the Kennan and Novikov telegrams, and what can they not establish from them?
  5. Why did events in Greece, Turkey, and Czechoslovakia matter for the future of Germany?
  6. Why did a dispute over a currency become a test of the European balance of power?
  7. How did German politicians, parties, and voters shape the outcome, and where were their choices limited by occupation authorities?
  8. Did the airlift resolve the Berlin crisis, or did it stabilize a division the victorious powers could not reconcile?

Historical Context

The Allied powers did not come to Germany in 1945 with a single plan. The "Grand Alliance" of the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union had been built for one purpose, the defeat of Nazi Germany, and it held together through the strain of a long war chiefly because each partner needed the others. Differences were present from the start: over the timing of a second front in western Europe, over the future of Poland, and over the treatment of Germany. At the Tehran, Yalta, and Potsdam conferences the leaders agreed on the principle of unconditional surrender, on joint occupation, and on the broad goals of demilitarization and denazification, while deferring many specific questions to later negotiation. At Yalta in February 1945 they also agreed that France should receive a zone, carved from the American and British areas.

The wartime experience shaped postwar perceptions. The Soviet Union had suffered perhaps twenty-seven million deaths, military and civilian combined, though estimates vary and cannot be stated with precision, and large parts of its western territory had been devastated. Soviet policymakers drew from this, and from the interwar history examined in Chapters 114 to 116, the conclusion that security required friendly governments on the Soviet western frontier and a Germany unable to threaten it again. The United States had emerged from the war with its territory untouched, its economy vastly enlarged, and a monopoly, as yet, on atomic weapons. American planners, remembering the failures of the interwar settlement, wanted an open international economy and a stable western Europe. Britain, victorious but nearly bankrupt, was struggling to meet its imperial and European commitments, and France, liberated and humiliated, sought security first against Germany.

Germany itself was in a condition few modern societies have faced. Its cities had been heavily bombed, its transport network was wrecked, and its currency, the Reichsmark, retained official value but little purchasing power because wartime controls had suppressed inflation rather than eliminated it. Food shortages were severe, especially in the winters of 1945 to 1946 and 1946 to 1947. Millions of expellees and refugees from eastern territories were arriving in the zones, as discussed in Chapter 123. The Nazi state and party had disappeared, but millions of Germans had belonged to its organizations, and the Allies had to decide who could be trusted to administer anything. The legacy of the Weimar Republic and its collapse, examined in Chapters 114 and 115, also weighed on German politicians who tried to rebuild democratic institutions.

The term "Cold War" was in circulation, though not yet standard, in the late 1940s. George Orwell used it in 1945 in an essay about the atomic bomb, and the American financier Bernard Baruch and the journalist Walter Lippmann gave it wide currency in 1947. Historians use it for the prolonged confrontation between the Soviet Union and the western powers, led by the United States, which stopped short of direct war between the principal antagonists. Its origins remain a subject of serious debate, discussed in Section 124.20. This chapter uses the term for the period when the confrontation took recognizable form in Europe, while avoiding the assumption that the later bipolar structure was already complete in 1945.

The chapter's evidence also requires care. Official documents record positions that governments wished to place on the record, and diplomatic telegrams reflect the assumptions and career incentives of their authors. Memoirs written after events may sharpen retrospective judgments, and propaganda from every side distorted public understanding. The evidence for Soviet policy was, until the 1990s, much thinner than that for Western policy, and historians still disagree about how far newly available documents confirm older views. Throughout the chapter, students should ask who is speaking, to whom, and with what knowledge, and distinguish documented decisions from interpretations of hidden motives.

Section 124.1

Germany after the War: Occupation, Sovereignty, and the Allied Settlement

Germany's surrender was unconditional. A military capitulation was signed at Reims on May 7, 1945, and ratified in Berlin on May 8, taking effect late that evening, which is why the Soviet Union marks victory on May 9. No German authority remained with the power to negotiate. Admiral Karl Dönitz's short-lived government at Flensburg, named by Hitler as his successor, was arrested on May 23. On June 5, the commanders in chief of the four victorious powers, Dwight Eisenhower, Bernard Montgomery, Georgy Zhukov, and Jean de Lattre de Tassigny, signed the Berlin Declaration. In it the four governments assumed supreme authority over Germany, including all the powers of the German government, high command, and state, municipal, and local authorities. The declaration stated that this did not amount to annexation.

The distinction mattered then and later. Occupation is a temporary exercise of authority by foreign powers over a territory, while sovereignty is the legal right of a state to govern itself, and annexation is the incorporation of territory into another state. The Allies held that Germany continued to exist as a legal entity even though it lacked a government, and they used the borders of December 31, 1937 as a reference point for administration, pending a final peace settlement. That settlement was never concluded in 1945, and Germans in both later states would repeatedly invoke the provisional character of the occupation as evidence that division was not final.

The geography of occupation was the product of wartime planning in the European Advisory Commission and of the Yalta agreements. The Soviet zone covered the east and center, including Saxony, Thuringia, Saxony-Anhalt, Brandenburg, and Mecklenburg. The British zone lay in the north and northwest, including the Ruhr industrial area. The American zone covered Bavaria, Hesse, and parts of southwestern Germany, together with the port enclave of Bremen. The French zone was carved from American and British areas in the southwest and along the Rhine, and the Saar was soon treated separately by France. Berlin, deep in the Soviet zone, was divided into four sectors. Territories east of the Oder and Neisse rivers, together with the area around Stettin, were placed under Polish administration pending a peace conference, and the northern part of East Prussia, including Königsberg, passed under Soviet control. These territorial arrangements, along with the expulsions of German populations discussed in Chapter 123, shaped the postwar map more drastically than the zonal boundaries within the remaining territory.

At Potsdam, held from July 17 to August 2, 1945, with Truman, Stalin, and, after the British election, Attlee, the powers agreed on common principles. Germany was to be disarmed and demilitarized, Nazi institutions abolished and war criminals punished, the education and judicial systems democratized, and political life decentralized, with local self-government and, eventually, central administrative departments under Allied control. The economy was to be treated as a single unit, with industrial capacity restricted to what peacetime needs required. These principles are often summarized as the four D's, disarmament or demilitarization, denazification, democratization, and decentralization, to which reparations were added as a fifth concern. The Potsdam Protocol also provided that each power would take reparations from its own zone, with the Soviet Union receiving additional equipment from the western zones in return for food and raw materials from its own, a compromise that left much open.

Applying shared principles in four different zones produced divergent practice. The Americans conducted systematic questionnaire-based denazification and later transferred much of the task to German tribunals. The British, with large industrial and administrative burdens in a hungry, crowded zone, tended to emphasize pragmatic administration. The French, whose own territory had been occupied and exploited, prioritized security and extracted resources from their zone. In the Soviet zone, Nazi officials and large landowners were removed from their positions rapidly, while the Soviet security services also held many Germans in special camps, some of them former Nazi concentration camp sites, where Soviet records document roughly 43,000 deaths other than executions during 1945–1950, chiefly in conditions of deprivation and disease. The ideological assumptions in each zone, liberal capitalist in the west and Marxist-Leninist in the east, meant that "democratization" did not mean the same thing to every occupier.

Germany's significance for Europe explains why these differences mattered so much. The Ruhr, with its coal and steel, was essential for any European recovery, yet Germany's neighbors feared its revival. A German economy held down for security reasons would be unable to feed its own people or contribute to continental recovery, while a revived German economy would raise old fears. The same question appeared in every later dispute: how to balance control and reconstruction, and who would control the balance.

Historical problem. Historians still ask whether occupation was ever truly a temporary mechanism for joint administration, or whether incompatible objectives were undermining national reunification from the beginning. The evidence suggests that both possibilities were open in 1945, but that the choices made within the zones during the first eighteen months steadily reduced the room for a unified outcome.

Historical Vocabulary
Occupation Zone, Four-Power Authority, and the Allied Control Council

An occupation zone was a defined territory in which one of the four Allied powers exercised direct military government. Zones were administrative divisions of a country that the Allies still regarded as one, not separate states. Four-power authority referred to the joint supreme authority of the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, and France, declared on June 5, 1945, over Germany as a whole. The Allied Control Council, seated in Berlin, was the body through which the four commanders in chief were to coordinate policy for Germany as a whole, acting by unanimity.

These terms matter because modern English usage can mislead. "Zone" does not imply a permanent border, and "control" in the title of the Council did not mean that it ruled the zones directly; it coordinated while each commander remained supreme in his own zone. The gap between joint authority in theory and separate authority in practice is the key to the chapter.

Section 124.2

Soviet Security Interests and Western Strategic Priorities

For Soviet leaders, Germany was the central security question of the postwar world. Twice within a generation, in 1914 and in 1941, German armies had invaded Russian and Soviet territory, and the second invasion had killed millions and devastated cities and farmland. Stalin and his advisers sought several things at once: compensation through reparations, a Germany unable to rearm, influence over its political and economic future, and a belt of friendly governments in eastern Europe. They also viewed the war's outcome through Marxist-Leninist ideology, which taught that capitalist states were prone to rivalry and might again turn against the Soviet Union. Documented Soviet policy in 1945 did not call for dividing Germany permanently. In fact, Soviet statements early in the occupation stressed preserving German unity and central administration, partly because a unified Germany offered access to the Ruhr and to reparations from the whole country.

The Western powers held overlapping but different priorities. The United States, under Truman from April 1945, sought to avoid a repetition of the interwar failure of reparations and economic collapse, favored restoring a functioning German economy within a multilateral international system, and promoted political pluralism. Early American policy had briefly entertained deindustrialization proposals associated with the Morgenthau Plan, but practical needs, the hunger and unemployment of the zones, and the cost of occupation pushed American officials toward recovery. Britain, represented by Attlee's Labour government and by Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin, faced an enormous balance of payments problem and bore the cost of feeding a densely populated, industrial zone, which made economic recovery in Germany a financial necessity as well as a strategic aim. France, under governments headed by Charles de Gaulle and then by others, wanted the Ruhr and Rhineland placed under international or French control, the Saar tied to France, and no strong central German government, and it used its veto in the Control Council to block central German agencies.

The principal personalities included Stalin, Truman, Attlee, and Bevin, together with American Secretary of State James Byrnes, who served until January 1947, and the military governors: Lucius Clay for the United States, Sholto Douglas and later Brian Robertson for Britain, Pierre Koenig for France, and Zhukov, then Vasily Sokolovsky, for the Soviet Union. Military governors were not merely executors. Clay in particular held wide discretion, and in May 1946 he halted reparations deliveries from the American zone to the Soviet Union when the Soviet side would not agree to treat Germany as an economic unit, a decision that exemplified how zonal choices affected the whole.

Disagreements crystallized around specific issues. Reparations were the sharpest. The Soviets wanted large deliveries of industrial equipment and current production, including from the western zones, while the Western powers, especially the Americans, did not wish to finance Germany's reparations indirectly through their own aid to feed the population. The Ruhr was another. Soviet leaders asked for a share in its control, which the Western powers refused, while France sought to separate it from Germany. Economic unity was a third; the Potsdam principle of treating Germany as a single economic unit required central agencies for transport, trade, and finance, which France blocked and which the Soviets and Western powers disagreed about in detail. The costs of occupation and the question of who would feed the western zones, which ran food deficits, added to the strain.

In September 1946 Byrnes delivered a speech at Stuttgart announcing that American forces would remain in Germany as long as other occupying forces did and signaling support for German self-government and economic recovery. Western policymakers increasingly believed that Soviet conduct in eastern Europe, including the establishment of communist-dominated governments, demonstrated expansionist intent. Soviet officials interpreted Western moves, such as the American economic initiatives and the creation of joint administrative arrangements in the west, as efforts to build a western bloc and to exclude Soviet influence from Germany's industrial heartland. Each side could cite evidence for its fears, but neither could see the other's internal deliberations.

Accuracy requirement. Historians distinguish carefully between what governments said and did, which can be documented, and what leaders secretly wanted, which has to be inferred. Stalin's long-term intentions for Germany, in particular, remain debated, since the archival record is incomplete and his statements varied with circumstance. The evidence supports the view that Soviet policy was flexible and often improvised in 1945 and 1946, though it consistently subordinated German questions to Soviet security and economic interests.

Section 124.3

The Allied Control Council: Four-Power Government and Its Limitations

The Allied Control Council held its first meeting on July 30, 1945, in the Kammergericht building in the Schöneberg district of Berlin. It consisted of the four commanders in chief, supported by a Coordinating Committee and directorates covering subjects such as transport, finance, and political affairs. The Berlin city government was overseen by the Allied Kommandatura, composed of the four sector commandants, which began operating in July 1945 as western troops entered their sectors. The two bodies were distinct: the Control Council addressed Germany as a whole, while the Kommandatura governed Berlin.

The Council could act only by unanimity. This rule reflected the equality of the victors, but it gave each member a veto over measures affecting the whole country. Each commander also retained supreme authority within his own zone, so that the Council's decisions needed to be implemented through four separate occupation administrations. In practice, decisions could be adopted but interpreted or applied differently, and zonal authorities could take actions that bypassed the Council entirely.

The Council did produce real results. It issued proclamations and laws abolishing Nazi legislation and organizations, regulated matters such as the Nazi party's dissolution and the status of the Prussian state, which it declared abolished in 1947, and agreed on directives about denazification and the fate of war criminals. It also coordinated much routine administration, such as postal services, air safety, and the management of the Spandau prison for the major Nuremberg defendants. The Council's Berlin Air Safety Center, which coordinated air traffic in the corridors, continued to function, a point of great importance later. Cooperation in technical matters often persisted when political cooperation was failing, which complicates any simple story of immediate collapse.

The central disputes were nonetheless severe. Reparations deliveries were the clearest example. The Potsdam arrangement relied on assumptions about the balance of exchanges between zones, but the Soviet zone removed industrial plants, rail track, and other assets on a large scale from its own territory, and Western authorities, noting that their zones needed food that the east, a food-producing region, was not delivering, concluded that the economic unity principle was not being honored. Industrial dismantling in the western zones, which was designed to remove war-making capacity, also generated German resentment and Western debate. Efforts to set up central German administrative agencies were blocked chiefly by France, though the Soviets and Americans also disagreed about their structure and powers. Different approaches to trade and to political reform, from land reform to party licensing, widened the gaps.

The logic of zones pushed Germany apart even without formal decisions. If one zone introduced land reform, nationalized heavy industry, or licensed particular parties, its economic and political structure diverged from the others. When American and British authorities moved to unify their zonal economies and when the Soviets built central German administrative bodies in their zone, each step created facts that the others had to respond to. As a result, the Council's failures accumulated rather than arrived in a single moment.

The institutional turning point came on March 20, 1948, when Marshal Sokolovsky left a Council meeting after objecting to Western discussion of the London conference, which was preparing arrangements for western Germany. The Soviet delegation did not return. Western governments continued to maintain that the Council retained legal authority, and the Soviet Union did not formally renounce four-power rights, but the Council ceased to function as a governing body. The breakdown was gradual, cumulative, and contested, not the result of one event in 1949. Section 124.12 shows that the Kommandatura in Berlin followed a similar path three months later.

Section 124.4

Berlin: Four Sectors inside the Soviet Occupation Zone

Berlin's legal and geographic position deserves its own treatment because it became the focal point of the crisis. The city had been the capital of Prussia and of the German Reich, and in 1945 it lay roughly a hundred and ten miles by road from the nearest point of the western zones, entirely surrounded by the Soviet occupation zone. The wartime planners had assigned it a special status: it was to be jointly governed by the four powers, who divided it into sectors although it was geographically surrounded by the Soviet zone and remained under distinct four-power authority. The Americans took the southwestern districts, including Zehlendorf and Kreuzberg, the British took the central western districts such as Charlottenburg and Spandau, the French took the northwestern districts of Reinickendorf and Wedding, and the Soviets held the east, including the historic center.

Soviet troops had captured the city in May 1945, and Western forces entered their sectors only in July, after a negotiated exchange in which the western zones of Thuringia and Saxony, held temporarily by American troops, were handed over to Soviet control. Access arrangements were agreed in a mixture of formal and informal understandings. The three air corridors, running from Hamburg, Hanover, and Frankfurt, were established by written agreement of the Control Council on November 30, 1945, each twenty miles wide. Surface access by road, rail, and canal was based on more limited and less precisely documented arrangements, and the Western powers would later argue that these rights flowed from the same occupation status. This asymmetry was legally and politically significant: the air corridors rested on the clearest written foundation, and the Western response to the blockade drew on that fact.

The city itself was in terrible condition. Bombing and the final battle had destroyed many buildings, and rubble clearance was done largely by hand, with women playing a major role in the so-called Trümmerfrauen. Transport networks were damaged, electricity and gas supplies were erratic, and housing was scarce. Its population, about three million, included many who had fled from the east. The Berlin municipal government was restored under Soviet and Allied supervision. Political parties were allowed from June 1945, and the city's first postwar administrations were formed within the sectors but under an overall structure that had to function across them.

Berlin was strategically vulnerable but politically important to all four powers. It was vulnerable because the western sectors could be isolated by controlling the surface routes through the Soviet zone. It was important because Berlin was a symbol of Germany's unity, a base for observation and influence in the Soviet sphere, and, for the Soviets, a presence of the West inside territory they considered theirs. For the Western powers, abandoning the city would mean abandoning its inhabitants, many of whom had shown strong political support for the Western-aligned Social Democrats, and would signal weakness to other European governments. For the Soviets, a Western enclave in their zone was an irritant, a source of refugees and information, and a lever against any separate western German state.

The relationships among the city government, the Allied authorities, and the political parties became critical. Berliners wanted food, fuel, housing, and a functioning city, and their political parties competed under four occupation commands. The Allied Kommandatura had to approve major municipal decisions by unanimity, giving the Soviet commandant a veto over actions that the western commandants and a majority of Berliners might support. This arrangement worked only as long as all four powers wanted it to.

Essential safeguard. West Berlin was not part of West Germany. It did not constitute a state of the Federal Republic, and its constitutional status differed from that of the federal states. Its delegates in the later federal parliament had restricted voting rights. The Western powers governed it under occupation authority, which they continued to claim well after the Federal Republic was founded.

The Deterioration of the Grand Alliance and the Origins of Containment

1946–1948

Section 124.5

Competing Interpretations of the Postwar World: Kennan, Novikov, and Mutual Suspicion

By early 1946 the diplomatic atmosphere had cooled. Disputes over Poland, Iran, the Turkish Straits, reparations, and the control of atomic energy had accumulated, and public rhetoric had hardened. On February 9, 1946, Stalin gave an election speech in Moscow that Western observers read as emphasizing the incompatibility of capitalism and communism. It was in this setting that George Kennan, the senior American diplomat in the Moscow embassy, sent his famous telegram of February 22, 1946. The Treasury Department had asked why the Soviet Union seemed reluctant to join the International Monetary Fund and World Bank, and Kennan used the occasion to deliver an eight thousand word analysis of Soviet behavior.

Kennan argued that Soviet leaders operated from a traditional Russian sense of insecurity, reinforced by Marxist-Leninist ideology that portrayed the outside world as hostile. This outlook, he claimed, allowed the regime to justify its internal control and to demand constant sacrifice from its people. He judged the Soviet leadership impervious to reasoning on its own terms but sensitive to the logic of force, and he concluded that the Soviet Union was not likely to risk a major war but would exploit weaknesses wherever it could. The response he recommended was a firm, patient, and informed policy of resistance, combined with maintaining the health and self-confidence of Western societies. The telegram circulated widely in Washington and strengthened the case of officials who favored a tougher line.

On September 27, 1946, the Soviet ambassador in Washington, Nikolai Novikov, sent his own long assessment to Moscow. It was prepared, according to the evidence usually cited, at the request of Foreign Minister Molotov. Novikov argued that the United States, enriched and strengthened by the war, was seeking world supremacy, pursuing military preparations such as bases and a large navy, and building an anti-Soviet alignment with Britain. He interpreted American insistence on open markets and American atomic policy as expressions of monopoly capital's drive for expansion. His analysis also noted that the United States could not be certain of public support for war, which suggested that Soviet policy should be cautious but vigilant.

The two documents offer an instructive comparison. Each was written by a professional diplomat for his own government, each used an interpretive framework rooted in the author's national and ideological assumptions, and each drew on partial knowledge. Kennan had considerable experience of the Soviet Union but limited access to Soviet decision-making; Novikov understood the United States from within Washington but wrote under pressure to produce an analysis that fit Soviet ideological expectations. Neither text should be read as an objective description of the other government's intentions. Each is better understood as evidence of what its author and the readers in his own government believed, and of how those beliefs could become policy.

Kennan's analysis reached a much wider audience in July 1947, when he published an article titled "The Sources of Soviet Conduct" in the journal Foreign Affairs under the pseudonym "X." The article restated the argument and used the phrase "containment" for the recommended policy, a term that quickly became associated with American strategy. Kennan later complained that readers interpreted the policy as primarily military and global, whereas he had in mind chiefly political and economic resistance in areas of strategic importance. The relationship between the 1946 telegram and the 1947 article illustrates how a diplomatic assessment became a public doctrine, with its meaning shifting in the process.

Mutual suspicion could be self-reinforcing. A Western effort to strengthen economic recovery appeared to Moscow as an attempt to build an anti-Soviet bloc, and Soviet efforts to secure friendly governments in eastern Europe appeared to Western observers as the first step in a wider expansion. Each side's defensive measures were read as offensive, which prompted counter-measures, which confirmed the original suspicion. Social scientists later called this a security dilemma, a term that helps explain the dynamic without assigning exclusive blame. It does not, however, mean that every action was equally defensible or that the two sides' systems were morally equivalent, matters on which historians continue to disagree.

Section 124.6

The Iron Curtain, Political Conflict, and the Widening European Divide

On March 5, 1946, Winston Churchill, no longer prime minister but still a figure of enormous prestige, spoke at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, with President Truman on the platform. In a speech titled "The Sinews of Peace" he called for close Anglo-American cooperation and warned that an iron curtain had descended across the continent from the Baltic to the Adriatic, behind which lay the capitals of central and eastern Europe, subject to Soviet influence and, in many cases, to increasing control from Moscow. The phrase was not entirely new. Churchill himself had used it in a telegram to Truman in 1945, and German propaganda had used similar imagery earlier. But the Fulton speech gave it lasting public force.

The phrase was influential because it condensed a complex process into a single image, and controversial for the same reason. Stalin answered in the Soviet press, comparing Churchill to a warmonger, and many in Britain and the United States regarded the speech as premature or provocative. Others thought it described reality accurately. For historians, the Iron Curtain is best understood as a political and ideological concept describing a growing division, not as a physical barrier. The heavily fortified frontier that later separated the two Germanys developed in stages, beginning in the early 1950s and culminating in the Berlin Wall of 1961. None of this existed in 1946.

Developments in eastern Europe supplied the background. Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and, differently, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, had all experienced wartime occupation and then Soviet military presence. Communist parties, supported by Soviet authorities and often holding key ministries, such as interior and justice, moved to dominate coalition governments. Western governments regarded the political conditions in Poland and Romania, in particular, as violations of the Yalta commitment to free elections. Soviet officials argued that governments friendly to the Soviet Union were a legitimate security need and pointed to Western influence in Greece, Italy, and elsewhere. Chapter 123 treated the first phase of political reconstruction in these countries, and Chapter 125 will examine consolidation; this section addresses only how the conflicting claims fed the wider confrontation.

The competing claims to legitimacy were many. Soviet-backed governments claimed popular mandates based on antifascist resistance and social reform. Western governments claimed to represent parliamentary democracy and pluralism. Communist parties claimed to represent workers, and noncommunist opposition parties, such as the Polish Peasant Party led by Stanisław Mikołajczyk, claimed to represent the majority. Governments-in-exile, particularly the Polish government in London, claimed continuity with the prewar state but lost recognition as Western governments recognized the new authorities. All parties appealed to the language of democracy, but they used the term to describe different things.

Berlin offered an early test of how political conditions affected voters. At the end of March 1946 the Social Democratic Party of Berlin, which had resisted merger with the Communist Party, held a ballot of its members in the western sectors, where the Allied authorities permitted it, and a large majority rejected immediate fusion. The Soviet authorities did not allow the same vote in their sector. The municipal elections of October 20, 1946, held in all four sectors under the Kommandatura, produced a strong result for the Social Democrats, who won roughly half of the vote, with the Christian Democrats and the Liberal Democrats receiving smaller shares and the Socialist Unity Party, despite advantages in the Soviet sector, winning under a fifth. Since Berliners voted under conditions that were comparatively open across the whole city, the result was widely read as evidence that a merged socialist-communist party lacked majority support when voters had a genuine choice.

Propaganda, radio, and the press played a growing role. In the western sectors, the American-sponsored radio station RIAS, which began broadcasting in Berlin in 1946, offered a counterweight to Soviet-controlled Berlin radio. Newspapers licensed by each occupation authority reflected its political line. Diplomatic statements and public speeches, including Churchill's, Molotov's, Byrnes's, and Truman's, framed the competition as one between rival accounts of freedom and security. The Soviet side stressed antifascism, social justice, and the danger of Western militarism, while Western spokesmen stressed political freedom and the danger of totalitarian expansion.

Central problem. How did conflicting accounts of democracy, security, and political freedom shape European public opinion before the formal division of Germany? The short answer is that they gave each side a language in which its own conduct appeared defensive and legitimate, while the other's appeared aggressive, and that these perceptions made compromise politically costly for governments on both sides.

Section 124.7

Greece, Turkey, and the Truman Doctrine: The European Origins of American Containment

Greece had been occupied by Germany, Italy, and Bulgaria during the war, and its resistance movement had been dominated by the communist-led National Liberation Front and its military wing, ELAS. After liberation in 1944, British forces supported the returning royalist-leaning government, and fighting broke out in Athens in December 1944. The Varkiza Agreement of February 1945 temporarily ended the conflict, but political polarization, a contested election and plebiscite in 1946, and renewed violence led to a full civil war by 1946 to 1947. Communist-led guerrillas received support from neighboring Yugoslavia, Albania, and Bulgaria, while the Greek government depended heavily on British assistance. The Greek conflict was primarily an internal one with deep domestic roots, though external involvement was substantial.

Turkey faced a different kind of pressure. Turkey had remained neutral for most of the war, but the Soviet Union demanded in 1945 and 1946 revision of the regime governing the Turkish Straits under the Montreux Convention of 1936, with a share in their defense, and raised territorial claims in eastern Anatolia. The Straits connected the Black Sea to the Mediterranean, and Soviet leaders had long sought security in that area. Turkey did not face civil war, but it maintained a large army and was financially strained.

In February 1947 the British government informed Washington that it could no longer sustain its financial and military assistance to Greece and Turkey beyond the end of March. The notice reflected Britain's postwar economic crisis, which was severe in the harsh winter of 1946 to 1947, and the decision to reduce overseas commitments. American officials, who had been moving toward greater involvement, concluded that the United States would have to take Britain's place. On March 12, 1947, President Truman addressed a joint session of Congress and asked for $400 million in economic and military aid for Greece and Turkey. He framed the request as part of a broader policy, stating that the United States should support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures. Congress approved the aid in May.

The speech has been called the beginning of the Truman Doctrine and, with it, of American containment. Containment was, as Section 124.5 showed, an approach to resisting the expansion of Soviet influence, and in 1947 its practice was still developing. It was initially focused on Europe and the Mediterranean, and it combined economic aid with limited military assistance, not a global military strategy. Later, more global formulations, especially after 1950, extended the idea much further. Students should distinguish the specific, bounded commitment of 1947 from the broader worldwide containment policy that followed.

American aid had implications for many actors. For Britain, it marked a transfer of strategic leadership in the eastern Mediterranean to the United States, a reminder of Britain's reduced capacity, though British officials continued to cooperate closely. For the Soviet Union, it was evidence, in the Soviet reading, of an American effort to build a position in a region adjacent to Soviet borders. For Greece and Turkey, it provided resources and tied their governments to the Western alignment, with Greece becoming dependent on American advisers and Turkey later joining NATO. For other European governments, it signaled that the United States was prepared to commit resources to the continent.

Critics, then and later, have argued that Truman's rhetoric simplified complex domestic conflicts into a binary struggle between freedom and communism. The Greek government that received aid was far from democratic in its treatment of political opponents, and Soviet involvement in the Greek communists' campaign was, according to evidence that emerged later, more limited than the American presentation suggested; in fact, Stalin was cautious about the Greek insurgency and, in 1948, his break with Tito complicated it further. The Greek civil war ended in October 1949 with the defeat of the communist-led forces. Defenders of the policy have argued that Congress and the public needed a clear and compelling argument and that the underlying strategic concern was genuine, given Soviet pressure on Turkey and the weakness of the Greek state.

Boundary. This section establishes the European origins of American containment. It does not narrate the later worldwide Cold War, and the Greek civil war's full course, including the Yugoslav dimension, appears here only as needed for understanding the move toward confrontation in Germany.

Section 124.8

The Marshall Proposal, Cominform, and Europe's Increasing Political Polarization

On June 5, 1947, Secretary of State George C. Marshall delivered a short commencement address at Harvard University. He argued that Europe's economic recovery was far slower than expected, that the continent's economies were still disrupted, and that the United States should assist a European initiative to recover. He stated that American policy was directed against hunger, poverty, desperation, and chaos, not against any country or doctrine, and he invited the European states to take the initiative in drawing up a program. American concerns were both economic and political: a prolonged European depression would damage world trade and might strengthen communist parties, especially in France and Italy, where they had large followings.

British Foreign Secretary Bevin and French Foreign Minister Georges Bidault quickly took up the invitation and invited Molotov to a conference in Paris at the end of June. Molotov attended, but after several days of argument about whether recovery planning would be national or coordinated and whether it would involve intrusive investigation of national economies, he left on July 2. The British and French then invited all European states except Spain, and sixteen countries met in Paris in July and later formed the Committee of European Economic Cooperation. The Soviet Union rejected participation and pressured the governments within its sphere to do the same. Czechoslovakia and Poland, initially inclined to attend, withdrew after Moscow's intervention. Finland also declined, for reasons connected to its delicate relationship with the Soviet Union.

Soviet leaders regarded the program as an instrument of American economic penetration and a bid to build a western bloc, and they set up their own countermeasures. At a conference at Szklarska Poręba in Poland in September 1947, delegates from the communist parties of the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia, Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, Poland, Czechoslovakia, France, and Italy established the Communist Information Bureau, Cominform. Andrei Zhdanov's report presented a world divided into two camps, imperialist and democratic, and criticized the French and Italian parties for insufficient militancy. Cominform's headquarters were in Belgrade, and its establishment signaled a more disciplined and confrontational communist line.

In Czechoslovakia, the postwar government, a coalition led by Edvard Beneš as president and Klement Gottwald, a communist, as prime minister, was caught between East and West. The communists had won about 38 percent of the vote in the Czech lands in the free elections of May 1946, and the government had a tradition of close relations with the Soviet Union. In February 1948, after a dispute about police appointments, twelve noncommunist ministers resigned, hoping to force new elections. Gottwald, supported by communist-led mass demonstrations, action committees, and control of the police, then pressed Beneš to accept a reconstituted government with communist dominance. Beneš did so on February 25. In March, the noncommunist foreign minister, Jan Masaryk, was found dead beneath his window in Prague; the circumstances remain disputed. In the West the Czechoslovak crisis was read as proof of Soviet intentions in Europe, though historians continue to debate the extent of direct Soviet involvement, noting that the Czechoslovak communists also had strong domestic support and actors of their own.

The break between Stalin and Tito complicated any simple picture of a monolithic communist bloc. Yugoslavia's leadership, which had come to power through its own partisan movement without Soviet armies, was ambitious and independent. Disputes arose over Yugoslav policy toward Albania and the Balkans, economic relations, and Soviet advisers in Yugoslavia, and Stalin demanded greater subordination. In June 1948 the Cominform expelled Yugoslavia in a resolution issued after a meeting in Romania. Tito held on, survived Soviet pressure, and pursued a distinct path, showing that communist-led governments were not a uniform political bloc and that ideological affiliation did not erase national interests.

Economic recovery proposals, ideological competition, and security concerns became increasingly interconnected in this period. The Marshall proposal was both an economic program and a political strategy, and its reception depended on the political context. Chapter 125 will examine the internal consolidation of the Eastern Bloc, and Chapter 126 will develop the Marshall Plan's implementation and its long-term effects, while this chapter keeps its attention on how these developments reinforced the division of Germany. Congress passed the legislation establishing the American program in early April 1948, and the European states formed the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation shortly afterward.

The Economic and Political Separation of Occupied Germany

1946–1948

Section 124.9

Bizonia, Reparations, and the Breakdown of German Economic Unity

The American and British zones were joined economically on January 1, 1947, in an arrangement commonly called Bizonia. The agreement had been reached in December 1946 and followed a proposal by Byrnes in July 1946 that any zone willing to merge with the American zone could do so; only Britain accepted. The motives were practical. The British zone, which contained the Ruhr and its large population but had little farmland, ran a heavy food deficit and was a major financial burden on Britain. The American zone, which was more agricultural, had a smaller deficit but was also costly. Joining the two promised to reduce occupation costs, coordinate food distribution, transport, industrial production, trade, and public finance, and move toward German self-support.

Bizonia's institutions, centered in Frankfurt, developed through stages. A Bizonal Economic Council, whose members were chosen by the state parliaments, was established in 1947 and enlarged in early 1948, with an Executive Committee representing the states and with directors responsible for economic administration, food, finance, transport, and communications. In March 1948, Ludwig Erhard, a liberal economist, was elected director of economic administration. These bodies were German in personnel but operated under the authority of the American and British military governors. They provided German politicians and officials with valuable experience in administration, and they were, in effect, a prototype of the later federal institutions.

Reparations were central to the economic breakdown. The Potsdam compromise had permitted each power to draw reparations from its own zone, which left the Soviet Union with a zone that was poorer in heavy industry than the west. The Soviet authorities removed factories, machinery, and rail track and took current production, often on a large scale, which hampered the zone's own recovery. In May 1946, as noted above, Clay suspended reparations deliveries from the American zone, and Western policymakers increasingly feared that the Soviet Union's approach would prevent Germany from becoming economically self-sufficient. The Soviets, for their part, regarded Western refusals to deliver equipment as a breach of Potsdam and as a sign that the West did not accept Soviet claims to compensation.

The difficulty of restoring German production while preventing a renewed military buildup was acute. Allied controls on industry, including "level of industry" plans, aimed to limit steel production and prohibit war industries, but in a country short of food, coal, and housing, limits on output made recovery slower. In 1946 and 1947 the western zones suffered a severe winter and hunger, with production far below prewar levels. American policy gradually moved toward the view that reconstruction would be impossible without a more productive German economy and that further delay would worsen political instability and give communists new openings. In 1947, American occupation directives shifted toward economic recovery.

Soviet officials concluded that Bizonia was the first step toward a separate western German state. They protested that the merger violated the economic unity principle and the Potsdam framework, though the Americans and British replied that the Soviets had themselves blocked unity by their reparations practices and by refusing to deliver agricultural products from their zone. France, whose zone was not included, objected strongly to anything that would strengthen German industry without securing French interests in the Ruhr and the Saar. Over the following year, French concerns were gradually addressed through negotiation, and the French zone was drawn into western coordination in stages. The term "Trizonia," sometimes used for the three western zones, should not be read as implying an immediately unified three-zone state; the French joined economic arrangements in 1948 and 1949 and ratified western German political arrangements only after conditions had been met.

The economy also diverged in design. In the Soviet zone, administration was centralized, large estates were redistributed, major industries were placed under public ownership, and a system of planning began to emerge, as Section 124.11 shows. In the western zones, Erhard and others favored restoring price signals and market mechanisms, though with continued rationing and some controls until mid-1948. German participants in the new institutions therefore pursued very different ideas of what a reconstructed economy should be. The difference was not purely ideological; it also reflected differing material conditions, since the east had food and less industry and the west had industry and less food.

Central argument. Economic division preceded the formal establishment of separate German states and helped make political division increasingly difficult to reverse. Chapter 126 will examine the western economic recovery in detail.

Cause and Effect

Bizonia, Reparations, and the Separation of the German Economy

Long-term causes

The wartime destruction of German industry and transport, the Potsdam compromise that left reparations to be taken largely from each power's own zone, the structural imbalance between the industrial and food-producing zones, and the divergent economic philosophies of the occupiers.

Short-term causes

The severe winter of 1946 to 1947, British financial exhaustion, the American suspension of reparations deliveries in May 1946, the failure to establish central German agencies because of French opposition, and Byrnes's offer of July 1946 to merge zones.

Event or transformation

The fusion of the American and British zones as Bizonia on January 1, 1947, with German economic institutions in Frankfurt.

Immediate effects

Coordinated food and transport policy in the two zones, rising Soviet fears of a separate western state, French pressure for guarantees, and a shift in American policy toward economic recovery.

Long-term consequences

Western German administrative experience, a platform for currency reform in 1948 and for the creation of the Federal Republic in 1949, and an economic border within Germany that made later reunification more complex.

Historical complexity

Bizonia was neither a plot to divide Germany nor an accident. It was a practical response to occupation costs, but it also reflected a choice to prioritize recovery in the western zones when four-power agreement proved elusive. Soviet policies on reparations helped create the conditions to which the West responded.
Section 124.10

Building Political Institutions in the Western Occupation Zones

German political life revived in 1945 under occupation licensing. In the western zones the principal parties formed gradually. The Christian Democratic Union, founded in the western zones and in Berlin in 1945, sought to unite Catholics and Protestants in a single party, and in Bavaria its counterpart was the Christian Social Union. The Social Democratic Party, led by Kurt Schumacher, a survivor of Nazi imprisonment who strongly opposed communism and merger with it, claimed the tradition of Weimar social democracy. Liberal parties, later unified as the Free Democratic Party, drew support from Protestant middle-class and business circles. The Communist Party was legal but had limited electoral success in the west.

Occupation authorities restored state governments in 1945 and 1946, and several new states were created, often from former Prussian provinces or combinations of earlier territories. States adopted constitutions and held elections between 1946 and 1947, and local elections began even earlier. These institutions operated under Allied supervision, with military governors able to veto legislation and appoint or remove officials. Nevertheless, by 1948 German politicians in the western zones had considerable practical experience in state-level parliamentary government.

The occupiers differed on how to design a future German state. Americans favored federalism, with substantial powers for the states, as a means of preventing excessive concentration of power. The French wanted strong states and a weak center, since they feared a revived centralized Germany. The British were more willing to accept a stronger central government, and the Social Democrats, who preferred a more centralized state, found some support among British officials. These preferences were not simply national traits; they reflected both historical memory of Prussian centralization and calculations about security.

The turning point came with the London Six-Power Conference, held in two sessions between February and June 1948 with the United States, Britain, France, and the three Benelux countries participating. Its recommendations, announced in early June 1948, called for a federal system of government in the western zones, international control of the Ruhr, and closer coordination of the three zones. The Soviet Union was not invited, and its protests, along with its withdrawal from the Allied Control Council in March, deepened the sense that Germany's future would be settled without Soviet participation. The French National Assembly approved the recommendations only narrowly in June.

On July 1, 1948, the three western military governors presented the minister-presidents of the eleven western states with the Frankfurt Documents. These authorized the German leaders to convene an assembly to draft a constitution, set out principles for a federal and democratic system, and invited proposals for revising state boundaries, and they laid out the terms of a future occupation statute. The German leaders were uneasy. They worried that a formal constitution for a western state would formalize the division of Germany. At a meeting in July at the Rittersturz near Koblenz they agreed to proceed but insisted on a provisional arrangement: a "Basic Law" rather than a constitution, a "Parliamentary Council" rather than a constituent assembly, and no popular referendum, so as to avoid giving the western state the character of a final settlement.

A committee of experts met at Herrenchiemsee in Bavaria in August 1948 to prepare a draft. The Parliamentary Council, whose members were chosen by the state parliaments, convened in Bonn on September 1, 1948, with Konrad Adenauer of the CDU as president. It had sixty-five voting members, with the CDU/CSU and SPD holding the largest numbers, and nonvoting delegates from Berlin. Bonn, a modest university city, was chosen partly because it was not a major center and was thus less likely to be seen as a permanent capital. The council's deliberations, and the outcome, are examined in Section 124.18.

The prevailing sentiment among many German politicians was that the provisional character of the new arrangement preserved the aim of eventual national unity. Schumacher and many Social Democrats, as well as members of the CDU such as Jakob Kaiser, who had been driven out of the Soviet zone party leadership, shared this concern even where they differed over methods. Nonetheless, critics at the time, including some in the east and some in the west, argued that any western institution would inevitably make unity harder. Both views contained truth, which is part of why the question remained disputed.

Boundary. The detailed analysis of the Basic Law and the formal establishment of the Federal Republic in 1949 appears in Section 124.18.

Section 124.11

Political Transformation in the Soviet Occupation Zone: The SED and New Institutions

The Soviet Military Administration in Germany, usually abbreviated SMAD, was the governing authority of the Soviet zone, headquartered at Karlshorst in Berlin. Led successively by Zhukov, Sokolovsky, and, from 1949, Vasily Chuikov, it issued orders with the force of law, supervised German administrations, and controlled reparations and economic policy. SMAD also quickly permitted political activity. In June 1945 it authorized the establishment of antifascist parties, and the Communist Party of Germany (KPD), re-established under leaders returned from Soviet exile, including Walter Ulbricht and Wilhelm Pieck, was joined by the Social Democratic Party (SPD), the Christian Democratic Union, and the Liberal Democratic Party. In July 1945 the parties were drawn into an "antifascist democratic bloc," which committed them to joint decision-making and gave the communists, who held key positions in local administrations and police, an influential position.

The most important political step was the creation of the Socialist Unity Party of Germany (SED) in April 1946 through the merger of the KPD and SPD in the Soviet zone. The idea of unity on the left had genuine appeal, since many Germans who had experienced Nazi persecution believed that the division of the left had enabled Hitler's rise. Some social democrats, such as Otto Grotewohl, supported merger, and Pieck and Grotewohl became co-chairmen of the new party. But Soviet authorities and the KPD also pressured, intimidated, and in some cases arrested social democrats who resisted, and in the western sectors of Berlin, where a vote was permitted, social democrats overwhelmingly rejected immediate merger. The SED therefore cannot be explained purely as either voluntary unification or Soviet imposition; it combined real support with coercion, and its leadership was dominated by former communists.

Noncommunist parties in the Soviet zone remained legally permitted but were subject to growing pressure. The Christian Democrats and Liberal Democrats won considerable support in the local and state elections of September and October 1946, which took place in an unequal environment, but their leaders were gradually removed or replaced by individuals willing to cooperate. In December 1947 the Soviet authorities forced out the CDU leaders Jakob Kaiser and Ernst Lemmer. Mass organizations such as the Free German Trade Union Federation and youth and women's groups were brought into the bloc structure, and local administrations were purged of "reactionary" elements.

The social and economic restructuring was extensive. A land reform in the autumn of 1945 expropriated estates above one hundred hectares and the holdings of Nazi officials, redistributing land to landless laborers, small farmers, and refugees under the slogan of placing the land of the great estate owners into peasant hands, ending the Junker class's economic base. In 1946, following a referendum in Saxony, enterprises of Nazi and war criminals were expropriated and nationalized, and larger industries were transferred into public ownership or into Soviet-owned joint-stock companies that produced directly for reparations. The German Economic Commission, established in June 1947 under Soviet authority, centralized planning and administrative control across the zone's states and was, in effect, a precursor of a central government.

The main German actors included Ulbricht, who shaped party organization and security policy; Pieck, a veteran communist and symbolic figure of unity; and Grotewohl, a former social democrat who became prime minister of the GDR. They worked under Soviet oversight but exercised their own initiative, and tensions existed among them and between German communists and Soviet officials. Within the zone, political resistance took many forms, from voting for noncommunist parties and refusing to join the SED, to emigrating to the west, which many did. At the same time, large numbers of Germans participated in building the institutions, as functionaries, teachers, administrators, and workers, some from conviction in antifascism and social reform, others for security or advancement.

Historical distinction. Soviet domination was decisive, but German actors also participated in creating and operating the new political system. Chapter 125 will examine the consolidation of Stalinist systems and their later development, not merely repeat their institutional origins.

Historical Vocabulary
SED, SMAD, Bloc Parties, and Soviet-Zone Institutions

The Socialist Unity Party of Germany (SED) was the ruling party of the Soviet zone and later the GDR, formed in April 1946 from the KPD and SPD. SMAD, the Soviet Military Administration in Germany, was the occupation authority that issued binding orders and supervised the German administrations. The antifascist democratic bloc, formed in July 1945, brought the licensed parties into common decision-making, and the term bloc parties is often used for the noncommunist parties inside this structure, whose independence diminished over time.

The word "unity" in the party's name reflected a real wish for working-class unity, but the term should not be taken at face value as evidence of voluntary merger. The word "democratic" in the bloc's title likewise meant something different from its use in western parliamentary systems. Students should read these labels as part of the vocabulary of the period and ask who used them and for what purposes.

Section 124.12

Currency Reform and the Breakdown of Four-Power Cooperation, June 1948

By 1948 the Reichsmark had become nearly useless as a medium of exchange. Wartime price controls and rationing had suppressed inflation, but there was far more money in circulation than goods to buy. People turned to barter and to the black market, where cigarettes served as a de facto currency, and farmers and producers hoarded goods rather than sell them at controlled prices for worthless money. A functioning economy required a new currency and the removal of many controls, a point on which all four powers agreed in principle. They disagreed about how to carry it out, on what terms, and under whose control.

The western powers, after the Council's collapse and the London conference, decided to proceed in their own zones. The currency reform of June 20, 1948, introduced the Deutsche Mark in the three western zones. Each person received a limited initial sum in new currency, wages, rents, and many obligations were converted one to one, and other money balances were converted at a much lower rate, which wiped out much of the monetary savings and favored owners of real assets. Erhard simultaneously removed many price controls on his own authority, and goods that had been hidden reappeared in shop windows. The reform is widely credited as the start of rapid economic recovery in the west, though historians note that other conditions, including Marshall aid and the existing capital stock, also contributed.

Soviet authorities denounced the reform and responded with a separate currency measure. On June 23 they announced a currency reform for their zone, using old notes stamped or marked for validity, and they declared that it would also apply in the whole of Berlin, which they argued was part of the Soviet zone economically. The Western commandants rejected this, because they regarded Berlin as a four-power area and did not accept that a Soviet currency should apply in their sectors. In response, the Western powers introduced the western Deutsche Mark, marked with a "B," into their sectors of Berlin on June 24 and 25. For some months, Berlin had two currencies, with consequences for wages, prices, and daily life.

Transport restrictions escalated at the same time. The Soviet representative had left the Berlin Kommandatura on June 16, ending the city's four-power administration at that level. Soviet authorities had already imposed harassing controls on Western traffic since early 1948, with interference in rail and road movements, and had disrupted allied access on grounds of "technical difficulties." In the days after June 20, passenger traffic was halted, freight traffic was stopped, and electricity supplies were cut from the Soviet zone to the western sectors. On June 24, 1948, road, rail, and waterborne access to the western sectors was effectively closed, which marks the formal beginning of what became known as the Berlin Blockade.

The two sides explained these events in contrasting terms. Soviet authorities viewed the western currency reform as a unilateral measure that broke the principles of four-power economic administration, which made a separate western state more likely. They described their actions as a response to an illegal western step. Western governments regarded the Soviet pressure on Berlin as an attempt to force them to abandon the city and halt the western German project. Both explanations drew on a mixture of true and exaggerated claims, and historians continue to debate the extent to which Stalin sought to prevent the western state, to drive the Western powers out of Berlin, or merely to gain bargaining leverage.

Central question. Was the Berlin crisis primarily a dispute about currency and occupation rights, or a test of the broader European balance of power? The currency question provided the occasion, but the stakes concerned the whole structure of postwar Germany and Europe.

The Berlin Blockade and Airlift: The First Major European Cold War Crisis

June 1948–September 1949

Section 124.13

The Soviet Blockade of Berlin: Objectives, Escalation, and the Risk of War

Beginning on June 24, 1948, Soviet authorities halted road, rail, and barge traffic between the western zones and the western sectors of Berlin, and cut the supply of electricity from the Soviet zone and sector. The western sectors held roughly two million people. They depended on the surrounding Soviet zone and on imports from the west for food, coal, and raw materials, and they had stocks sufficient for only a limited period, often estimated in weeks. The immediate consequences were shortages, power cuts, and uncertainty. Students should not exaggerate the totality of the closure: the Soviet restrictions applied to surface transportation and to supplies of electricity and food from the east, while the three air corridors remained open.

Historians debate the exact Soviet objectives, and the evidence supports a combination. The most commonly cited aim was to prevent or delay the establishment of a separate western German state, which the London recommendations and the currency reform had brought nearer. A second was to compel the Western powers to leave Berlin or to negotiate on Soviet terms, which would remove a Western presence in the middle of the Soviet zone. A third was to exploit what appeared to be a Western weakness, because the Western powers had limited forces in Berlin and appeared to have no obvious way to supply the city. Stalin also wished, according to most accounts, to avoid war, and Soviet officials took care not to interfere directly with flights in the corridors.

The Western powers decided not to abandon the city. In part this was a matter of obligation to the two million inhabitants, many of whom had demonstrated their political preferences in elections. In part it reflected broader strategic reasoning: a withdrawal from Berlin would destroy confidence in the Western commitment to Germany and western Europe, undermine the plan for a western German state, and signal to the Soviets that pressure succeeded. General Lucius Clay was determined to stay, and he argued that withdrawal would be followed by Soviet gains elsewhere. President Truman supported this position, and the British government, led by Attlee and Bevin, was strongly committed. The French, who were more cautious, joined the common line.

The Western options were limited. Withdrawal was rejected for the reasons given. Negotiation was pursued but not from a position of surrender. Armed ground action, such as sending an armored convoy along the autobahn to test whether the Soviets would block it, was considered and, on Clay's urging, at first favored by some officials, but it risked starting a war at a time when Western ground forces in Germany were small and the American Joint Chiefs of Staff and Truman were wary. Supply by air, by contrast, was initially viewed as a temporary stopgap that might demonstrate resolve while diplomacy proceeded. It turned out to be the means that sustained the city.

The air corridors made that response possible. They rested on the written Control Council agreement of November 30, 1945, which guaranteed Western aircraft access, and the Soviet authorities hesitated to declare the agreement void. The existing air traffic control arrangement, the Berlin Air Safety Center, was still four-power in composition. Soviet fighters did on occasion harass transports with close passes and the use of searchlights, anti-aircraft practice, and balloons near the corridors, but they did not shoot down any airlift aircraft. The Soviet decision to avoid direct confrontation on this point was a key reason the crisis did not escalate into war.

The risk of military escalation was nevertheless real. The United States moved B-29 bombers, a type capable of carrying atomic weapons, to bases in Britain in July 1948, though the aircraft were not in fact configured for nuclear weapons, and Washington regarded the move as a signal. Both sides hoped to avoid war, and each tried to leave the other room to retreat. The competing powers avoided direct conflict because of a combination of mutual caution, Soviet military and economic weakness after the war, American atomic advantage and economic strength, and the fact that neither saw Berlin as worth a general war.

Historical Vocabulary
Blockade, Air Corridor, Airlift, Occupation Rights, and Strategic Coercion

A blockade is the use of force or restriction to prevent movement of goods or people to or from a place; in this case it was imposed by land and water restrictions, not by armed naval action, and did not stop air traffic. An air corridor is a defined route in which aircraft have the right to fly; the three corridors to Berlin were each twenty miles wide. An airlift is the large-scale supply of a place by aircraft. Occupation rights were the legal claims of the Western powers to be in Berlin and to have access to it as a result of Germany's defeat and the four-power agreements. Strategic coercion means using pressure to change another government's behavior without open war.

These terms matter because the word "blockade" suggests a total sealing, but Berlin was never completely cut off. The success of the Western response depended on the distinction between closed surface routes and open air routes.

Section 124.14

Operation Vittles and Operation Plainfare: The Berlin Airlift as a Logistical System

The airlift is often told as an inspiring episode of heroic pilots and children waiting for sweets, but its significance lies equally in organization. The American operation was called Operation Vittles and the British operation Plainfare. They shared a purpose but differed in organization, bases, and equipment, and they were coordinated rather than fully integrated. The American effort began on June 26 with C-47 transports, which could carry about two and a half tons, and soon employed the larger four-engine C-54 Skymaster, which carried roughly ten tons. British forces used Dakotas, Yorks, and later Hastings aircraft, and also employed Sunderland flying boats, which landed on the Havel lake near Gatow and carried goods such as salt, whose corrosive qualities were less damaging to a marine aircraft.

Berlin's airfields were central. Tempelhof, in the American sector, was the main facility, with Gatow in the British sector, and a third, Tegel, in the French sector, was built in 1948 under pressure of need. The Tegel runway was constructed in a few months, largely by Berlin laborers, many of them women, working with limited machinery, and it opened in December 1948. Behind the Berlin end lay a network of bases in the western zones, including Wiesbaden and Rhein-Main for the Americans and fields in northern Germany such as Fassberg, Celle, and Lübeck for the British.

Operations improved dramatically over time under the leadership of Major General William H. Tunner, an experienced air transport commander who took charge of the combined effort in the late summer and autumn of 1948. His approach was to treat the airlift as a production line. He standardized aircraft types for efficiency, set rigid schedules with equal intervals between landings, required pilots who missed an approach to return to base without circling, and used precision instrument approaches in poor weather. Cargo handling was streamlined, and the unloading of an aircraft took a short time, often a matter of minutes, because Germans carried out the work with trucks and manual labor. Maintenance was organized as a continuous cycle, with a complex system of repairs and parts supply. Weather forecasting, radar-assisted ground-controlled approach, and air traffic control at Tempelhof and Gatow allowed flights to continue in winter conditions that would otherwise have grounded them.

The cargo was predominantly coal, which was needed for heat, power generation, and industry, alongside food, including flour, dehydrated potatoes, and other basics, and medicines, fuel, and industrial raw materials. Estimates of the minimum daily requirement for survival rations are commonly given in the range of several thousand tons, and the total including coal and fuel was much higher, so that the average daily deliveries had to rise severalfold from the start of the operation. The winter of 1948 to 1949 required careful planning of fuel stocks. The airlift also carried goods out of Berlin, including industrial products to maintain the city's exports, and it evacuated many children and sick people.

The personnel involved came from several countries. American and British aircrew formed the core, joined by Commonwealth air forces, including Canadian, Australian, New Zealand, and South African crews, and by French personnel who contributed to the Berlin end, while German ground crews, mechanics, laborers, and air traffic and meteorological support staff played an essential role. Many Germans who had been enemies three years earlier now worked alongside Allied personnel, an aspect that shaped later memory of the airlift as a moment of changing relationships. Dozens of airmen and ground personnel died, mostly in accidents, a reminder of the hazards of flying a dense schedule in difficult conditions.

The scale of the operation should be stated with care. A US Air Force operational history reports approximately 2,325,510 U.S. short tons (about 2.11 million metric tonnes) delivered into Berlin during the full airlift, and a commonly cited US Air Force tally gives 278,228 inbound flights by American and British crews. Outbound flights and other counting conventions yield different totals. These figures include the months after the Soviet blockade ended on May 12, 1949; the airlift formally ended September 30. Deliveries in the first weeks were small, in the hundreds of tons per day, and grew through the autumn and winter. In April 1949 the Allies achieved exceptionally high deliveries, including a single 24-hour surge, sometimes called the "Easter Parade," of nearly thirteen thousand tons, intended partly to show what could be done and to demonstrate to the Soviets that the airlift could continue indefinitely.

The airlift's success depended on logistics, infrastructure, and political determination rather than a single technological breakthrough. The aircraft were mostly wartime types, and the key innovations were organizational: scheduling, maintenance, standardization, and training. The political will to continue through a winter was equally essential, as was the willingness of Berliners to endure shortages. A well-organized system also served as a form of communication, since the steady stream of aircraft was visible evidence of Western commitment and capacity.

Section 124.15

Everyday Life in Blockaded Berlin: Civilians, Politics, and Psychological Conflict

For residents of the western sectors, the blockade meant a prolonged period of scarcity. Food was rationed, with allowances varying by category of work and age, and the staples were bread, dehydrated potatoes, dried vegetables, powdered milk and eggs, and little fresh produce. Calories were adequate for survival but monotonous, and Berliners complained about the dried foods even while accepting them. Electricity was supplied for limited hours each day, often at night, so that households and workplaces had to schedule cooking, washing, and work around the availability of power. Coal for heating was in short supply, and in the winter residents gathered wood, including from the Tiergarten park, which was largely stripped of trees.

Public transportation was reduced, unemployment grew as industries lacked power and materials, and shopkeepers faced limited stock. Schools operated with shortened hours, and emergency welfare provision grew. Housing conditions remained poor in a city where many buildings were destroyed. The experience cut across social groups unequally. Elderly people, small children, and those without access to relatives who could bring food or fuel were more vulnerable, while industrial workers, who received higher rations in some categories, faced the loss of employment and wages when factories cut back. Women carried much of the burden of household management, standing in lines and finding ways to stretch food and fuel, and many also worked in rubble clearance and construction projects.

The blockade also became a contest over political legitimacy. The Soviet authorities offered ration cards and food to western Berliners who registered in the eastern sector, hoping to undermine support for the West. Only a small fraction accepted. Western authorities and the Berlin political leadership emphasized that to give in would mean losing freedom. The airlift thereby changed the perception of the Western occupiers among many Berliners, from distant conquerors to protectors, a shift that had no equivalent in many other parts of Germany.

Berlin's political leadership was divided by the crisis. Ernst Reuter, a Social Democrat who had been elected mayor by the city assembly in 1947 but blocked by a Soviet veto, became the most visible spokesman of the western side. On September 9, 1948, a mass rally of several hundred thousand people gathered near the ruined Reichstag, where Reuter urged the world not to abandon the city. Earlier, on September 6, crowds organized by the SED had disrupted the city assembly, which then moved to the western sectors. The city administration split, with a separate eastern magistrate formed in late November 1948 and a December election held in the western sectors, which confirmed the Social Democrats and made Reuter mayor of West Berlin. The common municipal government of the city ended.

The media shaped how the crisis was understood. RIAS and other western radio stations, newspapers, posters, and official announcements framed the crisis as a struggle for freedom, and images of aircraft supplying the city became a durable symbol. The American pilot Gail Halvorsen began dropping candy to children by parachute in July 1948, an initiative that grew into "Operation Little Vittles," which became a famous symbol of friendship, though it was a tiny part of the airlift's tonnage. Soviet-controlled media portrayed the western position as a provocation by imperialists and warmongers and presented the Soviet sector as a place of stability.

Soviet-sector residents were historical actors, too. The eastern part of Berlin, with more than a million inhabitants, was supplied from the Soviet zone and experienced its own hardships, and its residents faced political pressure, including from the SED's growing control of institutions. Many people had family or work connections across the sector boundaries, and cross-sector commuting, trade, and personal relationships continued in complicated ways. Berlin's population should not be reduced to a single unified political voice, since voters had divided in 1946, and in 1948 and 1949 residents in both parts experienced different pressures.

Everyday Life and Social History

Household Decisions in Blockaded West Berlin

Consider a West Berlin household in the winter of 1948 to 1949: a mother, her two school-age children, and an elderly grandparent, with the father's factory working short hours because of power cuts. Decisions would turn on practical constraints. Because electricity was available only at fixed and inconvenient times, cooking would be timed to the supply, and the household would choose meals that could be prepared quickly. Rations of bread and dehydrated potato, with limited dried vegetables and powdered milk, would be supplemented by whatever trading with relatives or black-market exchange could provide, and a family would weigh whether to give up a valued household item for fuel.

Heating would be restricted to one room, and the household would decide whether to send a family member to gather wood or whether to pay for scarce coal. Schooling and employment would be affected by transport schedules, and parents would weigh whether to send a child, if the opportunity arose, to relatives in the western zones through the evacuation programs. Political decisions mattered too: the family might be asked to accept ration cards from the Soviet sector, which would mean registering there, and refusing would have material costs. These choices show how transport shortages, coal supplies, rationing, and the airlift shaped everyday decisions in ways that no tonnage statistic can reveal.

Central argument. The blockade became both a logistical confrontation and a contest over political legitimacy, and the second dimension was won, among western Berliners, by the combination of material support and visible Western commitment.

Section 124.16

Ending the Blockade: Diplomacy, Negotiation, and the Strategic Outcome

The negotiations that ended the blockade were slow and indirect. In the late summer and autumn of 1948, the Western powers made representations in Moscow, and in October they brought the question to the United Nations Security Council, where the neutral members attempted a compromise on the currency question that did not succeed. The diplomatic turning point came in early 1949. In January, in answer to written questions from an American journalist, Stalin spoke of lifting the restrictions if the Western powers delayed the creation of a western German state, and he did not mention the currency question as a condition, which Western diplomats took as a signal of flexibility. In February and March, the American diplomat Philip Jessup and the Soviet representative to the United Nations, Yakov Malik, held private conversations that clarified the Soviet position.

On May 4, 1949, the four governments announced an agreement under which the restrictions on traffic to and from Berlin would be lifted on May 12, and the Council of Foreign Ministers would meet in Paris later in May. The restrictions were lifted as agreed. The diplomatic agreement restored surface transportation but resolved few of the underlying disagreements. The Paris meeting, held from late May to June 1949, produced no breakthrough on Germany's future. General Clay, who had been central to the Western position, left Germany in May.

Several factors help explain why the Soviet Union ended the blockade. The airlift, by showing that the western sectors could be sustained, undermined the central purpose of coercion. In addition, the Western countermeasures, including restrictions on shipments of goods from the western zones to the Soviet zone, hurt the eastern economy. The blockade also had costly political effects for the Soviet Union, since it strengthened the Western resolve, encouraged the creation of a western alliance, reinforced West Berliners' attachment to the Western powers, and hastened, rather than prevented, the establishment of the West German state. Coercive restrictions could not force Western withdrawal, and the political cost of continuing grew.

The airlift continued after May. The Western powers wanted to build up stocks of supplies in Berlin as insurance against renewed restrictions, and they maintained flights at a high level for several months before ending the operation on September 30, 1949. The chronological distinction is essential: the blockade began on June 24, 1948, and ended on May 12, 1949, while the airlift began on June 26, 1948, and formally ended on September 30, 1949. The two events must never be assigned a single undifferentiated end date.

The immediate gains and limits differed by participant. For the Western powers, the outcome was a political success: they had kept their position in Berlin without war, shown their capacity and resolve, and consolidated support for the western German project and for transatlantic security cooperation. For the Soviet Union, the result was a setback in strategic terms, though it kept its zone, its position in eastern Berlin, and its ability to apply pressure later; Berlin remained a point of leverage that Soviet leaders would try to use again, most notably in the crisis that began in 1958. For Berlin, the result was the retention of Western protection but also an unresolved status and a divided city administration. For the German population as a whole, the crisis hardened the political split, and many Germans in the west became more strongly pro-Western as a result.

The crisis reinforced American involvement in European security and accelerated the emerging political division. American commitments to western Europe moved from the economic to the military sphere, as discussed in Section 124.17. The continuing legal status of Berlin, as an area subject to four-power rights though effectively split, remained, and with it the unresolved question of Germany's national future.

Central question. Did the airlift resolve the Berlin crisis, or did it stabilize a division that the victorious powers had failed to reconcile? Most historians conclude that it resolved the immediate crisis, preserved Western access, and demonstrated the limits of coercion, but that it did not resolve the underlying division and in some respects consolidated it. The State Department's historical account confirms the significance of the airlift, Western occupation rights, and the peaceful end of the confrontation.

The Formation of Two German States and the European Cold War Order

1948–1949

Section 124.17

The Brussels Treaty, NATO, and the New Western European Security System

Western European governments in 1947 and 1948 were concerned with security against two quite different dangers: a revived Germany and Soviet pressure. The first concern had produced the Anglo-French Treaty of Dunkirk in March 1947, which was directed at a possible renewal of German aggression. By early 1948, as the Marshall Plan took shape and Soviet-Western relations worsened, British Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin proposed a wider western European grouping. The result was the Brussels Treaty, signed on March 17, 1948, by Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. It combined commitments to economic, social, and cultural cooperation with a mutual defense obligation, and it named a possible renewal of German aggression as a danger, though by 1948 the Soviet threat was increasingly in the minds of its signatories.

The February 1948 crisis in Czechoslovakia and the Berlin Blockade strongly influenced Western planning. They persuaded many officials that a Soviet military move in Europe was conceivable and that Western Europe could not rely on its own weak forces. At the same time, Western Europeans wanted a firm American commitment. In June 1948 the United States Senate passed the Vandenberg Resolution, which indicated that the United States could associate itself with regional collective defense arrangements, thereby removing a major domestic obstacle. Exploratory talks began in Washington in the summer of 1948, and negotiations among the Brussels Treaty states, the United States, and Canada continued through the winter.

The North Atlantic Treaty was signed in Washington on April 4, 1949. The twelve original members were the United States, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Norway, Denmark, Iceland, Italy, and Portugal. The inclusion of Italy and Portugal, neither of which was on the Atlantic's northern shores in the strict sense, reflected strategic and political considerations: Italy's position in the Mediterranean and its need to be anchored in the West, and Portugal's Atlantic islands. Greece and Turkey joined in 1952, and the Federal Republic of Germany not until 1955. Students should distinguish several stages: negotiation, signature on April 4, ratification by national legislatures, entry into force in August 1949, and the later development of integrated military structures, which came largely after the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950.

Article 5 is the treaty's central provision. In it the parties agreed that an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America would be considered an attack against them all, and that each would take such action as it deems necessary, including the use of armed force, to restore and maintain the security of the North Atlantic area. The wording reflects a compromise. European governments wanted a firm guarantee, while the United States Congress had constitutional powers over war and did not want an automatic commitment. As a result, each member undertook to take the action it deemed necessary, which could include armed force, but not an identical, automatic military response in every circumstance.

The United States and Canada, as members, made the structure transatlantic. The involvement of an extra-European power, with the economic strength and, at that time, the atomic monopoly that the United States possessed, changed the European balance. American troops were already in Germany as occupation forces, and the treaty gave their presence a longer-term strategic rationale, though the large-scale American troop commitment to Europe followed in the early 1950s. Some Europeans, including the French, saw the alliance as a protection against both the Soviet Union and a revived Germany, and this dual function would shape later debates about German rearmament.

The alliance both responded to existing tensions and institutionalized the European division. The Soviet Union denounced it as aggressive, and the Soviet response over the following years included building up its own alliance system, which culminated in the Warsaw Pact in 1955. Chapter 125 examines the later development of NATO's military structures and the Warsaw Pact together with the consolidation of bloc politics in eastern Europe.

Critical safeguard. The Federal Republic of Germany was not a founding member of NATO in April 1949, since it did not yet exist as a state when negotiations were concluded, and its accession came in 1955. The Warsaw Pact was likewise not established until 1955. The alliance of 1949 was a Western security treaty among twelve states, and its German policy at that stage was to keep Germany disarmed and under control, not to recruit it.

Section 124.18

The Federal Republic of Germany: The Basic Law and West German Statehood, 1949

The Parliamentary Council in Bonn worked from September 1948 through the first months of 1949 under difficult circumstances: the blockade of Berlin was in progress, the occupying powers monitored the work, and the Germans wanted to build a lasting democratic order while insisting that it was provisional. The council had sixty-five voting members chosen by the state parliaments, and the CDU/CSU and SPD were evenly balanced, with smaller numbers from the liberals, the Communist Party, the German Party, and the Center Party. Adenauer presided, while the Social Democrat Carlo Schmid was a leading figure in the main committee. The Herrenchiemsee draft prepared in August 1948 served as a starting point, and the council debated key issues including the relative powers of the federal government and the states, the electoral system, the role of the president, and the status of Berlin.

The Basic Law, as the document was named, embodied lessons that the drafters drew from the collapse of the Weimar Republic and from the Nazi dictatorship. It begins with a catalog of fundamental rights, headed by the declaration that human dignity is inviolable, and these rights bind the legislature, executive, and judiciary. It established a federal state in which the Länder retain substantial powers and participate in federal legislation through the Bundesrat. It created a parliamentary system with a Bundestag elected by a mixed system combining direct constituency and proportional representation, a chancellor who is the effective head of government, and a largely ceremonial federal president elected indirectly by a federal convention, not by popular vote. The "constructive vote of no confidence," which allows the Bundestag to remove a chancellor only by electing a successor, was designed to prevent the instability of Weimar. The Basic Law also provided for a Federal Constitutional Court, though it did not begin work until 1951, and included an "eternity clause" protecting basic principles from amendment.

The name "Basic Law" rather than "constitution" was deliberate. Its drafters wished to indicate that it was a provisional framework valid until Germans in all parts of the country could adopt a constitution in free self-determination, which kept alive the objective of unity. The preamble called on the German people in the western states to act on behalf of those Germans to whom participation had been denied, and a provision on accession allowed other parts of Germany to join. This provision would later become the legal route used in 1990, as Chapter 132 explains.

The Parliamentary Council adopted the Basic Law on May 8, 1949, by a vote of fifty-three to twelve. The Western military governors approved it on May 12, with some reservations, including that Berlin could not be treated as a full state of the Federal Republic and that its representatives in the federal bodies would not have full voting rights. State parliaments ratified it in the following days, with Bavaria's parliament rejecting it but accepting its validity if two thirds of the others ratified. It was promulgated on May 23, 1949, and the German Federal Archives identifies that date as the founding date of the Federal Republic.

The first federal election was held on August 14, 1949. The CDU/CSU received about 31 percent, the SPD about 29 percent, and the liberal FDP about 12 percent, with the remainder divided among smaller parties. The Bundestag met in September, Theodor Heuss of the FDP was elected federal president on September 12, and Konrad Adenauer was elected chancellor on September 15, with a majority of one vote. He led a coalition of the CDU/CSU, FDP, and German Party. In a statement of policy, his government asserted that it was the only legitimately constituted German government and spoke for Germans who had not been free to participate.

Sovereignty was limited. The Occupation Statute, which took effect on September 21, 1949, set out the powers reserved to the occupation authorities, including foreign affairs, control of disarmament and demilitarization, control of the Ruhr, and the right to resume full authority in an emergency. The military governors were replaced by civilian high commissioners, and later agreements, such as the Petersberg Agreement of November 1949, widened German freedom of action. The Federal Republic was thus a state with real governing institutions but not yet full sovereignty. The Parliamentary Council voted 33 to 29 for Bonn over Frankfurt on May 10, 1949. The Bundestag confirmed Bonn as the provisional federal seat on November 3. Berlin remained the intended capital of a united Germany.

Section 124.19

The German Democratic Republic: Constitution, SED Leadership, and Soviet Influence, 1949

The establishment of the German Democratic Republic on October 7, 1949, was presented as a response to the creation of the Federal Republic, but its institutional groundwork had been laid earlier. The SED had promoted a "People's Congress" movement from late 1947, claiming to speak for all Germans on national unity and a peace treaty. The first congress met in December 1947 with delegates including some from the western zones, and the second in March 1948 elected a German People's Council of about four hundred members and approved a draft constitution in March 1949. Delegates to the third congress were selected through a single-list vote on May 15–16, 1949. The officially reported approval was 66.1 percent, but the count and freedom of the ballot were disputed. The congress approved the constitution in late May.

On October 7, 1949, the People's Council declared itself the provisional People's Chamber, or Volkskammer, and put the constitution into force, as the German Historical Museum confirms. A provisional chamber of the states was also formed. On October 10, the Soviet Military Administration transferred its administrative functions to the new government and was replaced by a Soviet Control Commission, headed by Vasily Chuikov, which retained supervisory authority. On October 11, Wilhelm Pieck was elected president of the republic, and on October 12 Otto Grotewohl was installed as minister-president, with Walter Ulbricht as one of his deputies and the actual leader of the SED apparatus. The Soviet Union recognized the new state quickly, and the eastern bloc governments followed.

The constitution, adopted in 1949, described a democratic republic with guarantees of rights, a legislature as the supreme organ of the state, and a federal structure with states. In form it resembled a parliamentary democracy, and it declared Germany an indivisible republic. In practice, power resided in the SED leadership and its control of the security services, the administration, and the bloc parties, so that the constitutional description differed from the actual distribution of political power. The existence of the nominally independent bloc parties and mass organizations, led under the SED's guidance, was an instrument of control.

The GDR claimed to represent the German people and to serve as a legitimate German state, and it designated East Berlin as its capital, even though Berlin's four-power status meant that this claim was contested by the Western powers. The new state inherited the institutions and practices of the Soviet zone: land reform, nationalized industry, central planning, and the structure of administration set up under the German Economic Commission, which was reorganized into the government. Soviet influence remained strong, through the Control Commission, the presence of Soviet troops, and control over reparations and economic matters.

The historical significance of the date requires care. The GDR was not established by fully competitive nationwide elections. Its institutions emerged from a process of staged congresses, single-list voting, and the reconstitution of an appointed council. It was also not fully sovereign: the Soviet authorities retained substantial rights and influence, and in practice Soviet approval was essential to key decisions. At the same time, the institutions had real German participants, ranging from convinced antifascists and socialists, who regarded the new state as a break with Nazism and capitalism, to officials and functionaries who accepted it for other reasons, while many others left for the west, and the emigration in these years was a significant political and social fact.

The founding of the GDR followed that of the Federal Republic by about four and a half months, and the two events should not be treated as simultaneous. Each side presented its state as the legitimate German state and the other as a creation of foreign powers. The Western government, as noted, claimed to represent all Germans, and the GDR declared itself the true expression of the German people's will for unity and peace. The competing claims would shape inter-German politics for decades.

Accuracy safeguard. Students should avoid suggesting that fully competitive nationwide elections established the GDR government in October 1949, or that the GDR was a fully sovereign state at its founding.

Section 124.20

Germany Divided: Explaining the Origins and Consequences of the European Cold War

By the end of 1949 Germany and Europe were divided in several overlapping ways. There were two German states, with different political systems, economic organization, and external alignments, and neither fully sovereign. There was a Western military alliance, the North Atlantic Treaty, and a communist bloc under Soviet leadership with its own instruments of coordination. There was a divided Berlin whose legal status remained contested and whose geography made it a potential flashpoint. Students should compare the two German states as they stood at the end of 1949. The Federal Republic, with roughly fifty million people, a federal parliamentary system, a developing social market economy, Marshall Plan assistance, and Western alignment, was limited by the Occupation Statute. The GDR, with a population of roughly eighteen million, an SED-led system of centralized administration and planning, a continuing burden of Soviet reparations and presence, and an alignment with the Soviet Union, was likewise limited by Soviet authority.

What explains the division? The chapter has identified several factors: Soviet security objectives and the Soviet determination to extract reparations and secure influence; Western economic and political priorities, including the need for German recovery and the commitment to parliamentary democracy; the ideological confrontation that gave each side reasons to distrust the other; the dispute over reparations and the economic unity of Germany; German political developments, including the choices of parties and voters; and the breakdown of four-power administration. No single factor suffices. The relative weight historians assign them is the subject of the debate presented below.

Historical Debate

Who or What Caused Europe's Cold War Division?

The question: To what extent was the division of Germany and Europe the result of Soviet expansion, American economic and political ambitions, mutual misperception, or the structural situation left by the war?

Interpretation A: Traditionalist. Early Western accounts, often associated with American historians and officials of the 1940s and 1950s, emphasized Soviet expansionism and the communist takeover of eastern Europe. In this view the United States and its allies responded, belatedly and defensively, to Soviet pressure, and Stalin's ideology and the nature of his regime made cooperation difficult.
Interpretation B: Revisionist. From the 1960s, historians influenced by the Vietnam War, including William Appleman Williams, Gabriel Kolko, and, for Germany, Carolyn Woods Eisenberg in her study of the American decision to divide Germany, argued that American economic goals and the desire for an open world economy under American leadership shaped policy, and that the United States, with its atomic monopoly and resources, bore significant responsibility for the breakdown. They noted that Western policy in the Ruhr and the creation of a western German state in effect preceded Soviet action.
Interpretation C: Post-revisionist and archival. Since the 1970s, and especially after documents from Soviet and eastern European archives became available in the 1990s, historians such as Melvyn Leffler and John Lewis Gaddis have offered syntheses that stress security fears, misperception, and the structural effects of power vacuums created by the war. Gaddis, in We Now Know, has placed substantial emphasis on Stalin's ideology and agency, while Leffler has stressed the role of American security concerns. Norman Naimark's study of the Soviet zone shows both Soviet constraints and the heavy cost of Soviet policies for Germans.

Evidence: Interpretations depend on diplomatic records, military governors' papers, economic data on reparations and production, Soviet and German party documents, and the testimony of participants. Disputes concern motives, which must be inferred, as well as the interpretation of events, such as whether the western currency reform or the Soviet blockade should be seen as the decisive escalation. Newly available archives have clarified Soviet decision-making but have not produced a single agreed conclusion.

Why it matters: The way historians distribute responsibility affects whether Germany's division is seen as avoidable, how Western and Soviet security claims are evaluated, and how the origins of later Cold War crises are understood. The interpretations are not equally supported on every point: the evidence strongly supports the view that both sides acted from security fears and that Soviet policies in eastern Europe and Germany were authoritarian and coercive, while also supporting the view that Western economic and political choices, including those in the western zones, contributed to the division.

Student task: Select two documents or events from this chapter and explain which interpretation each most strongly supports, then identify additional evidence that would help resolve the disagreement.

Berlin's continuing position in the system was distinct. It remained a divided city with a western enclave dependent on Allied protection and on access routes whose security rested on Soviet restraint. Its physical circumstances, as a Western outpost inside the Soviet zone, made it a potential site of future confrontation, as later crises would show. The Berlin Wall, built in 1961, does not belong to this period, and the sector boundary in 1949 was still relatively open to movement.

The postwar settlement had thus been transformed. NATO, the separate German states, different occupation and economic systems, and the wider political divide changed what had been planned in 1945 into something quite different. Historians also ask about alternatives. Could four-power administration have continued? Possibly in a limited form, if reparations and economic unity had been settled, but this would have required concessions that each side regarded as too costly. Could Germany have been unified on terms acceptable to all? Some proposals for a neutral Germany were discussed, including later Soviet proposals in 1952, but whether Moscow would have accepted a truly free and unified Germany remains debated. Contemporaries understood these possibilities in different ways: many Germans hoped for unity, while many in the western capitals feared unity on Soviet terms and many in Moscow feared a unified Germany aligned with the West.

Concluding thesis. Germany's division emerged from cumulative decisions, conflicting security perceptions, different systems of political and economic reconstruction, and the failure of wartime allies to agree on Germany's future. By the close of 1949, the European Cold War had acquired territorial, constitutional, diplomatic, and security institutions that would shape subsequent decades. Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the victorious Allied powers moved from wartime cooperation to confrontation and why Germany became the central arena of that confrontation. The Berlin Declaration of June 5, 1945, gave the four powers supreme authority over a defeated country without annexing it. Four zones and a four-sector Berlin created a framework of joint government, but the framework depended on agreement among powers whose security needs, economic interests, and political systems differed profoundly. Germany's division was therefore not predetermined in May 1945; it grew out of choices made over four years.

Soviet policy was shaped by the experience of invasion and devastation, by the wish for reparations, and by ideological assumptions, and it sought both a Germany unable to threaten the Soviet Union and influence over its future. Western policy combined the desire for economic recovery, a commitment to parliamentary democracy, and concern about Soviet influence in Europe, and the Western powers themselves differed, with France especially wary of a strong German center. The Allied Control Council, which could act only by unanimity, produced real results in some technical matters but was crippled by disagreements over reparations, economic unity, and central administration, and it ceased to function after the Soviet delegation withdrew on March 20, 1948. Berlin, deep inside the Soviet zone, was legally a four-power city yet strategically vulnerable.

In 1946 and 1947 mutual suspicion hardened. Kennan's Long Telegram and Novikov's assessment show how each government interpreted the other's conduct through its own assumptions, and Churchill's Fulton speech gave public form to the image of an Iron Curtain. The crises in Greece and Turkey led to the Truman Doctrine and the European origins of American containment. The Marshall proposal and the Soviet refusal to participate, the formation of Cominform, and the Czechoslovak crisis of February 1948 connected economic recovery, ideology, and security, though the break between Stalin and Tito showed that the communist side was not uniform.

Within Germany, economic and political separation advanced step by step. Bizonia began on January 1, 1947, in response to occupation costs and food shortages, and the western powers gradually drew the French zone into coordination. In the Soviet zone, SMAD, land reform, nationalization, the German Economic Commission, and the creation of the SED in April 1946 produced a distinct system, built with both coercion and real German participation. The London conference, the Frankfurt Documents of July 1, 1948, and the Parliamentary Council in Bonn established the process that would lead to a western German state, though German politicians insisted that it be provisional.

The western currency reform of June 20, 1948, introduced the Deutsche Mark and created an economic border within Germany. The dispute over its application to Berlin, coming after the breakdown of the Control Council and the Kommandatura, became the occasion for the Soviet closure of surface access to the western sectors on June 24. The Berlin Blockade, however, never closed the air corridors, and the Western powers refused to leave. The airlift, organized as an efficient logistical system by American Operation Vittles and British Operation Plainfare, supplied the city through a winter, with a total of more than 2.3 million tons delivered over its entire course according to the U.S. Department of Defense.

Civilians in West Berlin experienced rationing, power cuts, and uncertainty, and the crisis became a contest over political legitimacy that strengthened Berliners' attachment to the Western powers while splitting the city's administration. The blockade ended on May 12, 1949, after diplomatic contacts, because it had failed to achieve its objectives and imposed costs on the Soviet Union, while the airlift continued until September 30. The three milestones, the blockade's beginning, its end, and the airlift's end, are distinct.

The security dimension was institutionalized by the Brussels Treaty of March 1948 and the North Atlantic Treaty of April 4, 1949, with its twelve founding members and its Article 5 commitment to action each member deems necessary. The Federal Republic was founded with the promulgation of the Basic Law on May 23, 1949, followed by the election of the first Bundestag and Adenauer's election as chancellor, under the limits of the Occupation Statute. The German Democratic Republic was founded on October 7, 1949, through the conversion of the People's Council into a provisional People's Chamber, with Pieck as president, and it was dominated by the SED and subject to Soviet supervision. The two states were established at different times and neither was fully sovereign.

Historians disagree about the distribution of responsibility. Traditionalist, revisionist, and post-revisionist interpretations emphasize different factors, and archival openings have clarified but not settled the question. The evidence supports the view that security fears, ideological confrontation, economic conflict, and German political developments all contributed. By the end of 1949 the European Cold War had acquired territorial, constitutional, diplomatic, and security institutions that would shape subsequent decades, and Germany's future had become inseparable from the wider struggle over the organization of postwar Europe.

Glossary of Key Terms

The thirty terms below match the Core Vocabulary Preview in wording and order. Classify them by meaning: political concepts and diplomatic texts (1, 2, 8–14); governing arrangements and institutions (3–7, 16–17, 27–30); economic integration and monetary policies (15, 18–19); crisis events and logistics (20–24); and security agreements and alliances (25–26). Several terms overlap categories, but the Berlin Blockade and Airlift are operations and historical crises, not diplomatic documents.

1. Cold War
The prolonged political, ideological, economic, and strategic confrontation between the Soviet Union and the Western powers led by the United States, short of direct war between the principal antagonists, which took recognizable form in Europe between 1945 and 1949.
2. Grand Alliance
The wartime coalition of the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain, joined by others, formed to defeat Nazi Germany and held together largely by that common aim.
3. Occupation zone
A defined area of Germany in which one of the four Allied powers exercised direct military government; an administrative division of a country regarded as legally one, not a separate state.
4. Allied Control Council
The four-power body in Berlin, composed of the commanders in chief, intended to coordinate policy for Germany as a whole by unanimous decision; it ceased to function after the Soviet withdrawal on March 20, 1948. It differed from the Kommandatura, which governed only Berlin.
5. Allied Kommandatura
The four-power body of sector commandants that governed Berlin and supervised its city government; the Soviet commandant left it on June 16, 1948.
6. Four-power status
The legal condition of Berlin as a city jointly occupied by the four powers, distinct from the territorial and constitutional arrangements of the FRG and GDR and claimed by the Western powers to underpin their rights of presence and access.
7. Soviet Military Administration in Germany (SMAD)
The Soviet occupation authority in the eastern zone, based at Karlshorst, which issued binding orders until replaced by the Soviet Control Commission in October 1949.
8. Iron Curtain
A political and ideological image of Europe's division, popularized by Churchill's speech at Fulton on March 5, 1946; not to be confused with the later fortified physical frontier.
9. Long Telegram
George Kennan's lengthy diplomatic report of February 22, 1946, interpreting Soviet conduct as the product of insecurity and ideology and recommending firm resistance.
10. Novikov Telegram
Soviet ambassador Nikolai Novikov's report of September 27, 1946, assessing American foreign policy as an effort at global supremacy.
11. Containment
The American approach of resisting the expansion of Soviet influence, associated with Kennan's writing; in 1947 it was applied mainly through economic and limited military aid in Europe and the Mediterranean.
12. Truman Doctrine
The commitment announced by Truman on March 12, 1947, to support free peoples resisting subjugation, in practice a request for $400 million in aid to Greece and Turkey.
13. Marshall Plan
The American program of economic assistance to European recovery proposed by Secretary of State Marshall on June 5, 1947, and enacted in 1948; its implementation is examined in Chapter 126.
14. Cominform
The Communist Information Bureau founded in September 1947 to coordinate nine communist parties, which expelled Yugoslavia in June 1948.
15. Bizonia
The economic merger of the American and British zones, effective January 1, 1947; it was not a state and differed from the Federal Republic.
16. Socialist Unity Party of Germany (SED)
The party formed in April 1946 by the merger of the KPD and SPD in the Soviet zone, combining genuine support for left unity with Soviet-backed pressure, and later the ruling party of the GDR.
17. German Economic Commission
The central economic administration for the Soviet zone, created in June 1947, which coordinated planning and prefigured a central government.
18. Currency reform
The replacement of the nearly worthless Reichsmark with a new currency; here chiefly the western reform of June 20, 1948, and the rival Soviet-zone measure of June 1948.
19. Deutsche Mark
The currency introduced in the western occupation zones on June 20, 1948, and, with a "B" mark, in the western sectors of Berlin.
20. Berlin Blockade
The Soviet closure of road, rail, and waterborne access, and of electricity supplies, to the western sectors from June 24, 1948, to May 12, 1949; it did not close the air corridors.
21. Berlin Airlift
The Western supply of the western sectors by air from June 26, 1948, to September 30, 1949, continuing for months after the blockade ended.
22. Air corridor
One of three twenty-mile-wide routes, from Hamburg, Hanover, and Frankfurt, defined in the Control Council agreement of November 30, 1945, for flights to Berlin.
23. Operation Vittles
The American operation supplying Berlin by air.
24. Operation Plainfare
The British operation supplying Berlin by air, coordinated with but organized separately from Operation Vittles.
25. Brussels Treaty
The treaty of March 17, 1948, among Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg providing for cooperation and mutual defense.
26. North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)
The alliance founded on the North Atlantic Treaty signed on April 4, 1949, by twelve states; signature differs from later ratification and from the development of integrated military structures after 1950.
27. Basic Law (Grundgesetz)
The provisional constitutional framework of the Federal Republic, adopted May 8, 1949, and promulgated May 23, 1949, so named to preserve the aim of German unity.
28. Federal Republic of Germany (FRG)
The West German state founded on May 23, 1949, with limited sovereignty under the Occupation Statute of September 21, 1949.
29. German Democratic Republic (GDR)
The East German state founded on October 7, 1949, under SED leadership and Soviet supervision, without fully competitive elections.
30. German division
The process, extending across 1945 to 1949, by which a jointly occupied Germany became two separate states and part of a divided Europe; a cumulative outcome, not a single decision.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a continental order can emerge from a series of decisions whose cumulative effect no single actor intended. The victors of 1945 did not plan to divide Germany, yet their incompatible security needs and economic choices made a divided Germany the most practical outcome by 1949. The chapter therefore opens Volume VI with a lesson about contingency: the Cold War in Europe was made, step by step, and the steps could have been different.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that run through the rest of the volume. The link between economic recovery and political alignment, the use of alliances to institutionalize rivalry, the importance of divided cities and contested borders, and the role of ordinary people in sustaining or resisting political projects all recur. Germans appear as historical actors who built institutions, voted, resisted, emigrated, and endured, within limits set by occupying powers, and the later histories of the two German states in Chapters 125, 126, 127, and 129 continue that theme.

Several continuities survived beyond 1949. Berlin remained a point of friction, culminating in the crises of later years. The Basic Law's provisional status and its accession clause shaped the legal route to unification, examined in Chapter 132. The western security and economic arrangements begun here fed into the integration process that Chapter 133 examines, in which Franco-German reconciliation played a central role. The Soviet Union's search for security through control of its western borderlands continued to shape eastern Europe.

For the volume as a whole, the chapter connects the destruction and reckoning examined in Chapter 123 with the consolidation of rival systems and the new institutions of western Europe. It also reminds students that the "Iron Curtain" was first an idea, then a set of institutions, and only later a fortified frontier, and that the history of Cold War Europe should be read in its own time, not as the predetermined outcome of 1945.
