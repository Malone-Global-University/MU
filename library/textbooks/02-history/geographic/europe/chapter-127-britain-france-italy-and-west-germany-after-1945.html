Chapter Opening

In July 1945 Britain's voters removed the leader who had symbolized national survival, while in France, Italy, and Germany the very question of what kind of state could exist remained open. Four societies emerged from the same war, and each would build, restore, or reinvent democracy in a different way.

Polling in the British general election took place on 5 July 1945, but the count was delayed until 26 July so that votes cast by servicemen and servicewomen overseas could be gathered. When the results were announced, Winston Churchill, who had led Britain through the war and was at that moment attending the Potsdam Conference, had been rejected by the electorate. The Labour Party won roughly 393 of the 640 seats in the House of Commons, and its leader, Clement Attlee, formed a government with a very large parliamentary majority. Within a few weeks, Attlee himself was sitting at the same conference table as the victorious leaders of the United States and the Soviet Union. The episode is often remembered as a surprise, and it was a surprise to many politicians, but it also shows something fundamental about the political world of 1945: voters expected that the state that had mobilized the nation for war should now be used to secure employment, housing, health, and social protection in peacetime.

The same expectation existed across western Europe, but the political conditions in which it was expressed varied enormously. Britain had not been invaded or occupied, and its Parliament had continued to meet throughout the war. France had been defeated in 1940, governed in part by the collaborating regime at Vichy, occupied by Germany, and then liberated in 1944 by a combination of Allied armies and internal resistance. Italy had been the original fascist state, had changed sides in 1943, had suffered occupation in the north and fighting across the peninsula, and had experienced a civil war between the Italian Social Republic and the partisan movement. Germany, finally, had been defeated, occupied, and administered by four powers, and in 1949 its three western zones would be organized into a new state, the Federal Republic of Germany, whose history is the fourth case examined in this chapter.

These four starting points produced four constitutional settlements. Britain adapted an old and unwritten parliamentary constitution. France wrote a new parliamentary constitution in 1946, which lasted twelve years, and then replaced it in 1958 with a Fifth Republic that strengthened the presidency. Italy abolished its monarchy by referendum in 1946 and adopted a republican constitution that came into force on 1 January 1948. West Germany adopted a Basic Law on 23 May 1949, written in the shadow of the failures of the Weimar Republic and the crimes of the Nazi regime. Each settlement shaped how parties competed, how governments were formed and dismantled, how social reform was legislated, and how citizens remembered the war.

All four countries also operated under common pressures. The Cold War, examined in Chapters 124 and 125, made western European governments allies of the United States in a divided continent. Marshall Plan aid and the economic expansion described in Chapter 126 gave governments resources with which to pursue reform. Communist parties were large in France and Italy and politically insignificant in Britain and West Germany, which meant that the Cold War entered domestic politics in very different ways. Yet the similarities should not obscure the central point of this chapter: western European democracy after 1945 was not one political model reproduced in four places. It was a family of related but distinct arrangements, each built from particular histories, institutions, party traditions, and memories.

Historians reconstruct these developments from a wide range of evidence. Election returns and party programs reveal what voters were offered and what they chose. Constitutions and statutes show the formal rules, while parliamentary debates, cabinet papers, and party congress records show how those rules were contested and applied. Opinion surveys, newspapers, memoirs, diaries, films, and later oral histories provide evidence about public attitudes, although each carries limitations. Political leaders such as Attlee, de Gaulle, De Gasperi, and Adenauer wrote and spoke about their intentions, but their statements often justify choices already made. This chapter therefore keeps parties, legislatures, trade unions, churches, voters, and social organizations at the center of the account, and treats the leaders as actors operating within them.

This chapter belongs to Part XIX, Cold War Europe, and to Volume VI, which follows Europe from postwar division to the integrated and contested continent of the twenty-first century. It follows Chapter 126, which examined economic reconstruction, and precedes Chapter 128, which examines decolonization and its consequences for European societies. It concentrates on the years from 1945 to 1973, a period that begins with the democratic reconstruction of 1944 to 1949 and ends with Britain's entry into the European Economic Community and a new era of economic and political uncertainty. The stability of western European democracy by 1973 was real, but it was not inevitable, and in none of the four countries was democratic inclusion complete.

Historical Context

The democratic institutions examined in this chapter were shaped by developments that preceded 1945. In the interwar period, parliamentary government had collapsed or been suspended across much of Europe. Italy had experienced Mussolini's dictatorship from the 1920s, Germany had passed from the Weimar Republic to Nazi rule in 1933, and France had been divided by economic crisis and political polarization in the 1930s before the military defeat of 1940 brought the Third Republic to an end. Britain, by contrast, had maintained its parliamentary system throughout, though it had experienced mass unemployment in the 1930s, a political memory that became important for the Labour victory of 1945. These interwar histories are examined in Chapters 114 and 115.

The Second World War, examined in Part XVIII, shaped political life in each country differently. Chapter 120 compared systems of occupation, collaboration, and resistance, and Chapter 123 examined destruction, displacement, and the immediate postwar settlement. This chapter assumes that background and asks a narrower question: after the war, how were representative institutions rebuilt, and why did they develop in different directions? In France and Italy, resistance movements emerged from the war with claims to represent a renewed nation. In Britain, wartime coalition government and social planning produced a new set of expectations. In Germany, the defeat of the regime, Allied occupation, and revelations about its crimes made the legitimacy of any new German state dependent on a clear break with the past.

The economic setting, examined in Chapter 126, was also fundamental. Western European economies in 1945 were damaged, dependent on American assistance, and constrained by shortages of food, fuel, housing, and foreign exchange. Yet from the late 1940s, recovery gathered speed, and the long expansion of the 1950s and 1960s gave governments resources with which to extend social services and tolerate political disagreement. This chapter does not retell the economic story but asks how governments, parties, and electorates translated it into political institutions and policy.

International alignment mattered as well. Chapters 124 and 125 examined the division of Germany and the creation of NATO, and these decisions placed Britain, France, Italy, and West Germany within a Western security system led by the United States. They also made domestic communism an international issue. Both the French and Italian Communist Parties had gained prestige from their roles in the Resistance, held ministerial office in the first postwar governments, and commanded between a fifth and a third of the vote in free elections. The exclusion of communists from government in 1947, and the continuing efforts to limit their influence thereafter, shaped party systems in both countries for decades.

The evidence for this chapter is unusually rich but requires critical reading. Constitutional texts show formal intentions but not actual practice. Election results reveal voter choices but not their reasons, and electoral systems translated votes into seats in very different ways. Memoirs by political leaders, including those of Churchill and de Gaulle, are powerful but self-justifying. Later historical memory, including the national myths of a united resistance in France, the innocent Italian people, or a German "zero hour," often obscured as much as it revealed, and these myths are themselves subjects of historical analysis in Section 127.19.

Section 127.1

The Labour Victory of 1945 and Britain's Postwar Political Settlement

The general election of 1945 was the first British general election in ten years. It produced a Labour majority of roughly 145 seats, with Labour receiving about 47.7 percent of the vote and the Conservatives and their allies about 39.8 percent, while the Liberals, with around nine percent of the vote, won only a dozen seats. Turnout was about 72.7 percent. These figures are approximate, and the British single member plurality system magnified Labour's lead in seats, but even the vote share represented an unmistakable shift. The question for historians is why electors who admired Churchill as a war leader nevertheless voted for his opponents.

Several explanations work together. The wartime coalition government, formed in 1940, had included Labour ministers in positions of real responsibility, among them Attlee as deputy prime minister, Ernest Bevin at the Ministry of Labour and National Service, and Herbert Morrison at the Home Office. This gave Labour a credibility as a party of government that it had lacked in the 1930s. The wartime state had also demonstrated that it could plan production, ration scarce goods, and guarantee employment, and many voters concluded that these capacities should not be dismantled. The Beveridge Report of December 1942, which proposed a comprehensive system of social insurance, was widely read and discussed, and it was followed by an Education Act in 1944, a white paper on employment policy in the same year, and a Family Allowances Act in 1945. The coalition itself therefore created much of the framework of postwar reform, and the election turned partly on which party electors trusted to carry it out.

Memory of the 1930s also mattered. Mass unemployment in industrial regions, the means test, and the association of Conservative governments with appeasement left a political legacy that Labour exploited, and a polemical pamphlet of 1940 titled Guilty Men had helped fix the image of interwar Conservative leaders as responsible for unpreparedness. Churchill's personal prestige, meanwhile, was not easily transferable to his party. Contemporary opinion surveys had suggested a Labour lead, though many politicians doubted them, and the long delay in counting the results has encouraged the retrospective sense of surprise. Historians now emphasize that the outcome grew from social expectations, party organization, and wartime experience rather than from a sudden mood swing.

Labour's program, summarized in its manifesto, proposed full employment, public ownership of the Bank of England and of the fuel and power industries, inland transport, and iron and steel, a comprehensive national health service, extended social insurance, and a large housing program. It did not propose to abolish private enterprise. The new government's cabinet combined moderate and radical figures: Attlee chaired it as a patient manager, Bevin directed foreign policy, Hugh Dalton and later Sir Stafford Cripps handled the Treasury, Morrison coordinated domestic policy, and Aneurin Bevan, the minister responsible for health and housing, became the most celebrated advocate of the new welfare institutions.

The settlement that emerged was one of reform inside continuity. Britain had no written constitution, no constitutional court, and no revolutionary rupture. The monarchy, the House of Commons, the House of Lords, the civil service, local government, and the courts all continued. Reform was legislated by statute on the strength of an electoral mandate, and the Conservative majority in the Lords generally refrained from blocking bills that had been foreshadowed in the Labour manifesto, a restraint later associated with the Salisbury convention. In 1949 Parliament shortened the Lords' power of delay, but the House itself remained hereditary in composition. Britain's economic position, moreover, constrained the new government. The war had depleted overseas assets and left heavy external debts, Lend-Lease aid ended abruptly in August 1945, and a large American loan was negotiated in December on terms that required sterling convertibility, a condition that contributed to the crisis of 1947. These constraints are examined in economic terms in Chapter 126.

Primary Source Window

The Labour Party, Let Us Face the Future, 1945 election manifesto

Creator: The Labour Party, with drafting by party officials and the National Executive Committee · Date: spring 1945, for the general election of July 1945 · Type: Political party manifesto

Context: The manifesto was published as the wartime coalition drew to a close and was designed to present Labour as a party ready to govern. Its title, Let Us Face the Future, framed the election as a choice about postwar reconstruction.

Paraphrase of the manifesto's program The manifesto argued that the war effort had shown the value of organized national resources and promised to use similar public direction in peacetime. It committed Labour to full employment, to public ownership of the Bank of England, fuel and power, transport, and iron and steel, to a national health service, to an extensive housing program, and to social security. It did not call for the abolition of private enterprise. This is a paraphrase of the document's general argument rather than a quotation.

Analysis: The manifesto is valuable because it shows that the principal Labour reforms of 1945 to 1951 were publicly announced before the election and could therefore be presented as a democratic mandate. It also shows that Labour combined its older socialist commitment to public ownership with the wartime ideal of managed prosperity. Its limitations are those of any election document: it is a statement of intention and persuasion, written to attract votes, and it does not reveal the disagreements within the party or the compromises that governing would require. It also tells historians little about how voters themselves understood its promises.

Section 127.2

The Attlee Government, Nationalization, and the Welfare State

The Attlee governments of 1945 to 1951 enacted their program through a rapid sequence of statutes. The National Insurance Act of 1946 created a contributory system covering unemployment, sickness, retirement, maternity, widowhood, and death, and it came into operation in July 1948. The National Assistance Act of 1948 replaced the surviving Poor Law with a national safety net. The National Health Service Act of 1946 established a service that began operating on 5 July 1948. Housing legislation, together with the New Towns Act of 1946 and the Town and Country Planning Act of 1947, gave central and local government a larger role in directing building and land use. Chapter 126 examined the economic consequences of these programs. This section is concerned with the political decisions behind them and the institutions through which they were carried out.

The National Health Service shows the political bargaining involved. Bevan wished to bring hospitals into a national system, and the Act transferred voluntary and municipal hospitals to regional hospital boards. General practitioners, however, remained independent contractors paid by the state, and consultants were permitted to retain some private practice. These concessions answered resistance from the British Medical Association, whose members feared becoming salaried state employees, and they illustrate how Bevan secured a national service by negotiating with organized professional interests rather than overriding them. Local government kept a role in some community health services but lost its hospitals, a loss that reflected both the aim of national uniformity and the weak financial position of many municipal authorities.

Nationalization followed a similar pattern of legislated reform. The Bank of England was taken into public ownership in 1946, the coal industry passed to the National Coal Board on 1 January 1947, and the railways and other inland transport were brought under the British Transport Commission in 1948. Electricity and gas were nationalized in 1948 and 1949, and iron and steel became the most controversial case, with the Iron and Steel Act of 1949 delaying the actual transfer until 1951. The preferred model, associated with Herbert Morrison, was the public corporation, in which an appointed board managed an industry on behalf of the public with compensation paid to former owners. This model gave trade unionists a voice through consultation, but it fell short of the workers' control that some on the left had hoped for, and many miners and railwaymen found that daily relations with management changed less than they had expected.

The political context included strong constraints. Rationing continued, and many items were not freed from restriction until the early 1950s. The severe winter of 1947 produced a fuel crisis, the convertibility of sterling in July and August 1947 drained reserves and was suspended, and Marshall Plan assistance arrived in 1948. In 1948 the government negotiated wage restraint with the Trades Union Congress, and in September 1949 it devalued the pound. Trade unions were thus both supporters and constraints, and the government's relationship with them depended on a delicate bargain between pursuing full employment and avoiding inflation. After the election of February 1950, Labour's majority fell to only five seats, and tensions within the party became public in April 1951, when Bevan resigned from the cabinet along with Harold Wilson and John Freeman in protest over the introduction of charges for certain health service items to help finance rearmament during the Korean War.

The Conservative opposition criticized the pace and structure of reform, attacked nationalization of steel in particular, and worked to modify the details of the health service, but it did not campaign to abolish the institutions Labour created. This reluctance is one reason historians speak of a settlement. The historical importance of the Attlee government lay less in any single measure than in the establishment, through ordinary parliamentary procedure and with electoral consent, of the principle that central government was responsible for social security, health, and employment. The achievements were real, but so were the limits: shortages persisted, productivity problems remained, and Britain's international commitments, including the independence of India in 1947 and a costly defense program, constrained spending. Imperial withdrawal is examined in Chapter 128.

Section 127.3

Conservative Government, Party Competition, and the Postwar Consensus

The election of October 1951 returned the Conservatives to power under Churchill, now aged 76. Labour in fact received slightly more votes than its rival, about 48.8 percent to 48.0 percent, but the Conservatives won about 321 seats against about 295, an illustration of how the plurality system can separate vote share from seats. The Conservative governments that followed, under Churchill until 1955, Anthony Eden until 1957, Harold Macmillan until 1963, and Alec Douglas-Home until 1964, did not dismantle the welfare state. They reversed the nationalization of steel and road haulage, but they retained the National Health Service, the coal industry, the railways, and the insurance system. Conservative recovery in opposition had been shaped by a reappraisal of the party's economic message, including the Industrial Charter of 1947 associated with R. A. Butler, which accepted full employment as a central aim of policy.

Housing offers a good example of political competition within a shared framework. Macmillan, as housing minister, made a target of 300,000 new houses a year a political pledge, and the figure was reached in 1953, but the program relied heavily on local authority building and on subsidies that Labour had also supported. Rationing ended by 1954, consumer spending expanded, and unemployment remained low by prewar standards. In 1954 the magazine The Economist coined the term "Butskellism," blending the names of Butler, then the Conservative Chancellor of the Exchequer, and Hugh Gaitskell, his Labour predecessor, to describe what appeared to be a similar approach to economic management. Conservative election victories in 1955 and 1959, the latter with a majority of about 100 seats, reflected both genuine prosperity and the party's skill in presenting itself as a manager of rising living standards. Macmillan's speech in 1957, in which he suggested that many Britons had never had it so good, entered political memory as a symbol of the age.

Foreign affairs tested the governing parties and the idea of national strength. The Suez crisis of 1956, in which Britain and France colluded with Israel in an attack on Egypt and were forced to withdraw under American financial and diplomatic pressure, damaged Eden's government and led to his resignation in January 1957. The episode is treated in the context of decolonization in Chapter 128, but its domestic consequence was to expose the limits of British independent power and to deepen debates about Britain's international role. Macmillan's government recovered, but difficulties accumulated in the early 1960s, including economic stop and go cycles, a veto by de Gaulle in January 1963 of Britain's first application to the European Economic Community, and the Profumo scandal. Macmillan resigned in October 1963 because of ill health, and the succession went to Lord Home, who disclaimed his peerage under the Peerage Act of 1963 to become Sir Alec Douglas-Home. The selection process, conducted through informal soundings, was criticized, and in 1965 the Conservatives adopted a procedure for electing their leader by vote of MPs.

Labour, meanwhile, was divided. Disputes between the followers of Gaitskell, who became leader in 1955, and the left associated with Bevan concerned nationalization, nuclear weapons, and the party's identity. Gaitskell's attempt in 1959 and 1960 to revise the party's constitutional commitment to common ownership failed, and the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, founded in 1958, became a focus for left wing dissent. The label "consensus" therefore needs care. There was agreement on the basic institutions of welfare and on the goal of full employment, but there was persistent disagreement about steel, education, trade union law, the scale of public ownership, nuclear weapons, and how far to redistribute income.

Historical Vocabulary
Postwar Consensus

Postwar consensus is a historians' term for the apparent agreement among the major British parties, from the late 1940s to the 1970s, on a mixed economy, full employment, and a welfare state. The term was popularized in later accounts, notably those that contrasted the period with the more conflictual politics of the 1980s. It matters here because it summarizes a genuine convergence but may also conceal significant disputes. Students should not assume that the term was used by contemporaries in the same way, or that it means that the parties agreed on every issue.

Historical Debate

Was There a Postwar Consensus in British Politics?

The question: Did the Labour and Conservative parties share a common approach to economic and social policy between 1945 and the 1970s, or does the term "consensus" misrepresent a period of real party conflict?

Interpretation A: A consensus existed. Historians such as Paul Addison have argued that wartime experience produced a broad agreement on full employment, social security, and a mixed economy, and that the Conservative governments after 1951 maintained rather than reversed the Attlee settlement. In this view, differences between the parties were matters of emphasis within a shared framework.
Interpretation B: The consensus was exaggerated. Ben Pimlott, notably in his 1988 essay “The Myth of Consensus,” argued that accounts of a broad consensus can underestimate the real conflict over steel, housing finance, education, trade union reform, nuclear weapons, and relations with Europe, and that Conservative acceptance of welfare institutions was pragmatic, conditional, and accompanied by different goals. They also point to disagreement within each party and to the importance of electoral calculation in explaining Conservative caution.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on party manifestos, cabinet papers, parliamentary divisions, and the policy record, including the denationalization of steel and the retention of the health service. They differ in what weight they give to shared institutions as opposed to disputed purposes.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand both the stability of postwar Britain and the later political change of the 1970s and 1980s, since a strong consensus can appear either as the foundation of stability or as a constraint later challenged.

Student task: Identify one policy area in this chapter that supports the idea of consensus and one that complicates it. Then explain what kinds of additional evidence, such as private party papers or voter surveys, would help resolve the question.

Section 127.4

Britain in the 1960s: Political Modernization and Changing National Identity

Harold Wilson became Labour leader in February 1963 after Gaitskell's death and made modernization his central theme. At the party's conference that autumn he spoke of a scientific and technological revolution, a phrase often shortened to the "white heat" of technology, and argued that Britain's economic weakness required planning, education, and applied science. Labour won the election of October 1964 by only four seats, but in March 1966 it secured a majority of about 96. The government established a Department of Economic Affairs, issued a National Plan in 1965, created a Ministry of Technology, and promoted industrial reorganization. The plan was soon overtaken by recurrent balance of payments difficulties, and after strong pressure on sterling the government devalued the pound in November 1967.

Education policy illustrates both continuity and change. The Robbins Report of 1963, commissioned under the Conservatives, recommended a major expansion of higher education, and the Labour government accepted the principle. A government circular of 1965 asked local authorities to prepare plans for comprehensive secondary schools in place of the selective system created after the Education Act of 1944, though implementation depended on local decisions and varied widely. Wilson also championed what became the Open University, chartered in 1969, which offered degrees by distance learning to adults. Parliament also passed a series of social measures, many of them through private members' bills with government support, including the suspension and then abolition of the death penalty, the partial decriminalization of homosexual acts between adult men in England and Wales in 1967, the Abortion Act of 1967, and the Divorce Reform Act of 1969. Their causes and cultural setting are considered further in Chapter 130.

The government's attempt to reform industrial relations met resistance. A white paper of 1969, In Place of Strife, proposed legal limits on unofficial strikes, but opposition from trade unions and from within the Labour Party led to its abandonment. This was an early sign of the difficult relationship between governments and organized labor that would increasingly shape British politics. Labour lost the general election of June 1970 to the Conservatives under Edward Heath, which was a result that few observers had predicted.

Britain's international position was changing. The government announced in January 1968 that it would withdraw British military forces from most bases east of Suez, and it made a second application to join the European Economic Community in 1967 that de Gaulle again vetoed in November of that year. After Heath's victory, negotiations succeeded, an accession treaty was signed in January 1972, and Britain entered the Community on 1 January 1973. Heath's government explained this as a response to Britain's changed economic and strategic circumstances, while critics in both parties defended Commonwealth ties and parliamentary sovereignty. The institutional history of the Community is reserved for Chapter 133, but the decision belongs here because it altered how many Britons thought about their country's place in the world.

National identity within the United Kingdom was also less settled than the term "British" might suggest. Scottish and Welsh nationalism gained electoral visibility, with Plaid Cymru's Gwynfor Evans winning a by-election at Carmarthen in 1966 and the Scottish National Party's Winnie Ewing winning at Hamilton in 1967, and Wilson's government appointed a commission on the constitution in 1968. Northern Ireland posed a deeper challenge. Since 1921 it had been governed by a devolved parliament dominated by Unionists, and the Catholic and nationalist minority complained of discrimination in housing, employment, and local government. A civil rights movement organized marches from 1968, and a march in Derry on 5 October 1968 was violently broken up by police. After serious rioting in August 1969, British troops were deployed, and over the following years violence escalated, culminating in the direct imposition of rule from London in 1972. The Northern Irish crisis demonstrated that the apparently stable British parliamentary system could not resolve every question of political legitimacy within the United Kingdom. Wider imperial retreat is examined in Chapter 128.

Division I outcome. Britain emerged from this period as a comparatively continuous parliamentary democracy. Its institutions were not replaced, but the responsibilities of government expanded, party competition revolved around the management of a mixed economy and welfare state, and national self-understanding moved away from imperial certainty toward uncertainty about Britain's role in Europe and about the unity of the United Kingdom itself.

Division II France: Republican Reconstruction, Political Instability, and Gaullism Sections 127.5 to 127.8 · Principal period: 1944–1973
Section 127.5

Liberation, Political Reconstruction, and the Fourth Republic

France began its postwar political reconstruction without a legitimate constitution. The Third Republic had voted full powers to Marshal Philippe Pétain in July 1940, and the regime at Vichy had claimed to replace it. The provisional government of the French Republic, headed by Charles de Gaulle, took shape in 1944 out of the movement of Free France and the Resistance and moved to Paris after its liberation in August. De Gaulle declared that the Republic had never ceased to exist, a claim that made Vichy appear an illegitimate interruption rather than a regime with a significant base of support. This position, politically useful for national reunification, made the later reckoning with Vichy more difficult, and it shaped the memory politics examined in Section 127.19.

The new political order was defined by an ambitious social and democratic program. In March 1944 the National Council of the Resistance had issued a charter that called for the restoration of democratic institutions and proposed extensive social and economic reforms, including a comprehensive social security system and nationalization of key industries. A provisional government ordinance of April 1944 gave women the vote, and French women voted for the first time in municipal elections in 1945 and in a national consultation in October 1945. Social security ordinances of 1945 created the basis of a national insurance system. Major nationalizations included the coal mines, electricity and gas, several large banks, and the Renault automobile firm, and a planning commissariat under Jean Monnet was established in 1946. The Liberation also saw an épuration, a purge of collaborators that involved extrajudicial killings, formal trials, and the public humiliation of women accused of relations with Germans. Historians estimate roughly ten thousand extrajudicial killings, fewer than 800 formal executions after trial, and perhaps twenty thousand women whose heads were shaved, all figures that remain approximate.

The constitutional question was settled slowly. A referendum in October 1945 asked voters to approve the election of a constituent assembly and then to limit its powers, and they responded overwhelmingly to the first question and by roughly two to one to the second, which indicated rejection of a return to the Third Republic. The Communists, Socialists, and the Christian democratic Popular Republican Movement (MRP) each won about a quarter of the vote. De Gaulle was elected head of the provisional government but resigned in January 1946, frustrated by the parties' insistence on the Assembly's supremacy. A first draft constitution, supported by the Communists and Socialists and proposing a single powerful chamber, was rejected by referendum in May 1946. In a second constituent election in June, the MRP became the largest party. On 16 June 1946 de Gaulle delivered a speech at Bayeux in which he argued for a strong executive above party competition, a position that would eventually shape the constitution of 1958 but that was not adopted in 1946.

A second draft was approved by referendum on 13 October 1946, narrowly, with about 53 percent of votes cast in favor and a high level of abstention, and was promulgated on 27 October. The Constitution created a National Assembly elected by proportional representation and holding most legislative power, a Council of the Republic with limited authority, and a President of the Republic elected by Parliament with mainly ceremonial functions. The President of the Council, in effect the prime minister, required a vote of investiture from the Assembly, and dissolution of the Assembly was constrained by conditions that made it hard to use. The preamble affirmed the rights of 1789 and added social principles, including equal rights for women and a right to employment. The Constitution also created the French Union, whose colonial provisions are examined in Chapter 128.

Section 127.6

The Fourth Republic: Party Systems, Coalitions, and Government Instability

The Fourth Republic's party system included several strong forces. The French Communist Party (PCF), led by Maurice Thorez, drew on its resistance record and its base among industrial workers, and it won around a quarter of the vote or more in the elections of 1945 and 1946. The Socialists, the SFIO, were led by Guy Mollet from 1946 and combined a Marxist tradition with participation in republican government. The MRP represented Catholic democratic politics, and its leaders included Georges Bidault and Robert Schuman. The Radicals, a traditional republican party, retained influence in a reduced form, while conservative and peasant groups formed independent parliamentary groupings. In 1947 de Gaulle created the Rally of the French People (RPF), which condemned the party system and, in the 1951 elections, won about a fifth of the votes.

Between 1945 and 1947, the three largest parties governed together in a form of coalition often called tripartism. The Cold War ended it. In May 1947 the Socialist prime minister Paul Ramadier dismissed the Communist ministers after they declined to support the government's wage policy, and the Communists went into opposition, followed by widespread strikes in late 1947. From then on, governments depended on a variable alliance of Socialists, the MRP, Radicals, and moderate conservatives, a combination called the Third Force because it opposed both Communists on the left and Gaullists on the right. Its parliamentary base was narrow. The electoral law of 1951 allowed lists to ally with one another and share seats, a device designed to reduce the representation of the extremes. It was partly successful, but the underlying problem of securing durable majorities remained.

Between October 1946 and May 1958, France had more than twenty cabinets, with the average duration of a government measured in months. Yet the picture of constant chaos requires qualification. Ministers frequently reappeared in successive governments, and the senior civil service, including the new École nationale d'administration created in 1945, provided continuity. Governments carried through important policies: the Monnet Plan, social security, the rebuilding of heavy industry, and above all European initiatives such as the Schuman Plan of 1950, the European Coal and Steel Community of 1951, and the signing of the Treaty of Rome in 1957. The Fourth Republic was also a period of rapid economic growth, examined in Chapter 126. Instability was therefore more limited as a problem of governmental capacity than as a problem of decisions involving the deepest conflicts of the period.

The most serious such conflicts concerned the empire. The war in Indochina, which began in 1946, ended after the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, and the government of Pierre Mendès France negotiated a settlement at Geneva. In the same year, the Assembly refused to approve the proposed European Defence Community, which had been an initiative of French ministers, and the project collapsed after a vote on 30 August 1954. Insurrection began in Algeria on 1 November 1954, and the Suez expedition of 1956 failed. The 1956 elections also brought to Parliament some fifty deputies of Pierre Poujade's movement, an anti tax and anti establishment protest. These pressures revealed how a fragmented Assembly found it difficult to take clear decisions on matters that divided its own coalition. Detailed colonial warfare is reserved for Chapter 128.

Historians therefore distinguish unstable ministries from complete institutional paralysis. The Fourth Republic had many weaknesses, among them the dominance of the legislature, difficulty in dissolving the Assembly, and parties unwilling to accept the responsibilities of governing together. A revision of the Constitution in 1954 eased the investiture of the premier but did not resolve the problem. At the same time, government continued, policy was made, and the regime's economic and European record was substantial. Its final crisis resulted from a combination of institutional weakness and an external war that threatened the army, the settlers, and the political parties alike.

Section 127.7

The Crisis of 1958 and the Establishment of the Fifth Republic

The crisis began in Algiers on 13 May 1958, when demonstrators supporting French Algeria seized the government buildings, and a Committee of Public Safety was formed with military participation, including General Jacques Massu and General Raoul Salan. They demanded the return to power of de Gaulle, who had been out of office since 1946. The same day, the National Assembly invested Pierre Pflimlin as premier. Paratroops in Algiers took control of Corsica on 24 May, and some officers prepared plans for action in metropolitan France. De Gaulle said on 15 May that he was ready to assume the powers of the Republic, and negotiations between him and political leaders followed. On 29 May President René Coty informed Parliament that he would resign unless de Gaulle were chosen, and on 1 June the Assembly invested de Gaulle by 329 votes to 224. The transition was legal in form, though it took place under the threat of military insurrection, and it was supported by many who feared civil conflict as well as by those who admired him.

De Gaulle was given special powers for six months and authority to prepare a new constitution on principles set out in a constitutional law of 3 June 1958: universal suffrage, separation of legislative and executive powers, ministerial responsibility to Parliament, and judicial independence. The draft was prepared by the Justice Minister Michel Debré with the Council of State and a consultative committee. The resulting Constitution was approved by referendum on 28 September 1958 with 79.25 percent of valid votes in metropolitan France, or approximately 85.14 percent when the wider French Republic and overseas territories counted in the official proclamation are included, and promulgated on 4 October. These results have different geographic denominators and should not be conflated. It strengthened the executive in several ways. The president, initially chosen by an electoral college of local and national officials, was given responsibility to arbitrate between public authorities, appoint the prime minister, dissolve the Assembly, call certain referendums, and exercise emergency powers under Article 16. The government was responsible for determining national policy and could be overthrown only by an absolute majority of the Assembly in a motion of censure. The domain of parliamentary law was defined and limited, a Constitutional Council was established, and the government acquired procedural tools to advance legislation, including the later much used Article 49.3.

Historical Vocabulary
Semi-presidential Government

Semi-presidential government is a term political scientists, notably Maurice Duverger, later applied to systems like the Fifth Republic, in which a directly elected president with real powers coexists with a prime minister and government accountable to the legislature. It matters here because the 1958 Constitution did not describe itself in this way, and its early form involved an indirectly elected president. The term should not be read backward as a blueprint intended in 1958. It names a pattern that emerged through the constitutional amendments of 1962 and the practice of de Gaulle and his successors.

De Gaulle was elected president in December 1958 and took office on 8 January 1959, with Debré as prime minister. The Algerian war continued until the Evian Accords of March 1962, after which Algeria became independent, and its political and human dimensions belong to Chapter 128. In April 1961 several generals attempted a coup in Algiers, which failed, and de Gaulle used Article 16 emergency powers for several months. In August 1962 he narrowly survived an assassination attempt at Petit Clamart. This event strengthened his argument that the presidency needed a firmer popular base, and in October 1962 he proposed that future presidents be elected by direct universal suffrage.

The method he chose was controversial. De Gaulle submitted the proposal to a referendum under Article 11, which concerned laws organizing public powers, rather than under Article 89, which required the approval of both chambers for a constitutional amendment. The Council of State and the president of the Senate both criticized this as unconstitutional. The Assembly passed a motion of censure against Prime Minister Georges Pompidou on 5 October 1962, the only successful censure motion in the history of the Fifth Republic. De Gaulle dissolved the Assembly. The referendum on 28 October approved the change with about 62 percent of votes cast, and the elections the following month gave the Gaullist party and its allies a working majority. The first direct presidential election, in December 1965, forced de Gaulle into a second round against the Socialist François Mitterrand, whom he defeated with about 55 percent.

Cause and Effect

The Constitutional Transformation of France, 1958 to 1962

Long-term causes

A republican tradition of legislative dominance, a fragmented party system, the memory of Vichy and of the interwar crisis, and de Gaulle's long standing argument for a strong executive above parties.

Short-term causes

The Algerian war, the unwillingness of the army and settlers to accept a settlement, ministerial instability, and the fear of civil conflict in May 1958.

Event or transformation

The investiture of de Gaulle on 1 June 1958, the Constitution of 4 October 1958, and the 1962 referendum introducing direct presidential election.

Immediate effects

A strengthened government and president, a diminished role for parliamentary parties, an Algerian settlement, and a realignment of party politics around support for or opposition to Gaullism.

Long-term consequences: The president became the central figure of French political life, party competition reorganized around presidential elections, and the regime outlived its founder.

Historical complexity: The crisis cannot be reduced to one person or one cause. Algeria, army politics, parliamentary weakness, the willingness of many republicans to accept de Gaulle as a lesser evil, and the constitutional design all interacted. Many contemporaries on the left described the change as a coup, while supporters described it as a restoration of legitimate authority.

Section 127.8

Gaullism, Republican Authority, and France's Changing National Identity

Gaullism was less a systematic ideology than a combination of convictions: that the state should stand above partisan divisions, that executive leadership was needed to protect national independence, that France should retain a role of world significance, and that technocratic planning could modernize the economy. De Gaulle's memoirs open with a reflection on "a certain idea of France," and it conveys the sense of national mission he projected. His policies on foreign affairs followed from it. France conducted its first nuclear test in Algeria in February 1960, recognized the People's Republic of China in 1964, concluded the Élysée Treaty of Franco-German friendship in January 1963, vetoed British entry into the European Economic Community, and in 1966 withdrew from the integrated military command of NATO while remaining in the Atlantic Alliance.

The relationship between de Gaulle and the parties was ambivalent. His supporters organized the Union for the New Republic in 1958, later renamed, and it became the largest party in Parliament, but de Gaulle himself maintained a stance of distance from partisan politics and appealed to the electorate through referendums and television. His relationship with the army was tested by the Algerian war and the 1961 coup attempt. The Left, including the Communists, Socialists, and later Mitterrand's federation, opposed what they saw as personal power. The legislative elections of 1967 left the Gaullist majority very narrow, which indicated that the regime was not as secure as its early victories suggested.

The crisis of May and June 1968 started as a student protest and became a general strike involving millions of workers. It is examined in detail in Chapter 130, but its political importance for the Fifth Republic is clear. The government negotiated a package of wage and workplace concessions, known as the Grenelle agreements, in late May. De Gaulle briefly left Paris on 29 May to consult military commanders, and on 30 May he announced the dissolution of the Assembly. The elections in June produced a decisive victory for the Gaullists and their allies, which showed that a large part of the electorate responded to the disorder by seeking order. The result strengthened the regime politically, but de Gaulle's authority was damaged.

In April 1969 de Gaulle submitted a referendum proposal to reform the Senate and create regions, and he linked his continuation in office to the result. The proposal was rejected by about 52 percent, and he resigned immediately. Georges Pompidou, his former prime minister, won the presidential election in June 1969 against the centrist Alain Poher. Pompidou maintained the institutions, the nuclear program, and a broadly pro-industrial policy, but he was more open to European integration and to British entry. The transition showed that the Fifth Republic could survive the departure of its founder and was becoming an institutional system rather than one leader's personal government. Even so, many of its practices depended on presidents who commanded a majority in the Assembly, a condition that would later be tested in ways that lie beyond this chapter.

French national identity changed significantly during this period. The loss of empire, concluded by Algerian independence in 1962, shifted the nation's focus toward Europe and the modernization of its economy. Gaullist memory emphasized the resistance and the continuity of the Republic, and it placed collaboration at the margins. The close of the Algerian war also brought hundreds of thousands of settlers and others to metropolitan France, and left lasting bitterness that is examined in Chapter 128. Division II outcome. The Fourth Republic was unstable because of a fragmented party system, an Assembly with limited governmental discipline, and the burden of colonial war. The Fifth Republic changed the balance of authority by strengthening the president and the government, and Gaullism offered a distinctive answer to postwar democratic governance that combined popular legitimacy, executive leadership, and national independence.

Division III Italy: Republican Reconstruction, Christian Democracy, and Political Fragmentation Sections 127.9 to 127.12 · Principal period: 1945–1973
Section 127.9

From Fascist Dictatorship and Monarchy to the Italian Republic

Italy's route to a republic was shaped by defeat, division, and civil conflict. Mussolini was removed from power on 25 July 1943 by the Fascist Grand Council and the King, Victor Emmanuel III, and the new government under Marshal Pietro Badoglio announced an armistice with the Allies on 8 September. German forces occupied the north and center, and Mussolini was installed as head of the Italian Social Republic, a German dependent state based at Salò. The monarchy and the Badoglio government moved south under Allied protection. In the occupied north, a partisan resistance organized under a National Liberation Committee (CLN), which brought together Communists, Socialists, Christian Democrats, liberals, and the Action Party. The war in Italy ended with the insurrection and liberation of the northern cities on 25 April 1945, and Mussolini was captured and killed by partisans a few days later. The experience of the resistance gave the political parties of the new Italy a claim to legitimacy, but the war had also been a civil conflict in which many Italians had served the fascist regime, and the war's divisions remained part of political memory.

Women had already been given the vote by a decree of early 1945, and they voted in local elections in March 1946. The decisive vote was held on 2 June 1946, when Italians were asked in a referendum to choose between monarchy and republic and simultaneously elected a Constituent Assembly. Victor Emmanuel had abdicated in favor of his son Umberto II in May in an attempt to improve the monarchy's chances. The republic won with about 54 percent of the vote, with the monarchy winning around 46 percent. The regional pattern was striking: the north and much of the center voted for the republic, while the south and the islands favored the monarchy. Some monarchists alleged irregularities, but the result was accepted and Umberto departed in June. The Constituent Assembly of 556 members included 21 women, and its largest party was the Christian Democrats, with about 35 percent of the vote, followed by the Socialists and the Communists.

The Assembly's committee of seventy-five, working with the full Assembly, drafted a constitution that reflected a compromise among the Catholic, Socialist, Communist, and liberal traditions. The Constitution was approved in December 1947 and entered into force on 1 January 1948. It declared Italy a democratic republic founded on labor, established a parliament of two directly elected chambers with substantially equal legislative powers, created a president elected by Parliament in joint session with regional delegates as provided by the Constitution, and provided for a Constitutional Court, a judicial governing council, regional governments, and the referendum. In a decision of 1947, with Communist support, it incorporated the Lateran Pacts of 1929 between the Italian state and the Holy See into Article 7, a compromise that reflected the strength of the Catholic world and the Communists' desire to avoid a religious conflict. Electoral laws, not the Constitution itself, determined the system used in 1948: the Chamber of Deputies was elected proportionally, while the Senate employed single-member districts with a threshold exceeding 65 percent for a direct victory, and seats not filled by that method were allocated to candidate groups proportionally within regions. Only a small minority of Senate seats were won directly at that threshold. Regional delegates did not participate when Luigi Einaudi was elected president in May 1948, because the ordinary regional councils had not yet been constituted. Many of the Constitution's institutions were slow to be implemented: the Constitutional Court did not begin operation until 1956, and ordinary regional governments were not created until 1970.

The new republic's territory and sovereignty were also settled externally. The peace treaty of February 1947 required Italy to renounce its colonies, to cede most of Istria and other territories to Yugoslavia and the Dodecanese Islands to Greece, and to accept limits on its armed forces. The treaty created a Free Territory of Trieste, which was never fully established and was divided into two zones, and the city's status was resolved in practice only in 1954, when it returned to Italian administration. In South Tyrol, an agreement of September 1946 between the Austrian and Italian foreign ministers promised autonomy to the German speaking population, though its implementation was long contested. The continuity of the state apparatus was also significant: the purge of fascists was limited, an amnesty announced in June 1946 by the Communist Minister of Justice Palmiro Togliatti released many from penalties, and much of the fascist era's administrative staff, legal codes, and public institutions continued to operate. The republic was therefore built on an antifascist foundation but also on important administrative continuities.

Section 127.10

Christian Democracy, the Communist Party, and the Election of 1948

The Christian Democratic Party (DC) emerged in 1943 as the political heir of earlier Catholic political movements, with support from parish networks, Catholic Action, Catholic labor and peasant organizations, and, more cautiously but unmistakably, the Church hierarchy under Pope Pius XII. Its leader, Alcide De Gasperi, a former deputy from the Trentino who had been imprisoned and then sheltered in the Vatican under fascism, served as prime minister from December 1945 to 1953. The DC was an interclass party that included conservative landowners, Catholic trade unionists, and reformers, and its capacity to hold these constituencies together would be crucial to its durability.

The Italian Communist Party (PCI), under Togliatti, who returned from Moscow in March 1944, pursued a distinctive strategy. In what became known as the Salerno turn, Togliatti proposed that the Communists participate in a broad antifascist government and postpone the question of the monarchy. The party described itself as a mass party of a new type, committed to the constitutional framework, and built a large membership, probably around two million in the late 1940s, together with a network of local sections, cooperatives, newspapers, and cultural associations. Its strength lay in the industrial north and in the central regions of Emilia Romagna, Tuscany, Umbria, and the Marche. The Socialist Party (PSI), led by Pietro Nenni, was allied with the Communists, but it split in January 1947 when a group led by Giuseppe Saragat left to form a social democratic party.

International events transformed Italian politics in 1947. The Marshall Plan, announced in June, and the formation of the Cominform in September, made the choice between East and West a domestic political issue. In May 1947 De Gasperi formed a government without the Communists and Socialists. The general election of 18 April 1948 was therefore fought as a Cold War referendum. The Communists and Socialists ran on a joint list, the Popular Democratic Front, whose symbol was a portrait of Garibaldi. The Christian Democrats were supported by the Church, by a network of civic committees organized by Catholic Action, and by public statements from American officials, as well as private appeals from Italian Americans to relatives in Italy. The communist coup in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 gave anti communist campaigners a powerful argument. The Christian Democrats won roughly 48.5 percent of the vote and an absolute majority of seats in the Chamber of Deputies, while the Popular Front won about 31 percent.

Section 127.11

Coalition Government, Republican Institutions, and the Center-Left

The structure of the Italian system produced a distinctive political rhythm. Proportional representation encouraged many parties, and no party after 1948 won a majority of seats on its own. Christian Democracy nevertheless dominated governments because it was the largest party and because the Communists on the left and the neofascist Italian Social Movement (MSI) on the right were treated as unacceptable partners. Coalitions therefore depended on the DC and a rotating group of smaller allies. Within the party, factions organized around figures such as Amintore Fanfani, Aldo Moro, and later Giulio Andreotti competed for control of appointments and policy. Cabinets changed often, but personnel and policies were more stable than the number of governments suggests, and the Christian Democrats remained the central party for decades.

De Gasperi attempted to consolidate this structure in 1953 by introducing an electoral law that would give a bonus of seats to a coalition receiving more than half the vote. Opponents called it the "swindle law." In the election of June 1953 the governing coalition fell just short of the necessary majority, with the DC receiving about 40 percent, and the law was repealed. De Gasperi's authority declined, and he died in 1954. The following years were marked by short ministries and by efforts to use state institutions for development. The state holding company IRI, created under fascism, remained a central instrument, the national energy agency ENI was founded in 1953 under Enrico Mattei, and the Fund for the South (Cassa per il Mezzogiorno) was established in 1950 to finance infrastructure and development in the south. These institutions illustrate the continuity between the fascist and republican state and also how public bodies became channels of political patronage.

Political realignment on the left opened the possibility of a change in coalition. After the Soviet intervention in Hungary in 1956, the Socialists distanced themselves from the Communists, and their leader Nenni gradually accepted the framework of republican institutions. The Christian Democrats were divided over whether to cooperate with the Socialists. In July 1960, a government under Fernando Tambroni survived only with votes from the MSI, and when the neofascist party was permitted to hold a congress in Genoa, large demonstrations erupted. Police killed demonstrators in several towns, including five at Reggio Emilia on 7 July. Tambroni resigned, and the episode discredited any alliance with the neofascist right. Under Moro and Fanfani, the Christian Democrats then moved toward an "opening to the left." In 1962 a government supported by Socialist abstention nationalized electricity through the creation of ENEL, and in December 1963 the first government with Socialist ministers took office under Moro, with Nenni as deputy prime minister.

The center-left era brought major reforms, including a unified compulsory middle school in 1962, but many ambitions concerning regional government, planning, and urban reform stalled. In 1964 reports of preparations by military and police officers for emergency action during a government crisis, later known as the "Piano Solo" affair, raised alarm, and historians continue to debate how far it was a genuine coup threat. The Socialist and Social Democratic parties merged briefly between 1966 and 1969, but the merger failed. The presidency provided an institutional anchor: Giovanni Gronchi served from 1955 to 1962, and Antonio Segni and then the Social Democrat Giuseppe Saragat held the office in the 1960s.

Italian politics is sometimes summarized as a story of instability and corruption, and these themes were real. Patronage networks, factional bargaining, and the use of public bodies for party purposes were widespread. But the narrative needs balance. Republican institutions endured, the Constitutional Court became an important protector of rights after 1956, the Communist administered regions were often regarded as effectively governed, the Bank of Italy and other technical institutions maintained policy continuity, and the economy grew rapidly. The sources of Italian political fragility included the exclusion of the largest opposition party from national government, the fragmentation of the center, and the difficulty of reform in a system that required constant bargaining.

Section 127.12

The Economic Miracle, Regional Divisions, and Changing Italian Society

The expansion that Chapter 126 describes as the Italian economic miracle, a period of rapid industrial growth especially between the mid 1950s and the early 1960s, altered society profoundly. Annual growth rates of roughly five to six percent in the late 1950s and early 1960s accompanied the expansion of firms such as Fiat, Olivetti, and state enterprises, and ordinary households bought refrigerators, scooters, and small cars. Television broadcasting began in 1954, and the Autostrada del Sole, linking Milan and Naples, was opened in 1964. The agricultural labor force shrank sharply. Millions of Italians left rural areas, many of them moving from the south to the industrial triangle of Milan, Turin, and Genoa, while others emigrated to Switzerland, West Germany, Belgium, and elsewhere. Exact totals are uncertain and depend on the definition of migration used, but the scale of movement was one of the largest internal migrations in postwar Europe.

Southern migrants met housing shortages, overcrowded neighborhoods, discrimination, and cultural prejudice, and they also became the core of a new industrial working class. Their arrival affected politics. The Communist Party and the Catholic Church both sought to organize them, and employers and unions negotiated over wages and conditions. The south itself remained poorer than the north, despite the Fund for the South and agrarian reform measures begun in 1950. Public investment brought roads, power, and some industrial plants, but it did not close the gap, and in many areas migration drained population. Regional inequality was therefore a persistent structural feature of Italian society, as well as a political issue for parties that depended on southern votes.

Education and religion were also changing. The unified middle school of 1962 extended compulsory education to age fourteen, university enrollments grew, and in 1969 access to universities was widened. The Catholic Church remained influential in family, school, and welfare institutions, but the Second Vatican Council of 1962 to 1965 produced debates within Catholicism about the Church's role. Family law and gender relations were contested. The Merlin Law of 1958 closed licensed brothels, Constitutional Court decisions in 1968 and 1969 struck down the unequal criminal treatment of wives accused of adultery, and a law introducing divorce was passed in December 1970 over Catholic opposition, although a challenge by referendum would not be decided until 1974. The legal subordination of women in marriage was not fully reformed until the family law of 1975.

Labor relations were tense. The union confederations competed and cooperated, strikes grew in the late 1960s, and in 1970 the Workers' Statute gave employees new rights in the workplace. The detailed history of the protest cycle that began in 1968 and 1969 is reserved for Chapter 130. For this chapter, the central point is that rapid economic change altered social structures without producing a unified national political outlook. Northern industrial workers, southern peasants, Catholic families, Communist municipal subcultures, and a growing class of salaried employees lived in different worlds, and Italian political parties continued to organize around these differences rather than dissolve them.

Division III outcome. The Italian Republic functioned as a democracy with strong parties, competing mass political cultures, recurrent coalition change, and uneven social transformation. Its stability lay less in cabinets than in the persistence of constitutional institutions and party organizations, and its limits lay in regional inequality, the exclusion of the largest opposition party from government, and the unfinished implementation of the Constitution.

Division IV West Germany: Constitutional Democracy, Adenauer, and Political Renewal Sections 127.13 to 127.16 · Principal period: 1949–1973
Section 127.13

The Basic Law and the Constitutional Foundations of West German Democracy

West Germany's constitution was written under conditions of occupation and division. Chapter 124 examined the occupation zones and the process by which the western powers moved toward a separate western German state. In July 1948 the military governors of the three western zones presented the minister presidents of the German states, the Länder, with documents authorizing them to convene a constituent body. The German leaders were reluctant to produce a definitive constitution for only part of the country, and the result was a Parliamentary Council of 65 members chosen by the Land parliaments, which met in Bonn beginning on 1 September 1948 under the presidency of Konrad Adenauer. The CDU/CSU and the SPD had 27 members each, the liberal FDP had five, and several smaller parties had the rest. The text was approved by the Council on 8 May 1949, ratified by the Land parliaments, with the exception of Bavaria, which declined but accepted that the law was binding, and promulgated on 23 May 1949. It was called a Basic Law rather than a constitution to emphasize its provisional character pending German unity.

The Basic Law began not with institutions but with rights. Its first article declared human dignity inviolable, and the opening articles established fundamental rights binding on legislation, administration, and the courts. The state was defined as a democratic and social federal state, and the so called eternity clause protected the federal and democratic principles and the dignity provisions from amendment. The Federal Republic had a lower house, the Bundestag, elected by a system that combined constituency seats with proportional representation, and a Bundesrat representing the governments of the Länder, which had a real role in legislation affecting them. A Federal Constitutional Court, established by law in 1951 and based in Karlsruhe, could review legislation and decide disputes about rights and powers. The Federal President was elected indirectly and had primarily ceremonial functions, and the Chancellor, elected by the Bundestag, held the central executive authority, so that the system is sometimes called a chancellor democracy.

Historical Vocabulary
Constructive Vote of No Confidence

The constructive vote of no confidence, set out in Article 67 of the Basic Law, allows the Bundestag to remove a chancellor only by electing a successor by a majority of its members. It differs from the ordinary no confidence vote used in Britain, France, or Italy, in which a government can be defeated without any agreement on an alternative. It matters because it was designed to prevent the repeated government collapses and the reliance on emergency presidential rule that had marked the last years of the Weimar Republic. It should not be taken to guarantee stability in itself, since its effect depended on the party system and electoral law as well.

The framers drew political lessons from the failure of the Weimar Republic and the Nazi dictatorship, though the extent to which Weimar's institutions explain Hitler's rise is itself a matter of historical debate. They sought to avoid the emergency powers that Article 48 of the Weimar constitution had given the President, to limit the fragmentation of parties, to restrict the use of plebiscites, to make constitutional amendment more difficult, and to protect basic rights against parliamentary majorities. The concept of a democracy able to defend itself, sometimes called militant democracy, produced provisions allowing the Constitutional Court to ban parties that sought to undermine the free democratic order. A five percent threshold for parliamentary representation, introduced nationally for the 1953 election, aimed to limit small parties. The Basic Law was not only a reaction to Weimar, however. It also drew on German federal traditions, the democratic constitution of 1919, state constitutions adopted after 1945, and the preferences of the Western Allies, who favored a federal structure.

The Federal Republic's relationship to Germany as a whole was ambiguous. Its government claimed to be the only legitimate German government and, after 1955, refused diplomatic relations with states that recognized the German Democratic Republic, which had been founded in October 1949. West Berlin had a special status under Allied supervision. The Basic Law provided for the accession of other parts of Germany and stated that it would cease to apply on the adoption of a constitution freely chosen by the German people. Distinguishing the Federal Republic from Germany as a whole is therefore fundamental, since the new state governed only the three western occupation zones, with West Berlin in a special position, and the German question remained open.

Section 127.14

Konrad Adenauer, Christian Democracy, and West German Political Consolidation

The first federal election, held on 14 August 1949, produced a narrow result. The CDU/CSU received about 31 percent of the vote, the SPD about 29 percent, the FDP about 12 percent, and the Communist Party about 6 percent, with the remainder going to smaller parties. Konrad Adenauer, who had been mayor of Cologne before 1933 and was then 73 years old, was elected Chancellor by the Bundestag on 15 September with a majority of one vote, his own. He formed a center right coalition of the CDU/CSU, the FDP, and the German Party. The Christian Democratic Union, formed in 1945 in several regions and nationally organized in 1950, was an interconfessional party of Catholics and Protestants, designed to overcome the confessional division that had weakened Catholic politics before 1933, and it was allied in Bavaria with the more conservative Christian Social Union.

Adenauer governed until 1963 with considerable authority, and his style of leadership made the chancellorship the center of the political system. He pursued a policy of integration with the West. West Germany signed the treaty establishing the European Coal and Steel Community on 18 April 1951, and the treaty entered into force on 23 July 1952. It concluded the Luxembourg Agreement with Israel and Jewish organizations in 1952 on compensation, and, after the failure of the European Defence Community, entered NATO in May 1955 as the occupation regime ended and the Federal Republic regained sovereignty. A West German army, the Bundeswehr, was created in 1955 and 1956. The Social Democrats, led first by Kurt Schumacher, who died in 1952, and then by Erich Ollenhauer, opposed rearmament and the priority given to Western ties, arguing that they would make reunification less likely. The opposition's role was real but in these years it was an opposition without prospects of office.

The relationship between economic policy and political legitimacy was also central. The currency reform of June 1948 and the policies of Ludwig Erhard, economics minister from 1949, associated the Christian Democrats with a social market economy that combined market competition with social provisions. The CDU's early program of 1947, the Ahlen Program, had contained anti capitalist elements, but the Düsseldorf Principles of 1949 adopted the market oriented policy. The government also enacted measures that extended social protection, including the equalization of burdens law of 1952 that redistributed assets to help refugees and war victims, a coal and steel codetermination law of 1951 giving workers' representatives seats on supervisory boards, and the pension reform of 1957. The economic expansion itself is described in Chapter 126, but its political significance was that economic growth contributed to the acceptance of the new republic. In 1957 the CDU/CSU won over 50 percent of the vote, the only time any party has done so in a free federal election.

Political consolidation was also achieved by restricting some parties. The Federal Constitutional Court banned a neo Nazi party, the Socialist Reich Party, in 1952 and the Communist Party in 1956. Smaller parties declined, partly because of the threshold and partly because of absorption by the CDU/CSU, and the integration of refugees and expellees from eastern territories and other European states, approximately 7.9 to 8 million of whom were in the Federal Republic by 1950, contributed to stabilization when they found places in the economy and the parties. An additional population of approximately 3.6 to 4 million expellees had been absorbed in the Soviet occupation zone and later German Democratic Republic. Totals near twelve million refer to the two German states together, rather than to West Germany alone, and should not be confused with subsequent migration from East to West Germany. The same period saw the reintegration of many former officials of the Nazi state into administration and the judiciary, a matter returned to in Section 127.19. Adenauer's authority eventually weakened. In the election of 1961 the CDU/CSU lost its majority, and the Spiegel affair of 1962, when the government acted against a magazine accused of publishing military secrets, produced a political crisis and the resignation of Defense Minister Franz Josef Strauss. Adenauer, having promised to leave, retired on 15 October 1963 after signing the Élysée Treaty with de Gaulle earlier that year.

Section 127.15

From Adenauer to the Grand Coalition: West German Politics in Transition

The central change in the party system of the 1950s and 1960s was the transformation of the Social Democratic Party. After three successive federal election defeats, the SPD adopted a new program at a conference at Bad Godesberg in November 1959. It abandoned the claim to be a class party of the working class committed to Marxist theory, accepted the market economy while supporting planning where necessary, recognized national defense, and offered a more open relationship to the churches, in order to become a party of the whole people. In 1960 Herbert Wehner, an influential Social Democrat, announced the party's acceptance of Western integration and NATO in the Bundestag. Willy Brandt, the Governing Mayor of West Berlin since 1957, became the party's candidate for chancellor in 1961 and 1965. The party's share of the vote grew from about 29 percent in 1949 to about 39 percent in 1965.

Ludwig Erhard succeeded Adenauer in October 1963. As the architect of the economic recovery, he won the 1965 election for the CDU/CSU with about 47.6 percent, but his leadership of the party and coalition was weaker than Adenauer's. A recession in 1966 and 1967, modest in comparison with later economic crises but a shock after years of growth, together with disputes over taxes and the budget, led the FDP to leave the coalition in October 1966. Right wing extremism also appeared. The National Democratic Party (NPD) entered several Land parliaments in 1966 to 1968, which alarmed many observers, though it did not clear the five percent threshold in the federal election of 1969. Erhard resigned in November 1966, and on 1 December the CDU/CSU and SPD formed a grand coalition under Kurt Georg Kiesinger, a Christian Democrat, with Brandt as Vice Chancellor and Foreign Minister. Kiesinger's membership in the Nazi Party before 1945 was a source of continuing controversy.

The grand coalition aimed to restore confidence and economic stability. The Social Democrat Karl Schiller, as economics minister, implemented a Keynesian program of stabilization and growth, and the government's political significance lay in giving the SPD experience of government. It also made possible the passage of constitutional amendments, including the emergency legislation that was adopted on 30 May 1968. Emergency laws had been debated for years, and the Allies still retained certain reserve powers. Critics, including trade unions, intellectuals, and students, feared that the laws would allow restrictions on rights and recalled the Weimar emergency provisions, while supporters argued that the Federal Republic needed its own legal framework for crises so that Allied rights could be replaced.

The large governing coalition left only a small parliamentary opposition, the FDP, and this helped to give rise to the extraparliamentary opposition, known by its German initials APO, which brought together students and others who protested against the emergency laws, the Vietnam War, and the failure to confront the Nazi past. The shooting of the student Benno Ohnesorg by a police officer in Berlin on 2 June 1967 and the attempted assassination of the student leader Rudi Dutschke on 11 April 1968 intensified tensions. The wider student movement and the culture of 1968 are described in Chapter 130. The relationship between democratic stability and dissent was complex. The Federal Republic had stable institutions, but a significant section of a younger generation judged them insufficiently democratic, and the debates of the period pushed the political system toward reform. In March 1969, the SPD's Gustav Heinemann was elected Federal President with the support of the FDP, which was regarded as a signal of a possible change of government.

Section 127.16

Willy Brandt, Democratic Reform, and the Changing Meaning of German Identity

The federal election of 28 September 1969 gave the CDU/CSU about 46 percent of the vote and the SPD about 43 percent, but the Social Democrats and the FDP, which received about six percent, together held a narrow majority of seats. They formed a coalition, and on 21 October 1969 Willy Brandt was elected Chancellor with 251 votes, two more than the required majority. It was the first time since 1930 that a Social Democrat led a German government. Brandt, who had left Germany in 1933, worked in exile in Norway and Sweden, and returned to Germany after the war, symbolized both opposition to Nazism and generational change. His opponents sometimes used his exile and his birth name against him, which shows how unresolved the issues of responsibility and loyalty remained.

In his government declaration of 28 October 1969, Brandt promised reforms across domestic and foreign policy. He declared that the government wished to dare more democracy, a phrase that became a slogan of the era, and argued that democracy should extend to schools, workplaces, and public institutions. The government lowered the voting age to 18 in 1970, expanded university places and introduced a student grant program in 1971, revised the criminal code, and extended codetermination debates. The coalition's majority was narrow, and defections in 1970 to 1972 left it close to losing its majority. In April 1972, the opposition leader Rainer Barzel attempted a constructive vote of no confidence, the first serious test of Article 67, and failed by two votes. The episode showed that the constitutional mechanism could manage a real parliamentary crisis, and the government then won new elections in November 1972.

Division V Comparative Western European Democracy: Institutions, Reform, and Identity Sections 127.17 to 127.20 · Comparative period: 1945–1973
Section 127.17

Four Democracies, Four Constitutional Settlements

The four national histories can now be compared. The most visible difference is in the allocation of executive power. In Britain, a hereditary monarch is head of state with ceremonial functions, and the prime minister, who leads the party commanding a majority in the House of Commons, heads the government. Parliament is sovereign, there is no written constitution in a single document and no court able to strike down statutes, and the House of Commons is elected by single member constituencies on a plurality basis. In France, the Fourth Republic placed power in the National Assembly, elected by proportional representation, with a ceremonial president and a prime minister dependent on investiture, while the Fifth Republic created a dual executive with a strong president and a government accountable to the Assembly, elected by a two round system in single member districts. In Italy, a parliamentary republic combined two equally powerful chambers elected by proportional representation with a president who acted as guarantor of the Constitution, and the Constitutional Court and regional governments were added later. In West Germany, a federal parliamentary republic combined a powerful chancellor, a Bundesrat representing the Länder, a Constitutional Court, personalized proportional representation, and the constructive vote of no confidence.

Electoral rules shaped party systems. A long standing argument associated with the French political scientist Maurice Duverger holds that plurality voting in single member constituencies tends to produce two main parties, while proportional systems favor multiparty competition. Postwar Britain broadly fits the first pattern, although the 1951 result, in which the party with fewer votes won more seats, showed the system's distortions. Italy fits the second, with durable coalitions and the continuing exclusion of the extremes. West Germany's combination of proportional representation and a five percent threshold produced a smaller number of parties, and by the 1960s three were represented in the Bundestag. France moved between systems, and the change from proportional representation to single member districts in 1958 contributed to the Gaullist and later left right bipolarity.

Stability and accountability should not be equated. Britain and West Germany had governments that lasted for years, but Italy, with frequent cabinet changes, was not without governmental continuity, because the same party dominated successive coalitions. The Fifth Republic had stable governments, but its strong presidency was criticized as insufficiently accountable. Constitutional courts also differed: West Germany's was powerful from the 1950s, Italy's began late, France's Constitutional Council developed its role gradually, and Britain had none. These differences did not make some systems better versions of others. They reflected different histories of state formation, legitimacy, and fear: Germany feared its own past, France feared both weak governments and military intervention, Italy feared political exclusion and polarization, and Britain relied on long established practice.

Historical Debate

Constitutional Structure or Political Circumstance? Explaining Government Stability

The question: Why were some postwar governments more stable than others: because of constitutional design, or because of the circumstances in which the constitutions operated?

Interpretation A: Institutions mattered decisively. Scholars who emphasize institutions point to the Fifth Republic's executive powers, the German constructive vote of no confidence and electoral threshold, the British plurality system, and the Italian and Fourth Republic proportional systems. On this view, the rules governing elections, investiture, and dissolution shaped party behavior and the durability of governments.
Interpretation B: Circumstances mattered more. Others argue that stability reflected context: the Algerian war that overwhelmed the Fourth Republic, the exclusion of the Italian Communists, which made coalitions difficult, the economic prosperity that sustained West German legitimacy, and the dominance of a single party in Italy and in Germany for long periods. In this view, similar institutions could produce different results in different conditions.

Evidence: Both views draw on cabinet duration, election results, and the comparison of the Fourth and Fifth Republics, as well as the experiences of Weimar and the Federal Republic. Historians differ on whether the change in French institutions in 1958 caused or only reflected a change in circumstances after the end of the Algerian war.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether constitutions are seen as engines of political outcomes or as products of political conditions, and therefore how the achievements of the postwar democracies should be explained.

Section 127.18

Political Parties, Social Reform, and the Management of Democratic Conflict

Postwar party systems rested on a small number of political traditions that appeared in each country in different forms. Socialist and social democratic parties included the British Labour Party, the French SFIO, the Italian Socialists, and the German SPD, and they differed in their relationship to Marxism, to trade unions, and to communist rivals. Christian democracy was a major force in France through the MRP, and in Italy and Germany through the DC and the CDU/CSU, drawing on Catholic social teaching, the experience of persecution and resistance, and a determination to build interclass parties. Liberal parties included the German FDP, the Italian and French Radicals and liberals, and the small British Liberal Party. Conservatism took the form of the British Conservatives, who also drew on a strong organizational base, and the French and Italian right was divided among Gaullists, traditional conservatives, and the neofascist MSI. Communist parties were large in France and Italy, marginal in Britain, and banned in West Germany after 1956. Gaullism, a movement based on a charismatic leader and the idea of national unity above party, was specific to France.

Parties were embedded in broader social organizations. The British Labour Party was founded by trade unions, which retained influence through affiliation, while in France the largest union confederation, the CGT, was close to the Communist Party. Italian unions were divided among Communist, Catholic, and secular federations, and in West Germany the German Trade Union Confederation (DGB) was a unified organization formally independent of the parties, with codetermination in coal and steel after 1951. Churches mattered in different ways. The Catholic Church was central to Christian Democracy in Italy, an important background in West Germany, and part of the French MRP's origins, but in France the republican tradition of secularism limited the formal role of religion. Employers' associations and, in Britain, the Confederation of British Industry, founded in 1965, represented business interests in negotiation with governments and unions.

Welfare policies attracted support from different traditions for different reasons. Labour saw social services as a step toward a more equal society. Christian democrats emphasized family, community, and Catholic social teaching, and supported insurance based systems that preserved occupational and family structures. Conservatives accepted welfare institutions that were popular with voters and compatible with a mixed economy. Liberal parties generally supported a safety net while favoring market mechanisms, and the Gaullists linked social policy to national strength. The systems themselves differed. Britain's, following Beveridge, emphasized universal flat rate benefits and a tax financed health service. West Germany, France, and Italy relied more on contributory insurance organized by occupation, and France gave particular emphasis to family allowances as part of a policy to encourage population growth. These variations show that there was no single welfare model, and that cross party support did not mean agreement on aims.

Social reform did not end conflict. In France, the strikes of 1947 and 1953 and the general strike of 1968, in Britain, the labor disputes of the 1950s and 1960s, and in Italy, the clashes of the late 1960s showed that class and ideological divisions persisted. Governments sought to manage them through collective bargaining, incomes policies, codetermination, planning bodies, and the integration of unions into political bargaining. The overall effect was to channel conflict into institutions, though the results varied: West Germany had comparatively few strikes, while France and Italy experienced recurrent mass mobilizations. Postwar prosperity, shown in Chapter 126, made accommodation easier, but it did not remove disputes over who benefited from growth.

Section 127.19

National Identity, War Memory, and the Limits of Democratic Inclusion

After 1945 each country constructed a narrative of the war that supported its political order. In Britain, the dominant memory was of a people's war in which the nation had stood together, a memory associated with Dunkirk, the Blitz, and Churchill's leadership. Because Britain had not been occupied, there was no domestic reckoning with collaboration, though the memory of a common national effort supported the case for postwar social reform. The imperial dimension of national identity was weakening, and chapters 128 and 133 examine the consequences.

In France, the resistance myth was central. De Gaulle's government and many politicians presented the nation as having resisted collectively and portrayed Vichy as a temporary aberration. This narrative served national reconciliation, but it concealed the extent of cooperation by French officials, including in the persecution and deportation of Jews, and it dominated until the 1970s. Films and historical works of the early 1970s, such as Marcel Ophuls's documentary The Sorrow and the Pity and Robert Paxton's study of Vichy, challenged it, and historians later spoke of a Vichy syndrome. In Italy, antifascism was a founding idea of the republic, and all the major parties claimed the Resistance, but they interpreted it differently, with the Communists emphasizing popular insurrection and the Christian Democrats emphasizing national reconciliation. A self image of Italians as basically decent people, and the limited prosecution of fascist officials and of Italian war crimes abroad, allowed continuities to persist. In West Germany, the early postwar years saw a stress on German victimhood, including the expellees, the bombing war, and prisoners of war, as well as a focus on the idea of a new beginning. Denazification was largely ended in the early 1950s, laws in 1951 reintegrated many civil servants dismissed after 1945, and former Nazi officials served in administration, the judiciary, and politics. At the same time, the Federal Republic accepted compensation obligations, including the Luxembourg Agreement of 1952, and from the 1960s trials and public debate forced a deeper confrontation.

Democratic inclusion remained limited. Women had gained voting rights in France and Italy only in 1944 to 1946, and in all four countries legal and social inequality persisted. West Germany's Basic Law declared equal rights for men and women, but family law was brought into line only with legislation that took effect in 1958, and in France married women could work and manage property without the husband's consent only after a reform of 1965. Italian family law kept unequal rules until the 1970s, and in Britain the Equal Pay Act was passed in 1970. Migrants and minorities faced exclusion. Algerians in France, who held a restricted form of French status, experienced discrimination, and on 17 October 1961 a demonstration in Paris against a curfew was violently repressed, with deaths whose number historians estimate to have been at least dozens, though early official figures were far lower. Southern Italians in northern cities, Commonwealth migrants in Britain, and foreign workers recruited by West Germany from 1955, as well as expellees from the east in Germany, experienced different degrees of inclusion, and their legal and social status is examined further in Chapter 128. Roma and Sinti survivors of Nazi persecution encountered hostility in postwar Germany, and a federal court ruling of 1956 was used to deny many compensation claims before being revised in 1963. Homosexual acts between men remained criminal in West Germany, with reforms in 1969, and in Britain until the 1967 Act for England and Wales. Political constituencies were also excluded: the Communist Party was banned in West Germany, effectively kept out of government in France and Italy, and the Algerian nationalists were excluded from legal politics. National reconciliation, inclusion, and historical accountability were not completed by the early 1970s.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in Postwar Democracies

For many families, politics was experienced through housing, food, work, and health. In Britain, rationing of food and some goods continued into the early 1950s, and new council housing and new towns changed living conditions for many. In France, housing shortages persisted into the 1950s and large estates were built in the following decade. In Italy, migrants to northern cities lived in overcrowded rooms, while in rural areas the daily rhythms of agriculture and the Church remained powerful. West German cities rebuilt from rubble, and millions of expellees found housing in temporary and then permanent accommodation.

Consumer goods slowly changed domestic life: the radio, the refrigerator, the scooter, the television, and the small car became common during the 1950s and 1960s, though at different times in each country. Schools expanded and the school leaving age rose. Religious practice remained important, especially in Italy and in Catholic regions of Germany, but its role differed by region and generation. Women managed households while a growing share did paid work, often in part time or lower paid employment. Evidence for such everyday experience comes from surveys, oral histories, diaries, films, advertisements, and household budgets, and it should be used cautiously because it often reflects the viewpoint of those who recorded it.

Section 127.20

Democracy and Social Transformation in Postwar Western Europe: Historical Synthesis

By 1973 democracy in the four countries was more durable than it had been in the 1930s. Several factors contributed. Economic growth reduced social tension and provided resources for welfare policy. Constitutions and courts protected rights and procedures, though differently in each country. Party systems provided channels for competing interests, including parties of the Left and of Christian democracy that had previously been hostile to parliamentary politics or divided from each other. The Cold War provided security guarantees and a common external framework, but it also limited the choices of the Left and constrained Communist parties. International institutions, from NATO to the European Communities, reinforced economic and strategic ties, and Chapter 133 examines the latter. Finally, the experience of dictatorship and war had discredited authoritarian alternatives for most voters, at least for the time being.

Yet the same period shows that stability had many forms. Britain's continuity depended on well established institutions and on a party competition that accepted a mixed economy. France achieved stability through a change of constitution after a crisis in which democracy was threatened. Italy combined institutional durability with frequent cabinet change and the long exclusion of a major opposition party. West Germany built stability on constitutional safeguards, economic growth, a western orientation, and the gradual transformation of its party system. These differences should not be treated as defects measured against a single model. Each system resulted from specific historical conditions and had its own strengths and tensions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Democracy before and after 1945

What Changed

  • The state became responsible for health, social insurance, and employment to a degree not seen before 1939
  • Fascism and Nazism were discredited as political options in the four countries, and Italy and West Germany adopted new constitutions
  • Women in France and Italy gained national voting rights, and mass party politics expanded
  • Christian democratic and social democratic parties became parties of government
  • Britain's imperial role contracted, and France's changed after the Algerian war

What Remained Continuous

  • Britain's unwritten constitution, monarchy, and Parliament continued without rupture
  • Administrative and judicial personnel, and in Italy many state institutions, persisted across regime changes
  • Regional, religious, and class divisions continued to shape voting
  • Inequalities of gender, region, and citizenship persisted despite formal democracy

Who experienced change most directly? Voters in Italy and West Germany, who had lived under dictatorship, and women in France and Italy, who gained the vote. Who experienced continuity? British voters, civil servants, and many officials in the continental states. Why the pattern matters: It shows that democratic reconstruction combined breaks with continuities, and that the extent of each varied among and within the countries.

The unresolved questions were considerable. Empire had not been fully reckoned with. Britain was withdrawing from its empire, France had lost Algeria, and the societies that had imperial histories would have to decide who belonged within the nation. Migration from former colonies and from southern Europe was changing the population in ways that democratic institutions had not anticipated, and citizenship rules were contested. Economic conditions were also about to change in the early 1970s, and the confidence of the previous decades would be tested. Chapter 128 takes up empire, immigration, and citizenship directly, and Chapter 130 examines the movements that challenged the assumptions of postwar democracy. The democratic achievement was substantial, but it was neither uniform nor complete.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has compared the political reconstruction of Britain, France, Italy, and West Germany between 1945 and 1973. Its central argument is that western European democracy after the war was not one model reproduced in four countries. Britain adapted an old parliamentary system, France replaced one parliamentary republic and then restructured executive authority, Italy built a republic after fascism and monarchy, and West Germany created a federal constitutional order after Nazism.

In Britain, the Labour victory of July 1945 reflected wartime expectations of social security, memories of interwar unemployment, and the credibility Labour had gained in the coalition government. The Attlee governments created the National Health Service, a national insurance system, and a program of nationalization through ordinary parliamentary procedure. Conservative governments from 1951 accepted most of these institutions, though the term postwar consensus conceals real disagreements. The 1960s brought Wilson's modernization program, social legislation, a reorientation toward Europe, and challenges to the unity of the United Kingdom, above all in Northern Ireland.

In France, the Provisional Government and the constituent assemblies rebuilt the Republic, gave women the vote, and adopted a constitution in October 1946 that concentrated power in the National Assembly. The Fourth Republic combined political instability with substantial policy achievement, but it could not resolve the conflicts of decolonization. The crisis of 1958 brought de Gaulle to power and produced a constitution that strengthened the president and the government. The direct election of the president, introduced in 1962, and the survival of the regime after de Gaulle's resignation in 1969 showed that the Fifth Republic had become an institutional system.

In Italy, the referendum of 2 June 1946 abolished the monarchy, and the Constitution that entered into force on 1 January 1948 reflected a compromise among Catholic, Socialist, Communist, and liberal traditions. The election of 18 April 1948 established Christian Democratic predominance and western alignment, while the Communist Party remained a large opposition and social movement. Governments changed frequently, but the party system and republican institutions endured, and a center left coalition formed in the early 1960s. Economic expansion and migration transformed Italian society without erasing regional inequality.

In West Germany, the Basic Law of May 1949 placed fundamental rights first, created a federal system, a powerful chancellor, a Constitutional Court, and a constructive vote of no confidence, and responded to the perceived failures of Weimar. Adenauer's long chancellorship consolidated a western oriented democracy under a Christian democratic party, while the SPD, after Bad Godesberg, became a party of government in the grand coalition and then under Brandt. The 1960s brought dissent, demands for democratization, and public confrontation with the Nazi past, and Brandt's government introduced Ostpolitik.

The comparison shows that constitutional rules affected party systems and government durability, but that circumstances, including the Algerian war, the exclusion of the Italian Communists, and economic prosperity, mattered as well. Parties from different traditions supported welfare policies for different reasons, and the systems themselves varied between universal and insurance based models. Social reform channeled but did not end class and ideological conflict.

National identity and war memory shaped legitimacy. Britain remembered a people's war, France a united resistance, Italy a founding antifascism, and West Germany first its own victimhood and then, gradually, its responsibility. These narratives were partial and contested, and in each country the reintegration of officials and the treatment of collaborators or perpetrators raised unresolved questions.

Democratic inclusion was incomplete. Women faced legal and social inequality, migrants and colonial subjects encountered discrimination, minorities such as Roma and Sinti were poorly served, and political constituencies such as Communists were excluded or banned. The stability of the early 1970s was real but historically contingent, and it left open the questions of empire, migration, and citizenship that Chapter 128 addresses.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how democracy became durable in the part of Europe that had produced fascism and war within living memory. Its answer is not a single formula. Institutions, party traditions, economic growth, Cold War alignment, and memory all contributed, and their combinations differed from country to country.

The chapter also shows that democratic stability was historically contingent. The Fourth Republic fell, Italy's republic was shaped by the exclusion of its largest opposition party, West Germany's institutions were designed against a particular memory of failure, and British continuity concealed profound change in the responsibilities of government. None of these outcomes was predetermined.

These developments shaped later history in several directions. The Fifth Republic's executive framework, the German constitutional order, the Italian party system, and the British welfare institutions all became reference points for later debates, and the unresolved questions of empire, migration, and citizenship carried directly into Chapter 128. The experience of Christian democratic and social democratic cooperation also helped prepare the ground for the integration examined in Chapter 133.

For Volume VI, the chapter establishes that postwar Europe was not simply divided by the Cold War but internally varied. Western European democracies shared alignment and prosperity while remaining different in their institutions and identities, a diversity that later European integration would have to accommodate rather than erase.
