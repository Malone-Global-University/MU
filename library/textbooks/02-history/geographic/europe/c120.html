Chapter Opening

Between 1939 and 1945, Germany and its partners ruled, in widely differing ways, a large part of the European continent. The history of that rule is not a single story of domination and defiance. It is a comparative history of states without sovereignty, officials who stayed at their desks, movements that chose sides, families that tried to eat, and the underground institutions that claimed to be the real nation.

On 25 February 1941, workers in Amsterdam stopped work. Tram crews left their vehicles, dockworkers and factory hands walked out, and the stoppage spread within a day or two to neighboring towns. The immediate cause was the arrest, in the preceding days, of hundreds of Jewish men by German police in the city, after street violence involving Dutch Nazis and a growing German campaign against Amsterdam's Jews. The strike was suppressed within about two days, with arrests, a number of deaths, and German pressure on city authorities. Yet it stands out in the record of occupied Europe: a mass public protest, in a country under German civil administration, against the persecution of Jewish fellow citizens. The episode also reveals the limits that shaped the whole history of occupation. The strikers acted in a society whose civil service, police, railways, and factories continued to function under German supervision, and whose ordinary working life was inseparable from the occupier's economy.

This chapter examines how Nazi Germany and its partners governed occupied Europe, how institutions and populations responded, and why occupation produced such different patterns of accommodation, collaboration, resistance, repression, and civilian survival. It is a comparative political, institutional, and social history. Chapters 117, 118, and 119 have already examined the military campaigns that created the occupied zones, from Poland and the Fall of France to the Balkans and the Eastern Front. The present chapter asks a different question: what happened after the armies had passed, when German officials, police, and economic planners confronted the practical problem of controlling societies that were often larger, more complex, and less compliant than German planners had expected.

The central argument is that Nazi occupation was never a uniform administrative system. Policy differed according to racial ideology, economic priorities, military circumstances, geography, and the political arrangements that survived or failed to survive the conquest. In western and northern Europe, German authorities often retained existing civil services and police forces, because doing so was cheaper than replacing them. In occupied Poland and the Soviet territories, they pursued policies of exploitation, displacement, and deliberate destruction on a scale without parallel in the west. Within this framework, local actors made choices. Some embraced the occupiers' ideology, some cooperated for institutional or economic reasons, some adapted in order to survive, and many built clandestine networks, armed movements, or systems of rescue. The outcomes depended on the interaction between German coercive power and the choices, constraints, institutions, and experiences of occupied societies.

The chapter treats these responses with conceptual precision. Collaboration, accommodation, resistance, and rescue are different things, and a single person or institution could act in several of these ways at different moments. A mayor might distribute ration cards, resist a particular order, and comply with another. A police officer might protect a neighbor and arrest a stranger. The chapter explains motives without dissolving responsibility: understanding why someone cooperated does not erase the consequences of what cooperation made possible, and understanding why occupiers justified violence does not make the violence a legitimate response to resistance. Responsibility for crimes against civilians lies with those who ordered and carried them out.

The evidence is correspondingly varied. Historians draw on German occupation directives and administrative files, the records of local ministries and police, trial records from Nuremberg and from postwar national courts, clandestine newspapers and underground orders, intelligence reports sent to London and Moscow, diaries, letters, memoirs, oral histories, and the archives of postwar commissions. Each carries a distinct perspective. German files record what the occupier wished to control and what it feared. Underground publications record what resisters wished others to believe. Postwar testimony was shaped by trial, politics, and memory. The chapter therefore pays close attention to whose voice a source preserves and whose it omits, and it treats later commemorations, which often simplified the wartime record, as a subject of history in their own right.

Within Volume V, this chapter forms a bridge. Chapter 119 examined the war in the east as a military struggle; Chapter 121 will examine the Holocaust and Nazi genocide systematically; Chapter 122 will trace the Allied return and the defeat of Germany; and Chapter 123 will examine displacement, justice, and reconstruction. Chapter 120 contributes the political and social ground on which all of these developments rested. It shows how the occupied societies of Europe were governed, exploited, divided, and defended, and it explains why the end of German rule in 1944 and 1945 left questions of legitimacy, responsibility, and memory that outlasted the fighting.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines the structures and purposes of occupation. The second studies collaboration, accommodation, and political legitimacy. The third surveys underground states, resistance networks, and armed struggle. The fourth turns to repression, civilian survival, and the moral geography of everyday life, including rescue. The fifth follows the uprisings, liberations, and political settlements by which occupation regimes collapsed, and closes with a comparative assessment of what occupation revealed about states, citizens, and violence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among invasion, military occupation, annexation, protectorate rule, satellite government, and formally independent Axis alliance, and explain the legal framework of the 1907 Hague Regulations.
  2. Compare the occupation systems established in western, northern, central, and eastern Europe, identifying the roles of military administrations, civilian commissioners, and SS and police authorities.
  3. Explain how German authorities extracted food, raw materials, industrial output, and labor from occupied Europe, and distinguish among categories of work under occupation.
  4. Analyze collaboration, accommodation, resistance, and rescue as distinct historical categories, and explain the range of motives behind each.
  5. Assess Vichy France as an authoritarian regime with its own objectives and initiatives, including its antisemitic legislation, rather than merely an instrument of German pressure.
  6. Trace the development of the Polish Underground State, the French Resistance, eastern partisan movements, and the Yugoslav, Greek, and Italian resistance, and explain how their political aims differed.
  7. Interpret German policing, hostage taking, collective reprisals, and the Night and Fog Decree as documented instruments of repression, using primary evidence critically.
  8. Describe the social history of occupation, including shortages, the black market, billeting, family separation, and the participation of women, young people, and churches in resistance and rescue.
  9. Evaluate the causes, course, and consequences of the 1944 Warsaw Uprising, distinguishing it from the 1943 Warsaw Ghetto Uprising.
  10. Evaluate competing explanations for variation in collaboration and resistance, and explain how postwar politics shaped national memory of occupation.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did German occupation policy differ so sharply between western Europe and the territories of Poland and the Soviet Union, and what do those differences reveal about Nazi priorities?
  2. What does it mean to occupy a country whose lawful government has been removed or exiled, and how did international law attempt to define the occupier's powers?
  3. How can historians distinguish ideological collaboration from institutional accommodation and from coerced cooperation without excusing documented participation in persecution?
  4. In what ways did Vichy France exercise agency, and why does that question matter for French political memory?
  5. Why did resistance take such different forms in Poland, France, the Soviet borderlands, the Balkans, and Italy?
  6. How did economic exploitation and forced labor change the relationship between occupied populations and occupation authorities?
  7. What can sources produced by occupiers, by underground movements, and by postwar courts each tell historians, and what can they not establish?
  8. How did the liberation of Europe depend on the interplay of external armies, domestic insurrection, and competing claims to political legitimacy?

Historical Context

The occupations of 1939 to 1945 grew out of the collapse of the political order examined in Chapters 113 through 116. The Versailles settlement had created a ring of new and enlarged states in central and eastern Europe, many with significant national minorities, contested borders, and fragile parliamentary systems. By the 1930s, many of these states had turned to authoritarian rule, while Germany under Adolf Hitler pursued treaty revision, rearmament, and territorial expansion. The annexation of Austria in March 1938 and the dismemberment of Czechoslovakia in 1938 and 1939 preceded the invasion of Poland in September 1939. By then the German leadership held an explicit program that combined strategic expansion with racial hierarchy: the conquest of land and resources in the east, the subordination or removal of populations judged inferior, and the persecution of Jews.

Europeans did not enter this war without experience of occupation. During the First World War, Germany had occupied Belgium and parts of northern France and had administered large territories in the east, including the Ober Ost region in the Baltic lands and Poland. Those experiences produced both German administrative precedents and local memories. Belgian and French civilians remembered requisitions, deportations for labor, and executions of hostages, and in 1940 many expected a repetition. Some assumed that a second occupation would resemble the first, which helped shape early decisions by officials and ordinary people about whether to flee, stay, or cooperate. Such expectations were a poor guide to what followed, because Nazi policy differed in purpose and scale from that of the imperial German army of 1914 to 1918.

The legal framework available to contemporaries was the Hague Convention of 1907 and its annexed Regulations, which defined the powers and obligations of a belligerent occupant. The Regulations required the occupant to respect, unless absolutely prevented, the laws in force in the country, to take measures to restore and ensure public order and civil life, to respect private property and family rights, and to limit requisitions and contributions to the needs of the army of occupation. They did not envisage an occupant who aimed to destroy the occupied state, resettle its population, or exploit it as a permanent economic reservoir. German authorities used the Regulations selectively when it suited them and rejected them outright in the east, where they claimed that the Soviet Union stood outside the legal order and that the Polish state had ceased to exist. The four Geneva Conventions of 1949, including the Fourth Convention on the protection of civilians in occupied territory, were partly a response to the wartime experience and did not govern the events of this chapter.

Nazi racial thinking, examined in Chapter 115, structured how different peoples were treated. Germans and, to a degree, peoples classified as Germanic were privileged; Poles, Czechs, and other Slavs were to be subordinated, exploited, or in part displaced; and Jews and Roma were targeted for persecution that, by 1941 and 1942, became mass murder. These categories did not produce a single policy. Wartime shortages, military setbacks, the rivalry of German agencies, and practical necessity repeatedly pushed policy toward compromise, especially where Germany needed local cooperation to keep factories running, trains moving, and farms producing. German rule was therefore at once ideological and improvised, and it rested on a polycratic structure in which the army, the Foreign Office, the Four Year Plan, the Ministry for Armaments, Reich commissioners, and the SS and police competed for authority.

The chapter also depends on a body of evidence shaped by what happened next. After liberation, governments and courts collected testimony, prosecuted collaborators, and commemorated resisters, often with strong political motives. In western Europe, resistance became a foundation of national legitimacy; in eastern Europe, communist governments emphasized partisan struggle and downplayed rival underground movements; in Poland, Soviet and communist authorities persecuted the Home Army's veterans. Students should therefore treat both wartime sources and postwar narratives with care. The history of occupation includes the history of how occupation was remembered, a theme returned to in section 120.20 and in Chapter 123.

The Structures and Purposes of Occupation

The first division establishes the legal vocabulary, the administrative variety, and the economic purposes of German rule. It asks how occupation was organized before it asks how Europeans responded.

Section 120.1

Conquered Europe: Occupation, Sovereignty, and the Nazi Imperial Order

The vocabulary of conquest conceals important distinctions. An invasion is a military operation; a military occupation is a condition in which territory is placed under the actual authority of a hostile army; an annexation is a unilateral claim to incorporate territory into the conqueror's own state; a protectorate is a subordinate arrangement in which a nominally autonomous government continues under external control; and a satellite or allied government is one that remains formally sovereign while aligning its policy, willingly or under pressure, with a stronger power. Between 1939 and 1944, Germany created or relied on all of these arrangements, sometimes within a single country and sometimes in sequence.

The political and legal problem was substantial. Many invaded states lost their governments, either because rulers and ministers fled, as in Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, Poland, and Greece, or because defeat produced a new regime, as in France. Governments in exile, among them those of Queen Wilhelmina, King Haakon VII, the Belgian ministers, the Polish government, and General de Gaulle's Free French, continued to claim legitimate authority and were recognized, to varying degrees, by the Allies. At home, civil servants, mayors, judges, and police officers faced an immediate choice: remain in office to maintain public order and supply, or resign. The occupier wanted functioning administration, and in most of western Europe it kept the existing machinery in place, but cooperation with the occupier on routine tasks gradually became cooperation with its policies.

The contemporary legal framework was the Hague Convention (IV) of 1907 and its annexed Regulations, especially Articles 42 to 56 on military authority over the territory of a hostile state. Article 42 defines territory as occupied when it is actually placed under the authority of a hostile army, and limits occupation to the area where that authority is established and can be exercised. Article 43 requires the occupant to take all measures in its power to restore and ensure public order and safety, while respecting, unless absolutely prevented, the laws in force in the country. Article 46 requires respect for family honor and rights, individual lives, and private property; Article 47 prohibits pillage; and Articles 49, 51, and 52 limit contributions, taxation, and requisitions to the needs of the army of occupation and to what the resources of the country can bear. Article 55 treats the occupant as an administrator and usufructuary of public property, not an owner. These provisions presupposed that occupation was a temporary military condition. They did not confer sovereignty, and in law they did not legitimize annexation.

Article 42 identifies occupation by actual hostile military authority; Article 43 requires the occupant to restore and maintain public order while respecting existing laws unless absolutely prevented. Article 46 protects lives, family rights, religious practice, and private property, and Article 47 prohibits pillage. Article 48 governs existing taxes and administrative expenses; Article 49 permits additional financial contributions only for the occupying army or territorial administration; Article 50 prohibits collective penalties for acts for which a population is not collectively responsible; Article 51 prescribes written orders and receipts for contributions; and Article 52 restricts requisitions in kind and services to the army's needs and local resources. Article 55 treats the occupier as administrator and usufructuary of public property. Occupation was a temporary condition of effective control, not a transfer of sovereignty or authorization for annexation.

Occupation did not automatically confer sovereignty, and the Allies never accepted German annexations. Yet Germany did claim sovereignty over some territory. Western Poland, parts of Slovenia, Alsace and Lorraine, Eupen-Malmedy, and Luxembourg were treated as incorporated into the Reich, in several cases through administrative measures rather than formal treaty. Elsewhere Germany held direct authority while formally preserving a local government, as in the Protectorate, or relied on allied states, such as Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Slovakia, and Croatia, whose governments retained formal independence but varied greatly in autonomy. Italy, Hungary, Bulgaria, and Romania also occupied territory of their own, and the Soviet Union occupied eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and other areas in 1939 to 1941. The Nazi imperial order was thus not a single empire but a hierarchy of control, in which the degree of independence a local authority possessed depended on German interests at a particular moment.

Historical Vocabulary
Occupation, Sovereignty, Protectorate, Annexation

Occupation, in the legal sense of the Hague Regulations, is the condition in which territory is actually placed under the authority of a hostile army. It is a factual condition, not a transfer of title. Sovereignty is the supreme legal authority of a state over its territory and people; an occupier exercises control but, under the 1907 rules, acquires no sovereignty. A protectorate is a subordinate arrangement under which a local government continues to exist while a stronger power controls foreign policy, security, and in practice much more; the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia illustrates how the form preserved Czech offices while German officials held decisive authority. Annexation is the unilateral incorporation of territory into another state. Students should not use the words interchangeably: Germany occupied the Netherlands, annexed western Poland, imposed a protectorate on the Czech lands, and allied with Slovakia, and each arrangement carried different consequences for law, administration, and everyday life.

Section 120.2

Governing Western and Northern Europe: Military Administrations and Civilian Authorities

In occupied France the principal authority was the German military commander in Paris, the Militärbefehlshaber in Frankreich, under whom a military administration supervised French prefects and ministries. The armistice of June 1940 left the French State at Vichy in nominal charge of the whole country, while the northern and Atlantic coastal zone was subject to German occupation and the southern zone, until November 1942, was not occupied. Alsace and Lorraine were de facto annexed to the Reich, and the northern departments of Nord and Pas-de-Calais were attached to the military administration in Brussels. The German Embassy in Paris under Otto Abetz played a political role, and the SS and Security Police, led by Helmut Knochen and, from 1942, by the Higher SS and Police Leader Carl Oberg, steadily expanded their powers. In 1942 an arrangement between Oberg and the Vichy police chief René Bousquet gave French police a measure of operational autonomy in return for cooperation against enemies defined by German authorities, including communists and Jews.

Belgium and the two northern French departments were governed by a military administration under General Alexander von Falkenhausen, who relied on the Belgian secretaries general, the senior civil servants left in place when the ministers departed for exile. King Leopold III, who had surrendered the Belgian army in May 1940, remained in Belgium as a prisoner, while the government of Hubert Pierlot established itself in London. German officials in Belgium favored Flemish nationalists in some respects, while the Wallonia based Rex movement under Léon Degrelle and other groups offered a collaborationist alternative. In July 1944, after the failed plot against Hitler, Falkenhausen was removed and the Belgian military administration was replaced by a civil one under a Nazi Gauleiter, a change that illustrates how political rivalry within the German system affected occupation policy. Luxembourg, by contrast, was administered by a German civil authority under Gustav Simon, who treated it as a territory to be incorporated and Germanized; in August 1942 Luxembourg men were made liable for service in the German armed forces, which provoked a general strike and severe repression.

The Netherlands was placed under a civilian Reich Commissioner, Arthur Seyss-Inquart, who served from May 1940. Dutch government ministers and the Queen had left for London, but the Dutch secretaries general stayed at their posts, adopting a policy of remaining in office to protect Dutch interests. Seyss-Inquart's administration worked through the existing Dutch civil service, police, and municipal governments while an SS and police apparatus under Hanns Albin Rauter grew steadily more powerful. The German authorities encouraged Anton Mussert's National Socialist Movement, the NSB, but never gave it control of government. The Dutch case shows how civil rule could be more thorough than military rule. The Dutch administrative tradition, with its comprehensive population registers, meant that German authorities could identify and locate people more easily than in some other occupied countries, a fact with severe consequences for the Dutch Jewish population, examined in Chapter 121.

Norway and Denmark were occupied on the same day, April 9, 1940, but with strikingly different arrangements. In Norway, the king and government fled and resisted, and after a short period of experimentation Hitler installed a Reich Commissioner, Josef Terboven, in April 1940. Terboven governed through a combination of German officials and Norwegian administrators and, after the failure of attempts to negotiate with the existing political elite in the summer and autumn of 1940, banned all political parties except Vidkun Quisling's Nasjonal Samling. In Denmark, the government and the king remained, the army was largely stood down, and the cabinet accepted German "protection" under protest, retaining its own police, courts, and ministries, while German policy was directed principally by the Foreign Office through a plenipotentiary rather than by the SS. This arrangement served German interests: it provided agricultural exports and industrial goods, kept the occupation force small, and preserved a showcase of cooperative occupation. It was a negotiated arrangement under coercive circumstances, not a voluntary partnership.

Section 120.3

Poland, the Czech Lands, and Eastern Europe: Annexation, Exploitation, and Racial Rule

Poland was divided into several zones. Western regions, including the Warthegau around Poznań, Danzig-West Prussia, East Upper Silesia, and areas attached to East Prussia, were annexed to the Reich and placed under Nazi governors, notably Arthur Greiser and Albert Forster. Their policy was to Germanize the territory: to expel Poles and Jews to the east, settle ethnic Germans from the Baltic states and elsewhere, and classify remaining inhabitants through the German People's List according to ancestry and supposed loyalty. The remainder of German occupied Poland became the General Government, created by Hitler's decree in October 1939 and operating from 26 October, with its capital in Kraków under Governor General Hans Frank. It was not a state but a dependency, intended to supply labor and agricultural produce and to serve as a reservoir for deportees. Eastern Poland had been occupied by the Soviet Union since September 1939, and after June 1941 the district of Galicia was added to the General Government. German authorities targeted Polish elites in a series of operations in 1939 and 1940, including the arrest of the Kraków professors in November 1939 and mass executions of teachers, clergy, officials, and landowners. They closed universities and secondary schools and restricted Polish education to basic levels.

The Czech lands experienced a different system. The Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, headed by Reich Protector Konstantin von Neurath and a Czech government under President Hácha, retained Czech civil servants, police, and courts, while German authorities controlled foreign policy, security, and the economy and integrated the Czech industrial base into the German war effort. Czech universities were closed in November 1939 after student demonstrations, with several student leaders executed and well over a thousand students sent to concentration camps. When Reinhard Heydrich became acting Reich Protector in September 1941, he combined martial law and terror against resistance with improvements in food rations and social benefits for Czech workers, an approach designed to secure industrial cooperation. After Heydrich's assassination in 1942, repression intensified, as examined in section 120.14. The Protectorate shows that German rule could pair a formally preserved administrative apparatus with coercion, and that the apparent autonomy of local officials was bounded by German security authorities.

In occupied Soviet territory, administration was split among the army, which controlled rear areas near the front, and civilian commissariats. The Reich Ministry for the Occupied Eastern Territories under Alfred Rosenberg, created in July 1941, nominally supervised the Reichskommissariat Ostland, covering the Baltic states and much of Belarus under Hinrich Lohse, and the Reichskommissariat Ukraine under Erich Koch. In practice, authority was contested among Rosenberg's ministry, the Four Year Plan and Hermann Göring, the SS and police under Himmler, and the army, and local policy varied accordingly. Romania administered a zone between the Dniester and the Bug, called Transnistria. German planners anticipated that the occupation would produce a large food surplus for Germany at the expense of local populations, and the starvation of millions of Soviet prisoners of war and civilians was an intended or accepted consequence of food policy in several areas, a subject closely related to Chapters 119 and 121.

Nazi racial hierarchies shaped treatment but did not determine every policy. Germans and some "Germanic" groups were privileged; Poles and Czechs were treated as subordinate peoples, though policies differed, with some Czechs considered capable of Germanization while Poles were largely classified as a labor reserve; Ukrainians, Belarusians, and Russians were regarded as inferior, but German policy toward them oscillated between exploitation, limited cultural tolerance, and ruthless coercion; Jews and Roma were targeted for persecution that became mass murder. The practical decisions of German officials were also shaped by local resources, labor needs, partisan activity, and rivalry among agencies. German planners sometimes considered populations that could supply skilled labor or agricultural production differently from those they regarded as surplus, and differentiated treatment of those groups reflected utilitarian calculation within a framework of racial hierarchy.

Section 120.4

Economic Exploitation: Requisitioning, Industry, Agriculture, and Forced Labor

The financial system was a central tool. Occupation costs, nominally to cover the expense of maintaining German forces, were set far above any plausible need. In France, they were fixed at 20 million Reichsmarks per day, at an exchange rate of 20 francs to the mark that favored Germany, which produced a very large annual transfer. German forces also paid for goods in occupation currency and through clearing arrangements that left occupied countries with credit balances Germany did not intend to settle. Local central banks were compelled to finance German purchases, which fueled inflation and depleted national resources. Such practices stretched the Hague limits on contributions and requisitions beyond recognition, although German officials sometimes cited the Regulations in justifying them.

Agriculture and industry were reorganized to serve German needs. Occupied countries were required to deliver quotas of grain, meat, dairy products, and other food, and food policy in the east was explicitly designed to favor German consumers and the army. Denmark, the Netherlands, and France supplied large quantities of agricultural produce, with consequences for domestic supply. Industry in occupied western Europe often produced under German contracts, and after Albert Speer became Reich Minister for Armaments in early 1942 he promoted a system of "protected" factories in France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Italy, in which workers produced for Germany without being deported to the Reich. These arrangements meant that local employers and managers made decisions about whether, and for whom, to produce, and that industrial output became a form of cooperation, even where firms insisted that production was necessary to keep their workers employed and fed.

Labor was a growing preoccupation. As German men were conscripted into the armed forces, the economy turned to foreign workers: volunteers, workers recruited under economic pressure, prisoners of war, and civilians deported by force. In March 1942, Hitler appointed Fritz Sauckel General Plenipotentiary for Labor Deployment, with authority to assign quotas to occupied countries and to oversee recruitment in the east. Sauckel's methods combined propaganda, administrative pressure, and, increasingly, roundups. Workers from the Soviet Union and Poland, classified as "Eastern workers," were subjected to special restrictions, including identifying badges, and treated more harshly than workers from western Europe. In France, Pierre Laval's government introduced the Relève in June 1942, offering the return of prisoners of war in exchange for skilled workers, and in February 1943 the compulsory labor service, the Service du travail obligatoire, made young men liable for deportation to the Reich. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum records more than 7.5 million registered non German workers in the Reich in August 1944, the overwhelming majority being forced laborers, a figure that includes civilians from many countries and, depending on the count, prisoners of war, and that must be understood in terms of date, location, and worker category.

Students should distinguish among categories of labor under occupation. Some workers were paid employees who remained in their home country and worked for local firms, some of which supplied German orders. Others worked under economic coercion, accepting employment because unemployment, rationing, and the threat of conscription left little choice. Compulsory service meant legally imposed obligation, as under the Service du travail obligatoire. Forced deportation meant removal to the Reich by compulsion, often through roundups. Concentration camp labor involved prisoners subject to extreme violence, malnutrition, and a high risk of death, and often performed work for private companies as well as for the SS. These categories overlapped and were not always clearly distinguished by contemporaries, but they implied very different degrees of coercion and different risks, and conflating them obscures both the extent of German compulsion and the experiences of the people concerned.

Cause and Effect

Economic Exploitation and the Growth of Forced Labor

Long-term causes

Germany's dependence on imported resources, the strains of rearmament, Nazi ambitions for continental economic dominance, and the conception of conquered territory as a source of land, food, and labor.

Short-term causes

The mobilization of German men for the armed forces, the failure to win a quick victory in the Soviet Union, rising armaments targets in 1942, and the appointment of Fritz Sauckel in March 1942.

Event or transformation

Recruitment turned from largely voluntary or economically induced schemes into mass compulsion, including roundups, conscription laws such as the French STO, and deportation of civilians to the Reich.

Immediate effects

Millions of foreign workers in the German economy, harsh treatment of Eastern workers, evasion by young men, strikes, and a surge of recruits to underground movements.

Long-term consequences

Social disruption, family separation, postwar legal and compensation disputes, and lasting memory of forced labor as a defining experience of occupation.

Historical complexity

Forced labor cannot be explained by German policy alone. Local administrators, employers, and police shaped its operation, and evasion and resistance shaped its limits.
Division II · §§120.5–120.8 · 1940–1944

Collaboration, Accommodation, and Political Legitimacy

The second division asks why local institutions and political movements participated in, adapted to, or tried to limit German rule, and how contested claims to legitimacy shaped the choices they made.

Section 120.5

Collaboration and Accommodation: Motives, Institutions, and Degrees of Complicity

The word collaboration entered the political language of occupied Europe with a double meaning. In ordinary usage it described working together; in the circumstances of 1940 it came to signify cooperation with the enemy. Pétain used the term in October 1940 to describe a policy of cooperation with Germany, and after liberation the word became a term of condemnation. Historians have therefore sought more precise vocabulary. A useful distinction, introduced by the political scientist Stanley Hoffmann in his study of France, separates collaboration, the cooperation of a state or its institutions with the occupier for reasons of state, from collaborationism, the voluntary support of Nazi aims by individuals and movements for ideological reasons. Other categories describe different positions along a spectrum: institutional accommodation, in which officials kept administration running and complied with German demands; economic participation, in which firms and individuals worked for the occupier; coercion; and acts of cooperation aimed at personal or family survival.

Motives for collaboration varied widely. Some collaborators were convinced fascists, Nazis, or antisemites, and saw German victory as an opportunity to remake their own societies. Others were authoritarian nationalists or conservatives who disliked parliamentary democracy and communism and hoped to use the occupation to implement their own programs; the French National Revolution, discussed in section 120.6, is the clearest example. Some were anticommunists who regarded the Soviet Union as the main enemy and, after June 1941, found common cause with Germany. Others sought administrative continuity, believing that officials who stayed at their posts could protect the population from worse treatment. Still others sought advantage: a business contract, a promotion, a favorable position after expected German victory. Many acted from a mixture of motives that shifted as the war's course changed, and the proportion of calculation to conviction is often difficult to establish from the surviving sources.

Institutions mattered as much as individuals. Local police forces were indispensable to German control, since German police personnel were few. In France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Norway, and Denmark, indigenous police carried out arrests, maintained order, enforced regulations, and in some cases took part in actions against Jews and political opponents. Municipal governments administered ration cards, population registers, and requisitions. Civil servants drafted regulations and maintained records that German authorities used to identify and locate persons. Industrialists and bankers took German contracts, some from compulsion, some from enthusiasm, and some from the belief that production would keep their workforce employed. Journalists, editors, and publishers decided whether and how to publish under censorship, with some producing propaganda and others attempting to preserve limited independence. Political parties made their own decisions about participation, dissolution, or clandestine continuity.

The 1907 Hague Regulations created a particular dilemma. They presupposed that the local administration would continue to function and required the occupant to restore public order, which meant that officials could argue that remaining in office was consistent with the rules of war. Some officials believed, sincerely, that staying in post would shield the population. Others later invoked that argument to excuse conduct that went well beyond ordinary administration. The historian's task is to distinguish between routine administrative continuity, for example issuing ration cards, and participation in measures aimed at persecution, such as compiling lists of Jews or arresting people for deportation. A civil servant who processed a food registration form and one who drew up a list of persons to be arrested stood in different moral and legal positions, although both were serving under the same occupier.

Section 120.6

Vichy France: State Authority, Collaboration, and the Politics of Legitimacy

On 10 July 1940, the National Assembly, meeting at Vichy, voted by 569 to 80, with a small number of abstentions, to grant Pétain authority to draft a new constitution. The following day constitutional acts made Pétain head of the "French State," replacing the Republic, suspending parliament, and concentrating power in his hands. Many legislators did not foresee the permanent end of the Republic, but the vote gave the new regime a form of constitutional legitimacy that its opponents later contested. The regime adopted a slogan of Work, Family, Fatherland in place of the Republic's Liberty, Equality, Fraternity. It proclaimed a "National Revolution": a program of moral and political renewal that attacked the parliamentary republic, communism, the trade unions in their previous form, Freemasonry, and what its leaders regarded as the corrupting effects of liberalism and cosmopolitanism. It promoted corporatist ideas, rural life, family policy, youth organizations, and a favored position for the Catholic Church.

Vichy's antisemitism was an element of the National Revolution and not a mere response to German demand. On 3 October 1940, the regime promulgated the first Statut des Juifs, which defined who was a Jew, barred Jews from public service, the officer corps, teaching, the press, and the cinema, and imposed a wider set of exclusions. A second statute in June 1941 tightened definitions and restrictions and was accompanied by a census of Jews in the unoccupied zone. A Commissariat for Jewish Affairs was created in March 1941, and legislation for the "Aryanization" of Jewish property followed. Another law allowed the internment of foreign Jews, and French authorities ran camps at Gurs, Rivesaltes, and elsewhere, as well as the transit camp at Drancy, which French gendarmes guarded until 1943. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum cites the October 1940 statute as evidence that Vichy exercised agency in persecution. Historians such as Robert Paxton, in a book first published in 1972, argued that the regime pursued its own agenda, and the finding has been developed in later studies. It should be added that Vichy's measures did not at first include deportation to killing centers, but in 1942 French police arrested Jews for deportation, including in the Paris roundup of 16 and 17 July 1942, the Vel d'Hiv roundup, in which French police arrested some 13,000 Jews, among them thousands of children. Laval agreed to include children in the deportations, and the arrangement is documented. Chapter 121 will examine the Holocaust as a whole, and the French case is considered here as evidence of the regime's own role.

Vichy's antisemitism formed part of its own National Revolution, not merely a response to German demands. Its Statut des Juifs of October 3, 1940 defined Jews by ancestry and excluded them from public service and professions; a June 2, 1941 statute expanded restrictions. The Commissariat for Jewish Affairs was established in March 1941, and the regime legislated for the confiscation of Jewish property. French authorities operated internment camps, including Gurs and Rivesaltes; French gendarmes guarded Drancy until 1943. Robert Paxton and later historians have demonstrated Vichy's political initiative in these measures. French police also participated in deportations. In the Vel d'Hiv roundup of July 16–17, 1942, they arrested 13,152 Jews, including more than 4,000 children, according to a widely cited police tally. Laval's government agreed to the inclusion of children in deportations. This section examines French institutional agency; Chapter 121 treats the wider genocide.

The regime also developed instruments of its own. In January 1943 it created the Milice, a paramilitary force headed by Joseph Darnand, to fight what the regime called the enemies of the state: Resistance fighters, communists, and Jews. The Milice cooperated with German security services and became associated with torture and summary killing. Censorship, a controlled press, and propaganda supported the regime's policy. Economic cooperation continued, including supply of goods and labor, and Vichy's own law of February 1943 established the compulsory labor service. As German defeat approached, the regime hardened, and in August and September 1944, Pétain and many officials were moved to Sigmaringen in Germany.

Vichy did not govern without challenge to its legitimacy. On 18 June 1940, General Charles de Gaulle, an obscure junior general, broadcast an appeal from London urging the French to continue the fight. He founded Free France, which gained control of parts of the French Empire, notably Chad and other equatorial colonies in 1940, and created a political and military organization abroad. The Allies initially had contacts with both Free France and Vichy: the United States maintained diplomatic relations with Vichy until 1942. In Britain, the destruction of French naval forces at Mers-el-Kébir in July 1940 deepened Franco-British hostility. Within France, resistance movements emerged, at first independently of de Gaulle (section 120.10). In June 1943, the French Committee of National Liberation was formed in Algiers, and in June 1944 it became the Provisional Government of the French Republic. The French contest for legitimacy was therefore a contest among several claimants, each with a case: Vichy's constitutional continuity and territorial control, Free France's claim to embody the nation in arms, internal resisters' claim to speak for the people, and the French overseas authorities' own positions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

French Institutions before and after the Establishment of Vichy

What Changed

  • The Republic became the French State; parliament was suspended, elections ended, and executive power concentrated in Pétain.
  • Citizenship was redefined; Jews, and in some cases naturalized citizens, lost rights, and Freemasons and communists were persecuted.
  • Independent trade unions and political parties were dissolved or restricted, and censorship and a single approved ideology were imposed.
  • The unoccupied zone's sovereignty narrowed after November 1942 and the Milice created a new coercive instrument in 1943.

What Stayed the Same

  • The prefectoral system, the civil service, the police, the judiciary, and the school system largely remained in place, and most officials stayed at their posts.
  • Local administration, tax collection, and public supply continued, which gave the occupier a functioning bureaucracy and gave ordinary people a measure of continuity.
  • The Catholic Church, the army's officer corps, and large parts of the business elite continued to hold influential positions.

Who experienced change most directly? Jews, foreign refugees, communists, Freemasons, and republican politicians experienced exclusion and persecution most directly, while many rural civil servants and farmers experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: The mixture of institutional continuity and political transformation explains both why Vichy could function and why its legitimacy was so bitterly contested after the war.

Section 120.7

Norway, Denmark, Belgium, and the Netherlands: Contrasting Patterns of Cooperation

In Norway, Vidkun Quisling, leader of the small Nasjonal Samling, announced on the radio on 9 April 1940 that he was assuming power. The Germans, who had not planned to install him, withdrew their support within about a week and appointed an Administrative Council, then in September 1940 Terboven dismissed it and banned all parties but the Nasjonal Samling. In February 1942, Quisling was named minister president, but his authority was limited by Terboven and by German security organs. The party's membership never grew beyond a small minority of the population, and the regime's attempts to Nazify institutions provoked organized opposition. In 1942, Norwegian teachers refused to join a state controlled teachers' organization, and the arrest of more than a thousand teachers failed to break the refusal, after which the regime backed down; clergy of the Church of Norway resigned their state functions, and church leaders including Bishop Eivind Berggrav became symbols of non compliance. These episodes of institutional resistance were as important as armed sabotage, though Norway also produced significant operations, such as the destruction of the heavy water facility at Vemork in February 1943 by Norwegian operatives trained by the Special Operations Executive.

The Netherlands was governed by a civilian administration that relied on Dutch institutions. The policy of the secretaries general was to remain at their posts and limit damage, and this meant Dutch officials implemented German decrees such as the registration of Jews in January 1941. The February Strike of 25 February 1941 arose from German arrests of Jewish men in Amsterdam, and was organized by communists and supported by many ordinary workers. It lasted about two days, and it showed that opposition to anti Jewish persecution was possible, but it also shows its limits: the strike was suppressed, and the Dutch authorities' continued cooperation with German policy persisted afterward. In April and May 1943, a larger wave of strikes followed a German order to intern former Dutch soldiers, and again met severe repression. In September 1944, railway workers struck in support of Allied advances, and German reprisals contributed to the Hunger Winter of 1944 and 1945. The Dutch case shows that a society with a conformist administrative tradition could also produce mass protest, and that the two were not mutually exclusive.

Belgium experienced military rule in a society divided by language and politics. The Belgian secretaries general continued to administer the country, relying on a rationale of limiting German interference, and German authorities exploited Flemish and Walloon divisions by favoring Flemish nationalists in some matters, while Rex in Wallonia and other groups sought to align with Nazi Germany. The majority of the population did not support these movements, and a diverse resistance emerged, including the Armée secrète, communist led Partisans armés, clandestine press, and escape networks such as the Comet Line that helped Allied airmen. Belgium's secretaries general faced growing pressure over labor recruitment and the persecution of Jews, and the extent of administrative participation in those measures remains a subject of historical inquiry.

Denmark's experience was exceptional. On 9 April 1940, the Danish government accepted German "protection" within hours, avoiding the destruction suffered elsewhere. Under a policy of cooperation, the king, parliament, courts, and police remained, and German demands were negotiated. Denmark joined the Anti-Comintern Pact in November 1941 under pressure. In March 1943, the Danes held a parliamentary election with very high turnout in which the Danish Nazi party received only a small share of the vote. Sabotage and strikes increased during 1943, and on 29 August 1943 the Germans declared a state of emergency, demanded measures the government refused, and took direct control, after which the Danish government resigned. Civil servants, led by departmental heads, continued to run the administration, and a Freedom Council coordinating the resistance was established in September 1943. The Danish police were later disarmed and, in September 1944, many were arrested and deported. Danish cooperation had been conditional and was withdrawn when German demands exceeded what the political class would accept, a different trajectory from that of the Netherlands.

Section 120.8

Local Authorities and Political Movements in Central and Eastern Europe

Slovakia was a formally independent state under Jozef Tiso and his Slovak People's Party, with the paramilitary Hlinka Guard as its instrument. It owed its existence to German policy in 1939, joined the invasion of the Soviet Union, and adopted antisemitic legislation, including a Jewish Code in September 1941. In 1942, the government deported about 58,000 Jews, an action it financed and organized with German involvement. At the same time, the regime faced internal opposition, including from army officers and from a mixed group of democrats and communists, which produced the uprising of 1944. In the Protectorate, Czech officials and police continued to operate under German supervision, and the Czech population largely avoided open revolt until 1945, though a network of resisters operated and Czechoslovak parachutists dispatched from Britain carried out the attack on Heydrich in 1942. Under the Protectorate government, the line between administrative necessity and complicity could be thin, as in Western Europe.

Occupied Poland differs sharply from the cases above. German policy did not allow a collaborationist Polish government. Antagonism between Germans and Poles, combined with Nazi racial policy, meant that the occupier relied on repression and on a small Polish administrative apparatus, including a police force known as the Blue Police, which worked under German command. Polish political resistance created the Underground State (section 120.9). The occupation nevertheless produced varied responses. Some Poles sheltered Jews at great risk; others blackmailed or denounced them; and the contemporary death penalty, applied by German authorities for sheltering Jews in the General Government, shaped the choices of everyone. In the summer of 1941, in the Białystok region, which had been under Soviet rule since 1939, local perpetrators took part in massacres of Jewish neighbors alongside or at the instigation of German forces, a subject that remains actively researched and that is examined further in Chapter 121.

In the Baltic states, which the Soviet Union annexed in 1940, the NKVD arrested and deported thousands in June 1941, just before the German invasion. Some residents welcomed German forces as liberators, and local administrations were established under German supervision. Auxiliary police units and other local formations participated in German security operations and in the killing of Jews, and in 1943 Latvian and Estonian legions were established within the Waffen SS, partly through conscription. A provisional Lithuanian government declared itself in June 1941 and was dissolved by the Germans. Nationalist hopes for independence thus ran against German policy, and many nationalists who accepted German help in 1941 found themselves in conflict with the occupiers later. In western Ukraine, the faction of Ukrainian nationalists led by Stepan Bandera proclaimed a Ukrainian state in Lviv on 30 June 1941; the Germans arrested Bandera and suppressed the movement's leadership, while Ukrainian auxiliary police served the occupation in many localities, and the Ukrainian Insurgent Army later fought the Germans, the Soviets, and Poles (section 120.11). Prewar Soviet policy, including collectivization, the famine of 1932 and 1933, and the Great Terror, shaped local attitudes in ways that differed from region to region.

The binary of collaboration and resistance does not capture these situations. Local actors could resist particular German policies while participating in other forms of persecution, or maintain arrangements with occupation authorities while pursuing their own aims. Auxiliary police in the east defected to Soviet partisans in large numbers in 1943 and 1944, as German defeat became likely, even though some had participated in atrocities earlier. A Polish officer might fight the Germans, refuse cooperation with the Soviet partisans, and be unwilling to hide Jews, while a local peasant might do any of these. The chapter does not assign collective responsibility to entire ethnic, religious, or national populations. Responsibility is individual and institutional, and the study of each case requires attention to who did what, under what conditions.

Multiple Perspectives

Living under Occupation: Five Positions

From the perspective of a local administrator: Staying at a post could appear a duty to the population, since supplies had to be distributed and order kept, and the Hague rules assumed continuity. Yet each regulation drafted or list compiled made the occupier's aims easier to achieve, and the line between administration and complicity was often clear only afterward.

From the perspective of an ordinary civilian: Daily life required decisions about rations, work, and silence. Most people neither collaborated ideologically nor joined an underground, and their priority was survival for themselves and their families under shortages, curfew, and fear of denunciation.
From the perspective of a local police officer: Police received orders from superiors who in turn received orders from the occupier. Some enforced regulations routinely, some warned people of raids, some took part in arrests of Jews and resisters, and some resigned or defected. Institutional loyalty, career concerns, fear, and conviction all played a part.
From the perspective of a persecuted person: For Jews, Roma, and others targeted by racial policy, the question was not how to adapt but how to avoid arrest, find shelter, and obtain documents. The behavior of neighbors, officials, and police could decide life or death, and the options available were far narrower than for most other civilians.
Division III · §§120.9–120.12 · 1939–1945

The Underground, Resistance Networks, and Armed Struggle

The third division compares resistance as a set of institutions and political actors: underground states, clandestine networks, partisan armies, and the coalitions and rivalries that divided them.

Section 120.9

Poland's Underground State and the Home Army

Poland developed occupied Europe's most elaborate clandestine state. Although German and Soviet armies conquered its territory in 1939, the Polish government continued abroad, first in France and then in London under President Władysław Raczkiewicz and Prime Minister Władysław Sikorski. Underground organizations in Poland claimed continuity with the legal republic, coordinating both armed and civilian institutions under the government-in-exile.

Military resistance began in the last days of the September campaign, when the Service for Poland's Victory was established on 27 September 1939. In November 1939 Sikorski ordered the creation of the Union of Armed Struggle, which in February 1942 became the Home Army, the Armia Krajowa. The organization absorbed most of the armed underground and grew into the largest resistance army in occupied Europe, with estimates of its sworn membership by 1944 ranging from roughly 300,000 to 400,000. Its commanders included General Stefan Rowecki, arrested by the Gestapo in June 1943, and General Tadeusz Bór-Komorowski, who succeeded him. The Home Army carried out sabotage and intelligence work, trained officers, and prepared for a general uprising, while a special unit, Kedyw, carried out diversionary actions and assassinations of German officials.

The civil dimension of the Underground State was equally distinctive. A Government Delegation for Poland, established in late 1940 and headed by a Government Delegate, functioned as a clandestine civilian administration, with departments mirroring ministries, supported by funds sent from London. The Delegate's apparatus maintained underground courts that tried collaborators and criminals, operated a secret education system, published clandestine newspapers, and maintained contact with political parties. German authorities had closed Polish universities and restricted schooling to basic levels, so the underground organized secondary classes and even university teaching in private homes, in conditions of constant danger; tens of thousands of students took part. Underground publishing included the Home Army's Information Bulletin and many party and local papers. These institutions sustained a political culture and trained administrators for the postwar state, and they gave occupied Poland a form of government unavailable to other societies in the same period.

Polish resistance was politically diverse. Peasant, socialist, nationalist, and Christian democratic groups established a coordinating body; a Council of National Unity became an underground parliament in January 1944. The Peasant Battalions were largely incorporated into the Home Army, while the National Armed Forces remained partly outside it. The Soviet-backed Polish Workers' Party organized its own People's Guard, renamed the People's Army in 1944, and advocated a rival postwar order. Polish Jews also organized separate resistance movements, treated in Chapter 121. Relations among organizations ranged from cooperation to armed conflict.

Intelligence was a major contribution. The Polish underground produced information on German troop movements, industrial sites, and the German V-weapons program, including reports about the testing site at Peenemünde, and in 1944 transmitted components of a captured V-2 rocket to Britain. This information supported Allied planning and illustrates the importance of intelligence and liaison, which this chapter treats as a form of resistance in its own right.

The Red Army's approach posed a fundamental political challenge. Polish–Soviet relations had broken down in April 1943 after discovery of the Katyn graves, containing Polish officers murdered by the Soviet NKVD in 1940, and Polish demands for investigation. Sikorski died in an aircraft crash that July. Under General Tadeusz Bór-Komorowski's order of November 20, 1943, Operation Tempest sought to attack retreating Germans and present the Home Army and underground authorities as hosts to advancing Soviet forces. In Wilno and Lwów in July 1944, Home Army units fought alongside Soviet troops but were then disarmed; commanders were arrested and soldiers interned or pressed into Soviet-controlled formations. These events exposed the disparity between clandestine Polish sovereignty claims and Soviet political power, and shaped the Warsaw Uprising described in §120.18.

Section 120.10

The French Resistance: Networks, Jean Moulin, and the National Council

French resistance began as scattered initiatives rather than a unified movement. Early examples included de Gaulle's June 18, 1940 appeal, student demonstrations in November, clandestine leaflets, and small intelligence and escape networks. The Musée de l'Homme circle in Paris produced an underground newspaper and aided escaping prisoners before German police dismantled it in 1941. Wider coordination developed gradually, particularly after 1942.

In the unoccupied zone, the major movements emerged in 1940 and 1941: Combat, led by Henri Frenay; Libération-Sud, associated with Emmanuel d'Astier; and Franc-Tireur. In the northern zone, groups such as Libération-Nord, the Organisation civile et militaire, and Ceux de la Libération developed under tighter German surveillance. After the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, the French Communist Party, which had earlier been constrained by the German-Soviet pact, entered the armed struggle through the Front national and the Francs-tireurs et partisans. Armed attacks on German personnel began in August 1941, and the Germans responded with hostage executions. Intelligence networks, such as Alliance, which Marie-Madeleine Fourcade came to lead, and the Confrérie Notre-Dame, supplied information about German forces to Britain. Escape lines helped Allied airmen and others cross into Spain or Switzerland.

The clandestine press played a central role. Newspapers such as Combat, Libération, Franc-Tireur, and Défense de la France circulated in many thousands of copies, offering news that evaded censorship and creating a sense of community among resisters. A literary resistance also existed, including the underground publishing house Éditions de Minuit, which published Vercors' novella Le Silence de la mer in 1942. Producing and distributing such materials required printers, couriers, paper supplies, and safe houses, and it exposed participants to arrest. The press mattered both as a source of information and as an instrument of political organization.

Coordination required a link with the exterior. The Special Operations Executive, created by Britain in 1940, established its F Section to support resistance in France through parachuted agents, radio operators, and supplies of weapons and explosives; a separate section worked with the Free French. The Free French intelligence and action service, the BCRA, directed networks and liaison. Jean Moulin, a former prefect dismissed by Vichy, parachuted into France in the night of 1 January 1942 as de Gaulle's representative. He worked to persuade the independent movements to accept de Gaulle's leadership and to unify their forces, and in early 1943 helped form the Mouvements unis de la Résistance. Moulin's work culminated on 27 May 1943, when the National Council of the Resistance, the Conseil national de la Résistance, met for the first time in Paris. It brought together representatives of eight resistance movements, six political parties, and two trade union confederations, and it recognized de Gaulle as the leader of French resistance. Moulin was arrested at Caluire near Lyon in June 1943 and died in July after interrogation. The Fondation de la Résistance preserves documentation relating to the inaugural meeting and to the Council's later program.

The CNR's program of 15 March 1944 set out an immediate plan of action and a vision for post liberation France. It called for the coordination of armed forces of the interior, the expulsion of the Germans, and the punishment of traitors, and proposed a range of social and political reforms: an expanded system of social security, a planned economy with nationalization of major sectors, freedom of the press, and the restoration of republican liberties. It was a compromise among groups with different ideologies, and it influenced the policies of the Provisional Government after liberation. In April 1944, the Algiers authorities also granted women the vote. The program thus linked resistance to a social and political project, not only to national liberation.

Armed resistance grew substantially after the introduction of the compulsory labor service in February 1943, when young men evading deportation took to the hills and woods. These rural groups became known as the Maquis, a term derived from the Corsican word for scrub. They included farmers' sons, workers, students, and some former soldiers, and they faced shortages of weapons and food and German and Milice operations against them. Large concentrations, such as on the Glières plateau in March 1944 and in the Vercors in the summer of 1944, were destroyed by German forces when they attempted open confrontation. In February 1944, the armed groups were nominally merged into the French Forces of the Interior. Sabotage plans, including attacks on railways and telephone lines, were activated around the Normandy landings.

Historical Vocabulary
Clandestine Press, Maquis, Intelligence Networks, Sabotage

The clandestine press consisted of illegally produced newspapers, leaflets, and books that circulated in defiance of censorship; its importance lay in information and organization as much as in propaganda. The Maquis were rural groups of resisters, many of them evaders of compulsory labor service, who lived in forests and uplands; the term should not be applied to all French resistance. Intelligence networks collected and transmitted information on German forces and installations to Allied services, usually through radio operators and couriers, and they were organizationally distinct from armed groups. Sabotage means deliberate damage to transport, communications, or production, ranging from railway demolitions to deliberate slow working in factories; it could be carried out by organized units or by individual workers. These activities were different forms of resistance with different risks and effects, and not parts of a single undifferentiated campaign.

Section 120.11

Partisan Warfare in Eastern Europe: Belarus, Ukraine, and Occupied Territories

Partisan warfare in the German occupied Soviet Union was shaped by the scale of territory, by the political structure of the Soviet state, and by the brutality of German occupation. On 3 July 1941 Stalin called in a radio address for the creation of partisan units behind German lines, and the Soviet leadership organized a network of underground party committees and parachuted officers. In May 1942 the Central Staff of the Partisan Movement was established in Moscow, to direct and supply groups in occupied territory. Soviet partisan units therefore operated under a central command with political commissars, radio links, and supplies delivered by air, which distinguished them from many other resistance movements.

Geography mattered. The forests and marshes of Belarus and northern Ukraine, notably the Pripet marshes, gave partisans cover and bases that German units found difficult to penetrate, while villages offered food and recruits, and railways offered targets. Partisan strength grew substantially after the winter of 1941 and 1942, as the failure of the German offensive near Moscow and the harshness of the occupation produced new recruits, including escaped prisoners of war and men who feared deportation for labor. Estimates of the total number of Soviet partisans reach several hundred thousand at their peak, with concentrations in Belarus; such figures are uncertain, vary by source, and depend on definitions of membership. Partisan operations included attacks on German garrisons and collaborators, assassination of officials, and intelligence gathering for the Red Army.

The most important military contribution was sabotage of rail transport. In the "rail war" beginning in August 1943, partisans in Belarus and elsewhere carried out coordinated demolitions on German supply lines during the Soviet counteroffensives, and in June 1944 they again attacked rail lines in support of the Red Army's Operation Bagration, whose military course belongs to Chapter 119. Historians assess these attacks as significant in causing delays and forcing the Germans to commit large security forces, though the extent of the effect remains debated and Soviet postwar accounts exaggerated it. This section concentrates on the partisans as an organization and as part of occupation society.

Not all armed groups served Moscow. The Ukrainian Insurgent Army, formed in 1942 and 1943 from nationalist networks, fought against German forces, Soviet partisans, and Polish communities, and sought an independent Ukrainian state. In Volhynia in 1943 and 1944 its units and local allies carried out massacres of Polish civilians that killed tens of thousands, with estimates varying, and Polish groups carried out retaliatory killings of Ukrainian civilians. In the same region, Polish Home Army units and Soviet partisans also operated, sometimes in conflict. Jewish partisans, including those who escaped ghettos and camps, formed their own groups or joined others, and family camps such as that organized by the Bielski brothers in the forests of western Belarus sheltered large numbers of civilians. Chapter 121 considers Jewish armed resistance. The result was a landscape in which several armed forces with different objectives operated simultaneously.

German responses to partisan activity were brutal. Units conducted what the Germans called anti bandit warfare, a term that treated civilians and combatants as indistinguishable and justified mass killing. Large operations in Belarus, such as Operation Cottbus in 1943, killed thousands of civilians, and German forces burned hundreds of villages, often with their inhabitants, such as Khatyn in March 1943. Units such as the Dirlewanger brigade became notorious for atrocities. German policy aimed at creating empty zones around partisan areas, and the killing of civilians in reprisal was a deliberate instrument, not an accident of war. The partisan war thus involved the civilian population as a primary victim, and this intensified the sense that people had little to lose by joining the partisans.

Historians distinguish between military effectiveness and the later mythology of the partisan movement. Soviet postwar narratives emphasized heroic mass participation and an almost universal popular support, a picture that simplified a more complex situation. Partisans requisitioned food from villagers, sometimes by force; they executed suspected collaborators, often on thin evidence; they conscripted men; and in some instances committed violence against civilians. Their actions also provoked German reprisals against communities that had not supported them. Such records, studied by historians using archives opened after 1991, do not negate partisan courage or the significance of resistance but show its costs and ambiguities. The partisan war was both an instrument of Soviet strategy and a series of local conflicts among villagers, partisans, police, and occupiers.

Historical Vocabulary
Partisan Warfare, Political Resistance, Collaboration, and Civil Conflict

Partisan warfare is irregular armed struggle behind enemy lines by units that operate from forests, mountains, or villages and often depend on civilian support. Political resistance refers to organized opposition that may be unarmed, such as clandestine parties, strikes, publications, and shadow administration. Collaboration, as defined in section 120.5, describes cooperation with the occupier and should be distinguished from accommodation and from ideological collaborationism. Civil conflict arises when armed groups within the same society fight one another, as in Yugoslavia, Greece, and Italy, where resisting the occupier coincided with contests over the postwar order. These terms describe different phenomena, and many conflicts involved several at once: a single armed band could fight Germans, clash with a rival resistance movement, and be accused of attacking civilians.

Section 120.12

Yugoslav, Greek, and Italian Resistance: Political Coalitions and Civil Conflict

In Yugoslavia, the Axis invasion of April 1941 was followed by dismemberment: Germany, Italy, Hungary, and Bulgaria occupied or annexed territories; Serbia was placed under German military administration and a Serbian government under Milan Nedić; and the Independent State of Croatia, headed by Ante Pavelić and the Ustaša, was created as an Axis aligned state that carried out the persecution and murder of Serbs, Jews, and Roma. Two resistance movements emerged. The Chetniks, led by the royalist officer Draža Mihailović, were associated with the exiled monarchy and the Serbian officer corps and were committed to a Serbian dominated Yugoslavia; the Partisans, led by Josip Broz Tito and the Communist Party, claimed a federal and multi-ethnic Yugoslavia and began an uprising in the summer of 1941. The two movements cooperated briefly in 1941, then fought each other, and some Chetnik units made local arrangements with Italian and German forces against the Partisans, which is documented. The Partisans' multi-ethnic character owed much to Ustaša terror, which drove many Serbs of Croatia and Bosnia to join them. Allied liaison missions shifted their support to the Partisans in 1943, partly because they were seen as more effective against German forces. The Partisans proclaimed a provisional government at Jajce in November 1943 and, with Soviet help, took Belgrade in October 1944.

In Greece, occupation was divided among Germany, Italy, and Bulgaria, and the winter of 1941 and 1942 brought a devastating famine that killed many tens of thousands, driven by requisitions, the Allied blockade, and the collapse of trade. In September 1941 the National Liberation Front, EAM, was founded, led by the Communist Party but including socialists and other groups, and its military arm, ELAS, was created in December 1941. A rival organization, the National Republican Greek League, EDES, under Napoleon Zervas, operated chiefly in Epirus. In November 1942, ELAS and EDES cooperated with a British SOE mission to destroy the Gorgopotamos railway viaduct, an operation that disrupted German supply lines to North Africa. Cooperation gave way to open conflict in late 1943, and the Greek political order, with collaborationist security battalions, the royal government in exile, and a communist led resistance, fractured into the civil conflict that culminated in the clashes in Athens in December 1944 and later civil war. Greek resistance also included a large youth organization associated with EAM and the participation of women in roles ranging from welfare to combat.

Italy offers a distinct case, because the occupier was a former ally. When the armistice with the Allies was announced on 8 September 1943, German forces moved to disarm the Italian army and occupy the country, interning hundreds of thousands of Italian soldiers and freeing Mussolini, who headed the Italian Social Republic in the north. A Committee of National Liberation formed on 9 September 1943, bringing together anti fascist parties from communists and socialists to Christian Democrats and liberals, and a northern committee coordinated resistance in German held Italy. Partisan brigades were associated with different parties: the communist Garibaldi brigades, the Action Party's Justice and Liberty formations, socialist Matteotti brigades, Catholic and monarchist groups, and others. Estimates of partisan numbers vary and reach the low hundreds of thousands at peak. The Italian historian Claudio Pavone argued that the resistance was simultaneously a patriotic war of liberation, a civil war between Italians, and a class war, and the formulation is widely discussed. German reprisals, such as the Ardeatine Caves massacre in Rome in March 1944 and massacres in the Apennines in 1944, were severe.

The social composition of these movements varied. Peasants provided the bulk of fighters in Yugoslavia and Greece; workers and students were central in Italian cities; deserters from armies, such as Italian soldiers after the armistice, joined partisan formations; and youth were prominent in all three cases. Women served as couriers, nurses, organizers, and in the Yugoslav case as combatants in significant numbers, and Italian women known as the staffette carried messages and weapons. The distribution of risk and sacrifice in these movements, and the extent to which their participation was later recognized, is considered in section 120.15. The comparison underscores that resistance was not one thing: its character depended on who joined it, what they wanted, and who they were fighting.

Repression, Civilian Survival, and the Moral Geography of Occupation

The fourth division turns from institutions to experience. It examines how people lived, how occupiers punished and intimidated, who took part in resistance beyond the armed underground, and how some people tried to protect the persecuted.

Section 120.13

Everyday Life under Occupation: Food, Housing, Work, and Social Survival

Occupation imposed daily material pressures, especially through food shortages. Rations allocated scarce goods by coupon while requisitions and disrupted transport lowered urban supplies; families queued, bartered in the countryside, and improvised substitutes for fuel, clothing, soap, and shoes. Food policies differed sharply: Polish and eastern European civilians generally received less than western Europeans, with separate discriminatory rations for persecuted groups. Greece endured devastating famine in 1941–1942. In the western Netherlands, the Hunger Winter of 1944–1945, intensified by disrupted supplies and German restrictions, produced substantial famine-associated mortality. NIOD cites estimates ranging from roughly 16,000 excess deaths in part of 1945 to more than 22,000 direct and indirect victims over the crisis; these measures are not identical.

Where official supply failed, a black market arose. Black market trade was widespread in nearly every occupied society, and it included small scale bartering by city families, profiteering by merchants and farmers, and organized trafficking. Its moral character was ambiguous. A mother who bought extra bread illegally to feed her children and a profiteer who hoarded goods to sell at inflated prices were both violating regulations, but few contemporaries regarded the two as morally equivalent. Black market dealing could also, in some cases, enrich those who also served the occupier. The history of the black market shows that survival often required breaking rules and that rule breaking was not a political stance in itself.

Housing and movement were also affected. German forces requisitioned hotels, schools, and apartments for offices and billets, and families were forced to share space or leave. Curfews and blackout rules restricted evening life. Travel permits limited movement across zones, and in France the demarcation line between occupied and unoccupied zones separated relatives and economies. About 1.8 million French soldiers were held as prisoners of war in Germany, leaving wives and children to cope without male earners for years. Others were deported for labor, jailed as hostages, or imprisoned for political offenses. Schools were closed or restricted in Poland and the Czech lands and surveilled elsewhere. Children grew up in households marked by missing fathers, constant hunger, and fear, and many were sent to relatives in the countryside.

Urban and rural circumstances differed. Farmers could usually feed themselves and had goods to trade, though they faced requisition quotas and the risk of partisan or German reprisals in contested regions. City dwellers depended on rations and bartering, and the elderly, the poor, and refugees were most vulnerable. Privileged households with connections, foreign currency, or access to farms fared better, and persecuted households fared worst of all, since Jews and Roma faced exclusion from employment, restrictions on movement, and expropriation of property in addition to shortages. Displaced civilians, including those evacuated from bombed cities or expelled from annexed territories, faced an additional layer of insecurity.

Civilians sustained family and community life under these pressures. Religious services continued, often with added significance; markets and workplaces functioned; neighbors shared food, lent rooms, and cared for children whose parents had been arrested. Charitable and mutual aid organizations, such as national relief committees, distributed food and clothing, and some operated with German permission while sheltering clandestine activities. Education continued through informal classes. These networks of mutual assistance formed what historians sometimes call a moral economy of survival, and they were a basis for resistance as well as a way of maintaining ordinary life.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household's Experience of Occupation and Material Shortages

The following is an analytical composite, assembled from patterns documented in memoirs, diaries, and administrative records of occupied western Europe. It does not describe a single named family.

Consider an urban household in an occupied western city in 1943: two parents, three children, and a grandparent. The father works in a factory that has taken German orders; his employer says that refusing would close the plant and send its workers to labor service. The mother holds the ration cards, spends mornings in queues, and travels by train every few weeks to a relative's farm to exchange household goods for food. The eldest child, nearly of age for labor conscription, is deciding whether to register, hide, or seek false papers. A room has been requisitioned for a German clerk. Coal is rationed and the apartment is unheated for much of the winter. A cousin has been arrested, and the family does not know where he has been sent. In this household, a purchase on the black market, a refusal to inform on a neighbor, an act of sharing food, and the labor of the father at a plant working for Germany all occur in the same week, and they do not carry the same moral meaning.

The example shows why historians of occupation avoid reducing daily life to heroism or complicity. Material necessity compelled actions that would have been unremarkable before the war but acquired political significance under occupation. It also shows why different households had different options. A family with a farm connection ate better than one without; a household with a Jewish member faced dangers the rest of the street did not. Students should use sources such as diaries and oral histories to ask how households made choices, what information they had, and what resources they could draw on.

Section 120.14

German Policing, Hostages, Collective Reprisals, and the Night and Fog Decree

German repression involved overlapping authorities: the Gestapo and Security Police, the SS Security Service, military Secret Field Police, military courts, and regional Higher SS and Police Leaders. German units relied heavily on indigenous police, informers, and auxiliary groups, including the French Milice and Dutch Landwacht. These relationships shaped the scale and method of arrest, hostage-taking, deportation, and collective reprisals across occupied Europe.

German authorities used hostages and collective punishment as routine instruments. Hostage taking and the killing of hostages in reprisal for attacks were prohibited by the laws of war, yet in September 1941 the Wehrmacht High Command, under Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel, directed that for every German soldier killed in the context of communist insurgency, fifty to one hundred hostages should be executed. In October 1941, after the killing of German officers in Nantes and Bordeaux, dozens of French hostages were shot, including at Châteaubriant. Such practices were intended to deter resistance by making the population fear that attacks would be paid for by the innocent. They also had the opposite effect in many cases, as resistance leaders and local populations learned that reprisals were unavoidable, and as the policy eroded cooperation. In some places, such as Serbia and Greece, reprisals reached massacres of thousands in a single action, and reprisals in Greek villages such as Kalavryta in December 1943 killed hundreds of men and boys.

One of the most distinctive documents of repression is the decree of 7 December 1941, known as the Night and Fog Decree, from the German phrase Nacht und Nebel. It was issued under Hitler's authority and conveyed by Field Marshal Keitel, and it provided that persons in the occupied western territories who had committed offenses against the Reich or the occupation forces and whose cases were not judged likely to end in a death sentence within a short time were to be taken secretly to Germany. There, the prisoners would be held without information given to relatives, and tried by special courts or sent to camps. The intention was intimidation: families and communities would not know what had happened to the missing person. The decree and its implementing regulations were later presented at Nuremberg as document L-90, and the Avalon Project preserves a documentary edition. Students should treat it as evidence of a specific legal and administrative instrument of repression, aimed primarily at resisters and political opponents in western Europe, not as a description of every arrest or deportation of the occupation.

Two cases from the Protectorate and France illustrate the destruction of whole communities. After the attack on Heydrich in Prague on 27 May 1942, carried out by Czechoslovak parachutists trained in Britain, and his death on 4 June, German authorities launched a massive manhunt and a wave of executions. On 9 and 10 June they targeted the village of Lidice, near Kladno. The pretext was an alleged connection to the assassins, which German authorities had no real evidence to support. More than 170 men and boys were shot on the spot, the women were sent to Ravensbrück concentration camp, and the children were taken away: a small number judged racially suitable were placed with German families, and many others were murdered at Chełmno. The village was burned and razed. A similar action destroyed the hamlet of Ležáky later in June. German propaganda initially publicized Lidice as a deterrent; the news, however, provoked international outrage and became a symbol of Nazi brutality, and memorials around the world took its name.

On 10 June 1944, four days after the Allied landings in Normandy, soldiers of the 2nd SS Panzer Division Das Reich entered Oradour-sur-Glane in the Limousin. They separated the men from the women and children; the men were shot, and the women and children were locked in the church, which was set on fire. The death toll was 642. The village had no known role in armed resistance that could explain or justify the killing, and the motives of the German commanders have been debated, with explanations including intimidation of the region, retaliation for attacks on the division's march, and the brutalization of the unit by earlier service on the Eastern Front. The ruins were preserved as a memorial. The trial of former members of the unit in Bordeaux in 1953 produced a political controversy because many defendants were Alsatians conscripted into the SS, and an amnesty was passed.

Primary Source Window

The Night and Fog Decree, December 7, 1941 (Nuremberg Document L-90)

Document: December 7, 1941 directive (Nuremberg evidence L-90), with implementing orders separately reproduced. Read the complete document and related instructions at Yale Avalon Project.

Creator: Issued under Adolf Hitler's authority and transmitted by Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel, head of the Armed Forces High Command · Date: December 7, 1941, with implementing instructions issued shortly afterward · Type: A directive of the German military and legal administration governing the prosecution of offenses against the Reich or the occupation forces in the occupied western territories

Context: The decree was issued as resistance activity increased in western Europe after the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, and as German authorities debated how to punish attacks and sabotage. It was part of a sequence of directives that included the hostage orders of September 1941. The text was introduced as evidence at Nuremberg and is preserved in the published trial documents; the Yale Avalon Project hosts an edition as document L-90, and the Harvard Nuremberg Trials Project holds related materials. Students should consult the original text and its translation rather than rely on the summary below.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation In summary, the directive states that attacks on the Reich or the occupation forces in the western territories are normally to be punished by death, and that lesser sentences are inadequate as deterrents. It provides that, where a death sentence cannot be expected promptly, the accused are to be transferred secretly to Germany and dealt with there, with no information given about their fate or whereabouts. It sets out the respective roles of military authorities and the judiciary in selecting and transferring cases, and it explains the intended effect as deterrence through the uncertainty experienced by families and communities. This paraphrase summarizes the order's purposes and procedures as historians describe them; it is not a translation of any passage, and no wording here should be cited as the text of the decree.

Analysis: The document is valuable because it reveals the stated logic of intimidation: the occupiers aimed to deter resistance not only by punishing offenders but by making disappearance a public threat. It shows the interaction of military and judicial authorities and the extraordinary treatment of political suspects, who were removed from the legal protections of their home country. It also shows that German authorities regarded legal procedures as instruments of policy. Its limitations are significant. As an order, it states intentions and procedures and does not by itself reveal how consistently it was applied, how many people were affected, or what happened to individual prisoners, for which historians need camp records, court files, and survivor testimony. It represents the perspective of the perpetrators and does not capture the experience of the families, who lacked information, or of the victims. It also applied to specific categories of offenders in the western occupied territories, and it should not be generalized to all arrests or to the east.

Section 120.15

Women, Youth, Churches, Intellectuals, and the Social Networks of Resistance

Military histories of resistance often concentrate on armed fighters and leaders, but resistance was sustained by networks in which women, young people, workers, clergy, and professionals played indispensable roles. Intelligence, communication, shelter, documents, and funds were as essential as weapons. The roles of the people who performed these tasks have often been underrepresented in commemoration, in part because the tasks were less visible, and in part because postwar political cultures privileged armed combat and male leadership.

Women's participation spanned every form of resistance. They served as couriers who carried messages, weapons, and money, as intelligence agents and radio operators, as organizers of networks, as forgers of documents, as providers of safe houses and food, and in some movements as armed fighters. In France, Berty Albrecht helped found the Combat movement and died in German custody, Lucie Aubrac took part in resistance in Lyon, and Marie-Madeleine Fourcade led the Alliance intelligence network; the British SOE sent women agents to France, among them Noor Inayat Khan, who was executed at Dachau in 1944. In Belgium, Andrée de Jongh organized the Comet Line. In Poland, women worked as liaison officers and in the underground administration, and Irena Sendler served in the Żegota children's section. In Yugoslavia, women fought as Partisans in significant numbers, and in Italy the staffette moved through German held territory carrying messages. In Greece, EAM's youth and women's organizations carried out welfare, propaganda, and military tasks. The risks were great: arrest, torture, deportation, and execution. Recognition afterward was uneven: a very small number of women were among the Compagnons de la Libération in France, for example, and many were excluded from veterans' benefits or from public narratives.

Young people formed a major part of resistance. Students were prominent in Prague in November 1939, in the Paris demonstration of November 1940, in the clandestine paper Défense de la France, in Dutch student protests against loyalty declarations in 1943, and in the secret schools of Poland. The labor service conscription pushed many young men into evasion. Trade unions, which German and Vichy authorities restricted or dissolved, provided cadres and networks for strikes and sabotage, and organized labor played an important part in the Dutch strikes, in the Italian strikes of 1943 and 1944, and in the French railway workers' activities. Professional associations such as teachers' and lawyers' groups served as institutional bases for passive resistance, as in Norway.

The churches' record was varied and should not be summarized as uniformly resistant. In Norway, bishops and clergy resisted Quisling's attempts to control the church in 1942. In France, individual Catholic and Protestant leaders protested against deportations: in August 1942 Archbishop Jules-Géraud Saliège of Toulouse issued a public pastoral letter condemning the treatment of Jews, and the Protestant village of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon under Pastor André Trocmé sheltered refugees. In the Netherlands, church leaders protested persecution of Jews, and in Greece Archbishop Damaskinos sought to protect Jews. Yet other church leaders cooperated with Vichy or remained silent, and many religious institutions sought to preserve their own position. Individuals acted on religious conviction without institutional backing, and churches were as diverse as the societies they served.

Intellectual resistance included clandestine publishing, secret universities, and symbolic acts. The writer Vercors and the Éditions de Minuit, the Polish underground universities, and the Czech and Dutch underground presses preserved cultural life and asserted moral claims against the occupier. In Germany itself, the White Rose student circle in Munich, including Hans and Sophie Scholl and Christoph Probst, distributed leaflets in 1942 and 1943 criticizing the regime and its crimes and was arrested in February 1943; the Scholls and Probst were executed on 22 February 1943, and other members followed later. The White Rose belongs to a different category than resistance in occupied territory: it was opposition within the Reich to its own government, carried out under the domestic judicial terror of the People's Court. Comparing the two helps students distinguish resistance to a foreign occupier from dissent within the state that was the occupier.

Section 120.16

Rescue, Solidarity, and the Limits of Civilian Assistance

Organized rescue networks existed in several countries. In Poland, the Council to Aid Jews, Żegota, was founded in 1942 with the support of the Polish Government Delegation and the underground parties, and it provided funds, documents, shelter, and children's placement for Jews who were hiding or escaping ghettos. Poland's occupation was uniquely severe: German authorities in the General Government imposed the death penalty on anyone who sheltered Jews, and communities faced collective punishment. Individual Poles recognized by Yad Vashem as Righteous Among the Nations make up the largest national group in that program, though recognition reflects documented cases, and other Poles denounced or blackmailed Jews. In France, organizations such as the Jewish children's aid society and Christian networks hid children, and the village of Le Chambon sheltered hundreds of refugees; about three quarters of the Jews living in France in 1940 survived the war, though the proportion among foreign Jews was lower. In the Netherlands and Belgium, committees and networks hid thousands of Jewish children and adults, though the Dutch Jewish population suffered some of the highest proportional losses in western Europe. Diplomats such as the Portuguese consul Aristides de Sousa Mendes in 1940, and Chiune Sugihara, the Japanese consul in Kaunas, issued visas at personal risk. Chapter 121 considers the Holocaust and Jewish resistance fully.

The rescue of Danish Jews in October 1943 is the best known case of mass rescue and requires careful context. The Danish government's resignation on 29 August 1943 and the introduction of direct German rule created the conditions for an action against Danish Jews, which had been postponed while the cooperative arrangement lasted. A German official in Copenhagen, Georg Duckwitz, is reported by historians to have warned Danish politicians of the planned roundup, and the news spread rapidly. In early October 1943, as German police began arrests, thousands of Danes helped Jewish families to hide in homes, churches, and hospitals, and then to cross the narrow strait of the Øresund to neutral Sweden in fishing boats and other craft. The Swedish government announced it would receive the refugees. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum documents that more than 7,000 Danish Jews escaped to Sweden, while around 470 who were captured were deported to Theresienstadt, where a number died. The Danish authorities and others pressed for information and for contact with the deportees.

The Danish case should not be treated as a universal European pattern. Several features were unusual: the small size of the Jewish community, about 7,500 people; the country's proximity to neutral Sweden across a short stretch of water; the willingness of the Swedish government to accept refugees; the lack of previous German implementation of anti Jewish measures in Denmark, so that the community had not been registered or isolated; the high degree of public solidarity, reflecting political culture and the experience of the cooperation policy; and the limited German police presence. These circumstances did not exist in occupied Poland, the Baltic states, or the Soviet territories, where Jews were concentrated in ghettos and surrounded by hostile conditions, or in countries where deportations began earlier.

Comparison of risks across occupation systems clarifies the limits of rescue. In the west, rescuers who were caught often faced imprisonment or deportation but not usually immediate execution, whereas in Poland and much of the east the penalty could be death for entire families. The availability of safe havens, access to borders, the attitude of local police, the population density of the area, and the visibility of neighbors affected what was possible. Rescue was also dependent on resources, because hiding a person required food, space, and silence over long periods, and many rescuers acted at financial and personal cost. Even in places where rescue was extensive, it saved a minority of those persecuted, and those saved were often helped by networks that were improvised, fragile, and dependent on chance. Rescuers were a minority of the population, and so were collaborators; the great majority of people were bystanders in varied situations. Historical analysis should respect the courage of rescuers without implying that rescue could have prevented most of the killing.

Division V · §§120.17–120.20 · 1943–1945

Uprisings, Liberation, and the Political Legacy of Occupation

The final division follows the collapse of occupation regimes. It asks how changing military fortunes altered expectations, how uprisings and liberations unfolded, and why the political settlement remained contested.

Section 120.17

The Crisis of Occupation, 1943–1944: Resistance and Changing Expectations

From early 1943, the military balance turned against the Axis, and the effects were felt in every occupied society. The German defeat at Stalingrad in February 1943, the Axis surrender in North Africa in May, the Allied invasion of Sicily in July, and the fall of Mussolini on 25 July changed what people thought possible. The Italian armistice, announced on 8 September 1943, led to the German occupation of northern and central Italy, the disarming of the Italian army, and the rise of a large resistance movement. Soviet advances after Kursk and across the Dnieper, and preparations for an Allied landing in the west, encouraged those who had waited, a posture the French called attentisme, to take sides.

Expectations of liberation raised recruitment into resistance movements, but they also hardened German repression. In France, the compulsory labor service and the Milice produced both new recruits and new violence; in the Netherlands, the strikes of April and May 1943 were crushed; in Denmark, direct German rule followed the August 1943 crisis; and in Hungary, German forces occupied the country on 19 March 1944, after which the persecution of Hungarian Jews, examined in Chapter 121, accelerated. Germany's own situation encouraged radicalization: the occupation became both more exploitative, as demands for labor and goods grew, and more insecure. Underground organizations prepared for the moment of liberation by recruiting, stockpiling arms, planning sabotage, and creating shadow administrations that could take power when the Germans left. This preparation involved competing political claims about the postwar order, between communists and non communists, republicans and monarchists, and internal resistance and exile governments.

Resistance intelligence and liaison were increasingly valuable to Allied planners. The Allies parachuted agents, weapons, and radio equipment, and in 1944 organized the dispatch of Jedburgh teams, small groups of Allied and French officers, to coordinate local resistance with the invasion. Sabotage plans were timed to the Normandy landings. Allied planners treated resistance as a supplement to conventional forces, valuable for delay and information, and they were careful to keep control over strategy and to avoid premature uprisings. The Allies' own priorities, expressed at conferences such as Tehran in November and December 1943, did not always correspond with the political aims of resistance movements, particularly regarding the future of Poland, Yugoslavia, and Greece.

The Slovak National Uprising shows how an Axis aligned regime could be challenged from within. Slovakia, under Tiso, was an authoritarian client state that had allowed deportations of Jews and joined the German war effort. By 1944, an opposition of army officers, democrats, and communists, with contacts with the Czechoslovak government in exile and the Soviet Union, planned an uprising. It began on 29 August 1944, shortly after partisans killed German personnel, and German troops entered the country. Much of the Slovak army in central Slovakia joined the rebels, and a base was established around Banská Bystrica. The uprising received limited external help and was suppressed by German forces and Slovak units loyal to the regime by the end of October, after which many insurgents fled to the mountains and German units carried out reprisals. It illustrates the combination of political and military goals: it aimed to overthrow an authoritarian government and ally the country with the Allies while confronting German intervention.

By 1944, therefore, the central question for occupied societies was not only when liberation would come but who would hold power afterward, and on what terms. Relationships between national independence, social revolution, and political restoration were unsettled. The interests of the major Allies, especially the Soviet Union, Britain, and the United States, shaped what was feasible, and resistance leaders knew that their claims to authority depended on military events largely outside their control.

Section 120.18

The Warsaw Uprising of 1944: Resistance, Soviet Power, and the Destruction of a City

The uprising arose from Operation Tempest. By the summer of 1944, the Red Army's Operation Bagration (Chapter 119) had destroyed German Army Group Centre and advanced to the eastern bank of the Vistula, close to Warsaw. Soviet forces had established a rival Polish authority, the Polish Committee of National Liberation, at Lublin on 22 July. In those circumstances, the Home Army's command, under General Bór-Komorowski, and the Government Delegate faced a decision: to launch an uprising in Warsaw to liberate the capital and establish the authority of the legitimate Polish government before the Soviets entered, or to wait. Soviet radio broadcasts in late July urged Warsaw residents to rise, though their relationship to Soviet military plans is disputed. The decision was made on 31 July, and the uprising began at 5 p.m. on 1 August 1944.

Estimates of Home Army strength in Warsaw vary, from about 20,000 to 50,000 fighters, with only a fraction armed. They expected the battle to last a few days. The insurgents captured much of the city center in the first days but failed to take key bridges, the airport, and German strong points, and the Germans were reinforced by units under Erich von dem Bach-Zelewski, including police and SS formations such as the Dirlewanger brigade and Russian collaborationist units. Hitler and Himmler ordered harsh measures against the city and its population. In the first days of August, German units massacred civilians in the western district of Wola, killing tens of thousands, and similar atrocities occurred elsewhere. Later, the Germans reduced the indiscriminate killing but continued systematic destruction of districts. The insurgents fought house by house, using sewers to move between districts, while civilians sheltered in cellars, endured hunger and thirst, and helped with medical care, communication, and construction of barricades. Women and young people served as nurses, couriers, and fighters.

External assistance was limited and controversial. British and Polish crews flying from Italy, with South African and other Allied participation, dropped supplies at heavy cost, though many containers fell into German hands. Stalin initially denied that the Soviet Union had any connection with what he described as an adventure by irresponsible people, and the Soviet Union refused landing rights to Western aircraft for several weeks, then allowed one American shuttle mission in mid September. The Soviet army had meanwhile been halted east of the Vistula by German counterattacks and logistical overextension after its long advance, and it captured the Praga district on the east bank in mid September; units of the Soviet sponsored Polish First Army made a limited attempt to cross the river and suffered heavy losses.

Historians debate the causes of Soviet inaction. The evidence supports several factors that operated together. Operational conditions were real: the Soviet advance had outrun its supply lines, and the German counterattack near Warsaw in early August inflicted setbacks. Political motives also mattered: the Soviet leadership regarded the Home Army as a hostile political force, supported its own Polish allies in Lublin, and had no interest in aiding a movement loyal to the London government. Diplomatic disputes between Moscow and the Western Allies compounded the problem. Scholars differ on the relative weight of operational limits and political intention, and the Soviet archives opened after 1991 have contributed to a more detailed picture without ending the debate. The mixture of constraints and calculations suggests that neither a simple narrative of deliberate betrayal nor one of purely military necessity captures the evidence.

On 2 October 1944, after 63 days of fighting, the uprising capitulated. The surrender agreement recognized the Home Army fighters as combatants entitled to prisoner of war treatment, but the population was expelled. Hundreds of thousands of civilians passed through a transit camp at Pruszków, many were sent to labor in the Reich or to concentration camps, and the Germans then systematically demolished much of the remaining city. Civilian deaths are commonly estimated in the range of 150,000 to 200,000, and Home Army losses at about 16,000 killed, though figures vary. The Red Army entered the ruins in January 1945. Soviet and Polish communist authorities then dismantled the underground: the Home Army was dissolved in January 1945, and in June 1945 sixteen leaders of the Polish Underground State were tried in Moscow. In communist Poland the uprising was officially minimized or condemned; it was a major theme of memory only after 1989, with the opening of the Warsaw Uprising Museum in 2004. The event raises questions about the decision to launch an uprising without guarantees of external support, about the responsibilities of the Allied powers, and about German atrocities, which were the cause of most of the destruction.

Section 120.19

Liberation, Insurrection, and the Return of Political Authority

Liberation took different forms across Europe. It could be the arrival of an external army, an uprising by domestic forces, a political transfer of authority, or a long process of rebuilding institutions. In many places it involved all of these, and the relationships among them determined the postwar political order. The detailed military campaigns, including Normandy and the final defeat of German armies, belong to Chapter 122; this section considers the role of resistance and the return of political authority.

In France, the Normandy landings of 6 June 1944 prompted sabotage and attacks by the Forces of the Interior, and in August the Allied breakout opened the way to Paris. On 19 August the Paris police went on strike and an insurrection began, led by resistance forces including communist and Gaullist groups, with barricades appearing in the streets. The German commander, General Dietrich von Choltitz, negotiated truces and failed to destroy the city as Hitler had ordered. Allied commanders had intended to bypass Paris, but pressure from de Gaulle and the French command, and the insurrection itself, led to the dispatch of General Leclerc's 2nd Armoured Division, which entered the city on 24 and 25 August. Choltitz surrendered on 25 August, and de Gaulle spoke at the Hôtel de Ville the same day, emphasizing the role of the French people. The Musée de la Libération documents the sequence. The liberation of Paris combined internal insurrection, Free French military units, and American support, and later accounts that stressed one element over the others reflected political preferences.

The restoration of political authority followed. The Provisional Government under de Gaulle asserted its authority through commissioners of the Republic and by annulling Vichy legislation, and it received Allied recognition in October 1944. It sought to prevent rival resistance power centers, especially communist militias, from establishing parallel authority, and it absorbed the Forces of the Interior into the regular army. Disputes over legitimacy were therefore settled in practice by control of the state apparatus. In Belgium, Brussels was liberated on 3 and 4 September 1944, and the government in exile returned; the Netherlands was liberated in stages, with the south freed in autumn 1944 and the north and west only in May 1945; in Norway and Denmark the German forces surrendered in May 1945, and resistance forces assumed security functions. In Greece, the German withdrawal in October 1944 was followed by confrontation in Athens in December between the left and British supported forces. In Yugoslavia, the Partisans and the Red Army took Belgrade on 20 October 1944, and Tito's movement took control. In Czechoslovakia, a Prague uprising began on 5 May 1945 shortly before the arrival of Soviet forces.

In Italy, the resistance contributed to the final collapse of German control. After Allied forces broke through in April 1945, the northern committee called a general insurrection on 25 April, and resistance forces took control of Milan, Turin, Genoa, and other cities, in several cases before Allied troops arrived and sometimes accepting German surrenders. Partisans captured and executed Mussolini on 28 April. The date of 25 April became the national day of liberation, and in the political life of the Italian Republic the resistance became a founding myth, though one contested by different political groups.

Liberation brought reprisals as well as legal proceedings. French postwar estimates, reviewed by historian Henry Rousso, distinguish approximately 8,000–9,000 extrajudicial executions from a broader purge death toll that also includes executions after judicial proceedings; counting conventions differ. Women accused of intimate relationships with Germans faced public humiliation, including head shaving, in several countries. Special courts investigated collaborators: Pétain's 1945 death sentence was commuted, while Pierre Laval and Norway's Vidkun Quisling were executed following trials. Legal standards, political pressures, and later amnesties varied, and Chapter 123 examines postwar justice in depth.

Several factors help explain why resistance developed earlier or more extensively in some places. Where occupation was most brutal and offered the least room for accommodation, as in Poland and the Soviet territories, organized resistance appeared early because ordinary survival was threatened. Where occupation offered institutional continuity, as in Denmark, Vichy France, and the Netherlands, resistance developed more slowly and often gained momentum when German demands, particularly for labor, undermined the premises of accommodation. Geography mattered, since mountains, forests, and marshes made armed resistance feasible; so did borders and sea routes, which allowed rescue and escape. The existence of an exile government, an organized political party such as the communists, and external supply influenced organization. The military situation shaped timing, with recruitment surging as German defeat appeared likely. Certain forms of cooperation remained significant even as defeat approached, because they were embedded in institutions, economic interests, and fear of reprisal, and because some collaborators, having committed themselves, had little hope of safety under a restored government.

National narratives that divide wartime populations into resisters, collaborators, and passive bystanders are misleading. Most people occupied an intermediate position, and individuals moved among roles. After the war, governments emphasized resistance as a basis for national legitimacy, which in France took the form of a narrative of a nation united against occupation, in Italy of a founding myth of the republic, in the Soviet Union of a heroic partisan people, and in communist Poland of a struggle led by communists. In some cases these narratives suppressed the memory of collaboration, of rival resistance movements, or of the persecution of Jews. French historians called the dominant post war image the resistancialist myth; it was challenged from the 1970s. Political competition after the war shaped veteran recognition, commemoration, and judgments of collaboration, and students should treat memory as a historical force and not as an unmediated record.

The legal accountability applied after the war was distinct from the historian's task. Courts judged individuals by laws, evidence, and political circumstances at the time of trial, which sometimes produced inconsistent outcomes. Historians reconstruct actions and constraints in their contemporary setting, asking what people knew, what choices they had, and what the consequences were. This does not mean avoiding moral judgment, but it means distinguishing judgment from explanation and avoiding retrospective certainty about what could have been known.

Occupation exposed the vulnerability of European states. In 1940, parliamentary governments with large armies collapsed in weeks, and their institutions were used, to different degrees, to administer the conquerors' policies. The experience reshaped understandings of citizenship, because rights depended on a state's willingness to protect them; of state power, because police and civil service could serve any master; of violence, because civilians became targets; and of political legitimacy, which in 1944 and 1945 rested on resistance and military victory as much as on constitutional continuity. These questions shaped postwar settlements, the construction of welfare states, European integration, and the Cold War division of the continent. Chapter 121 turns to the genocide that proceeded within the occupation system, and Chapter 122 to the Allied military campaigns that ended it.

Historical Debate

What Explains the Variation between Collaboration and Resistance?

The question: Why did occupied societies differ so greatly in the extent and forms of collaboration, accommodation, and resistance? Was the variation produced mainly by the structure of occupation and the war's course, or by the political cultures and choices of the societies themselves?

Interpretation A: Structure and circumstance: Some historians stress the form of German occupation, the survival or removal of national governments, German economic and racial priorities, geography, and the timing of the war. In this view, the Danish and Dutch cases differ chiefly because of administrative and geographic conditions, and resistance rose in 1943 and 1944 because German demands and the changing military balance altered the cost and benefit of different choices.
Interpretation B: Political culture, social divisions, and choice: Other historians emphasize prewar political cultures and social cleavages, including the legacy of the First World War, the strength of fascist and communist movements, linguistic and ethnic divisions, and the role of elites. They argue that comparable German pressure produced different outcomes depending on how societies were already divided and which groups saw the occupation as an opportunity. Work on Vichy associated with Robert Paxton and studies of civil conflict in Italy and Greece fit this emphasis.

Evidence: The two interpretations draw on the same sources: occupation directives and administrative files, police and court records, underground publications, trial proceedings, and memoirs. They differ in weighting. Comparative studies by scholars such as Mark Mazower and the contributions in Conway and Romijn's collection suggest that the interpretations overlap, and that structure, culture, timing, and individual choice all mattered. Other historians treat variables such as the intensity of German repression or the availability of external support as additional factors.

Why it matters: If structure dominates, differences between countries reflect circumstances more than national character, and moral judgment should be sensitive to constraints. If political culture and choice weigh more, responsibility is placed more firmly on societies and elites. The interpretation also affects how national memory is understood.

Then explain which additional evidence, such as archival police files or oral histories, would help resolve the question, and why a single explanation is unlikely to be sufficient.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Nazi Germany and its partners governed occupied Europe, how institutions and populations responded, and why occupation produced varied patterns of accommodation, collaboration, resistance, repression, and survival. Its central argument is that German occupation was not a uniform system and that outcomes depended on the interaction of German coercive power with the choices, constraints, and institutions of occupied societies.

The first division showed that the vocabulary of occupation concealed different legal and administrative arrangements: military occupation, annexation, protectorate, satellite state, and alliance. The 1907 Hague Regulations provided the contemporary legal framework, and they did not confer sovereignty on the occupier. In western and northern Europe, German authorities generally supervised existing administrations, through military governments in France and Belgium and civilian Reich commissioners in the Netherlands and Norway, with Denmark an exceptional case of negotiated cooperation. In Poland, the Czech lands, and the Soviet territories, policy combined annexation, racial hierarchy, exploitation, and terror.

Economic exploitation was a central purpose. Occupation costs, requisitions, financial manipulation, control of agriculture and industry, and increasingly the recruitment and deportation of labor under Fritz Sauckel transformed occupied economies. The distinction among paid employment, economic coercion, compulsory service, forced deportation, and concentration camp labor is essential. Exploitation depended on local cooperation and, as demands grew, it fueled evasion and resistance.

The second division analyzed collaboration and accommodation. Collaboration of states and institutions differed from ideological collaborationism, and motives included conviction, opportunism, institutional loyalty, and survival. Vichy France was an authoritarian regime with its own program and initiatives, including antisemitic legislation, and its relationship with Germany changed over time. Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands, and Belgium showed contrasting patterns, and in central and eastern Europe local authorities and nationalist movements acted in a setting complicated by Soviet annexations and German racial policy. Collective responsibility was rejected throughout.

The third division compared resistance as institutions. Poland created an Underground State with a large army, civil administration, courts, and schools. The French Resistance emerged from separate movements, was linked to Free France through Jean Moulin, and was coordinated through the National Council of the Resistance and its program of 1944. Soviet partisans operated in the forests and marshes of Belarus and Ukraine, alongside nationalist and Jewish formations, and German anti partisan warfare targeted civilians. In Yugoslavia, Greece, and Italy, resistance combined struggle against the occupier with political rivalry and civil conflict.

The fourth division turned to experience. Rationing, shortages, the black market, billeting, family separation, and the dilemmas of survival shaped daily life, and everyday accommodation was not collaboration. German policing, hostage taking, and collective reprisals, documented in the Night and Fog Decree and in the destruction of Lidice and Oradour-sur-Glane, were instruments of terror for which responsibility lay with those who ordered and carried them out. Women, young people, churches, and intellectuals sustained resistance, and rescue networks, from Żegota to the Danish operation, showed the possibilities and limits of civilian assistance, with the Danish case being unusual.

The fifth division followed the crisis and collapse of occupation. After 1943, Axis defeats altered expectations, increased recruitment, and intensified repression. The Slovak National Uprising and the Warsaw Uprising illustrated the political and military dilemmas of insurrection, the latter ending in 63 days with tens of thousands of insurgents and civilians dead and the destruction of the city, and it must be distinguished from the Ghetto Uprising of 1943. Liberation combined external armies, domestic insurrection, and political transfers of authority, and it brought retribution, legal proceedings, and contested restorations of government.

The comparative assessment showed that variation arose from occupation structure, geography, the existence of exile governments and organized parties, the course of the war, and prewar political culture and social divisions, and that no single factor explains the differences. National narratives that divide populations into resisters, collaborators, and bystanders obscure the range of experience, and postwar politics shaped memory and commemoration.

The significance of the chapter lies in what occupation revealed about European states and societies: the fragility of sovereignty, the dependence of citizenship on state protection, the vulnerability of civilians to violence, and the contested basis of political legitimacy. These themes shaped the Holocaust examined in Chapter 121, the liberation and defeat of Germany in Chapter 122, and the postwar reckoning of Chapter 123.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Military occupation
A legal condition in which territory is actually placed under the authority of a hostile army. It is a factual situation governed by law, and it does not transfer sovereignty.
Hague Regulations
The rules annexed to the 1907 Hague Convention (IV) on the laws and customs of land warfare, including Articles 42 to 56 on occupation; the legal framework applicable in World War II, distinct from the 1949 Geneva Conventions.
Military government
Administration of occupied territory by the army, as in France and Belgium, where military officials supervised local civil servants and police.
Reichskommissariat
A German civil administrative unit headed by a Reich commissioner, used in the Netherlands, Norway, and occupied Soviet territory (Ostland and Ukraine).
Protectorate
A subordinate arrangement in which a nominal local government continues under external control, as in the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia.
Annexation
Unilateral incorporation of territory into the conqueror's state, such as western Poland and Luxembourg; the Allies did not recognize German annexations.
General Government
The German administered dependency in central Poland created in October 1939 under Hans Frank, used for labor, agriculture, and the deportation of populations.
Requisition
Compulsory demand for goods or services by an occupying force; the Hague Regulations limited requisitions to the needs of the army and the capacity of the country.
Economic exploitation
The extraction of resources, goods, finance, and labor from occupied territories for the benefit of the occupying power, going beyond the limits set by international law.
Forced labor
Work extracted by compulsion, including deportation of civilians to the Reich, compulsory labor service, and concentration camp labor; distinct from paid employment under economic pressure.
Collaboration
Cooperation with an occupier, by states, institutions, or individuals; analytically distinguished from ideological collaborationism and from accommodation, and carrying no single moral meaning without context.
Accommodation
Adaptation to occupation, such as continuing administrative or economic work and complying with regulations, in order to maintain order, protect interests, or survive; not equivalent to ideological collaboration.
Vichy France
The authoritarian French State led by Marshal Pétain from July 1940, which pursued its own National Revolution and cooperated with Germany, with its own agency in policy including antisemitic legislation.
Milice
A paramilitary police force created by the Vichy regime in January 1943 under Joseph Darnand, used against resisters and Jews and associated with torture and killings.
Nasjonal Samling
The Norwegian fascist party of Vidkun Quisling, the only legal party after 1940 and a minority movement without broad support.
Occupation police
German security organizations and the local police and auxiliary forces that carried out arrests, enforcement, and repression in occupied territories.
Underground state
A clandestine network of administration, courts, education, and armed forces that claimed the legitimate authority of a state in occupation, best illustrated by Poland.
Home Army (Armia Krajowa)
The principal Polish underground army, formed in February 1942 and loyal to the government in exile; the largest armed resistance organization in occupied Europe.
Clandestine press
Illegally produced and distributed newspapers, leaflets, and books that circulated news and political messages in defiance of censorship.
Special Operations Executive
A British organization created in 1940 to support resistance in occupied Europe through agents, radio operators, weapons, and training.
National Council of the Resistance
The French coordinating body created in May 1943 by Jean Moulin, uniting resistance movements, parties, and unions; adopted a program in March 1944.
Maquis
Rural groups of French resisters, many of them evaders of compulsory labor service, who lived in forests and uplands; the name derives from Mediterranean scrubland.
Partisan warfare
Irregular armed struggle behind enemy lines by units dependent on terrain and civilian support, as in Belarus, Yugoslavia, Greece, and Italy.
Sabotage
Deliberate damage to transport, communications, or production, ranging from railway demolition to worker slowdowns.
Intelligence network
A clandestine organization collecting information on enemy forces and installations and transmitting it to Allied services through couriers and radios.
Collective reprisal
Punishment of a community or group for acts attributed to others, including hostage executions and village destruction, prohibited by the laws of war and imposed by German forces as policy.
Night and Fog Decree
A directive of December 7, 1941 providing for the secret removal to Germany of certain political prisoners from western occupied territories, intended to intimidate by uncertainty; Nuremberg document L-90.
Rescue network
An organized group that hid, supplied, or moved persecuted people, such as Żegota; to be distinguished from individuals who acted alone and from political resistance.
Operation Tempest
The Home Army plan, ordered November 20, 1943, for local uprisings against German forces timed to the Red Army's advance in order to assert Polish authority.
Warsaw Uprising (1944)
The Home Army's rising against German forces in Warsaw from August 1 to October 2, 1944, ending in capitulation and the destruction of the city; distinct from the 1943 Ghetto Uprising.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because occupation was the condition under which most Europeans experienced the Second World War. Battles decided where armies stood, but occupation determined how millions lived, worked, hid, informed, protected, or were killed. By examining institutions as well as individuals, the chapter shows how state power operates when sovereignty is removed, and how much depended on the persistence of ordinary administrative routines.

The chapter also reveals the limits of simple moral categories. Collaboration, accommodation, resistance, and rescue overlapped, and the same institution could be an instrument of German policy in one month and a site of obstruction in the next. Recognizing these ambiguities does not weaken moral judgment. It makes judgment more exact, by requiring attention to what was done, with what knowledge, and under what constraints. The Holocaust, which Chapter 121 will examine, was carried out within this occupation system and with varied degrees of local participation, a reminder that the history of occupation cannot be separated from the history of genocide.

Within Volume V, the chapter connects the military narrative of the war to its political aftermath. The legitimacy claims of the Polish Underground State, the French National Council of the Resistance, the Yugoslav Partisans, and the Italian committees of national liberation shaped the postwar settlements that Chapters 123 through 125 examine. The memory of occupation, whether as a national myth, a contested trauma, or a suppressed history, influenced welfare states, European integration, and the politics of the Cold War, and it continues to affect public debate about collaboration and responsibility.

The chapter's larger lesson concerns the relationship among citizenship, state power, and violence. The collapse of parliamentary governments in 1940 and the ways in which their institutions were used or defended demonstrated that rights depend on structures that can be captured, and that political communities can survive in forms such as underground states and exile governments. Chapter 121 now turns to the genocide of European Jews and other victims, and Chapter 122 to the Allied campaigns that ended German rule.
