Democracy, Economic Crisis, and the Interwar Settlement

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 1918 many Europeans hoped that parliaments, universal suffrage, and international cooperation would replace the old dynastic order for good. Within fifteen years, an inflation that destroyed the value of money, a depression that threw millions out of work, and a politics of bitter polarization had broken that hope across much of the continent, though not everywhere, and not inevitably.

In November 1923, in the worst weeks of the German hyperinflation, the exchange rate reached roughly four trillion marks to the American dollar. According to contemporary accounts, prices were revised several times within a single day, wages were paid more than once a week so that they could be spent before they lost their value, and the paper money printed by the state became so cheap that banknotes were reportedly used as scrap. Nine years later, in the winter of 1931 to 1932, the problem in many industrial districts was the opposite one: money kept its value for those who had it, but there was not enough work. In Germany the number of registered unemployed approached six million by early 1933, in Britain it approached three million, and in agrarian southeastern Europe the collapse of grain prices pushed millions of peasant households into debt, hunger, and rural underemployment that no official statistic recorded. These two crises, one of monetary collapse and one of economic contraction, frame the history examined in this chapter.

This chapter asks why the democratic and international order created at the end of the First World War proved so fragile, and why its fragility took such different forms in different places. It examines parliamentary regimes that were new, contested, and often institutionally weak; the financial legacy of the war, including reparations, inter-Allied debts, and inflation; the uneven reconstruction of the later 1920s; the Great Depression and its transmission across Europe through banks, currencies, and trade; the social experience of mass unemployment; and the political polarization, paramilitary violence, and authoritarian turn that followed. It also asks how the settlement of 1919 to 1923 functioned in practice as an economic and diplomatic system, rather than only as a set of treaty clauses, a subject whose legal and territorial foundations were examined in Chapter 113.

The chapter begins from a warning against hindsight. It is easy to narrate the interwar years as a descent from Versailles to dictatorship, in which every event is read as a step toward 1933 or 1939. That reading misrepresents the evidence. Britain, France, Czechoslovakia, the Low Countries, Switzerland, the Scandinavian states, Finland, and the Irish Free State all preserved parliamentary government through the worst years of the Depression, and some of them reformed it. Other states had abandoned parliamentary rule before the Depression began. A large majority of European states had restricted or abandoned parliamentary government by 1939, though the exact count depends on how democracy is defined. The historical task is to explain both the breakdowns and the survivals, and to avoid treating either as predetermined.

The chapter also insists on distinguishing different kinds of crisis. Inflation, the collapse of banks, deflation and unemployment, agrarian indebtedness, constitutional deadlock, nationalist grievance, and political violence were related but separate problems, and they struck different regions at different times. The German hyperinflation of 1923 was largely over before the Nazi movement became a mass party, which should make historians cautious about the popular memory that links the two directly. The Depression, which began to affect Europe in 1929 and struck its most severe blows between 1930 and 1933, mattered far more for the electoral rise of extremist movements. Using exact dates and avoiding vague causal shortcuts is therefore a central discipline of this chapter.

The evidence for the period is unusually rich and unusually varied. Historians use election returns and parliamentary debates, cabinet minutes, central bank and treasury records, price and wage series, and the unemployment statistics compiled by governments and trade unions, all of which must be read critically, because registered unemployment excluded many workers and almost all of the agrarian underemployed. They also use newspapers, party programs, police reports, memoirs, diaries, novels, photographs, film, radio, and the early social surveys that sought to measure the human costs of unemployment, among them the study of the Austrian industrial village of Marienthal, published in 1933. Each of these sources represents a particular vantage point, and this chapter attends to whose experience each captures and whose it leaves out.

Within the larger architecture of the textbook, this chapter stands between the settlement examined in Chapter 113 and the authoritarian regimes compared in Chapter 115. It belongs to Part XVII, which traces the destruction of nineteenth-century European political stability and its troubled reconstruction, and to Volume V, which treats the era of world wars as a connected European catastrophe. The war economies, social upheavals, and imperial collapses of Chapters 110 to 112 produced the conditions examined here, and the dictatorships and the road to war examined in Chapters 115 and 116 grew from them. This chapter ends at the boundary of those developments, with the collapse of parliamentary government in Germany in January 1933, and leaves the construction of the Nazi dictatorship to Chapter 115.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the institutional forms of the new and restored parliamentary regimes of postwar Europe and identify the structural weaknesses that affected some of them.
  2. Explain how reparations, inter-Allied war debts, and wartime public borrowing shaped the postwar economies of Europe and the international financial system.
  3. Distinguish the causes, course, and social consequences of the inflations of the early 1920s, especially the German hyperinflation of 1923, from those of the Depression.
  4. Trace the uneven reconstruction of the years 1924 to 1929, including the Dawes Plan, the restoration of the gold standard, and the diplomacy of Locarno.
  5. Analyze how the Great Depression spread across Europe through the banking system, the gold standard, falling commodity prices, and trade protection.
  6. Compare the social experience of unemployment across industrial, agrarian, and minority communities, including differences of gender, age, and region.
  7. Assess why some European democracies survived the crisis while others gave way to authoritarian government, avoiding single-cause explanations.
  8. Evaluate competing interpretations of the collapse of parliamentary government in Germany between 1930 and 1933.
  9. Interpret a primary source on constitutional emergency powers and explain its significance for the history of the Weimar Republic.
  10. Contextualize the interwar settlement as an evolving economic and diplomatic system, including the role of the League of Nations and of revisionist and alliance politics.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did parliamentary democracy prove durable in some European states after 1918 and fragile in others, and which differences in institutions, social structure, and historical experience best explain the contrast?
  2. How did the financial legacies of the First World War, including debts, reparations, and inflation, shape domestic politics as well as international relations?
  3. In what ways did the stabilization of 1924 to 1929 conceal weaknesses that the Depression later exposed?
  4. Why did a downturn that began with falling prices and a financial crash in the United States become a European political crisis, and what role did policy choices play in spreading it?
  5. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct the lived experience of unemployment, and what limits do statistics and surveys impose?
  6. How did hyperinflation, depression, and political violence interact, and why should historians avoid treating one as a simple cause of another?
  7. Was the collapse of Weimar democracy the product of structural constraints, political decisions, or the interaction of both?
  8. How did the economic crisis alter the functioning of the interwar international settlement, including the League of Nations, reparations, and the politics of revisionism?

Historical Context

The war that ended in 1918 had been financed overwhelmingly by borrowing and by the creation of money rather than by taxation, a process examined in Chapter 110. Governments entered the postwar years with large internal debts, with wartime controls to dismantle, and with economies reorganized for munitions rather than for peacetime exchange. Britain and France had also borrowed heavily from the United States, which emerged from the war as the principal creditor of the Allied powers, while Britain was simultaneously a creditor of France, Italy, and others. Before 1914 the leading economies had been linked by the international gold standard, a system of fixed exchange rates that had supported a large volume of trade and lending. The standard had been suspended in the war, and the question of how, and at what rates, to restore it became one of the central economic questions of the following decade.

The political map had also changed. The collapse of the Romanov, Habsburg, Hohenzollern, and Ottoman orders, traced in Chapters 111 and 112, left new or reconstituted states in central and eastern Europe, among them Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria, Hungary, the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, together with an enlarged Romania, as examined in Chapter 113. These states faced the task of building administrations, currencies, and customs frontiers where an integrated imperial economy had stood. The Habsburg lands in particular had formed a large internal market in which the industrial regions of Bohemia and Austria had exchanged goods with the agricultural plains of Hungary and the Balkans. After 1918 that market was divided by tariffs and by competing national economic policies, and industrial Vienna in particular was left as the capital of a small state that many of its own leaders doubted could survive.

Politically, the war had greatly widened the franchise and the expectations of ordinary citizens. Universal male suffrage became general across the continent, and women gained the vote in Germany, Austria, Poland, Czechoslovakia, the Netherlands, the Scandinavian states, and Britain, though in Britain at first only for women over thirty. France, by contrast, did not enfranchise women until 1944, and Switzerland did not do so at the federal level until 1971, a reminder that the democratic expansion of 1918 was uneven. Mass parties of the left had grown, trade unions had won recognition in many countries, and the Russian Revolution of 1917, examined in Chapter 111, had split the socialist movement between social democrats who accepted parliamentary politics and communists who followed the new Communist International, founded in 1919. The political culture of the early 1920s was therefore marked by fear of revolution on one side and fear of counterrevolution on the other.

The revolutionary scare was not imaginary. Short-lived soviet republics appeared in Bavaria and Hungary in 1919, strikes and factory occupations swept northern Italy in the period known as the biennio rosso, and the Red Army advanced toward Warsaw in the Polish-Soviet War of 1920. In each case the defeat of the revolutionary movement was followed by repression, and the experience left durable networks of ex-soldiers, paramilitary leagues, and conservative officials suspicious of democratic politics. In the following sections these networks, and the failure of the new regimes to win the loyalty of all of their elites, form an important part of the explanation for what happened.

The evidence base for the economic history of the period includes official statistics that deserve careful handling. Unemployment series, for example, usually counted only workers registered with an employment exchange or insured under a benefit scheme, and so excluded many women, agricultural workers, the self-employed, and those who had exhausted their entitlement. Price indices were compiled differently from country to country, and national income estimates for the interwar years have been reconstructed by later economic historians. Historians therefore treat many interwar figures as approximations, and this chapter signals where a number is an estimate, a registered total, or a contemporary claim.

Division 1: The Postwar Democratic Experiment

Principal coverage: 1918–1923

Section 114.1

Europe after the War: The Burdens of Reconstruction

The European war ended through a sequence of armistices, revolutions, and military withdrawals rather than a single orderly reopening of civilian life. Railways had been overused and rolling stock requisitioned; soldiers returned to communities where food prices had risen faster than wages and where the civilian economy could not immediately absorb them. Coal distribution, damaged bridges, missing livestock, and the disruption of commercial credit hampered even countries whose towns had escaped direct bombardment. In devastated districts of northern France and Belgium, rebuilding meant clearing unexploded ordnance, restoring mines and drainage systems, and deciding who would pay for houses and factories. In Austria, Hungary, and the lands once linked by Habsburg transport networks, the obstacle was different: administrative borders and currencies now interrupted what had been routine interregional supply. These distinctions matter because reconstruction was both a physical undertaking and a political test of the newly proclaimed order.

The demographic consequences extended beyond the dead. Disabled veterans sought pensions and adapted work; widows defended household claims to compensation; children faced interrupted schooling and malnutrition. Spanish influenza had compounded wartime mortality, and epidemics, hunger, and disease remained serious in some eastern and central European districts after the fighting ceased. Refugees from changing borders, and people subsequently transferred or expelled by governments, enlarged the burden on housing and relief agencies. Charities, municipalities, veterans' organizations, ministries, and international agencies often worked at cross-purposes. A national budget might record pension expenditure as a fiscal liability, while recipients regarded it as a promised entitlement purchased by wartime sacrifice. The same payment could therefore be a macroeconomic constraint and an essential source of a family's food and rent.

A transition from military to civilian production required more than instructing armaments firms to make consumer goods. Contracts ended, inventories lost value, skilled workers shifted occupations, and exporting firms confronted unfamiliar tariffs or adverse exchange rates. Allied debtors were obliged to service foreign commitments; defeated states faced reparations and territorial losses; neutral countries confronted changed patterns of maritime trade. Governments were pressured simultaneously to reduce wartime controls and to keep prices and wages stable. Removing rationing too quickly could increase distress, while maintaining extraordinary regulation could be condemned as an attack on liberty. Postwar political legitimacy depended on choices about these competing demands, and the resulting conflicts provide the social setting for the constitutional debates that follow.

Reconstruction's distributional questions also determined whose recovery counted. A factory owner could consider renewed output a success while employees confronted longer hours and worse purchasing power; a land reform beneficiary might gain a plot but lack draft animals, cash, or access to grain markets. Former soldiers expected preference in employment even when women had sustained wartime industries. Racialized and colonial hierarchies constrained veterans and workers whose contribution to the war had not secured equal citizenship. The evidence is therefore not exhausted by aggregate production figures: historians compare wage books, municipal housing records, hospital admissions, school attendance, migration registers, and complaints to ministries to learn whether the postwar settlement brought practical security to different households.

Historical Vocabulary
Reconstruction and demobilization

Demobilization is the release and reintegration of armed forces into civilian life. Reconstruction includes repairing buildings and infrastructure, restoring peacetime supply, reconstituting governments and credit, and meeting the needs of veterans, widows, displaced people and damaged communities. These tasks depended on administrative resources and political legitimacy, not merely on ending military operations.

The terms should not be equated with economic recovery. A state could rebuild transport networks yet experience inflation and high unemployment; another might stabilize its currency while leaving rural poverty and housing shortages unresolved. Ask what is being reconstructed, for whom, and by which institutions.

Section 114.2

Parliamentary Democracy and the Promise of Political Representation

Mass political participation was not simply a constitutional label. Political parties had to enroll new voters, select candidates, finance local organizations, and translate speeches into workable coalition agreements. Trade unions, churches, veterans' associations, women's organizations, and newspapers supplied networks through which people learned to act as citizens. In newly sovereign states, the task was especially formidable: a voter who had once participated in an imperial assembly might now be asked to identify with a national constitution whose language and territory were disputed. School curricula and public holidays promoted national belonging, but ethnic, regional, and confessional attachments did not disappear. Democratic practice was therefore made in county offices, labor exchanges, factories, farms, and parish halls as well as in parliamentary chambers.

By 1920 most European states had adopted written constitutions that provided for elected parliaments and responsible ministries, but the forms of government varied widely. Britain, Belgium, the Netherlands, Italy, Denmark, Norway, and Sweden were parliamentary monarchies. France, Germany, Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and Finland were republics, though with very different balances between president and parliament: Germany gave its directly elected president extensive reserve powers, Finland a strong presidency, and Poland's constitution of March 1921 deliberately gave the president little power. Hungary was a kingdom without a king, governed by a regent, Admiral Miklós Horthy, from 1920. Ireland, after a war of independence from 1919 to 1921, a treaty with Britain in December 1921, and a civil war in 1922 to 1923, emerged as the Irish Free State within the British Commonwealth. The postwar democratic order was not one system but a family of different experiments.

The extension of women's suffrage exposed both change and continuity. German women voted in the January 1919 National Assembly election; British women voted under unequal age and property rules until 1928; France withheld national voting rights from women throughout the period under study. Even where voting was formally equal, parties varied in whether they nominated women or treated their claims to employment, childcare, and legal equality as central concerns. Parliament could also grant political rights while family law, professional exclusion, and customary expectations continued to restrict private autonomy. Constitutional comparison must therefore separate the franchise, eligibility for office, practical access to political institutions, and women's capacity to shape substantive policy.

Many of the new constitutions adopted proportional representation, which allocated parliamentary seats in rough proportion to votes cast. Germany, Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland, the Baltic states, the Netherlands, Belgium, and the Scandinavian states all used some form of it, while Britain retained single-member constituencies decided by plurality, and France experimented with different systems, including a mixed list system in 1919 that favored electoral alliances and a return to single-member districts in 1928. Proportional systems encouraged many parties and made coalition governments the normal outcome. Historians nonetheless caution against blaming proportional representation alone for democratic fragility: the Netherlands and the Scandinavian states used it and remained stable, while Britain's plurality system did not by itself prevent political upheaval. What mattered was how electoral systems interacted with the deeper cleavages of class, religion, region, and nationality, and with the readiness of party leaders to bargain.

Coalitions were not inherently pathological. When governments could negotiate budgets and distribute responsibilities across parties, proportional representation might incorporate competing groups and reduce the incentive to seek solutions outside parliament. Problems became serious when leaders regarded coalition partners as illegitimate, when a president or monarch could bypass elected bodies, or when armed organizations threatened opponents. Electoral rules interacted with constitutional design, the professional army, the administrative service, and political culture. Comparing a functioning Dutch or Scandinavian multiparty coalition with a German presidential cabinet makes more historical sense than assigning democratic collapse to the mere existence of several parties. The real question is whether losses at the ballot box could be accepted and whether officials would enforce the law impartially.

Historians reconstruct the working of these regimes from election returns, parliamentary debates, party programs, cabinet records, police reports, and the memoirs of politicians. These sources are rich on elite politics but thinner on the beliefs of ordinary voters, whose motives must often be inferred from patterns in the returns, from the party press, and from surveys of local politics. They also tend to emphasize the capitals, so that regional and rural political cultures, which were often decisive, can be overlooked.

Section 114.3

The Weimar Republic: Constitutional Promise and Political Vulnerability

The republic arose through defeat and revolution, but it also began with substantial electoral support. In January 1919 Germans chose a National Assembly through elections in which women participated nationally for the first time, and parties committed to the republican constitutional project secured a clear majority. Hugo Preuß's draft drew on competing models of parliamentary accountability, federal authority, social rights, and a popularly elected president. The constitution acknowledged freedom of expression, freedom of association, equality before the law, and obligations concerning work and welfare. Its extensive social provisions should not be ignored in narratives dominated by its emergency clause. They reflected the expectation that a legitimate postwar state would govern economic life as well as guarantee formal political liberties.

Most of these regimes inherited social and institutional foundations that had been shaped under monarchies. Civil servants, judges, army officers, and landowners of the old order continued, in most cases, in their posts, and the loyalty of these groups to the new republics was uncertain. In Germany the clearest evidence concerns the judiciary: the murders of the socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht in January 1919, the participants in the Kapp Putsch of March 1920, and the assassins of political opponents in the early 1920s, including the foreign minister Walther Rathenau in June 1922, were punished far more leniently when they came from the right than when political offenders came from the left. The republic existed, in other words, alongside a state apparatus that did not uniformly identify with it. Comparable patterns appeared in Austria, Hungary, and other successor states.

The constitutional design nonetheless created a potentially difficult relationship among the president, the chancellor, and the Reichstag. Article 53 authorized presidential appointment and dismissal of the chancellor and ministers; Article 54 required the confidence of parliament and provided that an explicit withdrawal of confidence obliged resignation. Article 48 allowed intervention when a German state failed to perform legal duties and exceptional measures against serious disturbances of public order, including temporary suspension of specified rights, but it also required immediate notification to the Reichstag and gave that body the power to revoke such measures. This was not a general authorization to suspend every constitutional safeguard. Its consequences depended on presidential behavior, cabinet countersignatures, parliamentary arithmetic, and political conventions that could not be permanently secured by printed text.

The Weimar Republic's constitution of August 1919 created a directly elected president, a chancellor and cabinet that required the confidence of the Reichstag, a proportional electoral system without a minimum threshold for entering parliament, and an emergency power in Article 48 whose later use is examined in the Primary Source Window below. The first governing coalition of Social Democrats, the Catholic Center Party, and the liberal German Democratic Party lost its majority in the election of June 1920 and never regained it. Thereafter governments were formed by shifting combinations, many were minority administrations, and the republic had roughly twenty cabinets before 1933. Presidents mattered: Ebert, a Social Democrat, used his powers to defend the republic, while Hindenburg, elected in 1925, was a monarchist and a symbol of the old order who nonetheless took the oath to the constitution and observed it, in his own fashion, until 1932.

Republican authority was also contested outside institutional design. The Kapp Putsch of March 1920, defeated through mass strike action and the refusal of parts of the bureaucracy to support it, illustrated both the vulnerability of the state and the ability of organized labor to defend it. Assassinations of republican figures, disparities in judicial treatment of political violence, the lasting prestige of wartime military leaders, and hostile nationalist newspapers undermined claims that all public officials identified with the constitutional order. Yet the Republic enacted legislation, held competitive elections, and administered a sophisticated industrial society for more than a decade. Its collapse was neither encoded in Article 48 alone nor guaranteed by the events of 1919; subsequent economic shocks and political decisions altered the balance among competing possibilities.

The Weimar Constitution of 11 August 1919, Articles 48 and 54, on Emergency Powers and Parliamentary Confidence

Creator: The German National Assembly, meeting in Weimar after the elections of January 1919 · Date: adopted 31 July 1919, promulgated 11 August 1919 · Type: A constitutional text, a legal and political document that established the structure of government of the German republic

Context: The constitution was drafted in the aftermath of defeat, revolution, and civil conflict, and it sought to balance parliamentary government with a strong, directly elected president who would serve as a stabilizing authority. Its drafters, who included the liberal jurist Hugo Preuss, were influenced by the fear of both revolutionary disorder and parliamentary paralysis. The text is in the public domain, and the passages below are summarized in paraphrase rather than quoted.

Paraphrase of Articles 48 and 54 Article 48 provided that if public order and security were seriously disturbed or endangered in the Reich, the president could take the measures necessary to restore them, using armed force if required, and could temporarily suspend a list of fundamental rights, including personal liberty, freedom of expression, freedom of assembly, and the secrecy of correspondence. The president was to inform the Reichstag without delay, and the measures were to be revoked at the demand of the Reichstag. Article 54 provided that the chancellor and ministers required the confidence of the Reichstag and had to resign if it was withdrawn by an express vote. This is a paraphrase of the sense of the provisions and not a translation of the exact wording.

Analysis: The source shows that the constitution contained two potentially competing principles: ministers answerable to the parliament, and a president with extensive reserve powers. In the early 1920s, Ebert used Article 48 many times, often to meet economic and political emergencies, and it was not in itself a device of dictatorship. In 1930 to 1932, however, the combination of the president's power to appoint chancellors and to dissolve the Reichstag with the use of decrees allowed governments to rule without parliamentary majorities. The text does not tell historians why presidents and chancellors chose to use these powers as they did, how much opposition the decrees encountered, or how citizens experienced them, and it was written before the particular crises of the early 1930s. It also reflects the viewpoint of an assembly of politicians, jurists, and party leaders and says little about the many Germans who had no part in drafting it.

Section 114.4

Austria and the Political Reconstruction of Central Europe

The Austrian republic inherited Vienna, the administrative and cultural center of a vanished multinational empire, but only a fraction of the territories that had provisioned its capital. Austria lost access to numerous industrial and agricultural regions and had to negotiate new commercial relations with successor states. In the early years, the question of whether a small independent Austria was economically viable was openly debated, and movements for union with Germany confronted treaty restrictions and the opposition of victorious powers. The loss of empire also changed the composition of the political community: German-speaking Austrians formed a small national state where imperial habits of bureaucracy, finance, and political patronage remained powerful. Economic crisis could not be disentangled from this profound contraction of territorial and political scale.

Germany was not alone. Austria suffered a severe inflation in 1921 and 1922 and received a League of Nations loan, with international supervision of its finances, under the Geneva Protocols of October 1922. Hungary stabilized in 1924 with a similar League loan, and Poland ended its inflation of 1923 with the reforms of Władysław Grabski and the introduction of the złoty in 1924. These League-supervised reconstructions exchanged financial assistance for fiscal austerity and external oversight, and they set a pattern in which small states' economic policy became dependent on the confidence of foreign creditors. The pattern was not limited to inflationary states: Greece, after the defeat of its army in Anatolia in 1922 and the forced population exchange agreed in January 1923, had to settle an estimated 1.2 million refugees from Asia Minor, eastern Thrace, and other areas, a task that required a League refugee loan in 1924. Meanwhile Britain pursued deflation, culminating in its return to gold, and France struggled with budget deficits that undermined the franc until 1926. Postwar financial instability thus took different forms in different societies, and each form had its own political consequences.

Austrian parliamentary politics developed around the Social Democratic Party and the Christian Social Party, which derived support from different social worlds and advanced rival visions of the republic. The Social Democrats dominated municipal government in Vienna and used housing, education, and public-health programs to demonstrate that democratic authority could improve daily life. Christian Social politicians had stronger networks in rural districts, Catholic organizations, and conservative milieus. Both operated within a competitive constitutional system, yet partisan language increasingly described opponents as dangers to civilization rather than legitimate rivals. The rise of armed political formations and the bloody July 1927 disturbances after an acquittal in a politically charged shooting case exposed the limits of civic trust well before the Great Depression.

Financial reconstruction brought this domestic contest into contact with international supervision. After rapid inflation, the 1922 Geneva Protocols associated Austria's stabilization with foreign lending and conditions placed on budgets and state administration. The League's arrangements helped restore confidence, but they also invited accusations that foreign oversight compromised sovereignty and that the social costs of adjustment fell unequally. Municipal Vienna's housing and welfare initiatives thus existed alongside a national government seeking fiscal discipline, and the resulting friction was political as well as financial. Comparing these programs avoids the false choice between a wholly failed republic and a uniformly successful laboratory of reform: Austria experienced important social innovation and increasingly hostile political polarization at the same time.

Finally, developments within the former Habsburg area did not follow one Austrian script. Czechoslovakia inherited major industrial assets and established comparatively durable multiparty arrangements; Hungary combined revisionist grievance with restrictive political competition; successor states struggled over railways, customs, currencies, minority claims, and agricultural markets. Trade barriers made it harder for enterprises whose suppliers or customers now lay across new borders. The Austrian case therefore introduces a wider interwar dilemma: political independence and democratic representation could be genuine achievements even when economic interdependence remained unresolved. Chapters 113 and 114 must be read together to distinguish the legitimacy of postimperial self-government from the fiscal and commercial hardships of putting it into practice.

Section 114.5

Democracy and Political Instability in Eastern Europe

Newly independent or enlarged eastern European states confronted problems that cannot be reduced to 'backwardness.' Many had been assembled from territories that used different administrative laws, rail gauges, legal codes, and tax systems. Poland incorporated lands formerly ruled by Russia, Prussia, and Austria; Romania governed populations from several imperial provinces; the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes had to reconcile competing understandings of monarchy, federation, and national identity. Establishing a civil service and defining citizenship while demobilizing armies demanded unusual administrative capacity. Electoral conflict over language and religion arose partly because the borders of the new states did not correspond neatly to the places where their populations lived. Minority protections negotiated in 1919 supplied some legal standards but did not automatically resolve local questions of schooling, landholding, or policing.

Czechoslovakia illustrates how cooperation could work. Its political system rested on a stable informal coalition of the leading parties, known as the petka or "five," and on the prestige of its first president, Tomáš Masaryk, who held office from 1918 until 1935. The state was nevertheless a multinational one, with large German, Slovak, Hungarian, and Ruthenian populations alongside the Czech majority, and its democracy coexisted with a continuing struggle over minority rights, a subject introduced in Chapter 113. Poland offers a contrasting case. Its first presidential election in December 1922 ended in the assassination of the president, Gabriel Narutowicz, days after taking office, and short-lived cabinets and sharp conflicts among parties and between ethnic groups weakened parliamentary authority until Marshal Józef Piłsudski seized power in a coup in May 1926 and established the authoritarian "sanation" regime.

Romania's agrarian reforms and enlarged territory increased the number of smallholders and voters, but its parties remained closely tied to patronage and the central administration. Under King Ferdinand and later the regency for the young Michael, the National Liberal Party and National Peasant Party struggled over the balance between national integration, redistribution, and executive intervention. In Yugoslavia, centralists and federalists clashed over whether a unitary parliament could adequately represent Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, and other communities. The assassination of Croatian deputies in parliament in 1928 preceded King Alexander's royal dictatorship in January 1929. These crises arose before the international Depression and had roots in disputes over the meaning of the state itself.

The Baltic republics varied substantially. Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania adopted different parliamentary and executive frameworks while conducting land reforms and building schools in national languages. Lithuania's December 1926 coup replaced competitive parliamentary government with a regime led by Antanas Smetona. Estonia and Latvia continued parliamentary life longer before their authoritarian turns in 1934, outside this chapter's central 1918–1933 narrative. Poland's May 1926 coup likewise occurred amid domestic instability before the worldwide financial collapse. By contrast, Czechoslovakia's coalition institutions retained more effectiveness despite nationality tensions and economic hardship. Comparative chronology prevents a false inference that the Depression produced every authoritarian regime at the same moment.

The historians' central question is not whether eastern European societies 'failed' a western standard. It is which coalitions could integrate farmers, urban workers, middle classes, national minorities, civil servants, and military officers into a government whose losses at election time would be accepted. Landownership, literacy, infrastructure, and the balance of regional versus central authority affected this process, but none predestined one outcome. A large agrarian population could support competitive peasant parties or provide the political resources of a restrictive regime. A strong bureaucracy could implement social policy or obstruct elected ministers. The region supplies multiple tests of theories that explain interwar democracy by political choices and institutional relationships rather than by geography alone.

Section 114.6

Britain and France: Established Democracies after Total War

Britain and France possessed institutions older than the peace settlement, but war placed new demands on their legitimacy. In Britain the Liberal Party's fragmentation opened space for Labour as a governing force, while Conservatives increasingly represented the dominant electoral organization of the right. Labour's first minority ministry in 1924 showed that political alternation could occur without a change of regime. Parliament confronted not only diplomatic questions and war finance but also Irish independence, coal-industry decline, housing shortages, and the costs of unemployment insurance. The political significance of these disputes lay in their management through elections, cabinet changes, and organized industrial action rather than through a permanent seizure of state power.

Britain and France, the victorious democracies, offered further variations. In Britain the Liberal Party declined after the war, the Labour Party rose, and in January 1924 Ramsay MacDonald formed the first, minority, Labour government, which fell within the year; the franchise was extended to women on the same terms as men in 1928. The country's flexibility owed much to a long tradition of parliamentary government, but it also faced the Irish crisis already mentioned, a coal industry in persistent difficulty, and the General Strike of May 1926, which ended after nine days without constitutional breakdown. In France the Third Republic experienced frequent changes of cabinet, with the Bloc National in office after 1919, the Cartel des Gauches after 1924, and Raymond Poincaré's government of national union from 1926, but the administrative state remained stable and the republic's institutions were not seriously challenged by an organized movement until the 1930s. French women were excluded from the vote throughout the period, a limitation on the very idea of democratic citizenship.

France's Third Republic relied on parliamentary coalitions that changed frequently without necessarily overturning the civil service or constitutional system. The right-wing Bloc National emphasized financial reconstruction, security, and German reparations; the left-wing Cartel des Gauches attempted fiscal and diplomatic adjustment; Poincaré's return in 1926 brought monetary stabilization. French governments had to finance rebuilding in devastated northern departments while coping with inflation, debt and concerns over German recovery. Parliamentary bargaining was often bitter, and hostile organizations existed outside the legislature, but governments still generally sought authority through constitutional procedures. Ministerial turnover alone is therefore a poor measure of regime instability: the meaning of a cabinet crisis differs when officeholders expect constitutional succession to continue.

Class conflict also tested the durability of older democracies. British coal employers demanded wage reductions and longer working hours against the background of depressed export markets and the return to gold at the prewar parity. The Trades Union Congress launched the General Strike in May 1926, bringing sympathetic workers from transport and other industries into the dispute; it ended the national stoppage after nine days, while the miners continued their struggle for months. No parliamentary government was overthrown, but the episode exposed large disagreements about the meaning of industrial citizenship and the state's powers during an emergency. In France, labor confederations, communist organizations, and socialist parties competed to represent workers whose political choices were also shaped by patriotism, religion, and local industries.

Comparison with newly formed states is illuminating but cannot yield a simple 'old democracies survive, new democracies fail' rule. Britain's first-past-the-post electoral system and France's parliamentary republicanism had different strengths and weaknesses, and neither protected every population equally. French women remained disfranchised, while colonial subjects faced unequal citizenship and coercive administration; British participation likewise reflected enduring divisions of class, empire and region. The crisis of 1931 subsequently generated a dramatic British cabinet realignment, and French polarization intensified later in the decade. Their resilience was an accomplishment of institutions and choices under pressure, not an absence of conflict or a declaration that their democracies had reached a final form.

Section 114.7

Italy and the Crisis of Liberal Parliamentary Government

Italy emerged from the war among the victorious states, yet its constitutional order faced an intense legitimacy crisis. Wartime mobilization produced debt, price increases, veterans' grievances, and uncertainty over employment. Nationalists described the peace as inadequate despite Italy's territorial gains, while socialists and trade unionists demanded more substantial reforms. The years 1919–1920, remembered as the biennio rosso, saw strikes, factory occupations, and rural agitation; employers and landowners increasingly funded armed groups that promised protection against socialism. None of this meant that a dictatorship was inevitable. Liberal parliamentary actors still possessed parties, newspapers, and opportunities for reform, but many elites began to prefer coercive solutions to negotiating a durable social settlement.

Fear of revolution shaped the political settlement. The short-lived Hungarian Soviet Republic of March to August 1919, led by Béla Kun, was followed by a violent counterrevolution and by the conservative authoritarian regime that Horthy led, which also introduced in 1920 the numerus clausus limiting Jewish admission to universities, the first major antisemitic legislation of postwar Europe. In Italy the strikes and factory occupations of 1919 and 1920 convinced many property owners that the liberal state could not protect them, a conviction that underlay the support for fascism examined in Chapter 115. The socialist movement divided: the social democratic parties, committed to parliamentary politics, competed with communist parties that accepted Moscow's leadership under the conditions set by the Comintern in 1920. This division, which began as a quarrel about the Russian Revolution, remained a structural feature of European politics until the mid-1930s.

Electoral change magnified the problem. The Catholic Popular Party, founded in 1919, brought a powerful new constituency into national politics, while the Socialists maintained broad working-class support and divided over revolutionary strategy. Liberal factions struggled to combine their local networks into an effective mass party. Fascist squads attacked union offices, socialist municipal administrations, and cooperatives, frequently operating with local tolerance or assistance from officials who feared the left. This erosion of the state's monopoly of force was not just background violence; it altered which citizens could meet, campaign, vote, or negotiate in freedom. A parliament may retain formal legal powers while organized intimidation narrows the choices available to its members and constituents.

In October 1922 Benito Mussolini's followers staged the March on Rome. King Victor Emmanuel III refused to approve a proposed state of siege and invited Mussolini to form a government on 30 October, even though the Fascists had not won a parliamentary majority. The appointment was a constitutional decision by the monarch under extraordinary coercive pressure, not a military conquest of Rome by a victorious army. Conservatives and some liberals expected that an alliance with the Fascist movement might contain labor radicalism and restore order; they badly underestimated its ambitions. Chapter 115 examines how this initial appointment was converted into a dictatorship, including the later destruction of opposition politics. Chapter 114's responsibility is to explain the liberal state's collapse before those institutions were remade.

The Italian case demonstrates a mechanism distinct from the financial crash of 1929. Here liberal authority weakened during the immediate aftermath of the First World War, amid fears of revolution, nationalist mobilization, fragmented elite leadership, and organized violence. Economic dislocation contributed, but politicians still made choices about police enforcement, the legitimacy of paramilitary groups, and whether to accommodate them in government. Comparisons with Weimar are useful only if differences remain visible: the Italian transition began earlier, occurred under a monarchy, and involved Fascist participation in office before a fully established one-party regime existed. Treating every interwar breakdown as an identical response to the Great Depression would erase these differences.

Section 114.8

Inflation, Public Debt, and the Crisis of European Money

Public debt and monetary instability need to be understood as relations among states, governments, banks, and households. Governments had financed the war through bonds, central-bank credit, and taxation in changing proportions; these methods distributed the burden differently. A pensioner holding fixed-income securities suffered when prices surged, while a debtor could repay obligations in depreciated currency. Exporters sometimes benefited temporarily from a cheaper exchange rate, but importers needed foreign currency for raw materials and food. If wages failed to keep pace with prices, a nominal pay increase could conceal a decline in purchasing power. It would be mistaken to assume that inflation made every worker poorer in the same proportion or that all industrialists gained. Outcomes depended on contracts, assets, occupation, and timing.

Inflation in Germany had begun during the war and continued in the early postwar years as governments covered deficits by borrowing from the central bank rather than by raising taxes. For some time the effects were mixed: exports were helped, unemployment was relatively low, and debtors, including many industrialists and farmers, saw the real value of their debts fall. The crisis became extreme in 1923. When Germany fell behind on deliveries of coal and timber, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr in January 1923, and the German government supported a campaign of passive resistance, paying the wages of the striking workers by printing money. Between the summer and November the mark collapsed, reaching roughly 4.2 trillion to the dollar by mid-November. The government of Gustav Stresemann ended passive resistance in late September, and a new currency, the Rentenmark, introduced in November and followed by the Reichsmark in 1924, restored stability together with a drastic program of budget cuts and dismissals.

Germany's inflation was especially severe because prewar fiscal institutions, wartime borrowing, postwar deficits, political uncertainty, and monetary creation interacted. The Ruhr occupation and state support for passive resistance accelerated an already unstable trajectory, rather than creating inflation from nothing. When the government ended passive resistance on 26 September 1923, French and Belgian troops did not immediately leave: the occupation continued until 1925. Monetary stabilization later in 1923, built around the Rentenmark and fiscal measures, therefore had to occur before the territorial dispute was fully resolved. The distinction between ending resistance and ending occupation is essential for understanding diplomatic bargaining and the political arguments of defeated Germany.

The social consequences were uneven and contested in their meaning. Those with fixed money incomes, such as pensioners, small savers, and holders of war bonds, lost most of their wealth, while owners of real assets, exporters, and debtors fared better, and wage earners faced rapidly eroding purchasing power until stabilization brought unemployment. Many middle-class Germans experienced the inflation as a collapse of the moral order, and an older historical tradition treated it as the origin of the republic's later political weakness. More recent work has questioned this linear account, noting that the electoral appeal of the Nazi movement collapsed after its failed putsch in Munich in November 1923 and revived mainly during the Depression, and that the long-term political damage of inflation was therefore more complex than popular memory suggests. What is clear is that in 1923 the republic nearly broke apart: the Reich government removed left-wing governments in Saxony and Thuringia by force in October, faced a separatist movement in the Rhineland and an unsuccessful right-wing putsch in Bavaria, and saw anti-Jewish disturbances in Berlin in early November in which the language of "profiteering" was used to blame minorities for economic distress. The significance of 1923 lay as much in the political memory it created as in its immediate political effects.

Austria, Hungary, and Poland experienced serious inflations of their own, but their fiscal institutions, reconstruction programs, and eventual stabilizations followed different sequences. League-assisted reforms in Austria and Hungary offered foreign loans and restored a degree of budgetary credibility under external conditions. Polish reforms under Grabski combined changes to taxation, central banking, and a new currency, although the subsequent stabilization also faced difficulties. These arrangements had social costs, especially when expenditures were reduced and public employees dismissed. Monetary confidence did not simply descend from outside experts: governments also had to persuade taxpaying publics that reforms would endure and that burdens would not be allocated exclusively to one social group.

Inflation differs analytically from the deflation of the early 1930s. Under inflation, the price level rises and fixed nominal claims lose purchasing power; under deflation, falling nominal prices and wages may increase the real burden of existing debts and weaken demand even as money retains or gains purchasing power. These conditions demand different explanations of business failure and household distress. Contemporary political actors sometimes employed the memory of hyperinflation to justify severe anti-inflation policies long after prices had begun to fall. Historians therefore distinguish the actual fiscal mechanics of 1923 from the political memory mobilized in later budget debates, rather than drawing a straight causal line from one crisis to dictatorship.

Historical Vocabulary
The Gold Standard

The gold standard was a system in which a country's currency was convertible into a fixed quantity of gold, so that exchange rates between participating currencies were fixed. Before 1914 it had operated as the monetary foundation of international trade and lending, and governments were expected to defend the parity of their currency, if necessary by raising interest rates and reducing spending. Its suspension during the war and its restoration in the 1920s, often at parities chosen for reasons of political prestige, produced a system in which currencies were tied to one another while economic conditions differed greatly.

The term is essential to this chapter because the commitment to defend a fixed gold parity shaped governments' responses to the Depression. Countries that held to gold had to accept deflation, with falling prices and wages and rising unemployment, in order to maintain their exchange rate, while those that left gold, beginning with Britain in September 1931, were free to lower interest rates and allow their currencies to depreciate. A large body of economic scholarship concludes that countries which left gold earlier generally recovered earlier, though the political and institutional reasons for staying on gold, including memories of inflation, remain an important part of the explanation.

Principal coverage: 1923–1929

Section 114.9

Reparations, the Ruhr Crisis, and International Financial Negotiation

The reparations settlement created obligations that were difficult to separate from the Allied debt problem even though the governments concerned argued over whether the two should be legally or morally connected. Britain and France had borrowed from American lenders and public authorities during the war; German payments were expected to help relieve reconstruction costs and fiscal pressure. The May 1921 London Schedule nominally stated 132 billion gold marks of German obligations, but its bond structure distinguished immediate payment expectations from additional contingent claims. Historians must therefore avoid treating the headline total as an annual demand or confusing nominal legal commitments with sums actually transferred. Chapter 113 addressed the treaty clauses; here the issue is how those claims entered public budgets, exchange markets, and electoral conflicts.

The financial settlement after 1918 depended on two interlocking sets of obligations. Germany owed reparations to the Allied powers under the Treaty of Versailles, a subject whose treaty basis is examined in Chapter 113. The Allied powers in turn owed war debts to one another and, above all, to the United States. Britain and France argued that their payments to America should be reduced or canceled if Germany paid less, while the American government insisted that war debts and reparations were separate matters. The London Schedule of Payments of May 1921 fixed Germany's obligation at 132 billion gold marks, but the schedule divided it into bonds of which only the first two series, together some 50 billion gold marks, were treated as realistically payable, with the remainder a political reserve. The reparations problem was therefore a financial dispute and a diplomatic one at once, and it tied the budget of each major state to those of the others.

By late 1922 disputes about deliveries and payments eroded confidence in the existing machinery. French and Belgian forces moved into the Ruhr in January 1923 after German defaults in scheduled deliveries, and German authorities supported passive resistance. Industry, workers, police, and administrators responded unevenly to occupation; resistance involved strikes, refusal to cooperate and a government commitment to financial support. These measures contributed to expanding fiscal deficits and currency collapse, but occupation also generated nationalist solidarity that could cross party lines. France had its own domestic political constraints and concerns about security, while Britain questioned whether continued coercion could yield a functioning German economy. The crisis thus revealed conflicting ideas of what enforcement meant: extracting payment, asserting treaty rights, or preserving a solvent debtor able to pay later.

Stresemann's decision to terminate official passive resistance in September 1923 was politically costly but did not amount to surrender of German claims against occupation. Restoring production and stabilizing public finance required negotiation over currency, revenue, and credit, while diplomacy sought to separate immediate economic restoration from longer-term security disputes. When the occupation continued into 1925, it became clear that ending monetary emergency was not identical with restoring full territorial administration. Political opponents could invoke that discrepancy in attacks on the republic, and politicians in France could contest any negotiated departure as weakening treaty enforcement. A complex solution required both economic adjustment and credible arrangements among governments, not merely a new promise to meet the old schedule.

Evidence for this crisis includes the Reparation Commission's decisions, diplomatic correspondence, parliamentary budget statements, German government decrees, and contemporaneous reports from the occupied districts. Each type of source must be read for institutional purpose. A French official might emphasize formal German noncompliance; a German municipal account might describe factory wages and relief expenditures; an international banker might calculate the external transfer capacity needed for foreign payments. None provides the whole story by itself. Students should contrast at least two such perspectives before concluding whether military enforcement strengthened or weakened the prospect of a durable reparations settlement.

Section 114.10

The Dawes Plan, Foreign Lending, and the Stabilization of Europe

The Dawes arrangements did not cancel reparations or erase the political conflict surrounding them. They adjusted annual payment expectations and associated payment with a new framework of fiscal and monetary supervision, alongside an external loan that signaled confidence to private creditors. New foreign borrowing financed municipal modernization, commercial activity, housing and industry, but obligations to foreign lenders could come due sooner than the investment projects generated revenue. This maturity mismatch was particularly hazardous when capital was borrowed in a currency different from a borrower's earnings. German recovery therefore linked domestic construction, taxation and production to decisions made by bankers in New York, London and elsewhere, where local German political conditions were only one consideration.

The Dawes Plan of 1924, prepared by an international committee chaired by the American banker Charles Dawes and adopted at a conference in London in August, reorganized the payment of German reparations on a more manageable and gradually rising schedule, reformed the Reichsbank, and arranged an international loan to Germany, most of it raised in the United States. The plan made possible a cycle of payments: American investors lent to German borrowers, Germany paid reparations to Britain, France, and other creditors, and these governments used the proceeds to service their war debts to the United States. The cycle worked only as long as American capital kept flowing, and from the later 1920s a large part of the lending to Germany was short-term, directed to municipal projects, housing, and industrial modernization, while the repayment obligations were not linked to Germany's export earnings. The system provided stability, but it rested on external credit that could be withdrawn.

The restoration of exchange-rate commitments was similarly uneven. Britain chose its prewar gold parity in 1925 and faced persistent competitiveness problems in established export industries; France stabilized at a lower parity following the 1926 political and financial crisis; Germany constructed a new currency and central-bank regime after its inflationary breakdown. Countries held different mixes of gold and foreign exchange reserves. A gold-exchange standard was attractive because it economized on metal reserves but dangerous because claims on foreign financial centers could be withdrawn together. Confidence, which appeared as a source of stability during expansion, could reverse quickly when a banking problem or adverse balance of payments threatened a currency.

Currency stability was also restored by a return to the gold standard. Britain returned at the prewar parity in April 1925, a decision of the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Winston Churchill, that left sterling overvalued and made British exports, particularly coal, more difficult to sell. France stabilized the franc under Poincaré in 1926, formally in 1928, at a level that was undervalued and helped French exports. By the end of the decade most major currencies were again tied to gold, and a small group of central bankers, notably Montagu Norman in London and Benjamin Strong in New York, cooperated to manage the system, with advice from the League of Nations Financial Committee. The restored system was in fact a gold exchange standard, in which many countries held part of their reserves in foreign currencies, and it was more vulnerable than the prewar system to shifts in confidence because the main reserve centers lacked the prewar financial dominance of London.

Reconciliation in international politics followed but did not perfectly track monetary stabilization. The Locarno agreements of 1925 committed several powers to arrangements concerning Germany's western frontiers and disputes; Germany entered the League of Nations in 1926. These achievements had limits, particularly because the eastern territorial settlement did not receive an equivalent guarantee. Diplomatic agreements also depended on domestic willingness to accept compromise. A citizen who saw factories reopening might favor international cooperation, while a nationalist could use the same agreements as evidence of continuing inequality. It is misleading to label the years 1924–1929 uniformly prosperous or apolitical: investment, consumer technology, labor negotiations, and cross-border conflicts unfolded together.

The appearance of normality concealed several asymmetries. Germany's reliance on American lending supported economic activity but increased exposure to a sudden withdrawal of funds; British unemployment remained chronic in coal and textile districts despite national improvement; agricultural exporters struggled with prices that had weakened before 1929. The 1929 Young Plan sought another reparations adjustment, while the Bank for International Settlements, established in 1930, was designed in part to administer the resulting arrangements. These reforms show that peacemakers recognized persistent problems even in periods described as recovery. The system could handle ordinary payments and negotiated revisions, but it had not been tested by a synchronized contraction of credit, output and international trade.

Section 114.11

Social Citizenship, Housing, Education, and Welfare Reform

The language of social citizenship developed because mass electorates increasingly expected government to protect a minimum standard of life. Welfare measures were neither invented in 1918 nor confined to socialist parties; prewar insurance institutions, church charity, municipal administration and trade-union mutual aid all formed part of their history. After the war, however, employment protection, pensions, health care, family allowances, housing and schooling became central subjects of national political competition. Governments confronted a practical problem: a benefit promised in law required a reliable tax base, administrative records and agreement about who qualified. In an inflationary or recessionary environment those conditions were difficult to maintain, and conflicts over the costs of social rights could threaten governing coalitions.

Welfare institutions expanded in the same years. Germany established a national system of unemployment insurance in 1927, Britain extended its unemployment insurance scheme, and many states expanded housing, pensions, and public health programs, often with local initiatives such as the municipal housing programs of socialist Vienna. These programs reflected the political weight of labor movements and the new expectations created by universal suffrage, and they also created fixed public commitments that proved difficult to finance when revenues fell. Women entered clerical and service employment in larger numbers and gained new legal and political rights in many states, though they continued to face wage inequality and cultural resistance, and the economic crisis soon challenged even these gains.

Germany's 1927 unemployment insurance legislation established a major nationwide framework for workers covered by the scheme, supported by employer and employee contributions. It institutionalized risk-sharing during periods of ordinary unemployment but proved financially vulnerable when job losses became unprecedented and prolonged. Britain had expanded its unemployment insurance after the war, yet eligibility rules and the treatment of workers who exhausted benefits became increasingly contentious. Other states employed different systems, ranging from contributory insurance to municipal relief. The existence of an insurance law should not be confused with universal coverage: domestic servants, small farmers, some casual laborers and people outside formal employment were frequently missed, and women often faced discriminatory assumptions about dependency and appropriate work.

Red Vienna offers an unusually visible study of municipal social reform. Under Social Democratic leadership, Vienna built substantial public housing, expanded health services and invested in education and recreation. The Karl-Marx-Hof, opened in 1930, became a symbolic example of the municipal program, but its significance was broader than one architectural project: housing could reduce overcrowding, support hygiene and demonstrate a municipal government's capacity to transform the lived environment. Financing arrangements, including local taxation, were politically controversial, and opponents regarded the program as an expression of partisan ambition. A balanced account therefore asks both what improvements residents received and how the political confrontation over municipal fiscal autonomy sharpened divisions between Vienna and conservative provinces.

The International Labour Organization, created in 1919 and linked to the League-era international order, introduced a different model of cooperation by assembling representatives of governments, workers and employers. Its conventions and recommendations addressed hours, employment conditions and social protection, but adoption and enforcement depended upon member states. Its existence demonstrates that international cooperation in the 1920s was not limited to guarantees of frontiers and debt negotiations. School reform, public-health campaigns, occupational safety, and municipal planning likewise reveal attempts to make democratic institutions meaningful beyond the polling booth. These innovations did not prevent the Depression, yet they shaped the choices available when social demands and falling state revenue collided.

Section 114.12

Labor, Employers, Class Conflict, and Industrial Compromise

Collective bargaining after 1918 occurred in an environment of enlarged unions, more powerful employers' organizations and anxieties about revolutionary politics. In some industries, workers had gained wartime recognition and expected it to continue; employers now faced lower demand, new competition and pressure to reduce costs. Disputes over the eight-hour day, wage scales and the authority of works councils became battles over the social meaning of the postwar order. Trade unions were not identical with socialist parties, and organized labor itself contained conflicting religious, political and craft traditions. A shop steward negotiating a wage schedule might seek an immediate contractual gain even if party leaders framed the same dispute as part of a struggle over the future of capitalism.

Prosperity in these years was real but uneven. In the industrial economies of Germany, Britain, France, and Czechoslovakia, firms adopted assembly-line methods, electricity spread, chemical and automobile industries expanded, and radio, cinema, and mass-circulation newspapers created a consumer culture, with its most visible centers in cities such as Berlin, Paris, and London. The success was partial. Britain's staple industries of coal, cotton, and shipbuilding remained depressed, and unemployment there stayed above one million throughout the decade. Germany never returned to full employment, with unemployment above one million even in the better years. In agriculture the situation was worse: world production of grain and other commodities exceeded demand, prices were falling from the mid-1920s, and the indebted peasant economies of eastern and southeastern Europe, which also faced tariff wars such as the German-Polish customs conflict that began in 1925, were already under stress before the Depression.

Britain's General Strike of May 1926 crystallized these dilemmas. Its immediate setting was the coal industry's pressure for longer hours and reduced wages, made more acute by international competition and sterling's high gold parity. The Trades Union Congress called sympathetic action by transport, printing and other workers, while the Conservative government prepared emergency supplies and portrayed the stoppage as a challenge to constitutional authority. The general stoppage ended after nine days without securing the miners' demands; miners continued their own strike for months and ultimately returned under unfavorable conditions. The episode exposed the unequal bargaining strength of employers, government and organized workers while showing that large-scale industrial conflict could occur without permanent destruction of parliamentary institutions.

France, Germany and the Low Countries experimented with different forms of consultation between labor, capital and government. Germany's Weimar Constitution articulated workers' participation, and German industrial relations involved agreements over wages and labor representation, but employers and unions repeatedly clashed over who would bear stabilization costs. In France socialist, communist and independent labor groupings did not automatically act together, and economic policy became entangled with debates about class conflict and national security. Some Scandinavian employers and unions developed habits of negotiated settlement that later supported broader social bargains; these practices were uneven and should not be presented as an already completed welfare-state consensus in the 1920s.

The social history of labor includes people absent from bargaining tables. Unorganized female clerical workers, domestic servants, agricultural laborers and casual dock workers often lacked the protection that industrial unions demanded. Migrant and minority workers could be treated as competitors in tight labor markets and excluded from certain occupations or union networks. Industrial relations were also geographically concentrated: the closure of a coal pit affected an entire town's shops and housing, while rural workers might be compensated through seasonal payments or access to land rather than a fixed weekly wage. A genuinely comparative labor history asks how workers organized and how institutions distributed the gains and costs of technological and financial change across these unequal settings.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with Unemployment, 1930 to 1933

In an industrial district such as the Ruhr, northern England, or the Bohemian borderlands, unemployment reshaped the daily rhythms of a household. Families reduced meals, sold or pawned household goods, fell into arrears on rent, and relied on relatives, neighbors, charities, and municipal soup kitchens. Garden plots and allotments allowed some to grow food, and informal exchanges of goods and services helped sustain neighborhoods. Public queues at the employment exchange became a defining sight of the period, and the national hunger marches organized in Britain in these years were a visible form of protest by the unemployed and their allies.

In agrarian southeastern Europe the experience was different. Peasant households were less likely to be unemployed in the formal sense, but the collapse of grain prices left them with debts that could not be repaid and cash incomes that disappeared, and they responded by reducing purchases, cutting consumption of what they sold, and in some regions losing land to creditors. Children left school early to work, and some families sent members to cities that offered few jobs. In every setting the effects are better documented for men's formal employment than for women's domestic and informal labor, which absorbed much of the crisis, and historians reading the records must keep in mind how much of the experience went unrecorded.

Section 114.13

Agriculture, Rural Poverty, and the Uneven European Economy

In much of interwar eastern and southeastern Europe, agriculture employed a majority of the population, but farms were neither economically nor socially homogeneous. Some districts had large commercial estates; others had families cultivating tiny plots, with labor partly supplied by relatives and livestock owned on credit. Land reform could increase ownership while leaving cultivators without adequate machinery, seed, fertilizer, roads or cooperative marketing arrangements. The price of grain on a distant export market mattered to a household even when that household rarely used cash for everyday subsistence, because taxes, interest and the purchase of tools had to be paid in money. Lower farm prices could thus translate into forced sales, postponed marriages and migration rather than into the unemployment registered by an urban labor exchange.

The agrarian world of eastern and southeastern Europe experienced political change through land reform. Romania, Czechoslovakia, the Baltic states, Poland, Yugoslavia, and Bulgaria all broke up or reduced large estates after the war, in part to satisfy peasant demands and in part to dispossess minority or foreign landowners, such as the Baltic German nobility in Estonia and Latvia. The reforms created many smallholders and strengthened peasant and agrarian parties, such as the Bulgarian Agrarian National Union, whose leader Aleksandar Stamboliyski was overthrown and killed in a coup in June 1923. But the reforms did not solve rural overpopulation, low productivity, and shortage of capital, and their results left the peasantry exposed when agricultural prices later collapsed. The agrarian question therefore connects the early postwar settlement to the crisis of the 1930s.

Agricultural distress began before Wall Street's crash. Wartime demand had encouraged the expansion of production outside Europe, and new transport links brought more grain onto global markets. As supply rose, European farmers faced falling prices while debt contracts and land taxes adjusted slowly. States attempted tariffs, price supports, credit programs and cooperatives, sometimes protecting producers at the expense of consumers who paid more for bread. Countries exporting grain confronted a further problem when trading partners restricted imports to defend domestic agriculture. The severe Depression intensified these pressures, but it did not create the underlying imbalance between farm production, rural purchasing power and external debt.

Political consequences varied. Bulgaria's agrarian movement attempted to organize peasants as a distinct political force, and its destruction in 1923 reflected conflicts over authority, class and the army. In Romania the National Peasant Party articulated demands for administrative reform and rural representation, while the state simultaneously contended with minorities and the legacy of territorial enlargement. In Poland land reform and regional integration were contested, and the German-Polish customs conflict impeded cross-border commerce. Peasant associations could support parliamentary bargaining or become receptive to authoritarian appeals promising stable prices, cheap credit and national solidarity. No direct line runs from a falling wheat price to a particular political ideology.

A rural comparison also clarifies why the Depression could be underestimated by national statistics. A factory that reduced its workforce created registered jobseekers; a farm employing the same family for fewer productive hours appeared unchanged in employment returns. Yet losses of cash income reduced demand for textiles, machinery, shoes and transport services, thereby transmitting agricultural weakness to towns. Historians use farm budgets, tax records, cooperative accounts, price series and local petitions alongside national output figures to reconstruct this interaction. Rural Europe was not simply a passive background to urban radicalization; it supplied voters, resources and political programs that influenced whether parliamentary compromise remained possible.

Section 114.14

Women, Families, Urban Life, and Mass Society

The social changes of the 1920s were often represented by metropolitan images: short hairstyles, cinema queues, department stores, radio receivers and newly independent wage-earning women. Such representations document real opportunities but also select for age, class and place. A shop assistant in Berlin or Paris might encounter new consumption and leisure cultures, while a peasant woman in Romania remained responsible for agricultural labor, domestic work, childcare and household provisioning. The new media extended the reach of advertising and political messages, yet radios were not equally affordable or accessible across Europe. Historians should use images of modernity as sources to analyze, not as evidence that all European households shared a single modern lifestyle.

The effects were shaped by gender, age, and region. The male breadwinner model of the family meant that unemployment threatened men's social standing as well as their income, while women, whose employment was often in lower-paid sectors such as domestic service and textiles, sometimes kept work while men lost it, which could create tension in the household. Governments and employers also targeted women's employment directly: campaigns against "double earners," meaning married women whose husbands also worked, led in Germany to dismissals of married women civil servants under measures taken in 1932, and in Britain the Anomalies Act of 1931 restricted married women's claims on unemployment insurance. Young people faced especially bleak prospects, with school leavers unable to find apprenticeships or work, and in Germany a voluntary labor service for the young unemployed was organized from 1931. Because borders were closing and overseas emigration, restricted by American immigration laws since 1924, was no longer an escape, the unemployed could rarely move to find work.

Women's formal rights expanded significantly in some countries after 1918, especially electoral participation and access to education or occupations. The legal and social consequences were incomplete. Many administrations pressured women who had worked in war industries to leave jobs for returning soldiers, professional advancement remained limited, and marital laws frequently assumed men's authority. Marriage and motherhood could be presented as patriotic duties in societies concerned about wartime losses and declining birth rates. Family budgets depended on unpaid labor and informal income that wage statistics seldom captured. These constraints help explain why an increase in women counted as voters did not always lead to a proportional increase in female officeholders or to equal economic rights.

Urbanization created new political questions about transport, sanitation, rental markets, recreation and the relationship between public and private space. Municipal authorities built housing estates or supported utilities and public schools, sometimes treating social planning as an expression of democratic duty. Modern architecture, the Bauhaus movement, and experiments in mass production promised functional environments, but public construction relied on tax receipts and borrowing vulnerable to downturns. Film, popular music, sports organizations and illustrated magazines changed patterns of leisure; churches and conservative groups responded with their own networks and campaigns. Cultural division often crossed economic class lines, and it is misleading to label every advocate of modern lifestyles politically democratic or every critic an enemy of parliamentary government.

When recession arrived, the household became an instrument of adjustment. Women searched for occasional domestic work, took in laundry, or reduced food and clothing expenditures; young adults delayed leaving their families; men who had assumed regular wages might confront prolonged unemployment. The study of everyday life is therefore essential to interpreting public-policy statistics. A reported decrease in registered employment cannot show who surrendered medical care, which children left school, or how debt was negotiated at local shops. Personal diaries, social investigations and welfare-case records can illuminate such choices, though their authors and subjects require careful attention to representation and consent. The Depression's social effects are best understood against these earlier changes in work, citizenship and urban life.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Parliamentary Government in Europe, 1918 to 1933

What Changed

  • The franchise expanded dramatically, with women voting in many states for the first time and mass parties of labor entering government
  • New states and constitutions appeared across central and eastern Europe, replacing four empires
  • The financial system of the gold standard, reparations, and war debts was rebuilt in the 1920s and then dismantled between 1931 and 1933
  • Several parliamentary systems gave way to military, royal, or conservative authoritarian regimes, and in Germany to a presidential regime and then to dictatorship
  • Governments assumed greater responsibility for welfare, employment, and economic management, and these responsibilities became politically explosive in the Depression

What Stayed the Same

  • Old elites, including officers, judges, officials, and landowners, retained great influence in many states
  • Agrarian societies in eastern and southeastern Europe continued to depend on low-productivity farming and on world grain prices
  • Britain, France, the Low Countries, and the Scandinavian states maintained long-established parliamentary traditions
  • Rivalry over frontiers, minorities, and security continued to shape international relations
  • Gender hierarchies in work and politics persisted despite legal gains, and sharpened in the crisis

Who experienced change most directly? Industrial workers, the lower middle class, and the peasantry of the indebted agrarian states experienced the economic shocks most directly, while the officials and officers of the old order often experienced greater continuity, a difference that helps explain their political choices. Why the pattern matters: The combination of rapid change in expectations and slow change in institutions and elites left many new regimes without secure loyalty from the groups with the most power to defend or undermine them.

Section 114.15

The Great Depression Reaches Europe

The Great Depression was not caused by a single stock-market event. American credit expansion and its reversal, weaknesses in the international gold-exchange system, banking fragility, structural agricultural overproduction and policy responses formed a complex international contraction. For some European borrowers, external lending was already becoming less available in 1928; the 1929 crash then intensified uncertainty and withdrawal. A German municipality might have used foreign loans for housing and utilities, while a bank financed longer-lived assets with short-term liabilities. When foreign creditors demanded repayment, the municipality's productive buildings could not be converted quickly into cash. This mismatch transmitted financial stress to investment, employment and public services even before local factories confronted falling export orders.

The depression that struck Europe after 1929 had several sources, and historians generally reject explanations that rest on a single event. Agricultural and raw material prices had been falling since the mid-1920s, American lending to Europe declined from 1928 as investors turned to the New York stock market, and the Wall Street crash of late October 1929 intensified a contraction already under way. The United States passed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff in June 1930, and other states raised their own barriers, so that the volume of world trade fell by roughly a quarter or more in the following three years and its value by considerably more. For Europe the immediate effect was the withdrawal of the American credit on which the stabilization of the 1920s had depended, and states and banks that had borrowed short-term faced demands for repayment that they could not meet.

The deflationary mechanism deserves particular care. A country committed to gold convertibility had to defend its exchange rate when investors withdrew funds. Raising interest rates, reducing domestic credit or cutting expenditure could slow reserve loss, but it also contracted demand at a moment of declining production. Falling prices increased the real value of debts fixed in nominal terms, forcing indebted borrowers to sell assets or reduce expenditure. Bank losses then damaged the credit system, feeding back into the real economy. Countries that abandoned gold eventually gained more room for monetary expansion, but timing, export structure and fiscal policy affected the pace of recovery. The international monetary mechanism neither eliminated domestic decisions nor made outcomes identical in every state.

The gold standard was the principal mechanism that carried the contraction from one country to another. A country that wished to defend its parity as capital flowed out had to raise interest rates and cut spending, which deepened its own slump, and falling prices increased the real burden of debts, whether of governments, farmers, or businesses. Banks, which held loans that were losing value and had often borrowed short-term themselves, became vulnerable to panic. In May 1931 the Creditanstalt, Austria’s largest bank and closely tied to industry across the former Habsburg lands, announced enormous losses, partly inherited from its absorption of another failing bank in 1929. The announcement and rescue of Creditanstalt failed to restore confidence; withdrawals and pressure on Austrian reserves spread financial alarm to Germany and beyond, although the institution was not simply liquidated in May 1931. In July the Danatbank in Berlin failed, the German government declared bank holidays and imposed exchange controls, and the Hoover Moratorium of June 1931 suspended intergovernmental debt payments for a year but could not halt the crisis.

Trade decline combined financial contraction with tariffs, quotas, and falling commodity prices. The Smoot-Hawley tariff of June 1930 became a symbol of protectionism, but agricultural prices had weakened beforehand and European restrictions were shaped by each state's domestic coalitions. A government supporting grain prices might protect farmers but raise food costs for urban consumers; a devaluation could help exporters but increase the cost of imported materials. Colonies and countries outside Europe suffered sharp declines in revenues from raw-material exports, which affected European trading firms and fiscal relations across empires. Interwar Europe cannot be understood as an isolated economic circuit, even when the political focus of this textbook remains European.

Historians distinguish the decline in the volume of goods exchanged from the often much larger decline in the monetary value of trade, which reflects falling prices as well. They distinguish registered unemployment from comprehensive labor-market distress and separate industrial-output indices from measures of living standards. An economic historian might calculate the timing of recovery from production data while a social historian emphasizes the longer persistence of unemployment in a particular mining region. Both accounts can be correct if their measures and populations differ. The chapter's chronology therefore follows credit, currencies, policy and households together instead of treating the October 1929 crash as a self-sufficient explanation.

Section 114.16

Banking Collapse, Mass Unemployment, and Social Emergency

The Austrian banking emergency became public in May 1931 when the Creditanstalt disclosed losses that threatened its capital. The bank had absorbed troubled institutions and held extensive industrial exposures, making a disorderly failure potentially catastrophic. It was rapidly recapitalized with support from the Austrian government, central bank and private owners; nevertheless, the disclosure prompted withdrawals and sustained pressure on monetary reserves. The episode is best described as a near-collapse and rescue that failed to restore confidence promptly, not as a simple liquidation on the announcement date. Austrian vulnerability intensified concern about debts, currency parity and foreign-credit exposures in other central European markets. The subsequent crisis in Germany was not caused by one bank alone: withdrawals, maturity mismatches, declining business profitability and political uncertainty interacted.

The scale of the depression varied by region and is easiest to measure in the industrial economies. German industrial production fell by roughly forty percent between 1929 and 1932, and the number of registered unemployed approached six million by early 1933, perhaps one worker in three among those covered by the statistics, with many more underemployed. Registered unemployment in Britain approached three million in early 1933, about a fifth of insured workers, concentrated in the north of England, Scotland, South Wales, and other regions of staple industry. Austria and Czechoslovakia also suffered severely, and in Czechoslovakia the heaviest unemployment fell on the German-speaking glass, textile, and porcelain districts of the borderlands, a fact of great political importance. Scandinavian economies suffered less and recovered earlier. In eastern and southeastern Europe, where most people lived on the land, industrial unemployment figures capture only a part of the crisis: the collapse of grain prices, combined with fixed debt payments, led to peasant indebtedness, falling consumption, and hunger in many regions.

The German banking panic deepened in July 1931, when Danatbank encountered severe trouble and the authorities imposed bank holidays and exchange controls. Emergency decisions aimed to prevent mass withdrawals and protect the payments system, but each intervention could also signal to foreign creditors that confidence had deteriorated. In Britain capital flight and a political struggle over budget policy contributed to sterling's departure from gold on 21 September 1931. The gold system was therefore fractured through a succession of national decisions rather than through a single international agreement to change monetary policy. Poland's later approach provides a further contrast: in April 1936 it imposed extensive exchange controls while formally retaining the złoty's gold parity; it should not be grouped without qualification with currencies that devalued their legal parities in 1935–1936.

For millions of Europeans the Depression was first of all a personal experience of lost work and reduced income. Unemployment insurance systems, where they existed, had been designed for short spells of joblessness, and they came under severe strain. In Germany the insurance scheme created in 1927 provided benefit for a limited period, after which the unemployed passed to a lower level of crisis relief and then to municipal welfare, and successive emergency decrees reduced benefits and shortened entitlement. In Britain, where the insurance system was older and wider, the National Government of 1931 reduced benefit rates and introduced a household means test for those who had exhausted their insurance entitlement, a measure deeply resented in the depressed regions. Many unemployed, especially women, the self-employed, and agricultural workers, received no benefit at all.

Social emergency followed the contraction of jobs, credit and tax revenues. Governments had designed unemployment insurance with assumptions about work interruptions that became untenable as the numbers without jobs surged. Relief rolls expanded even as the tax base contracted; local authorities reduced expenditure precisely when additional food, medical assistance and heating fuel were needed. Children experienced the consequences through nutrition and schooling, while young adults postponed entry into skilled occupations. Regions dependent on one industry were especially exposed: mining communities in Britain, export-oriented textile and glass districts of Czechoslovakia, and steel and manufacturing centers in Germany had fewer immediate alternatives than diversified cities. Aggregate national recovery could later conceal stubborn regional depression.

Minorities experienced the crisis in particular ways. Jewish communities, which in eastern central Europe were concentrated in trade and crafts, suffered from the general fall in demand and also from boycotts and discriminatory measures, including the restrictions on admission to universities in Hungary and, later in the decade, in Poland, as examined in Chapter 103 in the wider context of antisemitism. In Czechoslovakia the German-speaking minority of the industrial borderlands, hit hard by unemployment, turned increasingly toward a nationalist party, the Sudeten German Party, which had been founded in 1933 as the Sudeten German Home Front. In the Balkans, ethnic and religious divisions, including those between Orthodox Christians, Catholics, and Muslims in Yugoslavia and Albania, interacted with agrarian poverty and with the politics of the new states.

The Marienthal study, conducted after a textile plant in an Austrian industrial community had shut down by February 1930, brought social-scientific methods to these experiences. Marie Jahoda, Paul Lazarsfeld and Hans Zeisel's 1933 publication described altered routines, diminished associations and the slow reorganization of daily life around unemployment. Its findings should not be universalized to every European community: welfare provisions, local institutions, household resources and migration opportunities varied. Nor should the unemployed be represented as merely passive; political organization, mutual aid, informal work and family ingenuity persisted. Reading the study beside official employment figures demonstrates both the power and the limits of statistics and qualitative observation, particularly when historians reconstruct people whom state sources were not designed to see.

Historical Vocabulary
Banking panic and exchange controls

A banking panic arises when depositors or short-term lenders rapidly withdraw funds because they fear a bank cannot meet its obligations. Even a bank with valuable long-term assets may then be unable to obtain sufficient cash, while failures or near-failures spread uncertainty to other institutions. The 1931 Creditanstalt crisis involved emergency recapitalization and government support; it was not a simple liquidation on the announcement date.

Exchange controls restrict foreign-exchange trading, transfers or gold movements and are analytically different from a formal currency devaluation. Germany introduced emergency restrictions during the 1931 banking crisis. Poland imposed extensive restrictions in April 1936 but retained its official złoty gold parity; the distinction matters when comparing the responses of different European governments.

Division 5: Political Polarization and the Fate of European Democracy

Principal coverage: 1930–1933, with brief comparative epilogue

Section 114.17

Deflation, Austerity, and Competing Economic Responses

Austerity was never simply a technical deduction from an economic equation. Governments chose among policies under the constraints of monetary commitments, external debts, domestic political coalitions and the fear that investors would withdraw funds. In Germany Brüning's administration favored budget reductions, wage cuts and tax increases in order to defend fiscal credibility, maintain the currency and strengthen its position in reparations debates. These policies intensified a depression already underway, although historians differ over how much discretionary room the government had and which alternatives would have been politically feasible. A useful account holds together the foreign-exchange and public-finance pressures with the fact that particular burdens were deliberately placed on wage earners, welfare recipients and firms.

The states that preserved parliamentary government through the Depression did so by different routes. In Britain the Labour minority government of 1929, divided over how to reduce a budget deficit and satisfy foreign creditors, split in August 1931, and Ramsay MacDonald formed a National Government with Conservative and Liberal support, which won a large majority in an election in October. The government cut public spending, left gold in September, and in 1932 introduced general protective tariffs and a policy of cheap money. These measures, with a housing boom in the south and midlands, helped a gradual recovery, but long-term unemployment persisted in the older industrial regions. The politics of the period were conservative and cautious, and the British Union of Fascists, founded in 1932, remained a marginal movement.

Britain's 1931 crisis produced a different coalition and policy sequence. The Labour ministry divided over expenditure and foreign confidence; MacDonald's National Government adopted retrenchment, yet the departure from gold created greater monetary flexibility. Subsequent cheap-money policies and building activity helped recovery in some regions without eliminating prolonged unemployment in declining staples. The 1932 protective tariffs altered the country's commercial orientation. Thus 'austerity' is not an adequate description of the entire British response: initial budgetary measures were followed by monetary and trade changes with distinct distributional effects. Parliament remained operative throughout, although the politics of the National Government left Labour divided and the opposition unusually weakened.

Other democracies survived with great strain. France, committed to the gold bloc and slow to feel the Depression, experienced deflation and political instability, culminating in the riots of 6 February 1934 in Paris, when right-wing leagues and veterans' groups clashed with police outside the Chamber of Deputies, an event that mobilized the left and led to the Popular Front coalition, whose wider context is taken up in Chapter 116. Belgium and the Netherlands remained parliamentary despite the economic crisis and the emergence of small fascist movements. Czechoslovakia maintained its democratic system despite regional unemployment, nationality conflict, and the pressure of a neighboring Germany after 1933. In Finland, the anti-communist Lapua Movement, which used intimidation and violence between 1929 and 1932, was suppressed after the Mäntsälä revolt of February 1932, when the government and the president refused to give way, and the party system survived. The Irish Free State changed government peacefully in 1932 when Éamon de Valera's Fianna Fáil defeated the party that had governed since independence, though the new government soon entered a damaging tariff dispute with Britain.

France's attachment to gold and political disputes over deflation contributed to a slower, more difficult adjustment. French industrial and financial conditions differed from Britain's, and currency depreciation was long resisted; the delayed break with the old parity came in 1936, beyond this chapter's main endpoint. Scandinavian experiences also diverged. Sweden abandoned gold in 1931, and the Social Democratic–Agrarian agreement of 1933 combined social and agricultural measures; Denmark's Kanslergade compromise addressed currency, farm relief and social policy. These bargains did not arise from economists acting without politics. They reflected parties whose supporters had different needs but were prepared to exchange support for policies that could be defended to both constituencies.

Policy comparison must avoid claiming that one government possessed an effortless blueprint for recovery. Costs and gains varied by income group, region, and industry, and policymakers learned under conditions of imperfect knowledge. Some contemporaries worried that deficit spending would weaken confidence; others warned that cutting expenditure reduced purchasing power and further shrank tax receipts. Later scholarship emphasizes the powerful effects of leaving gold but also asks how political institutions shaped the timing of departure and the use of regained flexibility. Chapter 114 must therefore judge policy as a set of contested decisions with economic consequences, not as a timeless morality play in which every participant already knew the answer.

Section 114.18

Political Radicalization and the Erosion of Parliamentary Legitimacy

Political radicalization did not require every unemployed worker to become a supporter of an extremist party. Voters interpreted economic suffering through religion, class, regional identity, organizational loyalties, family history and the claims of party leaders. Some defended established socialist parties, others sought conservative stability, and others abandoned electoral participation altogether. The Nazi Party built an unusually effective mass organization and presented different themes to rural Protestants, urban middle-class voters, nationalist veterans and other constituencies, while the Communist Party won support among sections of the industrial working class. Election figures document changes in support, but a vote share does not disclose one uniform motive or establish that every voter approved every aspect of a party's future policies.

The German case, the most consequential, can be traced in detail. The last coalition government to command a parliamentary majority, a Grand Coalition under the Social Democrat Hermann Müller, fell in March 1930 over a dispute about the financing of unemployment insurance, a dispute between parties representing labor and business on how to share the burden of the crisis. President Hindenburg then appointed Heinrich Brüning, a Center Party leader, to head a cabinet that depended on his support and on the president's emergency powers rather than on a parliamentary majority. When the Reichstag rejected his budget, Brüning had it enacted by decree under Article 48 and the president dissolved the chamber. In the election of 14 September 1930 the Nazi Party increased its vote from 2.6 percent in 1928 to 18.3 percent, and the Communist Party also gained. Brüning's deflationary policy of budget cuts, wage reductions, and tax increases, meant to preserve the currency and demonstrate that reparations could not be paid, deepened unemployment, and the banking crisis of 1931 increased it further.

A central feature of the Weimar crisis was the erosion of parliamentary cabinet government before Hitler's appointment. From March 1930, presidents and chancellors increasingly used the constitution's exceptional mechanisms to govern without stable majority support. Brüning, Papen and Schleicher had different programs, but each relied on Hindenburg's authority amid an inability or unwillingness of parties to sustain normal cabinet coalitions. The July 1932 intervention against Prussia removed an important elected regional government and weakened a bastion of republican administration. The Nazi electoral peak in July did not automatically confer a majority: its vote declined in November, and Hitler still required decisions made by Hindenburg and conservative intermediaries to receive the chancellorship. These choices matter precisely because the system had not reached a mechanically predetermined outcome.

From 1930 to 1933 Germany was governed by what contemporaries and historians call presidential cabinets, which depended on the confidence of the president rather than of the Reichstag. Hindenburg, reelected in April 1932 with the support of the parties of the center and the Social Democrats against Hitler, who received about 37 percent in the second round, dismissed Brüning in May 1932 and appointed Franz von Papen, a conservative aristocrat with little party support. Papen lifted the ban on the Nazi SA, and on 20 July 1932 he used emergency powers to remove the Social Democratic-led government of Prussia, the largest German state, in what is called the Prussian Coup. In the Reichstag election of 31 July the Nazis won 37.3 percent, becoming the largest party, though they won less, 33.1 percent, in the election of November 1932; the Social Democrats and Communists together won more than a third of the vote, but they did not cooperate. General Kurt von Schleicher, who succeeded Papen in December, failed to build a stable majority.

Street violence changed the political environment in which constitutional decisions were made. The Nazi SA staged marches and intimidated opponents; communist and nationalist organizations engaged in confrontations; the republican Reichsbanner also organized defense of the parliamentary order. The Communist Red Front Fighters' League had been formally banned in 1929 but remained active underground, so descriptions of later confrontations must not imply that it operated as an unrestricted legal organization. Police, courts and administrative officials responded inconsistently, and political actors exploited fear of disorder to demand emergency powers. At the same time, the social democratic and communist split prevented sustained cooperation between two major working-class parties. Comintern rhetoric against 'social fascism' made that antagonism sharper during the crisis years.

On 30 January 1933 Hindenburg appointed Hitler chancellor of a coalition cabinet in which the Nazis held only three of eleven posts, with Papen as vice chancellor. Conservative leaders around the president, who believed that they could control Hitler and use his mass following for their own ends, had pressed for the appointment, and Hitler was named in accordance with constitutional forms. The decision belonged to a small circle of political and military leaders, some of whom feared a military clash if they chose otherwise and others of whom wanted to reduce the influence of the Social Democrats. The destruction of parliamentary government in practice, however, had occurred before this date, since the Reichstag had been bypassed for nearly three years, and the construction of the dictatorship, including the Reichstag Fire Decree of 28 February and the Enabling Act of 23 March 1933, belongs to Chapter 115.

The constitutional end point selected for this chapter is 30 January 1933, when Hitler became chancellor at the head of a coalition cabinet. The subsequent Reichstag Fire Decree, Enabling Act and consolidation of Nazi dictatorship are assigned to Chapter 115. That division is substantive: it allows students first to explain the social, economic and institutional circumstances that enabled an anti-democratic movement to enter government, and then to study the construction of the dictatorship on its own terms. The German experience should not swallow the European comparative inquiry, because authoritarian transitions in Italy, Poland, Portugal, Yugoslavia and the Baltic region occurred at different moments and by different mechanisms. Identifying those differences is indispensable to any explanation of Europe's interwar political transformations.

Section 114.19

International Cooperation and Its Economic Limits

International economic cooperation had produced real institutions in the 1920s, including League-sponsored stabilization programs, committees that gathered data, and arrangements for managing German payments. Yet each institution depended on national governments whose electorates and budgets did not necessarily share its priorities. The Hoover Moratorium announced in June 1931 proposed a one-year suspension of intergovernmental debt and reparations payments; it addressed immediate liquidity pressures but could not reconstruct confidence in banks or remove domestic opposition to foreign obligations. When the Lausanne Conference in 1932 proposed a reduced final reparations arrangement, the resulting settlement was not ratified as a definitive legal replacement. Its practical importance lay in the effective ending of transfers, not in a treaty that automatically entered into force.

The reparations system did not survive the crisis. After the Hoover Moratorium, a conference at Lausanne in June and July 1932 agreed in principle to replace Germany's remaining obligation with a single final payment, an agreement that was never ratified because the Allied governments linked it to a settlement of their own war debts. Between 1932 and 1934 most European debtors stopped paying their debts to the United States, and only Finland continued to pay in full. The collapse of the debt and reparations structure ended the Dawes cycle for good, and it left both the political arguments and the financial grievances behind it unresolved, a legacy that mattered for the diplomacy examined in Chapter 116.

Efforts to coordinate trade and monetary policy met comparable obstacles. States protected domestic agriculture and industry with tariffs, quotas or exchange controls, while governments that maintained gold parities were reluctant to endorse monetary expansion. The 1932 Ottawa agreements strengthened preferential trading relationships within the British Empire and Commonwealth rather than restoring an open multilateral system. Proposals for Danubian economic cooperation, including French efforts associated with the Tardieu Plan, confronted conflicting national fears about German influence and postwar sovereignty. The failure of the Austro-German customs-union proposal illustrates how a project framed as commercial integration could be understood by neighbors as a diplomatic challenge to territorial restrictions. The crisis exposed an institutional mismatch: finance operated across borders, while the political responsibility for unemployment and taxation remained overwhelmingly national.

The economic crisis also reshaped the interwar settlement as an international system. The Locarno agreements and the League of Nations had rested on the expectation of continued economic recovery and of Germany's integration into a revised order, and the Depression undermined both. The League's response to the Japanese seizure of Manchuria in 1931, with the Lytton Commission's inquiry of 1932 followed by no effective action, demonstrated the limits of collective security. The World Disarmament Conference, which met in Geneva from 1932, failed to resolve the dispute about German equality of rights, and the subsequent German withdrawal from the conference and League in October 1933 belongs to the developments assessed in Chapter 115. Economic nationalism, with tariffs, exchange controls, and bilateral clearing arrangements, replaced the multilateral trading order that the Dawes cycle had helped sustain, and the subsequent expansion of Germany’s bilateral clearing trade in southeastern Europe is a development treated in the following chapters.

The London Monetary and Economic Conference opened in June 1933, after the chapter's chosen German political endpoint. It is included only as a brief epilogue showing the direction of the international crisis: governments could not agree on exchange stabilization, prices and recovery policies, and the meeting adjourned without its hoped-for settlement. The U.S. administration's resistance to an immediate currency-stabilization plan contributed to the impasse, but European disagreements over gold, debts and trade also mattered. This event should not be inserted into the pre-January 1933 sequence as though it occurred before Hitler's appointment. Likewise, later German withdrawal from the League and changing bilateral trade arrangements are flagged here solely as subjects for the ensuing chapters.

The League's political work faced the same problem of limited enforcement, although financial assistance and humanitarian programs had achieved some successes. Public argument about Manchuria and disarmament exposed the gap between international inquiry and decisive joint action; specific military and diplomatic developments belong primarily to Chapters 115 and 116. What Chapter 114 contributes is the economic logic of these limits. Governments already struggling to provide employment and secure external credit found it harder to convince voters that obligations to distant institutions should prevail over immediate national priorities. The international settlement survived as a network of rules, conferences and claims while many of the conditions that had sustained cooperation were dissolving.

Section 114.20

The Interwar Settlement Assessed: Resilience, Breakdown, and Historical Contingency

The history of the interwar democratic experiment yields no single formula connecting economic depression to authoritarian government. Britain suffered profound regional unemployment but maintained parliamentary alternation; Czechoslovakia endured national tensions and severe distress without abandoning competitive politics during the period studied; Finland resisted an extra-parliamentary challenge even after an earlier civil war; Denmark and Sweden forged labor–agrarian agreements that broadened support for governing coalitions. Germany's constitutional order suffered from specific conflicts over executive power, a hostile conservative establishment, deep financial contraction and calculated elite accommodation of Nazi leadership. Italy's liberal collapse preceded the Depression and developed under a monarchy through paramilitary coercion. These outcomes are incompatible with an explanation that treats all European states as interchangeable units exposed to one economic shock.

Historians have proposed many explanations for these survivals. Earlier experience of parliamentary politics, as in Britain, Scandinavia, and the Low Countries, provided habits of bargaining and legitimacy. Stronger state capacity, higher incomes, and more diversified economies helped. Cross-class alliances, particularly between labor and agriculture, broadened support for governments. The status of victor or neutral spared some states the humiliation and the territorial grievances that fueled revisionist nationalism. But no one of these factors determines the outcome, and the exceptions are instructive: Czechoslovakia was a new state with a multinational population, and Finland had endured a civil war in 1918, yet both preserved parliamentary government for the time being. The best explanations therefore stress the interaction of institutions, social alliances, elite choices, and the specific timing and severity of crisis in each state.

Economic institutions nonetheless set powerful boundaries around political choice. The international debt system encouraged states to connect domestic fiscal policy to foreign transfers; credit dependence made banks and local authorities vulnerable to sudden withdrawal; gold parities could transmit deflation; agrarian debt widened suffering beyond the official unemployment totals. Recovery in the later 1920s was neither fictional nor universally shared, and its real achievements created expectations that became politically painful when reversed. Social-insurance systems and municipal welfare programs enlarged democratic citizenship but faced funding problems precisely when they were most needed. A valid synthesis recognizes these achievements and contradictions together rather than writing the entire decade as a preface to catastrophe.

Political institutions and leaders mediated the effects of those constraints. Electoral systems mattered, but so did whether competitors accepted losses, whether courts prosecuted violence impartially, whether generals respected civilian authority, and whether presidents used exceptional powers as temporary instruments or alternative foundations of government. In some countries bargaining between workers and farmers sustained coalitions; elsewhere fear of revolution pushed elites toward coercion. Political violence could distort the field of constitutional choice without abolishing all formal rules. The central lesson is not that well-designed institutions are invulnerable, but that their survival requires continuing relationships of legitimacy, administrative responsibility and social compromise.

Historical comparison should be disciplined about chronology and evidence. What voters believed in 1922 cannot be inferred mechanically from 1932 election returns. Germany's inflationary catastrophe and its deflationary depression had distinct causes and distributional consequences. The 1931 Creditanstalt crisis was an emergency rescue accompanied by panic, not simply a completed liquidation. Poland's later exchange controls did not constitute the same formal devaluation as those carried out elsewhere. A refugee count, an unemployment rate and an industrial-production index each refer to different populations and measurement practices. These distinctions may seem small, but they determine whether a causal account rests on evidence or merely on retrospective association.

The chapter ends by posing a problem rather than declaring inevitability. Europe's reconstructed political order supported democratic representation, substantial social reform and international experiments in cooperation, yet it also permitted deep exclusion and harbored formidable opponents. The Depression accelerated some breakdowns while other democracies demonstrated that coalition building and policy adaptation remained possible. Chapter 115 now examines the ideologies and systems of Fascism, Nazism, Stalinism and other authoritarian governments, keeping their differences in view. Chapter 116 then follows the international crises that destroyed the remaining peace. Understanding the interwar settlement requires holding these chapters together without allowing later outcomes to erase the alternatives that contemporaries actually faced.

Cause and Effect

The Banking Crisis of 1931 and the End of the Gold Standard System

Long-term causes

The war debts and reparations system, the dependence of central and eastern Europe on short-term foreign credit, the weakness of the restored gold exchange standard, the fall of commodity prices after the mid-1920s, and the fragility of banks tied closely to industry and to governments.

Short-term causes

The decline of American lending, the Wall Street crash, the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, the revelation of the Creditanstalt's losses in May 1931, political tension over the proposed Austro-German customs union of March 1931, and loss of confidence in the German government's finances.

Immediate effects

Bank runs and failures in Austria, Hungary, and Germany, bank holidays and exchange controls, the Hoover Moratorium, and, in September 1931, Britain's departure from gold.

Political consequences and alternatives

The formation of competing currency blocs, the end of reparations, rising protectionism and economic nationalism, deeper unemployment in countries that held to gold, and political polarization that weakened democratic governments.

Historical complexity: The crisis cannot be reduced to a single cause. It combined structural weaknesses of the international financial system, specific bank failures, and political decisions about whether to defend gold parities or to depreciate, and historians still debate the relative weight of each. The same crisis had different consequences for creditors and debtors, for exporters and importers, and for countries that left gold and those that did not.

Comparative interpretation: The same cross-border contraction contributed to Weimar parliamentary collapse but did not extinguish British, Swedish, Danish, Finnish or Czechoslovak democracy by January 1933. Explain the roles of executive power, coalition bargaining, policing and party leadership before attributing political outcomes to banking crises alone.

Historical Debate

Was the Collapse of Weimar Democracy Avoidable?

The question: Did the structural constraints on German economic policy and the weaknesses of the constitution make the collapse of parliamentary government in 1930 to 1933 close to inevitable, or did political decisions by Brüning, Hindenburg, and the conservative elites open or close alternatives?

Interpretation A: Constraints severely limited alternatives. The economic historian Knut Borchardt argued in 1979 that the Weimar economy was weighed down by wage and welfare burdens, reparations, and dependence on foreign credit, and that the memory of inflation and the need to preserve credibility left Brüning little room for expansionary policy. On this reading the government's deflationary course was a response to real constraints, and the republic's political order could not survive a depression of such severity.
Interpretation B: Choices mattered and alternatives existed. Other economists and historians, including Carl-Ludwig Holtfrerich, argued that Brüning had more room for maneuver than he claimed, that earlier abandonment of the gold standard, a public works program, or an earlier settlement of reparations were possible, and that conservative elites, including Hindenburg and his circle, chose not to defend the constitution and to bring the Nazis into government. Political historians have also emphasized the failure of the Social Democrats and Communists to cooperate and the strategic miscalculations of the conservatives.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on cabinet records, the files of the Reichsbank and finance ministry, price and trade statistics, and the papers of politicians. They differ on the counterfactual question of what policies were feasible, a question that cannot be settled by records alone. Many historians now combine elements of both: severe structural constraints existed, but the choices made within them, and the political decision of January 1933, were not predetermined.

Why it matters: If constraints were decisive, then the collapse of democracy appears as a tragedy of circumstance. If choices mattered, then responsibility lies in part with identifiable decision makers, and the case becomes a study in how elites weaken the institutions they claim to protect. The debate also affected later policy, since postwar German and European economic institutions were shaped in part by competing lessons drawn from the 1920s and 1930s.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence would help determine whether Brüning had meaningful alternatives. Then explain why a decision on the question does not by itself explain the appointment of Hitler.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the fragile democratic order that followed the First World War and the economic crises that tested it. The collapse of four empires left a continent of new and reconstituted states with new constitutions, wider franchises, mass parties, and powerful fears of revolution and counterrevolution. These regimes inherited old elites, war debts, and divided economies, and their institutions varied widely: some gave presidents great powers, others little, and electoral systems and party cleavages differed from state to state.

The financial legacy of the war created an interlocking system of reparations and inter-Allied debts that tied domestic politics to international relations. The London Schedule of 1921, the occupation of the Ruhr in 1923, and the resulting German hyperinflation showed how monetary and political crises could reinforce one another. Inflations in Germany, Austria, Hungary, and Poland redistributed wealth, shaped political memories, and left several states dependent on foreign loans and supervision, while the settlement of refugees in Greece showed the links between population movement and economic stress.

Between 1924 and 1929 an apparent stabilization took place, built on the Dawes Plan, the restoration of the gold standard, and the diplomacy of Locarno. It produced real growth, new industries, and expanded welfare systems, but it rested on short-term American credit and on exchange rates that were poorly adapted to conditions in particular countries. Agriculture and the older industries were in difficulty throughout, and the eastern and southeastern European peasantry was already under strain. Authoritarian regimes were also taking shape in this period, in Poland, Lithuania, Portugal, Yugoslavia, and elsewhere, a reminder that the crisis of democracy did not begin with the Depression.

The Great Depression, intensified by the decline of American lending and the collapse of commodity prices, spread through Europe by way of the gold standard and the banking system. The Creditanstalt and Danatbank crises of 1931 turned a recession into a financial catastrophe, Britain left gold in September 1931, and the reparations system collapsed in 1932. Unemployment approached six million in Germany and three million in Britain, and rural indebtedness spread across agrarian Europe. The scale and timing differed by region, and the statistics understate the crisis in agricultural societies.

The social experience of the crisis was shaped by class, gender, age, and region. Unemployment insurance systems were strained, benefits were cut, and many people were excluded from relief altogether. Studies such as that of Marienthal documented resignation, while other sources record solidarity, shame, and radicalization. Women faced hostility as "double earners," young people lacked prospects, and minority communities, from Jews in eastern Europe to Germans in the Czechoslovak borderlands, experienced the crisis through the lens of the national and religious tensions of the new states.

Political responses varied widely. Britain's National Government relied on retrenchment, protection, and cheap money; the Scandinavian states built cross-class alliances between labor and agrarian parties; France held to gold, with political instability; and Czechoslovakia and Finland preserved democratic government under severe pressure. Historians explain these survivals through a combination of earlier parliamentary experience, state capacity, social alliances, and elite choices, and they caution against treating any single factor as decisive.

In Germany, the last parliamentary coalition fell in March 1930, and governments dependent on the president and on Article 48 ruled until January 1933. The Nazi share of the vote rose from 2.6 percent in 1928 to 37.3 percent in July 1932, while the left remained divided between social democrats and communists. The appointment of Hitler on 30 January 1933 resulted from decisions by a small circle of conservative leaders, in a context shaped by economic crisis and by the political choices of the preceding three years. The historical debate about whether alternatives existed shows how constraints and choices must be considered together.

The interwar settlement, finally, was eroded step by step. The League of Nations failed to respond effectively to the Manchurian crisis, the Disarmament Conference foundered, Germany left the League in October 1933, and economic nationalism replaced the multilateral order of the 1920s. The questions of frontiers, minorities, and security remained unresolved, and they form the background of the dictatorships and the road to war examined in Chapters 115 and 116.

A ministry supported by two or more political parties, often necessary when no party commands a legislative majority.

Broad acceptance that public authority should be exercised under established and recognized constitutional rules.

Exceptionally rapid, accelerating price increases that undermine confidence in a currency.

A general decline in the price level that can raise real debt burdens and depress spending.

Monetary framework in which states maintained gold-linked exchange rates and could hold reserves partly in foreign currencies.

An official reduction in the external value or parity of a currency relative to gold or another reference unit.

Legal restrictions on foreign-currency transactions, gold movements or cross-border transfers.

The claim that membership in a political community entails access to social protection and opportunities beyond voting.

Negotiation between employers and workers’ representatives over pay, working conditions or related contractual matters.

Coordinated work stoppage across multiple industries, often in support of a wider industrial or political demand.

Joblessness on a scale exceeding ordinary labor-market turnover and overwhelming established relief systems.

Rapid withdrawal of deposits or short-term funding driven by fears that a bank will not meet its liabilities.

Policy aimed at retrenchment of public spending, deficits or monetary expansion, often during financial stress.

An organized political or social force using military-style discipline and tactics outside the regular armed forces.
Political polarization
An intensification of antagonism across political groups that can make ordinary compromise and shared rules difficult to sustain.
Authoritarianism
Government that significantly restricts political competition and accountability without necessarily following one uniform ideology.

The principle that outcomes depend on particular conditions and choices and need not be treated as predetermined.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can be weakened by the interaction of economic shocks, institutional fragility, and elite choices, without any one of these being sufficient alone. The democratic hopes of 1918 were not an illusion, and they were not simply defeated by an unavoidable fate: many democracies survived, and in several places the crisis produced reform and new forms of social bargaining. Yet the same crisis, in states with weaker institutions and deeper grievances, opened the way to authoritarian rule.

The chapter also reveals how closely the history of states and societies was bound to the international financial system. Reparations, war debts, gold parities, and bank credit tied the domestic politics of Vienna, Berlin, London, Paris, and Budapest together in ways that few citizens understood, and the collapse of that system in 1931 to 1933 shaped the world that followed. It connects the western and eastern halves of Europe, since the agrarian crisis of the Danubian lands and the industrial crisis of Germany and Britain formed parts of a single process, and it links Europe to the wider world through the American credit cycle, the sterling area, and the colonial economies affected by the collapse in commodity prices.

Several of these patterns outlasted the period. The memory of inflation shaped German and later European attitudes toward monetary stability, the memory of mass unemployment shaped postwar commitments to welfare and full employment, and the Scandinavian bargains of the 1930s provided models for later social democratic politics. The lessons that later generations drew from the interwar years were not always accurate, and the historian's task is to distinguish what the evidence shows from the simplified memory built around it, a task that matters again when later chapters turn to the Marshall Plan and the origins of European integration.

For Volume V, this chapter forms the bridge between the war and its settlement and the era of dictatorship, renewed war, and genocide. It shows the conditions in which the regimes examined in Chapter 115 came to power and the diplomatic landscape within which the events of Chapter 116 unfolded, without treating either as predetermined. Chapter 115 now turns from the crisis of democracy to the authoritarian systems that emerged in its wake, comparing Fascist Italy, Nazi Germany, and Stalin's Soviet system, and distinguishing carefully among them.
