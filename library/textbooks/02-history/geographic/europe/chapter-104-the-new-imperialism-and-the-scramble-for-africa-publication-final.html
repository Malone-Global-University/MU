The New Imperialism and the Scramble for Africa

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On March 1, 1896, near the town of Adwa in the highlands of Tigray, an Ethiopian army defeated an invading Italian force that had been sent to turn Ethiopia into an Italian protectorate. The victory did not stop the partition of Africa, but it proved that the partition was contested, that European armies could lose, and that the outcome of conquest was decided by African as well as European choices.

The Battle of Adwa is a revealing place to begin a chapter on European imperialism, because it reverses the usual order of the story. Most accounts of the "Scramble for Africa" begin in Europe, with a conference table in Berlin, a map spread across it, and diplomats drawing lines. This chapter begins instead with an African army in the field. The Ethiopian forces assembled by Emperor Menelik II, with the political and logistical support of Empress Taytu Betul, were larger than the Italian columns that advanced against them under General Oreste Baratieri. Sources and modern estimates differ on exact numbers, but historians agree that the Ethiopian army was equipped with large quantities of modern rifles and some artillery, that it had been mobilized across much of the empire, and that it was supplied and commanded with considerable skill. The Italian force, which included many Eritrean soldiers serving in colonial units, suffered a catastrophic defeat, with several thousand killed and many more wounded or taken prisoner. In October 1896 Italy signed a treaty recognizing Ethiopia's independence.

Adwa was exceptional, and this chapter does not suggest otherwise. Between roughly 1870 and 1914, European states and chartered companies claimed, invaded, and administered almost the entire African continent. At the start of that period, European territorial control was concentrated in a few zones: French Algeria, the British Cape Colony and Natal, French trading posts in Senegal, Portuguese coastal holdings, and scattered commercial enclaves. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia retained recognized independence, and even those states faced severe external pressure. The speed and scale of this transformation make the Scramble one of the most consequential episodes in the history of both Africa and Europe. It reshaped African political authority, land tenure, labor, trade, and daily life, and it altered the diplomatic relations, finances, ideologies, and self-images of the European states that carried it out.

This chapter is nevertheless a chapter in a history of Europe, and it explains why European governments, investors, missionaries, military officers, and publics pursued empire with such intensity after 1870. The motives were several: industrial interest in markets, raw materials, and investment; strategic concern for the Suez Canal, the Nile valley, and sea routes; the competitive prestige politics of newly unified Germany and Italy and of the established powers; humanitarian and religious claims about ending slavery and spreading Christianity and "civilization"; and racial ideas of the kind examined in Chapter 103. Yet the chapter's historical center of gravity remains Africa. Its subjects include sovereign states such as Ethiopia, Asante, Dahomey, the Sokoto Caliphate, Buganda, and the Mahdist state; commanders such as Samori Touré, Béhanzin, and Mkwawa; religious leaders and communities who built resistance movements; soldiers, porters, interpreters, and clerks without whom colonial armies and administrations could not have functioned; and the farmers, herders, workers, and households who bore the costs of conquest.

Three distinctions organize the entire chapter and are not mere qualifications. First, partition was not conquest. European powers could claim territory by agreement with each other, but converting a claim into control usually required years of military campaigns, negotiated alliances, and administrative expansion. Second, African societies were not politically empty territories. They contained kingdoms, empires, federations, city-states, commercial networks, and religious institutions with their own histories and rivalries, and European expansion was imposed on those existing orders. Third, the Berlin Conference did not draw Africa's modern borders. The conference of November 15, 1884, to February 26, 1885, established rules for commercial access, navigation, and the notification and legitimacy of certain European claims, but the boundaries themselves were negotiated bilaterally, mostly afterward, and no African representative took part.

The evidence for this history is unusually uneven, and the chapter pays attention to that unevenness. European treaties, parliamentary papers, military reports, missionary letters, travel narratives, photographs, and company records survive in abundance, but they were produced by those who conquered, and they often use euphemisms such as "pacification," "protection," and "civilization" to describe violence and dispossession. African perspectives survive in oral traditions recorded in later decades, in Arabic and Ge'ez manuscripts, in royal regalia and works of art, in letters written by African rulers and educated intermediaries, in testimony collected by missionaries and investigators, and in the physical record of destroyed towns, mass graves, railways, and plantations. Historians reconstruct the Scramble by reading these sources against one another, asking in each case who produced the source, for what audience, and whose voice is missing.

The chapter belongs to Part XVI, "Europe in the Age of Empire," and to Volume IV, which traces Europe's transformation through industry, revolution, and global empire. It builds on the industrial and technological capacities explained in Chapters 93–95 and 101, the national rivalries produced by the unifications of Chapter 100, and the racial ideologies of Chapter 103. It leads to the broader global imperial order of Chapter 105, the comparison of European political systems in Chapter 106, and the pre-1914 international crises of Chapter 107. The principal account closes in 1914, and the wartime fate of the colonies belongs to Chapters 108 to 110. Neither European conquest nor the later end of colonial rule, examined in Chapter 128, should be read as inevitable. Both were the outcomes of contested events.

Historical Context

Africa in 1870 was a continent of immense political and cultural variety, and any summary risks flattening it. It included large states such as the Ethiopian highland empire, the Sokoto Caliphate in the central Sudan, the Asante kingdom in the forest and savanna of the Gold Coast hinterland, the kingdom of Dahomey, the Zulu kingdom and its successors in southeastern Africa, the Buganda kingdom north of Lake Victoria, and the Sultanate of Zanzibar with its commercial and political reach along the East African coast and into the interior. It also included societies without centralized kingship, such as many communities in the forests of Central and West Africa, as well as Muslim scholarly networks, Christian communities in Ethiopia, long-distance trading diasporas, and pastoral peoples across the Sahel and eastern Africa. Egypt was an autonomous Ottoman province under a hereditary governor, the khedive, who had his own imperial ambitions up the Nile.

European involvement before 1870 was real but geographically limited. Britain held the Cape Colony and Natal and had coastal forts and trading interests in West Africa. France had invaded Algeria in 1830 and conquered it over decades of violent warfare, and held Senegal's coastal and river posts. Portugal retained old coastal holdings in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea. Beyond these, European merchants traded at African ports on terms that frequently depended on African rulers and merchant elites. As earlier chapters on Atlantic slavery and the European economy show (see Chapters 68 to 73), European states had long profited from the trade in enslaved Africans. Britain's abolition of its slave trade in 1807 and the later suppression campaigns changed the Atlantic commerce, but they did not end slavery. Enslaved labor expanded within Africa, in plantation agriculture and in the caravan trade, and the shift toward "legitimate commerce" in palm oil, peanuts, ivory, and rubber often increased demand for coerced labor.

European expansion inland was constrained by several factors that began to change in the nineteenth century. Tropical diseases, particularly malaria, killed European soldiers and officials in large numbers until the regular use of quinine prophylaxis spread after the mid-century. Geography and transport costs limited movement, since many rivers were interrupted by rapids and few regions had draft animals suited to long overland carriage. African states commanded large armies, and several acquired firearms through commerce. Most importantly, European governments had little incentive for the costs of territorial administration while trade could be conducted from coastal enclaves. By the 1870s this calculation was shifting. Steamships, the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, telegraph cables, breech-loading rifles, and later the Maxim machine gun reduced European costs and risks, while industrial and strategic competition raised the perceived stakes. The boundaries of what European states judged feasible and necessary moved, and the Scramble followed.

Students should keep four terms apart throughout the chapter: sovereignty (recognized political authority), commercial influence (trade leverage without political rule), claim (a statement of title, usually directed at other European powers), and administration (actual government, taxation, courts, and police). The Scramble was largely the process of turning the second and third into the fourth, and that conversion was often slower, more violent, and more incomplete than maps suggested.

Section 104.1

Africa before the Scramble: Sovereign States, Commercial Networks, and Existing European Enclaves

Any account of European conquest must begin with what the conquerors encountered. Africa in the 1870s was not a blank space awaiting political organization. It was a patchwork of states, empires, federations, towns, religious communities, and trading networks with long and often turbulent histories of their own. The political formations discussed in this chapter were of many kinds. Ethiopia was a Christian highland empire with an ancient written tradition, a powerful monarchy, and a regional aristocracy whose competing ambitions had been brought under imperial control in the later nineteenth century. In the central Sudan, the Sokoto Caliphate, founded in the early nineteenth century after the jihad led by Usman dan Fodio, governed a large Muslim state organized into emirates, with scholarly networks, markets, and an economy that rested heavily on enslaved labor. On the Gold Coast, the Asante kingdom, with its capital at Kumasi, commanded a well-developed bureaucracy and army and a sophisticated tradition of political authority symbolized by the Golden Stool. Dahomey, its ruler served by a large standing military, dominated the trade of the Bight of Benin hinterland. The Edo Kingdom of Benin had an old and elaborate court tradition. Buganda, north of Lake Victoria, was a centralized kingdom whose rulers, the kabakas, managed the arrival of Muslim, Protestant, and Catholic missionaries with considerable political skill. In southern Africa the Zulu kingdom, built in the early nineteenth century, still ruled a large territory under Cetshwayo until 1879. In Katanga the Yeke kingdom of Msiri controlled the copper and ivory trade. In Sudan, the Mahdist movement that would soon build a state was only about to emerge.

Other societies were organized differently. Many communities, in forests, river basins, highlands, and grasslands, governed themselves through councils of elders, lineage heads, age-set systems, religious specialists, or confederations of villages, without a centralized king. European observers frequently misread such societies as "stateless" or "tribal," terms that concealed their institutions, and colonial administrators later often invented chiefs where none had existed. Historians therefore use terms such as "kingdom," "state," and "chiefdom" cautiously and with attention to local evidence.

These societies were linked by dense commercial networks. Trans-Saharan caravans carried salt, gold, cloth, copper, and enslaved people between the Sahel and the Mediterranean. On the eastern side of the continent, the Indian Ocean trade connected the Swahili coast and its interior routes to Arabia, India, and beyond. The Sultanate of Zanzibar, with clove plantations worked by enslaved laborers, dominated this trade and extended influence along caravan routes into the interior. On the Atlantic coast, European merchants had for centuries traded manufactured goods, firearms, and textiles for gold, ivory, and enslaved people, and after the suppression of the Atlantic slave trade shifted toward palm oil, peanuts, and other goods. African merchants, such as the Duala and Efik middlemen of the Bight of Biafra, were not passive partners; they exercised commercial and political power and defended their positions against European traders trying to reach the interior directly.

The Atlantic slave trade's abolition by European states, a process examined in relation to Chapter 70, did not end slavery in Africa or the exploitation of African labor. Enslaved labor expanded within Africa in the nineteenth century, in Sokoto's plantation economy, on Zanzibar and Pemba, and in the production of "legitimate" commodities. European involvement in "legitimate commerce" often depended on these arrangements. Later colonial governments would claim to be suppressing slavery while introducing compulsory labor of their own, a contradiction examined repeatedly in this chapter.

European territorial holdings before about 1880 were concentrated and limited. In North Africa, France had invaded Algeria in 1830 and spent decades suppressing resistance, notably that of Abd al-Qadir, while settling European colonists. In West Africa, France held Saint-Louis and Gorée and had extended influence along the Senegal River under Louis Faidherbe in the 1850s and 1860s. Britain held Sierra Leone, Lagos (annexed in 1861), forts on the Gold Coast that became a formal colony in 1874, and, in the south, the Cape Colony and Natal, alongside the independent Boer republics of the Transvaal and the Orange Free State. Portugal retained coastal footholds in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea, with limited effective control inland. Liberia, founded by African American settlers and independent since 1847, was an anomaly: a state governed by a settler minority over indigenous populations.

Why had European territorial rule remained so limited? Several factors operated together. Tropical diseases killed Europeans in very large numbers, especially in West and Central Africa. Geography limited movement: many rivers were navigable only in segments, and in much of Africa the tsetse fly prevented draft animals from surviving, so goods were carried by human porters. African states and societies fielded armies that Europeans could not easily defeat, as Asante demonstrated in the 1820s, and European governments had little incentive to bear the costs of administration when trade could proceed from coastal enclaves. Historians therefore emphasize that the later conquest was not a natural result of European power at any moment but of changing conditions, in which technology, cost, and perceived competition altered what European states were willing to attempt.

Comparing African Political and Colonial Maps, c. 1870, 1885, and 1914

Using a historical atlas that distinguishes African states from European claims (for example, those listed in the Documentary Source Guide), students should compare three maps and note carefully what each shows and does not show.

  • c. 1870: European holdings concentrated in Algeria, the Senegal River region, the Cape and Natal, and scattered coastal posts. Locate Ethiopia, Sokoto, Asante, Dahomey, Buganda, the Zulu kingdom, Zanzibar's mainland influence, and Egypt's extension into Sudan. Notice that a map of "African states" in 1870 omits many societies without centralized monarchies.
  • c. 1885: Locate the new German protectorates in Togoland, Kamerun, and South West Africa; the French protectorate in Tunisia; the British occupation of Egypt; the claimed Congo Free State; and the Mahdist state in Sudan. Notice how many "claimed" areas were colonial claims on paper rather than territories under administration.
  • c. 1914: Locate the British, French, German, Belgian, Portuguese, Spanish, and Italian territories, and the two independent states, Ethiopia and Liberia. Notice that boundaries were often straight lines drawn through regions poorly known to the negotiators, and that the 1914 map also conceals large differences in how fully each territory was governed.

Historians often say that roughly one tenth of Africa was under European rule around 1870 and nearly all of it by 1914. Such round figures depend heavily on how "control" is defined, and they should be treated as approximations.

Section 104.2

Why the Scramble Accelerated: Economic Interests, Strategic Rivalry, National Prestige, and Imperial Ideology

No single motive explains the acceleration of European expansion in Africa after about 1870, and historians have long disagreed about which motives were most important (see the Historical Debate in Section 104.20). The first set of explanations concerns economic interest. Industrial economies needed raw materials, from palm oil used in soap and lubricants to rubber, cotton, copper, and later minerals, and hoped to find markets for manufactured goods such as textiles and hardware. The long depression in prices that affected much of Europe from the 1870s encouraged merchants and manufacturers to seek protected markets, and some, particularly in Germany and France, supported tariffs and colonial claims. Investors sought opportunities beyond Europe, and financiers backed railways, mines, and plantations. The discovery of diamonds and gold in southern Africa in 1867 to 1886 drew enormous capital. Yet it is important not to exaggerate. In the early 1880s, much of tropical Africa promised little immediate profit, trade with the continent was a small share of European commerce, and many companies lobbying for claims were speculative and undercapitalized. Expectation often mattered more than established returns.

Strategic considerations were equally important. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 turned Egypt and the Red Sea into the main route to India and the East for Britain, and British governments came to see control of the Nile valley as essential to protecting that route. Britain's concern about the Cape route and the Nile helped drive its interest in Egypt, Sudan, and Uganda. France, after its defeat by Prussia in 1871, saw colonial expansion as a way of restoring national standing and linking its North African, West African, and Equatorial holdings. Strategic anxiety became self-reinforcing: each power's move seemed to require a response, and claims made to forestall a rival were later defended as vital interests.

A third factor was national prestige. The unification of Germany and of Italy (see Chapter 100) introduced new competitors, and public opinion, colonial societies, geographical societies, and the popular press made overseas possessions into symbols of national greatness. Bismarck was initially reluctant to take on the costs of colonies, but in 1884 he reversed course, partly in response to commercial lobbying by Hamburg and Bremen firms and partly for domestic and diplomatic reasons. Italy, late in unification and conscious of its weaker position, sought a colony in the Red Sea region for status. For Portugal, defending historical claims in Angola and Mozambique became a matter of national pride, which explains the sharp reaction to Britain's ultimatum of January 1890 that forced Portugal to abandon plans to link the two colonies across central Africa.

Ideological arguments gave these interests moral and intellectual language. Missionary societies, Catholic and Protestant, argued that expansion would open Africa to Christianity, and the campaign against the East African and Saharan slave trades, strongly promoted by figures such as the Catholic cardinal Charles Lavigerie and by British humanitarians, became a persuasive public justification for intervention. The idea of a "civilizing mission," present in French and British public rhetoric, held that Europeans had a duty to bring law, commerce, and Christianity to peoples they described as backward. These ideas drew on the racial hierarchies discussed in Chapter 103, which this chapter treats as an available justification rather than reexamining. It is essential to distinguish public justification from outcome. Governments that claimed to suppress slavery frequently imposed compulsory labor, taxation in labor, and dispossession of land, and several of the worst episodes of colonial violence examined later in this chapter were committed by regimes that publicly described themselves as humanitarian.

Motives also differed among states. Britain was driven primarily by strategic concern for Egypt and the Nile, by commercial interests on the Niger and in southern Africa, and by pressure from settlers and chartered companies. France combined strategic ambitions in West and North Africa, a desire to restore national standing, and the activism of military officers in the field, who sometimes expanded French claims in advance of Paris's instructions. Germany sought commercial footholds, then prestige, and eventually settler territory in South West Africa. Belgium's case was unusual because the initiative was the personal project of its king, Leopold II, not of its government. Portugal aimed to preserve and extend old claims with limited resources. Italy sought status and Red Sea influence. The Scramble was thus not a single plan. Strategic anxiety, commercial lobbying, local conflicts, and decisions by colonial officers on the spot repeatedly changed policy and often committed home governments to expansion they had not originally intended.

Technology reduced the cost of expansion without causing it. Quinine prophylaxis lowered mortality among European personnel, steamships and river steamers extended reach along the coasts and rivers, telegraph cables improved communication, and breech-loading rifles and later the Maxim machine gun, which entered service in the 1880s, gave colonial forces firepower advantages in particular battles, such as Omdurman in 1898. But these advantages were neither universal nor decisive in every case. Ethiopia, Asante in earlier wars, and the Mahdist state at Khartoum in 1885 defeated European or Egyptian-led forces, and colonial victories depended heavily on the labor and military service of African soldiers, porters, interpreters, and allies.

Historical Vocabulary
New Imperialism, Colonialism, Protectorate, and Sphere of Influence

New Imperialism is the term historians use for the intensified European, and later American and Japanese, territorial expansion of roughly 1870 to 1914. The word "new" signals a contrast with earlier overseas empires, though many historians note substantial continuities with older commercial and colonial systems. Colonialism refers to the political, economic, and cultural domination of one society by another through settlement or administration. A protectorate was a legal arrangement in which a state claimed to protect a territory while the local ruler formally continued to exist; in practice it ranged from near-sovereignty to full colonial control. A sphere of influence was an area in which a European power claimed exclusive political or commercial rights as against other powers, usually without formal annexation or administration.

These terms matter because the same territory might be described differently at different times and by different parties. A treaty might create a protectorate on paper, and administration might follow years later, if at all. Students should not assume that a protectorate or sphere of influence meant effective rule.

Section 104.3

Explorers, Missionaries, Merchants, Chartered Companies, and the Instruments of Expansion

European expansion rarely began with armies. It began with expeditions, missions, trading firms, and companies whose activities created information, claims, and conflicts that governments later adopted. Geographical expeditions were central to the public image of Africa in Europe. David Livingstone, the Scottish missionary and explorer, traveled across southern and central Africa from the 1840s, and after his death in 1873 his African companions Chuma and Susi carried his body and papers for months to the coast, an act that made publication of his journals possible. Henry Morton Stanley, a Welsh-born journalist, met Livingstone at Ujiji in 1871 and later traveled across the continent from east to west in 1874 to 1877 and then worked for Leopold II in the Congo. Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza, an Italian-born French naval officer, explored the Ogooué and signed a treaty in 1880 with the Teke ruler known as Makoko that France later used to claim the north bank of the Congo.

Students should be careful with the language of "discovery." The rivers, lakes, and trade routes these Europeans described were already known to African societies, who had named, traveled, and traded along them for centuries. What Europeans produced was a new body of printed maps, narratives, and claims that circulated in Europe and made African regions legible to European governments and investors. These expeditions depended entirely on African labor and knowledge. African guides, interpreters, porters, soldiers, and merchants, often numbering in the hundreds for a single expedition, carried the goods, negotiated passage, and mediated relations with local rulers. Stanley's expeditions, for example, relied on large caravans of porters and soldiers recruited largely on the coast of East Africa, and many of them died.

Missionary societies were a second instrument. Protestant organizations such as the Church Missionary Society and the London Missionary Society, and Catholic orders such as the White Fathers, established stations across the continent, translated Scripture into African languages, opened schools and clinics, and sent home reports that shaped public opinion. Missionaries were not a single force. Some criticized colonial abuses and sided with African communities, and others welcomed colonial protection. In Buganda, rival Protestant, Catholic, and Muslim factions linked to different European and Arab-Swahili networks became deeply involved in the kingdom's politics, with consequences examined in Section 104.15.

Chartered companies were a third and especially important instrument. Governments reluctant to bear the costs of administration granted commercial associations the right to claim territory, make treaties, collect revenue, and maintain armed forces. The Royal Niger Company, chartered in 1886 under George Goldie, controlled trade on the lower Niger and Benue rivers and claimed administrative authority in much of what became northern Nigeria. The Imperial British East Africa Company, chartered in 1888, held territory in what is now Kenya and Uganda before running into financial difficulty. The British South Africa Company, chartered in 1889 under the influence of Cecil Rhodes, acquired the right to administer lands north of the Limpopo. The German East Africa Company, led by Carl Peters, was authorized to administer territory on the East African mainland. Leopold II worked through a series of associations, culminating in the International Association of the Congo. These companies were not governments, but neither were they only businesses. They blurred the line between commercial enterprise and territorial administration, and when they proved unprofitable or provoked uprisings, governments took over, as in Northern Nigeria in 1900, in British East Africa in 1894 and 1895, and in German East Africa, where the imperial government assumed direct administration in 1891 after the coastal uprising of 1888 to 1889.

Treaties were the central legal device. European agents obtained signatures or marks from African rulers on documents that purported to cede territory, trading rights, or sovereignty, and these documents were then presented to other European powers as evidence of title. The reliability of such treaties varied enormously. Some reflected genuine negotiation. Many were obtained through pressure, misrepresentation, gifts, or failure to translate meaningfully. Some rulers understood themselves to be granting limited commercial access, not alienating sovereignty. The Rudd Concession of 1888, in which the Ndebele king Lobengula gave Rhodes's agents mining rights, became a notorious example: Lobengula later disputed the terms as explained to him, and sent a delegation to London, but the concession was used as the basis of the British South Africa Company's charter. The treaties of Carl Peters in East Africa similarly raised doubts about whether the signatories had understood the documents or possessed the authority to cede territory.

African rulers were not passive. They pursued strategies of their own: negotiating treaties to play European powers against one another, granting limited commercial concessions to avoid larger ones, forming alliances against local rivals, refusing demands, acquiring firearms, sending embassies, and, when diplomacy failed, fighting. Menelik II in Ethiopia acquired large numbers of rifles from French and Italian sources even while negotiating with Italy. Samori Touré obtained firearms through British-controlled Sierra Leone. African decisions were taken in the context of constrained and rapidly changing circumstances, and the choices they faced were seldom between clearly good and clearly bad options.

Section 104.4

The Berlin Conference of 1884–1885: Diplomatic Rules, Effective Occupation, and the Limits of European Agreements

The Berlin West Africa Conference is among the most frequently misdescribed events in modern history. Popular accounts claim that European diplomats gathered in Berlin and divided Africa among themselves with a ruler and a map. The documentary record is different, and the German Federal Foreign Office, which holds the original General Act, states plainly that the conference did not draw specific borders but established a framework under international law for claiming territory with a reduced risk of war among the powers. Its purposes were to agree on freedom of trade in the Congo basin and freedom of navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers, and to set rules for claims on the African coasts.

The background was a set of conflicts over the Congo. In February 1884 Britain and Portugal signed a treaty that would have recognized Portuguese claims at the mouth of the Congo in exchange for commercial guarantees, which alarmed France, Germany, and others. Leopold's International Association of the Congo was simultaneously seeking recognition. Bismarck, with French cooperation, invited the powers to Berlin. Fourteen states took part: Germany (as host), the United States, the Ottoman Empire, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Denmark, France, Britain, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Spain, and Sweden-Norway. The list is itself informative. Several participants had no significant interest in the Congo, and none of the participants represented an African sovereign community. No African ruler, merchant, or delegate was consulted at any point, and African interests played no role in the proceedings.

The General Act of February 26, 1885, declared freedom of commerce throughout a defined "conventional basin of the Congo," prohibited slave trading within it, provided for the neutrality of the region in certain circumstances, and issued separate acts regulating navigation on the Congo and the Niger. Two later articles, Articles 34 and 35, concerned the conditions under which European powers could claim territory. They required any power taking possession of territory on the coasts of Africa, or establishing a protectorate there, to notify the other signatories, and they recognized an obligation to ensure that authority was established in occupied coastal regions sufficient to protect existing rights and, where applicable, freedom of trade and transit. These provisions are the origin of the principle usually called effective occupation, discussed in the vocabulary box below.

The wording of these provisions is critical. Strictly, Articles 34 and 35 referred to acquisitions on the African coast, not to the interior, and the conference did not define what degree of authority would be "sufficient." Nor did the Act make claims binding on non-signatories, much less on African societies. Even so, the principle had enormous effect. It turned flag-raising and treaty-signing into insufficient bases for title, and encouraged powers to demonstrate real presence through garrisons, stations, and administration, thereby intensifying the push inland. The German Foreign Office's own summary emphasizes that the conference did not originate the Scramble, which had already begun, but catalyzed it, and that most borders were drawn afterward through bilateral negotiations based on the Act's principles.

The Congo Free State's position was a special case. The recognition of Leopold's International Association as a sovereign entity was achieved through separate bilateral recognitions by the United States, Germany, France, Britain, and others, but the association was not one of the fourteen states represented at the conference or an original signatory of the General Act. Instead, the International Association of the Congo adhered separately to the Act under Article 37 on February 26, 1885. Leopold became sovereign of the Congo Free State in 1885, a state formally distinct from Belgium. Recognition by foreign governments did not create control over the African communities within the claimed territory, an issue explored in Section 104.9.

After 1885, the real work of partition went on in a long series of bilateral treaties, including the Anglo-German agreement of 1890, which traded the North Sea island of Heligoland for German recognition of British interests in Zanzibar and East Africa, the Anglo-Portuguese treaty of 1891, the Anglo-French agreement of 1898 and 1899 regarding the Niger and the Nile, and many others. Boundary commissions surveyed lines in regions with little or no European knowledge, and their decisions often cut across the territories of African societies. Diplomatic claims on European maps then had to be converted into possession by military action, or by negotiated cooperation with African rulers, or by a combination of both. The remaining sections of this chapter examine that process.

Historical Vocabulary
Effective Occupation and the Partition of Africa

Effective occupation is the name given to the principle, derived from Articles 34 and 35 of the General Act of 1885, that a European power's claim to African territory should be supported by the establishment of authority sufficient to protect existing rights and freedom of trade, and not by symbolic acts alone. In the text of the Act the requirement applied to coastal acquisitions, though the principle was soon applied far more broadly in diplomacy.

The partition of Africa refers to the division of African territory among European powers, achieved mostly through bilateral agreements and then enforced, where it was enforced, by military campaigns and administration. Students should note that "partition" describes European diplomatic claims, and that conquest and administration were distinct processes that continued long after the lines were drawn. The popular phrase "Scramble for Africa" captures the speed and rivalry of the process but can obscure its contested character.

Analysis: The articles are modest in language and enormous in effect. The text applies on its face to the coasts, and its extension to the interior was a matter of later diplomatic practice. The source tells historians how European governments chose to regulate competition among themselves, and thereby shows what they considered legitimate. It cannot tell historians what happened on the ground, how African societies understood the claims made over them, or how far any claim was backed by actual authority. For those questions, historians turn to treaties, military records, oral histories, and African sources.

The Conquest of West Africa: French, British, and German Expansion

Section 104.5

French Expansion in West Africa: Senegal, the Western Sudan, and Samori Touré

French expansion in West Africa began from the Senegal River, where the posts of Saint-Louis and Gorée had been held for generations, and moved inland in stages. Under Louis Faidherbe, governor in the 1850s and 1860s, France built forts along the river, created a force of African infantry, and fought the Toucouleur state of al-Hajj Umar. After 1880, with the new energy of French colonial policy, military officers of the marines and colonial infantry pushed eastward toward the upper Niger. Their aims combined strategic, commercial, and personal ambition: to link Senegal with the Niger, to secure trade routes, to forestall British and German competition, and to build careers. Officers often acted with considerable independence, since communications with Paris were slow, and the French government sometimes accepted faits accomplis. The Toucouleur state of Ahmadu Tall, centered on Ségou, fell to Louis Archinard's forces in 1890, and French detachments entered Timbuktu in late 1893. A more secure French military position emerged in early 1894 amid continuing Tuareg opposition, a reminder that entering a town did not amount to controlling its wider region.

The most formidable opponent of this advance was Samori Touré. A Muslim Mande trader and military commander, Samori built a large state, sometimes called the Wassoulou empire, in the upper Niger and Guinea highlands from the 1860s and 1870s, with its center at Bissandugu. His state was held together by a professional army, including infantry and cavalry, a network of workshops, and a system of taxation and tribute. Samori combined military leadership with political and commercial adaptation. He bought modern breech-loading rifles, chiefly through Freetown in the British colony of Sierra Leone, paying with gold and with captives taken in war, and his smiths repaired and attempted to copy European weapons. His wars of expansion, like those of many African and European rulers, involved enslavement of captives, and his rule was not welcomed by all neighboring peoples, some of whom allied with the French against him.

Samori's dealings with France illustrate the diversity of African diplomatic strategy. In 1886 and 1887 he signed treaties with the French that, in French accounts, placed his territory under their protection, although the extent to which he understood or accepted them is doubtful. By 1891 he was in open conflict with Archinard's columns. Samori then adopted a strategy that combined resistance and withdrawal. Rather than defend his heartland against superior artillery, he attempted to avoid decisive battle, harassed French forces, and in the late 1890s moved his state and army eastward, toward the region that is now northern Côte d'Ivoire, using scorched-earth tactics against populations in his path. This movement displaced many people and caused significant hardship. In 1898 French forces under Captain Henri Gouraud captured him at Guélémou, on September 29, and he was exiled to Gabon, where he died in 1900.

French conquest depended on African soldiers. The tirailleurs sénégalais, recruited from Senegal and the western Sudan and led by French officers, formed the bulk of French forces. Many recruits were poor men, captives, or people who had been purchased out of slavery in an arrangement the French called redemption, and the army offered pay and status in exchange for service in campaigns against other African societies. African auxiliaries and porters accompanied columns. Conquest also relied on alliances with rivals of the major resisting states, who saw the French as potential allies against a stronger neighbor. These facts do not make African soldiers "collaborators" in any simple sense. They acted under constraints that included poverty, coercion, ambition, and local political calculation.

The capture of Samori did not complete French rule. Administration, in the sense of taxation, courts, labor recruitment, and police, spread slowly, and in many districts resistance, flight, and local revolts continued into the early twentieth century. African political responses varied widely: some rulers resisted, others negotiated protection, others tried to exploit rivalries between France and Britain. The French victory in the western Sudan helped create the territorial framework of French West Africa. A government-general was instituted in 1895, but effective administrative integration took longer and was reorganized in 1902 and 1904. Within this uneven colonial framework, many former political units were subordinated to new administrative districts.

Section 104.6

British Expansion in the Gold Coast and Nigeria: Asante, the Niger, and the Sokoto Caliphate

Britain's West African possessions in the 1870s were small and commercially oriented, yet they bordered some of Africa's most powerful states. Asante, with its capital at Kumasi, controlled the forest and savanna hinterland of the Gold Coast, with a developed administration, a standing military organization, and a political culture centered on the Asantehene, the king, and the Golden Stool, which was understood as embodying the spirit of the Asante nation. Conflict with Britain was recurrent over trade routes, coastal allies, and authority. In 1874 a British expedition under Sir Garnet Wolseley, composed largely of West African and West Indian troops with a core of British soldiers, entered Kumasi, burned parts of the town, and compelled the treaty of Fomena. The British withdrew without establishing permanent rule, but in the same year they declared the coastal Gold Coast a colony.

Asante's later history shows the combination of internal politics and external pressure. In the 1890s, Britain pressed Asante to accept protection. In January 1896, British forces under Colonel Francis Scott occupied Kumasi without a major battle, after Asantehene Prempeh I and his advisers, wary of renewed war, chose not to fight. The British arrested Prempeh, deposed him, and sent him into exile, first to Sierra Leone and later to the Seychelles. They also seized royal property, an episode that illustrates the link between conquest and cultural dispossession that is examined further in Section 104.7. The scholarly literature on the occupation of Kumasi documents the deposition of the king and the removal of royal treasure.

The conflict of 1900, often called the War of the Golden Stool, shows how conquest bred renewed resistance. The British governor, Frederick Hodgson, demanded to be given the Golden Stool, apparently without understanding its meaning, and Asante leaders concluded that the British intended to destroy the kingdom's sacred authority. A leading figure in the resistance was Yaa Asantewaa, the queen mother of Ejisu, whose position carried real political and religious authority, and who urged Asante leaders to fight. The Asante besieged the British in the fort at Kumasi, until relief columns, again composed largely of African soldiers, defeated them. Yaa Asantewaa was exiled to the Seychelles, where she later died. Britain annexed Asante in 1901. The British did not obtain the Golden Stool, which was hidden and later recovered by Asante custodians, and its survival became an enduring symbol of Asante identity.

On the Niger, British expansion took a different form. The Royal Niger Company, headed by George Goldie, secured treaties with riverine rulers, excluded rival traders and African middlemen from the river, and claimed a monopoly. Its use of force against coastal and delta communities prompted resentment, including the attack on the company's depot at Akassa by Brass traders in 1895, who had been deprived of their trade. The charter was revoked in 1899 and, on January 1, 1900, the Niger Coast Protectorate and the company's territories were reorganized into the Protectorates of Southern and Northern Nigeria, with Frederick Lugard as the first high commissioner of the north.

The Sokoto Caliphate was the largest state in the region, and Britain's conquest of it was swift in military terms but gradual in administration. Lugard's forces, which included the newly formed West African Frontier Force, captured Kano in February 1903 and Sokoto in March. The caliph, Attahiru I, withdrew eastward and was killed at the battle of Burmi in July 1903. Britain then installed a new sultan, who acknowledged British authority, and a system emerged in which the emirs retained administrative functions under British supervision, an arrangement later described as indirect rule. Military occupation was not the same as administration: the colonial government continued to build its tax and judicial machinery for years, and the legal abolition of slavery as a status and the actual end of dependent labor did not coincide.

British conquest was driven by African as well as British participation. West African soldiers, porters, guides, interpreters, and intermediaries made up the bulk of British forces, and many local rulers and communities allied with Britain against stronger neighbors, whether out of calculation, resentment of their previous overlords, or a sense that resistance was hopeless. Institutions such as the emirates and the Asante political hierarchy did not vanish, but they operated under new constraints. The colonial state governed through, and at the same time altered, African authority.

Section 104.7

Dahomey and Benin: Military Conquest, Royal Authority, and Cultural Dispossession

Dahomey and the Edo Kingdom of Benin were old and wealthy monarchies whose political institutions, royal courts, and commercial relationships were deeply intertwined with the Atlantic world. Dahomey, centered on Abomey, had grown in the eighteenth century into a militarized state that took part extensively in the Atlantic slave trade, and after abolition it shifted toward the export of palm oil, though enslaved labor remained central to its economy. Its army included a corps of women soldiers, whom European observers called "Amazons" and whom the Fon called by other names, a distinctive feature that made the kingdom famous in Europe. Benin, in what is now southern Nigeria, had a royal court of great ceremonial and artistic sophistication, with guilds producing works in brass, ivory, and wood that recorded the history and authority of the Oba.

French pressure on Dahomey intensified in the late 1880s, as France established posts at Cotonou and Porto-Novo on the coast and claimed rights that Dahomey rejected. Béhanzin, who became king in 1889, resisted. Fighting broke out in 1890 and again in 1892, when France sent a column of mixed African and French troops under Colonel Alfred-Amédée Dodds, himself an officer of Senegalese and French parentage, who advanced on Abomey. The Dahomean army, equipped with modern rifles and some artillery, fought hard but could not stop the advance. French forces occupied Abomey on November 17, 1892. Béhanzin ordered parts of his own capital burned and withdrew, continuing resistance until early 1894, when he surrendered. He was exiled to Martinique and later to Algeria, where he died in 1906. The kingdom's sovereignty ended, though the French at first installed a successor ruler and later suppressed the monarchy.

Britain's confrontation with Benin followed a different path. The British government wanted to open the trade of the Benin hinterland, particularly palm products, which the Oba controlled through royal monopolies. In 1892 a British vice-consul, Henry Gallwey, concluded an agreement with Oba Ovonramwen, which the British treated as a protectorate treaty and which the Oba's court may not have understood in the same way. In January 1897 Acting Consul-General James Phillips led a small, lightly armed party toward Benin City, in spite of requests by the Oba's court to delay because of religious ceremonies. The expedition was presented as a visit, but Phillips had earlier sought authorization for armed intervention to depose the Oba; it should not be characterized simply as an innocent diplomatic mission. The party was ambushed and Phillips and most of his companions were killed. The British response was a much larger punitive expedition of more than a thousand soldiers. In February 1897 it captured and burned Benin City. The Oba fled, surrendered in August, and was tried by the British and exiled to Calabar, where he died in 1914.

The expedition's most lasting consequence in the wider world was the removal of royal objects. British forces seized thousands of works from the palace, including plaques and heads in brass or bronze, carved ivory tusks, and objects in wood and coral. The history of these objects is documented by museum and archival sources, such as the catalogue and research published by National Museums Scotland on the raid. Objects were sold by the British government to defray costs, and by officers and dealers, and entered museums and private collections in Britain, Germany, the United States, and elsewhere. The term "Benin Bronzes" is a convenient shorthand, but the seized works include several materials and forms, and many are brass. In the twenty-first century the objects have been the subject of extensive public debate and negotiation over return to Nigeria, with some institutions transferring objects and others deliberating.

Dahomey and Benin reveal how European conquest attacked political sovereignty and cultural institutions together. The Oba's regalia and the royal altars were not only treasures but instruments of political and religious authority. Their seizure, along with the removal or exile of rulers, was part of dismantling the institution that conquered peoples looked to. It is also an example of how conquest changed the meaning of African art, which entered European museums as "primitive art" and as evidence for racial theories while its original political and religious context was lost. The loot of Benin was both a result of conquest and an argument used by contemporaries to justify it, since it was displayed as evidence of African "barbarism" alongside an admiration for the objects' craft.

Section 104.8

German West Africa and the Diversity of Colonial Relationships: Togoland, Kamerun, and Regional Comparisons

Germany's African empire began in 1884, when Bismarck, responding to commercial lobbying and diplomatic opportunity, placed several coastal trading stations under imperial protection. In Togoland, the explorer and consul Gustav Nachtigal concluded a treaty with local rulers on the coast in 1884, and in Kamerun German firms and officials signed treaties with Duala leaders, notably the kings of Bell and Akwa, in July 1884. The Duala were experienced coastal brokers, and their agreements were not simple surrenders. They sought to protect their position as intermediaries in trade with the interior, and viewed the German connection as advantageous against British rivals. As German authority grew, the Duala found that the Germans intended to bypass them by trading directly with the interior, and relations deteriorated, eventually leading to dispossession of land at the coast and, in 1914, the execution of Rudolf Duala Manga Bell for alleged treason after he protested expropriation.

The main problem for Germany, as for other powers, was to turn treaties with coastal rulers into authority over inland peoples who had not been party to them. In Kamerun the German government and firms sponsored expeditions into the interior, which negotiated, fought, and built stations among societies such as the Bamum, Bali, and many smaller communities. The process took more than two decades and was marked by repeated military campaigns. In Togoland, the German administration extended inland more peacefully but still relied on forced labor for road and railway building, and collected taxes. Colonial publicists sometimes called Togoland a "model colony" because it covered its own costs, but that reputation reflected in part the imposition of labor and tax on its population.

The economies of these colonies rested on the export of palm products and cocoa, rubber, and timber from plantations and from African producers, and on infrastructure such as railways and ports built for evacuation of exports. African merchants, coastal elites, farmers, and communities farther inland adapted in different ways. Some found new commercial opportunities as cocoa farmers or traders, and others lost land and labor to plantations. Conflicts over commercial taxation, land, and the recruitment of labor were chronic.

Comparison with neighboring territories shows the range of colonial relationships. The French employed military conquest followed by a centralized administration under governors-general, the British combined chartered companies, protectorate treaties, and later indirect rule, and the Germans, with fewer resources, relied on military expeditions and on cooperation with selected local authorities. The Portuguese presence in Guinea, Angola, and Mozambique reflected a much older colonial system. Portugal had held coastal footholds for centuries but exercised effective control over the interior only in limited districts. After the Berlin Conference, and especially after the humiliation of the 1890 British ultimatum, Portuguese governments launched campaigns to occupy the interior, which in Mozambique included the defeat of the Gaza ruler Gungunhana in 1895 and in Guinea and Angola continued into the 1910s and beyond. Portuguese expansion thus combined an old colonial system with new territorial ambitions.

Taking West Africa as a whole, conquest produced varied political outcomes. Some rulers were deposed and exiled, as Prempeh, Béhanzin, and Ovonramwen were. Some institutions, including the emirates of northern Nigeria, were incorporated into the colonial order under supervision. Elsewhere, negotiation with local authorities allowed colonial governments to establish a presence at lower cost. In every case, new colonial bureaucracies, with their taxes, courts, police, and labor demands, were built on top of African societies and changed them. The relationship between commercial influence, military coercion, and negotiated authority varied by place, depending on the strength of African states, the resources available to the European power, and local political rivalries.

Section 104.9

Leopold II and the Creation of the Congo Free State, 1876–1885

Leopold II, king of the Belgians from 1865, ruled a small constitutional monarchy that had no appetite for colonies. He did, and for years searched for a territory that he could control personally. In September 1876 he convened a Geographical Conference in Brussels that created the International African Association, presented as a scientific and humanitarian body devoted to exploration and the suppression of the slave trade. It quickly became a vehicle for his own ambitions. When Stanley returned from his crossing of the continent, Leopold hired him, and from 1879 to 1884 Stanley established stations along the Congo River and its tributaries, building roads around the rapids and obtaining agreements from local leaders. Leopold's organizations, successively the Committee for the Study of the Upper Congo and the International Association of the Congo, claimed hundreds of such treaties.

The treaties require careful reading. African political authorities in the Congo basin, many of them local chiefs whose authority was limited to particular communities, signed or marked documents that were drafted in European languages and, in many cases, translated imperfectly. The documents purported to transfer territory and sovereignty in return for cloth, trinkets, and promises of protection. It is doubtful that signatories regarded them as transfers of permanent political authority, and in many cases the individuals concerned lacked the authority to cede land at all. Historians treat these agreements as devices to produce evidence of title for European diplomacy, not as records of consent.

Between 1884 and 1885, Leopold's association won recognition in a series of bilateral diplomatic arrangements, beginning with the United States, then Germany, France, and others, in the diplomatic setting of the Berlin Conference described in Section 104.4. Great powers favored a neutral buffer state under a weak sovereign over the prospect of the Congo falling to a rival. In 1885 Leopold became the sovereign of the Congo Free State, a state that was formally separate from Belgium and whose territory was far larger. Between 1885 and 1908 it was Leopold's personal domain, ruled by him through officials in Brussels and agents in Africa, without a Belgian parliament's control. It was not a Belgian colony in the ordinary sense, and students should not equate the Free State with the Belgian state.

Recognition abroad did not give control within. The Free State at first held a string of river stations, and most of the interior remained under the political authority of African communities. To extend its reach, it created the Force Publique, which recruited African soldiers, many of them from outside the areas where they were deployed, under predominantly European and later some other European officers. The Force Publique fought wars against Swahili-Arab commercial networks in the east in the early 1890s, supported by many Congolese allies, and against numerous African communities in the interior. In Katanga, the Yeke kingdom of Msiri controlled the trade in copper and ivory and had refused agreements. In December 1891, the expedition commanded by William Grant Stairs confronted Msiri. Stairs sent his subordinate, Captain Omer Bodson, to summon the ruler; Msiri was shot by Bodson, who was then fatally wounded. European expedition accounts and Yeke oral histories differ on the circumstances of the shooting. The expedition subsequently imposed Free State authority, while local Yeke political institutions continued in altered form. The Royal Museum for Central Africa preserves documentary and oral-history evidence of this encounter and of the distinction between diplomatic recognition and military occupation. The case shows the contrast between recognition by foreign governments and the reality of control in Africa.

Section 104.10

Rubber, Ivory, Forced Labor, and the System of Violence in the Congo Free State

The Free State was expensive to run, and Leopold, who had borrowed heavily, needed revenue. The solution was extraction. Ivory was the first major export. From the 1890s, the rapidly growing global demand for rubber, driven by bicycle and later automobile tires and by electrical insulation, made wild rubber from the Congo's forests extremely valuable. The state responded with a series of decrees in 1891 and 1892 that claimed "vacant lands" and their products as state property, and divided the territory into zones in which commercial activity was reserved to the state or to concession companies. Some concession companies, such as the Anglo-Belgian India Rubber Company (ABIR) and the Anversoise, in which the state held shares, were granted vast territories in which they collected rubber with the state's support.

The system required labor, and the labor was obtained by compulsion. Communities were required to deliver set quantities of rubber as a form of taxation in labor, regardless of whether the villagers wished to trade. Collecting wild rubber meant long journeys into the forest, away from the fields that supported families, and quotas were often beyond what was possible. State agents and company agents, whose pay often included commissions tied to output, enforced deliveries with the aid of the Force Publique and company sentries. Documented practices included flogging, imprisonment, taking women and children hostage until quotas were met, burning of villages that failed to deliver, and killing. Missionary and official sources describe the practice of requiring soldiers to account for cartridges by presenting evidence of kills, which led to the severing of hands. This practice was documented in multiple contemporary sources and became a symbol of the system.

The consequences were far-reaching. Violence killed directly, but the greater human cost came from the disruption of agricultural life, famine, flight, epidemic disease spread by troop and labor movements, and falling birth rates caused by hardship and the separation of families. Sleeping sickness spread as populations were moved. Whole districts were depopulated or fled to the forests. The effects were uneven, being most severe in the rubber-producing regions and lighter in others.

The overall demographic cost is uncertain. There was no census before or during Free State rule, and estimates of the pre-colonial population of the Congo basin are themselves approximations. Modern estimates of population loss during the Free State period range widely, from a few million to figures often cited as about ten million, and historians disagree about how much of the loss to attribute to violence, famine, and disease, and about the baseline from which losses are calculated. Students should treat any single figure as an estimate and not as a census-based finding, while recognizing that scholars agree on the documented existence of widespread violence, forced labor, and demographic catastrophe in the rubber regions.

Responsibility did not rest with Leopold alone. The system was operated by state officials, company directors and shareholders, military officers, and European agents in the field, by African soldiers who enforced it under European command, and by chiefs and intermediaries who were pressed into delivering labor. Leopold's personal role in constructing the legal and financial framework was central, but a purely personal explanation misses the institutional incentives, such as commissions and quotas, and the international commercial demand that sustained the system. The scholarship of Julia Seibert and others also stresses that coercive labor regimes continued in modified forms after the end of the Free State, in the Belgian Congo, showing that forced labor was a feature of colonial economic organization and not just of one king's personal rule.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession Company, Forced Labor, and Colonial Extraction

A concession company was a private enterprise granted by a colonial state the exclusive right to exploit resources, such as rubber and ivory, in a defined territory, often with the state's armed support and sometimes with the state as shareholder. Forced labor refers to work exacted under threat of punishment, whether by taxes payable in labor, quotas, conscription for public works, or hostage-taking; historians distinguish it from slavery in the legal sense while recognizing that the two shaded into one another in practice. Colonial extraction describes the transfer of resources and labor from colonized societies to colonial states and European firms, usually on terms set by the colonizer.

These terms are essential to understanding the Congo and French Equatorial Africa, where the system gave companies both economic rights and quasi-governmental powers. The language used by contemporaries was often euphemistic: "taxes" and "contributions" described compulsory deliveries, and "recruitment" could mean seizure.

Section 104.11

Exposing the Congo Atrocities: African Testimony, International Reform Campaigns, and Belgian Annexation

Evidence of abuse reached European and American audiences through several channels. As early as July 1890, George Washington Williams, an African American minister, lawyer, and historian who had visited the Congo, published an open letter to Leopold II that criticized the Free State's treatment of Africans, its commercial monopoly, and its misrepresentation of its aims. Williams's account was an early and influential indictment. The 1890s and 1900s saw further evidence from missionaries, among them William Henry Sheppard, an African American Presbyterian missionary in the Kasai region who documented the effects of Free State and company rule among the Kuba and neighboring peoples, and Alice Seeley Harris, a British missionary whose photographs of mutilated Congolese, taken in 1904, offered visual evidence that circulated widely through public lectures with lantern slides.

The most important official investigation was that of Roger Casement, the British consul in the Congo, who traveled up the river in 1903 and interviewed Congolese witnesses, missionaries, and officials. His report, published in 1904, described the compulsion and brutality of the rubber system and the condition of the populations. Casement's report did not rest on one kind of evidence: it combined testimony, direct observation, commercial records, and comparison with earlier accounts. E. D. Morel, a shipping clerk in Liverpool who noticed that the Congo's exports to Belgium were not matched by corresponding imports of trade goods, inferred that the exports were being obtained by compulsion. Morel, with Casement's help, founded the Congo Reform Association in 1904 and led a determined campaign of publication and lobbying in Britain and the United States.

Leopold's reply was to deny, discredit, and contain. He established a commission of inquiry in 1904 whose findings partly confirmed the criticisms, funded publicists and lobbyists, and attacked missionaries and critics as hostile or mistaken. Under growing pressure from public opinion and governments, and with the Belgian parliament increasingly uneasy, Belgium took over the Free State in 1908 and transformed it into the Belgian Congo, under a colonial charter. The new administration reformed some of the worst abuses of the rubber system, but coercive labor, compulsory cultivation, and unequal legal rule continued.

The reform campaign should be distinguished from anticolonial opposition. Most critics, including Williams, Morel, and many missionaries, objected to the abuses of Leopold's system and the commercial monopoly, but did not demand Congolese independence. Many assumed that a reformed European administration would be benevolent. Congolese themselves were not only witnesses but actors, who resisted through flight, rebellion, and refusals of labor, and whose testimony was essential to the evidence the reformers gathered. This is an example of how humanitarian campaigns could both expose atrocities and leave the structure of colonial rule intact.

Section 104.12

Central Africa beyond Leopold: French Equatorial Expansion, Portuguese Angola, and Colonial Economies

The Congo Free State's neighbors developed related systems. France's claim north of the Congo rested on Brazza's treaty with Makoko in 1880 and on the occupation of the Ogooué and Ubangi regions, which became the territories of Gabon, Middle Congo, Ubangi-Shari, and later Chad, eventually grouped as French Equatorial Africa. French officials considered these vast territories costly to administer, despite commercially valuable rubber and ivory in parts of the region. Unwilling to finance extensive administration directly, the French government turned in 1899 to a concession system, granting some forty companies exclusive rights over vast territories in return for modest fees and a share of profits. As in the Free State, the companies collected rubber and ivory through forced deliveries, hostage-taking, and armed agents. A government inquiry led by Brazza in 1905 found abuses; Brazza died in September 1905 on the return journey, and the full impact of his report was muted, though a scandal about colonial abuses in the French Congo surfaced in the French press and parliament.

Portugal sought to consolidate its claim to Angola and connect coastal settlements to the interior. This required military campaigns against African states and communities, such as the Ovimbundu kingdoms of the central highlands and the Kwanyama farther south, which continued for decades, with effective occupation in some districts not achieved until the 1910s. Labor was a persistent concern. Portuguese authorities used contract labor systems that were closely connected to forced recruitment, and workers were shipped to the cocoa plantations of São Tomé, a practice that provoked international criticism in the first decade of the twentieth century. As in Central Africa generally, disputes among European governments over boundaries and access to rivers continued through bilateral agreements, including those between the Congo Free State and France in the 1880s and 1890s, between Britain and Portugal, and between Germany and its neighbors.

Across these territories, military occupation led to the creation of taxes, customs systems, administrative districts, and labor obligations. The aim was to make colonies pay for themselves. Colonial governments imposed head taxes or hut taxes payable in cash or labor, required cultivation of specified crops, controlled movement by pass systems, and recruited porters for transport, since roads and railways were few. Comparison of the Belgian, French, and Portuguese cases shows both similarities and differences: all relied on coercion, but they differed in the legal status of companies, in the degree of state control, and in the scale of military force. The Free State was unusual in being the personal possession of a monarch, whereas French concessions were granted by a state that exercised only weak oversight, and Portuguese administration was thin and often dependent on local intermediaries.

African intermediaries made these systems work. Local chiefs, appointed or recognized by colonial officials, collected taxes, recruited laborers, and judged disputes, and interpreters, clerks, catechists, traders, and soldiers staffed the colonial economy. Their position was unstable: they could gain status and wealth, but they were also squeezed between the demands of administrators and the resentment of their communities. African political and economic institutions persisted, adapted, or were disrupted according to local conditions. Households shifted production, migrated to avoid taxes and labor demands, or sought work on plantations and in new towns, and some chiefs used the colonial position to expand their authority.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household Confronting Colonial Taxation, Labor Demands, and Agricultural Change

This feature describes patterns documented in administrative, missionary, and scholarly sources on the Congo Free State, French Equatorial Africa, and Portuguese Angola. It is a generalized description and not the testimony of any individual.

Before conquest, a farming household in the forest and savanna regions of Central Africa organized its year around clearing fields, planting staples such as cassava and plantains, hunting and fishing, and seasonal trade, with labor divided by age and gender and obligations owed to kin and local authorities. Colonial rule introduced new, external claims on these resources. A household might be required to pay a head or hut tax, in cash, in produce, or in labor, at a time when little money circulated locally. The most readily available way to obtain the required cash or satisfy a quota might be to gather rubber, carry loads for the administration, or work on a plantation or railway, tasks that often took men away for weeks or months.

The consequences fell on families unequally. When men were absent, women carried a larger share of farm work while also facing demands for food supplies for state posts and soldiers, and in some districts women were taken as hostages to compel male labor. Children and elders were left with reduced food security. Taking time to gather rubber could mean fields were planted late or not at all, raising the risk of hunger. Movement was often restricted by passes, while flight into the forest or across a border to a neighboring colony was a common response. Households also adapted: some turned to cash-crop production, trade, or migration to new towns, and some accommodated by supplying chiefs and intermediaries who could shield them from the worst demands. These everyday choices, made under constraint, shaped the larger economies of the colonies.

Section 104.13

North Africa and the Mediterranean: Egypt, Tunisia, Morocco, and Libya

North Africa occupied a distinctive place in European imperial expansion because of its proximity to Europe, its Mediterranean commerce, and its long political connection to the Ottoman Empire. France's colonial presence in Algeria, begun with the invasion of 1830, predates the Scramble. The conquest took decades, involved brutal warfare against resistance such as that of Abd al-Qadir, and produced a settler colony in which European settlers held land and political privilege over the Muslim majority. Algeria is therefore best understood as an earlier colonial experience that shaped French approaches, and not as part of the post-1880 partition.

The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, constructed by a French company with Egyptian labor and capital, made Egypt strategically vital. The Egyptian ruler, the khedive Ismail, borrowed heavily from European lenders for modernization projects and military ventures, and by 1876 Egypt was effectively bankrupt. European financial control, exercised through a debt commission and later the Anglo-French Dual Control, produced resentment among Egyptian officers, officials, and landowners. The Urabi movement, led by the army officer Ahmad Urabi, demanded constitutional government and an end to foreign domination. When riots in Alexandria in June 1882 offered a pretext, Britain bombarded the city in July and defeated Urabi's army at Tel el-Kebir on September 13, 1882. British forces then occupied Egypt, promising to leave once order was restored, and they stayed. Egypt remained formally under Ottoman suzerainty, with a khedive, until the outbreak of the First World War, when Britain proclaimed a protectorate in December 1914. The occupation of 1882 was therefore not the establishment of the protectorate, and British officials such as Evelyn Baring, later Lord Cromer, ruled indirectly through Egyptian ministers while retaining real power.

France established a protectorate over Tunisia in 1881, after a military expedition justified by border incidents and underpinned by financial and strategic motives, through the Treaty of Bardo, which left the bey of Tunis as titular ruler under French supervision. The arrangement was confirmed in 1883. Italy, which had hoped for influence in Tunisia, resented the move, a resentment that pushed it toward the alliance with Germany and Austria-Hungary.

Morocco, independent and ruled by a sultan, became the subject of an international contest. After the Anglo-French agreement of 1904 recognized French interests in Morocco in exchange for British primacy in Egypt, Germany challenged the arrangement, provoking the Tangier crisis of 1905 to 1906, and again the Agadir crisis of 1911. These crises, whose diplomacy is examined in Chapter 107, ended with France gaining recognition for a protectorate, established by the Treaty of Fez in March 1912. Spain received a northern zone and a southern strip under an arrangement made with France. The French protectorate, led by Resident-General Hubert Lyautey, maintained the sultan and his court as formal authorities while exercising control, and the pacification of the Atlas and the south continued for years afterward.

Italy, a late participant in imperial expansion, declared war on the Ottoman Empire in September 1911 and invaded Libya, seeking prestige and resources. Italian forces occupied coastal towns, and the peace of October 1912, the Treaty of Ouchy, transferred Ottoman claims. But occupation of the coast was not conquest of the interior. Libyan resistance, including that of the Sanusiyya religious order, continued, and Italian control of the interior was not achieved until the 1930s.

Section 104.14

The Nile Valley and the Mahdist Sudan: Independence, Reconquest, and Fashoda, 1881–1899

Sudan had been under Egyptian rule since the 1820s, a regime that many Sudanese experienced as harsh, financially exploitative, and dependent on slave raiding and taxation. In the 1870s Egypt's government, under European pressure, appointed European officials such as General Charles Gordon to suppress the slave trade, which disrupted the livelihoods of merchants and others. In 1881 Muhammad Ahmad, a religious teacher from the Nile region, proclaimed himself the Mahdi, a divinely guided redeemer expected in Islamic tradition. His movement drew support from those with religious, economic, and political grievances: pious Muslims who condemned Egyptian rule as corrupt, traders and slave dealers affected by suppression, and pastoral and riverine communities burdened by taxes.

The Mahdists defeated Egyptian forces, including the army of William Hicks, destroyed in November 1883, and in January 1885 they captured Khartoum, where Gordon, sent by Britain to organize the evacuation of Egyptian garrisons, was killed. The news provoked outrage in Britain. The Mahdi died in June 1885 and was succeeded by the Khalifa Abdallahi, who ruled the Mahdist state from Omdurman. Its government combined religious legitimacy with an administration, an army, taxation, and a state treasury. It also faced internal conflicts among regional and tribal groups, famine in the late 1880s, and recurring war, and it was not a simple theocratic monolith. The state continued to engage in commerce with neighbors, and its rule was felt differently by different communities.

British interest in reconquest grew in the 1890s, driven by concern about French and Belgian activity on the upper Nile and by the strategic importance of the river for Egypt's water supply. An Anglo-Egyptian army under Herbert Kitchener advanced up the Nile with a railway, gunboats, and Maxim guns. On September 2, 1898, at the Battle of Omdurman, the Mahdist army was destroyed by concentrated firepower. Contemporary and later estimates agree that Mahdist deaths numbered in the many thousands, while the Anglo-Egyptian force lost only a few dozen men, which makes the battle an example of technological asymmetry. Even here, historians caution that military technology was combined with logistics, railway construction, and Mahdist strategic errors. The Khalifa was killed in 1899, but Mahdist religious and political influence survived into the twentieth century and into modern Sudanese politics.

Reconquest brought Britain into confrontation with France. A French expedition under Jean-Baptiste Marchand, having crossed Central Africa, reached the Nile at Fashoda in July 1898, in an effort to establish a French presence in the upper Nile. Kitchener, after Omdurman, arrived at Fashoda in September and a tense standoff ensued, which both governments treated as a crisis that might lead to war. France, diplomatically isolated and cautious, withdrew in December 1898, and an Anglo-French agreement of March 1899 defined spheres of influence. Britain and Egypt established the Anglo-Egyptian Condominium in Sudan in January 1899, a joint arrangement under which, in practice, British officials controlled the administration. The formal joint sovereignty concealed British dominance. The longer diplomatic consequences of Fashoda for Franco-British relations are considered in Chapter 107.

Section 104.15

British East Africa: Uganda, Kenya, Railways, and African Political Responses

East Africa had been integrated into Indian Ocean commerce for centuries. Swahili towns along the coast, the Sultanate of Zanzibar, and caravan routes reaching into the interior carried ivory, cloth, and enslaved people, and Arab-Swahili and African traders were important political and economic actors as far as the Great Lakes. Missionaries of the Church Missionary Society and the White Fathers arrived in the 1870s, and by the late 1880s the kingdom of Buganda, a centralized state north of Lake Victoria, was divided among rival Protestant, Catholic, and Muslim factions, each connected to different external networks, in conflicts that shaped the arrival of European power.

Britain's expansion began through the Imperial British East Africa Company, chartered in 1888, which was meant to administer the territory cheaply. The company, undercapitalized, was unable to meet the costs, and its agent Frederick Lugard intervened in Buganda's factional struggles in the early 1890s, an intervention that favored the Protestant faction. When the company withdrew, the British government declared the Uganda Protectorate in 1894 and the East Africa Protectorate in 1895. In Uganda, the British depended on Buganda's chiefs as allies. The 1900 Uganda Agreement, negotiated with Buganda's leaders, confirmed their status and allocated land in a way that created a landed elite. Other kingdoms had different experiences. Bunyoro, under King Kabalega, resisted British expansion in a prolonged war in the 1890s; it was defeated, with the loss of territory to Buganda, and Kabalega was captured and exiled to the Seychelles in 1899.

The Uganda Railway, begun at Mombasa in 1896 and reaching Lake Victoria at Kisumu in 1901, was a strategic project, designed to secure Uganda and the upper Nile and to reduce the cost of transport. Kenya's national archives preserve planning documents that connect the railway to British strategic interests. The labor was recruited mostly in India, with tens of thousands of workers, many of whom died or were injured; African laborers and porters also worked on the line. The railway produced new towns such as Nairobi, which began as a railway depot, and it altered patterns of commerce, migration, and land use. Because the line crossed highlands with a temperate climate, the colonial government encouraged European settlement, and in the early twentieth century large areas of land were alienated for settlers, with the Maasai relocated under agreements in 1904 and 1911 and Kikuyu and other communities losing land.

British methods combined treaties, local allies, African troops, and direct force. In regions without centralized states, such as among the Nandi, British officials appointed chiefs and expected them to collect taxes and supply labor, and fought repeated campaigns against those who resisted. The colonial government thus relied on African institutions and intermediaries while subordinating them, and in doing so it reshaped local politics, creating new elites and tensions that would last through the colonial period.

These arrangements also produced different experiences of colonial landholding. In Buganda, agreements with the kingdom's chiefs formalized important estates and strengthened some landowners, but cultivators and tenants did not necessarily benefit from the same rights. In the highlands of the East Africa Protectorate, railway access and officials' expectations of European settlement encouraged alienation of land used by African communities. Tax collection, policing, and labor recruitment varied by district and depended on whether officials governed through established kingdoms, appointed headmen, or military posts. The British claim that the railway would bring prosperity therefore requires a further question: which groups controlled the new land, transport, and commercial opportunities, and who bore the costs?

Section 104.16

German East Africa and the Maji Maji War, 1885–1907

German claims on the East African mainland began with Carl Peters, who, with associates, concluded treaties with local leaders in 1884 and founded the German East Africa Company. The imperial government issued a charter of protection in 1885. As with other treaties, the documents were of dubious legitimacy: the signatories often did not hold the authority they were credited with, and their understanding of the agreements is uncertain. When the company tried to take over the coastal customs in 1888, the coastal population rose in revolt under Abushiri ibn Salim, a coastal planter and trader. The revolt was suppressed by 1889, Abushiri was hanged, and the German government took over direct administration in 1891.

Inland, German forces faced the Hehe, a powerful state in the southern highlands under Chief Mkwawa. In 1891, at Lugalo, Hehe warriors ambushed and destroyed a German column, a major blow to German prestige. The Germans responded with a long campaign, storming Mkwawa's fortified capital at Kalenga in 1894, and Mkwawa continued guerrilla resistance until 1898, when he died, according to most accounts by suicide, as German forces closed in. The Hehe wars illustrate how African military leaders adapted their tactics and how conquest could take more than a decade.

German colonial administration relied on military posts, taxation, and the use of local intermediaries known as akidas and jumbes, often from the coast, who were resented in the interior. The colonial state imposed a hut tax payable in cash, promoted plantations, and used forced labor for roads and for cultivation of cash crops. In southern East Africa, officials required villages to grow cotton on communal fields for export, work that was compulsory and brought little return while taking time from food crops. Combined with taxation, forced labor, and the harsh conduct of administrators, this created deep grievances.

In July 1905, the Maji Maji War began among the Matumbi hills in the south, after people uprooted cotton plants on communal fields. It was connected with a religious movement centered on a spirit medium known as Kinjikitile Ngwale, who distributed maji, water medicine, which was believed to protect against German bullets. Kinjikitile was arrested and executed by the Germans in August 1905, but the movement spread across a wide region of southern and central East Africa, drawing in many communities of different languages and political traditions. The war encompassed groups whose motivations and strategies were not identical: some fought for the restoration of local authority, others against particular taxes or officials, and some joined late or fought on the German side. Thaddeus Sunseri's study of the war in the Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History provides the basis for this emphasis on its regional diversity.

The German response, under Governor Gustav Adolf von Götzen and reinforced by troops from Germany and from other colonies, was severe. German forces defeated the insurgents in battle, using machine guns against massed attacks that were spiritually guided, and then adopted a scorched-earth strategy, destroying villages and food stocks to compel surrender through hunger. The war ended in 1907. The number of deaths is uncertain because no reliable census was conducted. German official estimates cited roughly 75,000 African deaths, while later scholarly estimates have reached approximately 250,000 to 300,000. The estimates differ in their methods and coverage, but agree that starvation and disease following the destruction of food supplies accounted for a large part of the mortality. None of these totals is a census-based count.

The war was followed by changes in German colonial policy. A reform program associated with the Colonial Office under Bernhard Dernburg and with Governor Albrecht von Rechenberg from 1906 sought to reduce the worst abuses, encourage African peasant agriculture, and improve colonial administration. Historians generally see this as a reaction to the shock of the war, and as a modification of the system, since taxation, labor demands, and the colonial hierarchy remained in place.

Section 104.17

Ethiopia, Italy, and the Battle of Adwa: Defending African Sovereignty, 1889–1896

Ethiopia in the later nineteenth century was an empire in the process of reconsolidation. After decades of regional fragmentation, the emperors Tewodros II, Yohannes IV, and then Menelik II rebuilt central authority. Menelik, previously king of Shewa, became emperor in 1889. He and his wife, Empress Taytu Betul, managed a court that combined the political traditions of the Christian highlands with a pragmatic interest in European technology and diplomacy. Menelik acquired large quantities of modern rifles, including from France, Russia, and, before the crisis, from Italy itself, and used his diplomacy to cultivate relations with several European powers, so that no single one could dominate.

Italy, a young state seeking colonial status, had established a presence on the Red Sea coast, and in 1890 formally created the colony of Eritrea. In May 1889 Menelik and Italy signed the Treaty of Wuchale. The Amharic and Italian versions differed on Article XVII. The Italian text, as Italy understood it, obliged Ethiopia to use the Italian government for its dealings with other powers, thereby making Ethiopia a protectorate, while the Amharic text made the use of Italian good offices optional. When Menelik learned of the discrepancy, he rejected the Italian interpretation and, in 1893, denounced the treaty. Italy proceeded to claim its protectorate and prepared for war.

Ethiopian preparations for war were extensive. Menelik called up an army from across the empire, with regional lords commanding contingents. He built stocks of rifles, ammunition, and some artillery, and organized supply, a difficult task in highland terrain. Taytu played a prominent political role, supporting resistance and reportedly urging that the army advance, and she is credited with a significant part in the siege of Italian forces at Mekele in January 1896, where Ethiopian forces took control of the water supply. Italian forces under General Oreste Baratieri advanced into Tigray, were short of supplies, and were under pressure from Rome to act. On March 1, 1896, Baratieri's columns, which had become separated over difficult terrain and were operating with imperfect maps, met a much larger Ethiopian army at Adwa. The Ethiopians overwhelmed them, killing several thousand Italian and Eritrean soldiers and capturing many more, while sustaining heavy losses of their own.

The causes of the Ethiopian victory were several. Ethiopia had mobilized more men, with modern rifles, and had better intelligence and logistics than its opponents. Italian command was divided and uncertain, its maps unreliable, and the political pressure on Baratieri to deliver a victory led to a risky advance. The result was a decisive defeat. By the Treaty of Addis Ababa in October 1896, Italy recognized Ethiopian independence and abandoned its claim to a protectorate, though Italy retained Eritrea. Adwa's international importance was immense. It was a victory by an African state over a European invading army, widely noted in Europe and across Africa and among people of African descent elsewhere, and it ensured that Ethiopia would remain a recognized independent state, and a symbol of African sovereignty, during the colonial era. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African Art documents Adwa's significance and preserves Ethiopian artistic representations of the battle. Raymond Jonas's study provides a comprehensive scholarly treatment.

Menelik's victory must be understood in its fuller context. His state was itself an expanding empire. During the 1880s and 1890s Ethiopian armies conquered territories to the south and west, including Oromo, Sidama, Wolayta, and Kaffa lands, which were incorporated through warfare, taxation, land confiscation, and, in many cases, enslavement. For many peoples in these regions, Ethiopian conquest was a form of imperial rule. Adwa's defense of independence against Italy therefore coexisted with the coercive incorporation of other African communities, and a historically careful account acknowledges both.

Section 104.18

Southern Africa: Mineral Wealth, Settler Expansion, the South African War, and Union

Southern Africa differed from most of the continent because of its settler populations, its mineral wealth, and the presence of two white-ruled Boer republics alongside British colonies. The discovery of diamonds near Kimberley in the late 1860s and 1870s, and of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886, transformed the region. Mining capital poured in, Johannesburg grew into a major city, railways were built to the coast, and mining companies recruited large numbers of African men as migrant laborers, housed in compounds, under conditions of low wages and heavy discipline. Cecil Rhodes, who built a fortune in diamonds through De Beers and became prime minister of the Cape Colony, represented the alliance of capital and imperial ambition, and he used his wealth to promote British expansion northward.

British expansion also meant war against African states. After a British ultimatum in December 1878, the Anglo-Zulu War broke out in January 1879. The Zulu army destroyed a British force at Isandlwana on January 22, a famous defeat, but Britain reinforced and, at Ulundi in July, broke the Zulu army. King Cetshwayo was captured, and the kingdom was divided into chiefdoms under British supervision, ending Zulu sovereignty. North of the Limpopo, the British South Africa Company, using the Rudd Concession as a basis, sent a column to Mashonaland in 1890. In 1893 it provoked war against the Ndebele kingdom, whose king, Lobengula, died in 1894. In 1896 and 1897, Ndebele and Shona communities rose in a major rebellion, known in Zimbabwean tradition as the First Chimurenga or Umvukela, which was suppressed after heavy fighting.

Rivalry between Britain and the Boer republics, the South African Republic (Transvaal) and the Orange Free State, centered on control of the gold fields, the treatment of foreign (uitlander) miners, and British claims to paramountcy. Rhodes's role in the Jameson Raid of December 1895, an unsuccessful attempt to provoke an uprising in Johannesburg, deepened distrust. War broke out in October 1899. The Boers won early victories and besieged Mafeking, Kimberley, and Ladysmith, but British reinforcements under Lord Roberts and then Lord Kitchener occupied the republics' capitals in 1900. The Boers then turned to guerrilla warfare, which Britain countered by systematic destruction of farms, a network of blockhouses, and the internment of civilians in concentration camps. Camp conditions, marked by overcrowding, disease, and inadequate supplies, caused tens of thousands of deaths. Historians generally estimate that about twenty-eight thousand Boer civilians, the majority children, died, and that at least fourteen thousand Black Africans died in separate camps, with some estimates higher, since African deaths were poorly recorded.

The war was often described as a "white man's war," but Black South Africans were deeply involved as laborers, scouts, wagon drivers, and in some cases armed auxiliaries, and many were displaced from land or imprisoned. African communities expected that a British victory would improve their situation, and were disappointed. The Treaty of Vereeniging, signed on May 31, 1902, ended the war, brought the republics under British sovereignty, and left the question of African political rights to be decided later, in effect by white governments. In 1910 the Union of South Africa brought the four colonies together under a constitution that restricted membership of the Union Parliament to whites. The Cape retained a limited, property- and literacy-qualified franchise under which some African and Coloured men could vote for white representatives. Natal had only a tiny number of eligible non-white voters, while the Transvaal and Orange Free State restricted the parliamentary franchise to whites. In 1913 the Natives Land Act sharply limited new purchases and leases across racial lines outside scheduled African areas. The statute contained exceptions, and its application varied by province, but its restrictions and anti-tenancy measures reinforced racial land segregation and dispossession. The Cambridge History of South Africa emphasizes the connections among the war, imperial power, white political consolidation, and the protection of white supremacy.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Power, Landholding, and African Rights in Southern Africa before and after 1902

What changed?

  • The independent Boer republics, the Zulu kingdom, and the Ndebele state lost their political independence, and a unified, white-controlled Union of South Africa emerged in 1910
  • The mining economy made migrant labor a central institution and created new towns and compounds
  • Legislation such as the Natives Land Act of 1913 made racial restrictions on landholding a matter of national law

What remained continuous?

  • The new Union reserved parliamentary seats for whites while retaining the limited Cape franchise for some African and Coloured male voters and only a handful of non-white voters in Natal
  • African dispossession of land, begun in earlier settler expansion, continued
  • African communities continued to organize political and religious institutions, which would later contribute to the founding of political organizations in the early twentieth century

Who experienced change most directly, and who continuity? African workers in mines and displaced rural communities experienced rapid change, while the settler political elites, Boer and British, experienced reconciliation among themselves and continuity of white dominance. Why the pattern matters: The unification of South Africa reconciled the two white communities partly at the expense of African political rights, a reminder that political consolidation could reinforce, not dissolve, racial exclusion.

Section 104.19

German South West Africa and the Genocide of the Herero and Nama, 1904–1908

German involvement in what is now Namibia began when the merchant Adolf Lüderitz purchased land at Angra Pequena in 1883, and the German government placed the area under its protection in 1884. The territory was home to several communities, including the Ovaherero, cattle pastoralists in the central highlands, the Nama, who lived in the south and had their own political leaders, and the Ovambo in the north, whom Germany did not subdue in this period. These societies had dealt with traders, missionaries, and colonial officials for decades, and relations were shaped by competition for water and grazing, trade, debt, and shifting alliances. In the 1890s, under Governor Theodor Leutwein, Germany expanded by treaties and alliances, exploiting rivalries, while European settlement grew. The rinderpest epidemic of 1897 devastated African cattle herds, strengthening settler economic power, and the building of railways and a growing settler population increased pressure on land. Credit practices, under which traders loaned goods against cattle and then claimed seizures for debt, further stripped Ovaherero of livestock and land.

In January 1904, Ovaherero leaders under Samuel Maharero launched an uprising. They attacked farms and killed German settlers, mostly men, in the first days. Germany sent reinforcements under General Lothar von Trotha, who arrived in June 1904 with instructions to crush the rebellion. In August 1904, at the Waterberg, German forces attacked and defeated the main Ovaherero force, which included large numbers of non-combatants, many with their cattle. The Germans then pursued the survivors into the Omaheke desert and sealed off the waterholes, so that many died of thirst. In October 1904, Trotha issued a proclamation, commonly called the extermination order, stating that any Herero found within German borders would be shot, and that women and children would be driven back. The order was later rescinded after pressure from within the German government, but the policy of expulsion and killing had already been carried out.

Survivors who surrendered were placed in camps, such as those at Swakopmund and, for Nama, Shark Island near Lüderitz, where they were used as forced labor for the military, railways, and private firms, with very high mortality from exposure, disease, and hunger. Meanwhile, the Nama, led by Hendrik Witbooi and later fighters such as Jakob Morenga, rose in October 1904 and fought a guerrilla war that lasted until 1907 to 1908. Witbooi was killed in October 1905. The Germans responded with the same combination of military force, forced labor, and internment. The camps were closed in 1908, and the colonial government expropriated the land and cattle of the defeated communities and introduced laws restricting their movement and requiring them to carry passes.

Casualty figures must be treated cautiously. There was no reliable census before the war, and the available estimates rely on German administrative figures, missionary records, and demographic reconstruction. Historians commonly estimate that Ovaherero numbered several tens of thousands before the war and that a majority of them died, and that a substantial share of the Nama, perhaps around half, also died, but the precise numbers are uncertain. The scale of destruction is nonetheless not in doubt, and in 2021 the German government formally acknowledged the atrocities as genocide and began a reconciliation process with Namibia, a process that has continued to involve debate about its terms.

The escalation of violence resulted from the interaction of several factors: settler demands for land and labor, a military command that regarded the conflict as a "race war," German racial ideology, administrative policies of expropriation and forced labor, and the decisions of specific officers and officials. The case requires independent treatment, not treatment as an illustration of generic colonial violence, because it involved an explicit policy of destroying communities, the systematic use of desert and camps as instruments of death, and a long afterlife in German and Namibian memory. It also deserves attention for the agency of Ovaherero and Nama people, who resisted, fled across borders into British territory, negotiated, survived in the camps and as laborers, and rebuilt communities and social relationships in the aftermath.

Section 104.20

Africa by 1914: Colonial Systems, African Resistance, Economic Transformation, and the European Balance of Power

By 1914 most of Africa was under European colonial control, but the character of that control varied. Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Spain, and Italy held territories that ranged from settler colonies, as in South Africa and Algeria, to protectorates governed through African rulers, as in Northern Nigeria, Uganda, Tunisia, and Morocco, to concessionary regimes, as in French Equatorial Africa, and to the Belgian Congo, which had been annexed from Leopold's personal rule. Only Ethiopia and Liberia retained recognized independence. Both faced severe pressures: Ethiopia from the powers around its borders, and Liberia from debt, border disputes with neighboring colonies, and its own internal inequality between the descendants of settlers and indigenous peoples.

Colonial administrations differed in method. Direct rule replaced African authorities with European officials and appointed agents, indirect rule governed through selected African rulers under European supervision, concessionary government delegated authority to private companies, and settler colonialism gave land and political power to European immigrants. In practice these categories overlapped, and in all of them African intermediaries were indispensable. The consequences for African sovereignty were profound. African rulers were removed or subordinated, African law was displaced or codified under colonial supervision, land was alienated, and taxation and compulsory labor reshaped economic life.

Economically, colonial governments encouraged export-oriented production: palm products, cocoa, cotton, and peanuts from African farmers, rubber and minerals from concessions and mines, and plantations. Railways and ports were built to carry exports to the coast, and their layout reflected the interests of colonial exporters more than those of African internal commerce. Whose interests these infrastructures served is a central historical question. They brought new commercial opportunities for some Africans, such as cocoa farmers and traders, and also brought dispossession, forced labor, and vulnerability to world markets. Education and health institutions, usually run by missions, expanded, creating a small educated class that would later shape political movements, and religious change, especially the spread of Christianity and Islam, continued.

African responses took many forms: armed resistance, religious mobilization, as in the Mahdist state and Maji Maji, diplomacy and adaptation, labor resistance such as flight and strikes, and efforts to preserve cultural and political institutions. Resistance was neither universal nor uniform. Cooperation with colonial authorities often reflected constrained choices, and not consent. Historians therefore avoid the older dichotomy between "resistors" and "collaborators" and instead examine the strategies of individuals and communities under changing conditions.

The Scramble also altered Europe's international position. Colonial rivalry produced crises, at Fashoda in 1898 and over Morocco in 1905 and 1911, that shaped alliance politics and, as Chapter 107 explains, contributed to the diplomatic alignments before 1914. Colonial resources, soldiers, and ambitions would be drawn into the First World War, whose colonial dimensions are treated in Chapters 108 to 110. Chapter 105 places Africa within the broader global imperial order, and Chapter 106 compares the domestic political systems of the powers that carried out these policies. Neither European conquest nor the later African independence examined in Chapter 128 was historically inevitable. Each emerged from contested decisions and circumstances.

Historical Debate

Economic Interests, Strategic Rivalry, Ideology, and African Resistance in Explaining the Scramble

The question: Why did European powers partition Africa so quickly after 1870, and which factors deserve the greatest weight?

Interpretation A: Economic explanations. Writers such as J. A. Hobson, in 1902, and later Marxist authors including V. I. Lenin, argued that capitalist economies required overseas outlets for surplus capital and goods and that imperialism followed from this need. Later historians have refined and challenged the argument, noting that most European overseas investment went to places other than tropical Africa, but others emphasize the role of financial and commercial interests and the extraction of African resources and labor.
Interpretation B: Strategic and political explanations. Ronald Robinson and John Gallagher, in Africa and the Victorians (1961), argued that the British takeover was driven mainly by strategic concern for the route to India, especially after the crisis in Egypt in 1882, and by the reactions of policymakers to crises on the periphery. Others stress national prestige, domestic politics, and the "social imperialist" use of colonial success to build public support.
Interpretation C: Ideology and African-centered explanations. Some scholars emphasize racial and civilizing ideologies as shaping what was thinkable and justifiable, while others, drawing on African history, in the tradition associated with the UNESCO General History of Africa, stress that events inside Africa, such as the crises of states, the activities of African rulers and merchants, and African resistance, shaped the timing and form of conquest, so that the Scramble cannot be explained by European motives alone.

Evidence: Each interpretation relies on different evidence: trade and investment statistics, diplomatic correspondence and cabinet papers, missionary and colonial propaganda, and African oral and written sources. Many specialists now accept that multiple factors interacted, differing by region and by power, and that no single cause explains every case.

Why it matters: The weight assigned to economic, strategic, ideological, or African factors affects how historians describe responsibility, how they interpret the gap between public justification and outcome, and whether the Scramble is told as a European story or as a contested transformation of African societies.

Cause and Effect

Partition, Conquest, Colonial Administration, African Responses, and the Consequences of Empire

Long-term causes

Industrial growth and global commercial networks, the earlier abolition campaigns and missionary involvement, existing European coastal enclaves, and technological change (quinine, steamships, rifles) that lowered the cost of expansion.

Short-term causes

The Egyptian financial crisis and the occupation of 1882, Leopold's Congo ambitions, the German entry into colonization in 1884, Franco-British and Italian rivalries, and the pressures of chartered companies and local officers.

Event or transformation

Between about 1880 and 1914 treaties, the Berlin framework, and military campaigns converted claims into colonial rule over most of the continent, while African states and communities resisted, negotiated, or adapted.

Immediate effects

Loss of political independence for most African states, destruction of ruling institutions, taxation and forced labor, land alienation, population loss in the Congo, East Africa, and South West Africa, and the creation of colonial bureaucracies and armies.

Long-term consequences

Export economies and railways oriented toward the coast, colonial boundaries that later became state borders, new African elites and political movements, durable settler land systems in southern Africa, and a legacy of memory and restitution debates.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the Scramble. Outcomes varied with African military and diplomatic choices, geography, disease, finance, and European rivalry, as Adwa and Omdurman together show.

Historical complexity: Conquest depended on African soldiers, porters, and intermediaries, and colonial rule rested on partnership with some African authorities, even as it dispossessed many others. Consequences also reached Europe, in diplomatic crises, military institutions, and public debates.

Chapter Summary

Between about 1870 and 1914, European states and chartered companies partitioned and conquered most of Africa. This chapter has argued that the process cannot be understood as a single diplomatic act or as the inevitable result of European technological superiority. It was a series of linked processes, claim, treaty, conquest, and administration, each of which depended on African as well as European decisions.

Africa before the Scramble contained kingdoms, caliphates, empires, city-states, merchant networks, religious institutions, and societies without centralized rulers. European holdings were limited to a few zones such as Algeria, Senegal, the Cape, and coastal posts. Disease, geography, African military power, and the low incentive to bear administrative costs had restrained territorial expansion. Changes in technology, finance, and strategic rivalry altered the calculation after 1870.

European motives were multiple and interacted: commercial expectations, strategic anxiety about the Suez Canal and the Nile, national prestige after the unifications of Germany and Italy, missionary and humanitarian claims, and racial ideologies of civilization. The chapter distinguished public justification from outcome, since governments that claimed to suppress slavery frequently imposed compulsory labor. Policy was shaped by lobbyists, chartered companies, and officers on the spot as well as by governments.

The Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885, attended by fourteen states and no African representative, set rules on trade and navigation in the Congo basin and on the notification and sufficiency of authority for coastal claims. It did not divide the continent or draw its borders. By strengthening the principle of effective occupation, it accelerated the push inland, and boundary-making followed through bilateral treaties.

In West Africa, France fought Samori Touré and other rulers with the help of African soldiers, Britain deposed Asante's king and conquered the Sokoto Caliphate and Niger basin, and both destroyed or subordinated Dahomey and Benin. The seizure of royal objects from Benin shows how conquest attacked cultural as well as political sovereignty. German colonization in Togoland and Kamerun, and Portuguese expansion from older footholds, show the variety of colonial relationships.

In Central Africa, Leopold II's Congo Free State, a state formally separate from Belgium, built a system of extraction based on rubber quotas, hostage-taking, and armed force, with catastrophic human consequences whose exact scale is uncertain. Congolese testimony, missionary reports, Casement's inquiry, and a reform campaign led to Belgian annexation in 1908, but coercive labor survived. French concessions and Portuguese Angola show related systems.

In North and East Africa, the Egyptian crisis, French protectorates, and Moroccan and Libyan crises show strategic rivalry in the Mediterranean, while the Mahdist state, Omdurman, and Fashoda link African resistance and conquest to Anglo-French rivalry. British Uganda and Kenya, and German East Africa and the Maji Maji War, show railways, taxation, forced cotton cultivation, and military repression. In Ethiopia, Menelik II and Taytu Betul defeated Italy at Adwa, though Ethiopian expansion itself subordinated other peoples.

In southern Africa, mineral wealth, settler expansion, the Anglo-Zulu War, the South African War, and the Union of 1910 consolidated white political power and entrenched racial exclusion, culminating in the Natives Land Act of 1913. In South West Africa, settler land hunger and military policy produced genocide against the Ovaherero and Nama. By 1914 most of Africa lived under colonial rule, yet African resistance, adaptation, and institutions remained significant, and the Scramble had reshaped European diplomacy and prepared the setting for the First World War.

Key Terms

New Imperialism
The intensified territorial expansion of European and other powers from about 1870 to 1914, marked by the partition of Africa and much of the Pacific and parts of Asia.
Colonialism
The political, economic, and cultural domination of one society by another through settlement or administration.
Protectorate
A territory whose ruler formally remains but whose external relations, and often internal affairs, are controlled by a protecting power.
Sphere of influence
An area in which a power claimed exclusive rights as against other powers, without necessarily governing it.
Effective occupation
The principle, associated with Articles 34 and 35 of the 1885 General Act, that claims must be supported by authority sufficient to protect existing rights.
General Act of Berlin
The treaty of February 26, 1885, on trade and navigation in the Congo basin and on conditions for coastal claims, which did not divide Africa.
Chartered company
A commercial enterprise granted by a government the right to claim, administer, and defend territory, such as the Royal Niger Company.
Concession company
A firm granted exclusive rights to exploit resources in a defined colonial territory, often with state support.
Forced labor
Work exacted under threat of punishment, through tax obligations, quotas, conscription, or hostage-taking.
Colonial extraction
The transfer of resources and labor from colonized societies on terms set by the colonizer.
Direct rule
Colonial government carried out mainly by European officials and appointed agents who replaced existing authorities.
Indirect rule
Colonial government that used selected African rulers under European supervision, as in Northern Nigeria.
Settler colonialism
Colonization in which immigrants take land and political power, as in Algeria, Kenya's highlands, and South Africa.
Condominium
A territory under the joint sovereignty of two states, as the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, where Britain held practical control.
Suzerainty
A relationship in which a dominant state holds nominal overlordship over another that governs itself, as the Ottoman claim over Egypt until 1914.
Mahdi
In Islamic tradition, a divinely guided redeemer; the title adopted by Muhammad Ahmad in 1881.
Caliphate
A state led by a caliph, a successor to the Prophet; here the Sokoto Caliphate, founded in the early nineteenth century.
Asantehene
The king of Asante, whose authority was symbolized by the Golden Stool.
Oba
The king of Benin, whose court produced the royal brass and ivory works seized in 1897.
Force Publique
The army of the Congo Free State and later the Belgian Congo, composed of African soldiers under European officers.
Askari / tirailleur
African soldiers serving in European colonial armies, such as the tirailleurs sénégalais in French forces.
Maxim gun
An early automatic machine gun, used by colonial forces at Omdurman and in other battles.
Scorched earth
The deliberate destruction of crops, villages, and food stores to deny resources or compel surrender.
Concentration camp
A camp for internment of civilians without trial, as used by Britain in the South African War and by Germany in South West Africa.
Genocide
The deliberate destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group; the term was coined in the twentieth century, and historians apply it to the Ovaherero and Nama case.
Civilizing mission
The claim that colonial rule would bring progress, law, and religion to colonized peoples, used to justify conquest.
Legitimate commerce
Nineteenth-century trade in goods such as palm oil and peanuts promoted as an alternative to the slave trade, often reliant on enslaved labor.
Hut tax
A colonial tax levied per household, often payable in cash or labor, which pushed households into wage work or cash crops.
Pacification
A colonial euphemism for military subjugation of a population.
Maji
The Swahili word for water, used for the protective medicine of the Maji Maji movement.

Reading colonial-era sources

Documents by colonial officials often use euphemisms such as "pacification," "protection," and "civilization." Retrospective artistic works, oral traditions recorded decades later, archival transcriptions, editorial paraphrases, and scholarly interpretations should be distinguished and cited as such.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 104 matters because it shows that the Scramble for Africa was a contested transformation and not a diplomatic drawing of lines. Treaties, conferences, and maps recorded European intentions, but control had to be won, negotiated, and maintained in Africa, through campaigns, alliances, taxation, and labor, and it often rested on African soldiers and intermediaries. Africans resisted, adapted, and negotiated, and in Ethiopia they defended their independence by force.

The chapter also reveals how deeply empire was embedded in European history. The motives behind expansion came from industrial economies, national rivalries, religious movements, and racial ideas, and the consequences returned to Europe in the diplomatic crises of Fashoda and Morocco, in new colonial armies and bureaucracies, in the profits and scandals of companies, and in debates over humanitarian reform. Chapter 105 places these developments within the global imperial order, and Chapters 106 and 107 show how political systems and alliance politics were shaped by them.

For Volume IV, this chapter sits at the point where the industrial, ideological, and political transformations of the nineteenth century combine in empire. It shows that the same technologies and ideas that transformed European societies could also be used to dispossess and coerce others, and that violence in the Congo, East Africa, and South West Africa was bound up with institutions of the modern state and economy. The memory of these events, in restitution debates, commemorations, and legal recognition of genocide, continues into the present.

Finally, the chapter's insistence on African agency is a methodological as well as a moral point. A history that begins with Adwa, and that gives Samori, Béhanzin, Yaa Asantewaa, Mkwawa, the Ovaherero, and the Nama their own strategies, shows that outcomes were not inevitable. The colonial order of 1914 was powerful but recent and contested, and it would be transformed again by the First World War and, as Chapter 128 explains, by decolonization after 1945.
