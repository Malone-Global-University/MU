Byzantium after Justinian

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 565 the emperor in Constantinople ruled a Mediterranean empire that reached from southern Spain to the borders of Persia. By 718 the same imperial office, in the same city, governed a much smaller state centered on Anatolia, the Aegean, and a few detached western territories. The empire had not ended. It had survived by changing.

Chapter 35 followed Latin Christianity as it moved north and east, into Scandinavia, Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary. That story can easily give the impression that the history of Christian Europe was the history of the Latin West. This chapter begins Part VI by correcting that impression. In the very centuries when Frankish, Anglo-Saxon, and Lombard kingdoms were consolidating in the former western provinces, the Roman Empire continued to exist in the East, governed from Constantinople, taxing its subjects, minting gold coin, maintaining armies and a bureaucracy, and understanding itself without hesitation as Roman. As Chapter 28 established, the fifth-century fragmentation of western imperial authority did not end the Roman Empire. It ended Roman rule in the West.

The period examined here, from the death of the emperor Justinian in 565 to the defeat of a great Arab siege of Constantinople in 717 and 718, was one of the most severe tests any European state has survived. The empire lost most of Justinian's western conquests to Lombards and Visigoths. It faced Avars and Slavic-speaking groups in the Balkans, saw Persian armies occupy Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, and then watched Arab armies, newly united under the early caliphate, take those same provinces permanently within a generation of Persia's defeat. Constantinople was besieged repeatedly. The empire's fiscal base shrank, its cities changed, and its military and provincial administration was gradually rebuilt on different foundations.

The chapter's central argument is that Byzantine survival was not the preservation of an unchanged late Roman state. It was a transformation. Territorial contraction, plague, warfare, and administrative experiment produced a smaller, more compact, more Greek-speaking, and more militarized empire. Yet continuity was equally real: the emperor, the imperial capital, Roman law and political identity, Christian imperial ideology, taxation, diplomacy, and a defensible territorial core all persisted. The seventh century is therefore best read not as the fall of Byzantium but as the crisis that made medieval Byzantium possible.

A word about names is essential at the outset. “Byzantine” is a modern scholarly label, derived from the old name of the city, Byzantion, and popularized in western Europe long after the empire itself had disappeared. The people who lived in the empire called their state the Roman Empire and called themselves Romans, in Greek Rhomaioi. This textbook uses “Byzantine” as a convenient analytical term, but readers should remember that no emperor, soldier, tax collector, or bishop of this period would have said that he governed a “Byzantine Empire.” Section 36.2 explains the problem in detail.

The evidence for these two centuries is uneven and often difficult. Contemporary narrative history is thin, especially for the mid-seventh century, a period sometimes described as a historiographical dark age. Historians depend on chronicles written in Greek, Syriac, Armenian, Latin, and Arabic, many composed later and from different religious and political viewpoints. They also use poems and sermons composed for the court and church, papal letters, military handbooks, lead seals, coins, inscriptions, papyri from Egypt and the Negev, hagiography, and archaeological evidence from fortifications, churches, and urban sites. Each type of source preserves some experiences and silences others.

Within Volume II, this chapter sets a different tempo from the chapters of Part V. Where Part V traced the construction of new western kingdoms and the expansion of Latin Christendom, Part VI treats eastern Christian Europe as a central component of European history, not as a peripheral counterpart to the Latin West. The geography matters: Constantinople straddled the straits between the Balkans and Anatolia, a position introduced in Chapter 2, and the empire it ruled was at once Balkan, Anatolian, Aegean, and Mediterranean. Chapter 37 will follow the transformed empire through Iconoclasm and recovery. The present chapter ends at the moment when its survival was demonstrated, in 717 and 718, before that story begins.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the territorial extent and principal strains of the empire Justinian left behind in 565.
  2. Explain why “Byzantine” is a modern label and how contemporaries understood their state as Roman.
  3. Trace the loss or fragmentation of imperial authority in Italy, Spain, and the Balkans after 565.
  4. Analyze the exarchates as an administrative response to distance and military emergency, distinguishing them from the later theme system.
  5. Explain the course and consequences of the last great Roman-Persian war, from 602 to 628.
  6. Assess the reasons Constantinople withstood the siege of 626 without describing the city as impregnable.
  7. Evaluate multiple interacting causes of the loss of Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and North Africa to the early caliphate.
  8. Compare imperial territory around 565, 628, and 700, and explain why contraction did not end the empire.
  9. Interpret the historical debate over the origins of the themes and avoid attributing them to a single reform.
  10. Connect demographic, urban, and fiscal change to the everyday experience of different communities.
  11. Distinguish contemporary sources from later chronicle tradition when reconstructing seventh-century events.
  12. Evaluate competing periodizations of the transition from Late Antiquity to the Byzantine Middle Ages.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the eastern Roman Empire survive the military, territorial, demographic, and administrative crises of the sixth and seventh centuries?
  2. What does it mean to call a state “the same” empire when its territory, language of administration, and institutions have changed substantially?
  3. Why did Justinian's western conquests prove so difficult to hold, and what were the consequences of overextension?
  4. Why did a spectacular Roman victory over Persia in 628 fail to secure the eastern provinces?
  5. How should historians weigh military, fiscal, demographic, political, and religious factors in explaining the early Arab conquests, without relying on a single cause?
  6. What made Constantinople so resilient, and in what ways was that resilience produced by geography, infrastructure, and human decisions together?
  7. How do the limits of the surviving evidence shape what historians can and cannot say about the seventh century?
  8. Is it more useful to describe the seventh century as decline, collapse, or transformation, and what is lost by choosing only one of these words?

Historical Context

The empire examined in this chapter was the product of developments traced in Part IV. Diocletian's administrative reforms and Constantine's founding of Constantinople as a new imperial capital (Chapter 25) established a Christian empire with an eastern center of gravity. The councils and theological controversies of Late Antiquity (Chapter 26) left lasting divisions among Christians of the eastern provinces, above all between those who accepted the Council of Chalcedon of 451 and those, especially many Christians in Egypt and Syria, who rejected its formulation of Christ's nature. The migrations and invasions of the fourth and fifth centuries (Chapter 27) then broke up western imperial authority (Chapter 28) while the East, with its larger cities, richer tax base, and more defensible capital, survived.

The sixth century brought a determined effort to reverse western loss. Justinian, who reigned from 527 to 565, directed the reconquest of Vandal North Africa in 533 and 534, a long war in Ostrogothic Italy beginning in 535 and ending in the 550s, and the acquisition of a strip of southeastern Iberia in the 550s. He also codified Roman law, built or rebuilt fortifications across the Balkans and the eastern frontier, and sponsored the great church of Hagia Sophia, dedicated in 537. Chapter 29 has already examined the kingdoms that Justinian's armies displaced in Italy and Iberia. These achievements were purchased at heavy cost. The wars required taxation that provincial populations resented, the eastern frontier with Sasanian Persia remained dangerous and sometimes required tribute, and from 541 and 542 bubonic plague struck the empire and recurred at intervals for roughly two centuries, a catastrophe whose demographic scale scholars continue to debate.

The empire's two principal rivals shaped its fate. Sasanian Persia, ruled from Ctesiphon in Mesopotamia, was a sophisticated imperial state whose wars with Rome had been fought, with interruptions, for centuries over Armenia, Mesopotamia, and the Caucasus. In the north, the steppe corridor introduced in Chapter 2 continued to deliver new powers to the Danube frontier. The Avars, a steppe confederation that arrived in the Carpathian basin in the 560s, and various Slavic-speaking groups, whose organization and identities are poorly documented, confronted the Balkan provinces. In Arabia, meanwhile, developments largely invisible to Constantinople before the 630s were transforming the political world to the southeast.

Students should also understand the languages and geography of imperial government. Latin remained important in law and in parts of the army and administration, but Greek was the common language of most eastern provinces and of the church. Egypt and Syria had rich Coptic and Syriac literary traditions. Anatolia, which Chapter 2 identified as a peninsula neighboring the Balkans and separated from them by the straits, contained diverse populations of Greek speakers, Armenians, and others. The empire was thus multilingual and multiethnic, and its Roman identity was political and legal, not national.

Finally, this chapter rests on evidence unlike that available for the Latin West. Imperial coinage and lead seals attest to administrative and fiscal practice. The Chronicon Paschale, compiled in Constantinople in the 620s, preserves a contemporary account of the siege of 626, while the court poet George of Pisidia composed verse celebrating Heraclius. The Armenian history attributed to Sebeos and several Syriac chronicles preserve eastern perspectives. Theophanes the Confessor, who wrote early in the ninth century, is indispensable for the chronology of the seventh, but he used earlier sources that are now lost and shaped them to his own concerns. Arabic historical writing about the conquests, such as that of al-Tabari, dates from the ninth and tenth centuries. Historians must therefore read across languages and across a considerable gap between events and their earliest extended narration.

Section 36.1

The Empire Justinian Left Behind, 565

When Justinian died in November 565, the emperor in Constantinople still ruled Constantinople and the Balkans, Anatolia, Syria and Palestine, Egypt, North Africa, a substantial part of Italy including Rome and Ravenna, Sicily and other Mediterranean islands, and a stretch of coast in southern Iberia. The map was impressive, and it is easy to read it as the culmination of a restored Roman world. It is more useful to read it as a snapshot of an empire under strain. The outline of this textbook does not give Justinian a chapter of his own, so this section provides only the baseline that students need in order to understand what came after.

Justinian's legacy was mixed and enduring. The reconquest of Africa and Italy brought rich provinces and the city of Rome back under imperial rule, but the Italian war was long and devastating, and the western territories required garrisons and revenue that the empire could barely supply. The compilation of Roman law, completed in the 530s and later known as the Corpus Juris Civilis, preserved and organized the legal inheritance of Rome and would shape legal thought in both East and West for centuries. Fortifications on the Danube, in the Balkans, and along the eastern frontier were costly, and their very number suggests how many frontiers the state was trying to hold. Hagia Sophia, dedicated in 537, expressed an imperial Christianity that joined the emperor to the church. Religious policy, meanwhile, sought compromise between Chalcedonian and miaphysite Christians without achieving lasting reconciliation.

The plague that appeared in 541 and 542 compounded these pressures. Contemporaries such as Procopius described heavy mortality in Constantinople, and recurrent outbreaks continued into the eighth century. Historians disagree about the scale of mortality and about how far plague, rather than war and fiscal pressure, explains later weakness, and some have proposed that climatic disturbances in the 530s and 540s also contributed to hardship. The responsible conclusion is that recurring epidemic disease reduced the empire's labor force, tax base, and manpower to a degree that is real but difficult to measure.

The analytical question for this chapter is therefore not whether Justinian succeeded or failed. It is how difficult the enlarged empire would be to defend after his death. His successors inherited a state with long frontiers facing Persia, the steppe, and the Danube, western provinces dependent on sea communication, severe fiscal strain that Justin II's new regime portrayed as an exhausted treasury, and a population affected by recurrent plague. They inherited a Roman Empire, not a different civilization. The challenge was to maintain it.

Section 36.2

Romans, “Byzantines,” and a Changing Empire

Why do textbooks call the surviving eastern empire “Byzantine”? The modern historical label derives from Byzantion, the ancient name associated with Constantinople. Western medieval and early modern writers often distinguished the eastern empire from ancient or western Rome in various ways, and the now-standard scholarly use of “Byzantine Empire” developed gradually rather than through one identifiable act of naming. The empire's inhabitants normally called their state Roman, in Greek Basileia ton Rhomaion, and themselves Rhomaioi, Romans. The adjective “Byzantine” could occasionally appear in narrower geographical or cultural senses, but it was not their ordinary political name for the empire. Constantinople was “New Rome,” its senate was a Roman senate, and its emperor held the Roman imperial office. Many neighboring traditions, especially Arabic and Persian ones, used names derived from Rome.

Historical Vocabulary
Romans and “Byzantines”

Byzantine is a modern convention for the Roman Empire as it existed from roughly the sixth or seventh century, centered on Constantinople, until 1453. Historians disagree about where the label should begin, and some use it for the whole post-Constantinian eastern empire. In this textbook it is an analytical convenience, not a claim that the empire's inhabitants ceased to be Roman. The term does not describe a different state, a new ethnicity, or a self-conscious break with the past. Its danger is that it encourages students to treat the eastern empire as something merely derivative or exotic. Chapter 28 has already shown that the Roman Empire continued in the East after 476, and this chapter assumes that continuity throughout.

Nevertheless the empire changed in the seventh century, and changes can be named without inventing a sharp date on which Rome became Byzantium. Territorial contraction removed Latin-speaking western provinces from effective control and reduced the share of the empire where Latin had been an administrative language. Greek, already the language of most eastern provinces and of the church, became increasingly predominant in administration and imperial ceremony. Heraclius adopted the Greek title basileus in preference to older Latin-derived titles, an adoption traditionally dated to about 629, though no constitutional act made the empire Greek. The structures of the army and provincial government were reshaped, as later sections will show, and cities changed in size and function. Christian imperial ideology, already powerful under Constantine and Justinian, was reinforced by the crises of the century, which many contemporaries interpreted in religious terms.

At the same time Roman law, the imperial office, fiscal institutions, diplomatic practice, and political identity continued. Emperors issued laws and received embassies as Roman emperors. Tax collectors operated within a system inherited from late Roman fiscal practice. The Roman army, though reorganized, remained the imperial army. This distinction between change and continuity governs the whole of Part VI. The empire examined here was Roman in its self-understanding and in much of its institutional substance, and medieval in the sense that its territory, economy, and political culture differed substantially from those of the age of Constantine or even of Justinian. The following sections trace how that combination came about.

Section 36.3

Lombards, Avars, and the Fragmentation of Justinian's West

The Italian settlement of Justinian did not last a generation. In 568 a Lombard confederation under King Alboin, which included groups of other origins, crossed into northern Italy. Italy had been devastated by the long Gothic war and by plague, and imperial garrisons were thin. The Lombards occupied Pavia and much of the Po valley and soon established dukedoms, notably at Spoleto and Benevento, in the center and south. Constantinople, which was also dealing with Persia and the Danube, could not mobilize the resources to reconquer the peninsula. The result, for more than two centuries, was a divided Italy in which Lombard kingdoms and duchies coexisted with imperial enclaves. The imperial territories included Ravenna and its surrounding Adriatic coast, Rome and its duchy, Naples, Calabria and Apulia in parts, Sicily, and the lagoon communities of the northern Adriatic that would become Venice.

This was not a simple division between “Roman” and “barbarian.” Lombard rulers adopted much Roman administrative and legal practice over time, and by the seventh century many were Catholic Christians rather than Arian or pagan, a process connected to Chapter 29 and Chapter 35. Imperial Italy had its own character. Rome was increasingly dominated by its bishop, whose relations with the emperor were sometimes close and sometimes tense. Ravenna remained the seat of imperial administration and the center of the exarchate discussed in Section 36.4. Sicily, which was less exposed, became an important source of grain, revenue, and eventually imperial attention. Imperial Italy remained part of the Mediterranean Roman world even as its political center lay far away.

Spain followed a different path. The province Justinian's forces had established in the south-east had always been small and difficult to defend against the Visigothic kingdom. In the early seventh century the Visigothic kings progressively reduced it, and by around 624 the last imperial possessions on the Iberian mainland were gone, a development relevant to Chapter 29's account of Visigothic Iberia. North Africa, which had been recovered from the Vandals, remained under imperial control for the moment, defended by local commanders and treated as a province of great importance, but its Mediterranean position would become a point of vulnerability later.

The Balkans experienced the most consequential pressures. The Avars, a steppe power that established itself in the Carpathian basin in the 560s, dominated much of the Danube frontier and demanded tribute from the empire. Slavic-speaking groups, whose political organization was fragmented and who left no written records of their own, moved across the Danube in raids and, increasingly, in settlements. The imperial city of Sirmium fell to the Avars in 582. Raiding parties reached the walls of Thessalonica, and the collection of miracle stories associated with Saint Demetrius provides a rare, if hagiographical, glimpse of the siege of that city.

Students should avoid the shorthand that “the Slavs invaded and replaced the population.” The evidence, much of it archaeological and toponymic as well as textual, indicates something more complicated. Raids, warfare, migration, and settlement overlapped. Local populations did not disappear, and cities such as Thessalonica, along with coastal and island communities, remained under imperial control. Some inland regions slipped from effective imperial administration for generations, while Slavic-speaking communities sometimes accepted, resisted, or negotiated relations with the empire. Later centuries, as Chapter 38 and Chapter 39 will show, brought imperial reconquest and reintegration of many Balkan districts. The seventh-century Balkans are best described as a zone of shifting control and mixed populations, not of sudden replacement.

Section 36.4

Maurice, the Exarchates, and Governing an Overstretched Empire

Justin II (565–578) refused to continue paying tribute to the Avars and reopened war with Persia in 572, with unhappy results. His successor Tiberius II (578–582) struggled with the same pressures. Maurice, who took the throne in 582, was an experienced general who attempted to stabilize all fronts at once. In Persia, a civil war gave him an opportunity. When the Sasanian king Khusrau II was driven from power in 590, Maurice supported his restoration, and the settlement of 591 gave the empire advantageous borders, including more of Armenia. In the Balkans he sent armies across the Danube against the Avars and Slavic groups in the 590s, with mixed results and at heavy fiscal cost.

Maurice's difficulties were structural. The empire's revenues could not easily support campaigns on several distant fronts at once, and attempts to economize angered the army. In 602 troops on the Danube, ordered to winter north of the river, mutinied and acclaimed an officer named Phocas. Maurice, whose rule had also been weakened by fiscal measures and by tensions in Constantinople, was overthrown and killed. The event is a major turning point because it ended a period of relative stabilization and, as the next section shows, handed Khusrau II a pretext for renewed war.

Maurice's reign is also where historians most clearly see administrative adaptation to the problem of distance. In Italy and Africa the empire had long maintained a division between civil and military authority. The strain of the Lombard war and the need for rapid decision-making, in provinces where messages and reinforcements by sea might take weeks, led to the appointment of exarchs, officials who combined high civil and military powers. An exarch of Ravenna is attested by the 580s, and an exarch of Carthage by the 590s.

Historical Vocabulary
Exarchate

An exarchate was a western imperial province, notably centered at Ravenna for Italy and Carthage for Africa, governed by an exarch who combined broad civil and military authority under the emperor. The institution responded to distance, military emergency, and the need for powerful regional commanders able to act without waiting for instructions from Constantinople. It brought the two kinds of authority together in strategically exposed territories. It was not, however, the later theme system. The exarchates were western provinces under a particular combination of conditions, and the themes developed from a different set of circumstances in Anatolia. The comparison is useful because both illustrate how emergencies pushed the empire toward combining civil and military command, but they should not be confused.

The significance of the exarchates extended beyond local governance. The exarch of Carthage, Heraclius the Elder, was the father of the future emperor, and it was from Carthage in 610 that the younger Heraclius sailed to overthrow Phocas. Distant provinces, with their own resources and powerful commanders, could now influence the center. The exarchates thus illustrate a theme of this chapter, which is that the empire's survival depended on institutional flexibility as much as on traditional continuity.

Primary Source Window

The Strategikon and the Problem of Military Adaptation

Creator: Traditionally attributed to the emperor Maurice; the actual author is uncertain · Date: c. late sixth or early seventh century · Type: A Greek military handbook for commanders

Context: The Strategikon is a practical manual for army officers, composed in the context of the Persian, Avar, and Slavic wars described in this chapter. Its authorship and exact date remain debated, and while it is conventionally linked with Maurice's reign, scholars treat the attribution cautiously. A standard English translation by George T. Dennis is available in libraries.

Paraphrase of the sense of the work The text instructs commanders in the organization, training, and deployment of cavalry and infantry. It devotes separate chapters to the characteristic methods of different enemies: Persians, Avars and other steppe peoples, Slavic groups, and others. It advises commanders on ambushes, river crossings, foraging, camp security, and the use of scouts. On the Slavs, it describes dispersed communities living in difficult wooded and marshy terrain, and recommends tactics suited to that environment. This is a summary of the work's content, not a quotation.

Analysis: The Strategikon shows how Roman military writers conceptualized warfare against several different kinds of opponents and how seriously they treated adaptation. It reveals an army that expected to fight on multiple fronts under varied conditions. But the source describes how a military writer thought campaigns should be conducted, not necessarily how every army behaved. It was written by and for officers, not for ordinary soldiers or the civilian populations who bore the costs of war, and its characterizations of enemies reflect Roman assumptions as well as observation.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about how the empire conceived of its different opponents?
  2. Who created it, and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from it about military organization and adaptation around 600?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, such as actual battlefield practice or civilian experience?
Section 36.5

Phocas, Heraclius, and the Last Great Roman-Persian War

Khusrau II declared that he was avenging Maurice, his former benefactor, and in 603 the Sasanian armies began a war that would last until 628. The conflict had ample geopolitical causes beyond the pretext, since Persia sought the territories and revenues of the frontier provinces, and the Roman regime of Phocas was unpopular and unstable. It would be a mistake to describe the war as Christianity against Zoroastrianism. It was an imperial struggle for territory, tribute, and strategic position, although religious language and symbols were later mobilized on both sides.

The war went badly for Rome. Persian armies took fortified cities in Mesopotamia and Armenia, advanced into Syria, and reached Anatolia. Phocas's regime lost authority, and in 610 Heraclius, arriving by sea from Carthage with his supporters, overthrew him and was acclaimed emperor in Constantinople. The new emperor inherited a catastrophe. Antioch fell in about 611, Damascus and other Syrian cities followed, and in 614 Persian forces took Jerusalem. The capture was accompanied by violence and by the removal of the relic of the True Cross. The role of Jewish communities in these events is described differently in different sources and is debated by modern historians. The Persian capture was a military and political event, not a permanent religious conquest, and the Persian authorities did not abolish Christianity in the city. In 619 Egypt, including Alexandria, fell to Persian forces. Roman loss of Egypt meant the end of the grain that had fed Constantinople and a crippling blow to imperial finances.

By the early 620s the empire seemed close to destruction. Persian armies were in Anatolia, Egypt was lost, and the Avars on the Danube posed a constant threat. Heraclius responded with a combination of reforms and risk. He reorganized the army, drew on church resources through loans that were later repaid, secured a truce with the Avars, and, beginning in 622, undertook a series of campaigns that shifted the war onto Persian territory. He used the sea and Armenia as avenues of approach and forged an alliance with forces of the Western Turkic Khaganate, whose khagan coordinated operations with him in the Caucasus; some later sources describe these allies in Khazar terms. Rather than concentrate on recovering the lost provinces directly, he struck at the Persian homeland, forcing the Sasanian regime to respond.

The strategy worked. At the end of 627, near the ruins of Nineveh, Heraclius's army defeated a Persian force, and the Sasanian court, already under strain from the war's costs, began to disintegrate. Khusrau II was deposed and executed in early 628, and his successors made peace and agreed to withdraw from Roman territory. The True Cross was returned to Jerusalem, in ceremonies traditionally dated to 630. Heraclius had accomplished something that few in 610 would have predicted.

The theme of this section is that the empire appeared close to destruction and then achieved an extraordinary military recovery, but that victory left both empires exhausted. Long war, plunder, and demographic and fiscal loss had weakened both Rome and Persia, while the political structure of Persia collapsed into succession struggles. Heraclius should not be portrayed as a modern national Greek liberator. He was a Roman emperor, born of a family with Armenian connections, who fought for imperial survival and Christian imperial prestige in a world of dynastic and imperial politics. The war's consequences would be shaped less by his personal triumph than by how much each empire had been weakened when a new power emerged in Arabia.

Section 36.6

Constantinople under Siege: 626

While Heraclius's army campaigned in the East, Constantinople faced its gravest threat so far. In the summer of 626 a Persian army under the general Shahrbaraz advanced to the Asian shore of the Bosporus at Chalcedon, while the Avar khagan brought a large force, accompanied by Slavic-speaking allies, to the Theodosian Land Walls. The Avars and Persians evidently coordinated their operations, and the aim was to cut the city off. Heraclius was far away, and defense rested on the patriarch Sergius, the patrician Bonus, and the city's garrison and population.

The Avars brought siege engines against the land walls and sought to ferry Persian troops across the straits using Slavic boats. The imperial fleet, however, controlled the waters, and the planned crossing failed. Slavic craft entering the Golden Horn were destroyed, and in early August the Avars withdrew. The contemporary Chronicon Paschale, compiled in Constantinople in the following years, and the poem of George of Pisidia on the siege provide the main near-contemporary evidence. Both are shaped by a religious interpretation in which the protection of the Virgin Mary figured prominently. Contemporary accounts strongly associated the city's deliverance with the protection of the Virgin, while later liturgical and historical tradition elaborated stories of Marian relics and images carried in protective processions. Scholars debate which objects, especially icons, were actually processed during the siege itself. Historians distinguish carefully between what the sources report about the siege and the memory that grew around it.

Why was Constantinople so difficult to take? The answer begins with geography. The city stands on a triangular peninsula, bounded by the Sea of Marmara, the Bosporus, and the Golden Horn, so that the landward approach is narrow. Constantinople's Theodosian Land Walls, built in the early fifth century and repaired thereafter, consisted of a moat, an outer wall, and a high inner wall with towers, forming a layered defense that the siege technology of the period could not easily overcome. The sea walls along the Marmara and Golden Horn, together with a harbor chain across the Horn, completed the system. Naval control mattered as much as the stone, since a besieger who could not cut the sea lanes could not starve the city. The state, moreover, concentrated resources, workshops, and supplies in the capital, and its government remained functional.

Yet Constantinople was not impregnable. Its survival was the result of defenses, geography, naval strength, logistics, political cohesion, and the specific weaknesses of its attackers acting together. The Avars lacked sea power, and the Persians could not transport their troops. A coordinated attack with naval superiority or internal betrayal could, and in 1204 did, take the city, as Chapter 41 will show. The siege of 626 demonstrated a model of survival that would recur: the capital withstood pressure that took the provinces.

Section 36.7

The Rise of Islam and the Loss of the Eastern Provinces

In the very years of Heraclius's recovery, a new political and religious movement was taking shape in Arabia. Muhammad had led the community of Muslims at Medina, and after his death in 632 his successors, the first caliphs, united much of Arabia and directed armies northward. The early caliphate, under the Rashidun caliphs until 661 and then the Umayyad dynasty, was not a single event but an expanding political order, and the Arab armies that confronted Rome and Persia drew on tribal and regional mobilization that gave them speed and flexibility. Terminology matters here. This textbook refers to Arab or early Muslim armies and to the caliphate, not to “Saracens,” a term used by Roman and western writers that blurs distinctions among peoples and periods.

The Arab campaigns against the empire began in the 630s. Imperial forces suffered defeats in southern Palestine and Syria, and in August 636, by the traditional dating, an Arab army defeated a Roman force at the Yarmuk river. Some scholars date the battle to 637 and the sources disagree on details. Yarmuk was a major defeat that opened the way to Damascus and the rest of Syria, but it was not the single instant at which the Near East was lost. Cities negotiated, resisted, or surrendered at different times, and Jerusalem submitted around 637 or 638. Heraclius, an aging emperor who had seen the provinces recovered and lost within a decade, withdrew toward Anatolia. Egypt was invaded in 639, and Alexandria capitulated in 641 or 642 after the emperor's death in 641. Over the following decades Arab forces advanced across North Africa, taking Carthage in 698 and ending the exarchate there, though the conquest of the Maghrib was gradual and met local resistance.

Why did the empire lose these provinces so quickly after its victory over Persia? Historians reject any single cause. The imperial army and treasury were exhausted by the war, and the recovered provinces had barely been reintegrated. Taxation had been heavy and the administration in the East had been disrupted by Persian occupation. The Arab armies were highly mobile, motivated, and politically unified, and they benefited from the collapse of the Sasanian state, which removed the principal competing power in the region and allowed the new caliphate to concentrate its forces. The geography of the East made it difficult to defend all frontiers at once, and imperial commanders often chose to preserve armies and fortified places while yielding open territory.

Religious divisions in the eastern provinces were real but should not be made determinative. Many Christians in Egypt and Syria were miaphysite, a term preferred today to the older and polemical word “Monophysite,” and they had a long history of tension with the Chalcedonian imperial church. Heraclius had tried to heal this division through compromise formulas, notably Monotheletism, which proposed that Christ had a single will, but the policy generated controversy rather than reconciliation. It would be wrong, however, to say that miaphysite Christians welcomed Arab rule universally or betrayed the empire. Responses varied by locality, class, and circumstance, and many communities simply adapted to a new regime that left their religious institutions in place and taxed them. The plague also did not cause the conquests, though it contributed to the long-term demographic weakness of the whole region.

The central analytical point is that Heraclius's victory over Persia restored Roman territory only briefly. The strategic world had changed again, and the loss of the East, which had provided much of the empire's revenue and manpower, would define its character for centuries.

Section 36.8

Losing Territory, Surviving as an Empire

The scale of change can be grasped by comparing the empire at three moments. Around 565 it included the Balkans and Anatolia, the whole of the Levant, Egypt, North Africa, a large part of Italy, Sicily, and territory in Iberia. By around 650 the Near East, Egypt, and much of the Balkan interior had been lost, and North Africa, Sicily, and parts of Italy remained under strain. Around 700 the imperial core was increasingly Anatolia and Constantinople, together with parts of the Balkans and the Aegean, some Italian territories, Sicily, and selected Mediterranean possessions. Syria, Palestine, Egypt, North Africa, southern Spain, and effective control of the Balkan interior were gone. Frontiers in this period were zones, not lines, and the extent of control varied with local circumstances, so these descriptions are approximate.

The fundamental question is why losing so much territory did not end the Roman Empire. The answer is that state continuity rested on more than territorial maximum. The emperor remained a recognized source of law and legitimacy. Constantinople remained the seat of government and the focus of ceremonial and religious life. The army, though smaller, remained a professional force paid by a functioning, if reduced, fiscal system. A bureaucracy maintained records and collected taxes. Roman law and political identity persisted, the church provided a shared Christian framework, and diplomacy allowed the empire to manage rivals through payments, marriage alliances, and the manipulation of divisions among enemies. The loss of Egypt and Syria hurt, but the remaining core was more compact and, in important ways, more defensible. The state had shrunk to a size at which its resources and its defenses were better matched.

Section 36.9

From Late Roman Provinces toward the Themes

Older textbooks often said that Heraclius created the theme system. Modern scholarship does not support so simple a statement. The late Roman provincial structure of civil provinces, dioceses, and prefectures was inherited from the age of Diocletian and Constantine (Chapter 25), with civil and military authority generally separated. The crises of the seventh century undermined that structure. As eastern territories were lost, surviving field armies were redeployed to and increasingly maintained from Anatolia. Military commands became associated with particular regions, while late Roman civil administration changed unevenly alongside them. Names later associated with themes, such as Armeniakon, Anatolikon, Opsikion, and Thrakesion, begin to appear in military contexts during the later seventh and early eighth centuries, but the evidence does not show a single moment when armies were granted territorial provinces or soldier-farmers were settled on hereditary military holdings.

Historical Vocabulary
Thema (theme)

The Greek word thema and related terms later associated with the themes refer, in seventh-century evidence, primarily to military forces and commands. Their connection to stable territorial administrative districts developed gradually, and the mature thema as a province governed by a strategos with broad civil and military authority is securely documented only later. Themes should not be described as feudal land grants to soldiers, an old oversimplification, and the classic picture of hereditary soldier-farmers is no longer accepted for the seventh century. This chapter treats the themes as the outcome of a gradual process, and Chapter 37 will examine the system in its mature form.

Historians disagree about the chronology and origins of this process. The interpretation associated with Georg Ostrogorsky, which dominated much of the twentieth century, saw a deliberate reform under Heraclius. Later scholars, notably John Haldon, argued that the evidence supports a more gradual development through the seventh century, with specific features emerging at different times and in response to particular crises, and recent reassessments place the fully territorial thema later still. The sources are sparse and mostly later than the events, which is why the debate continues. What is generally accepted is that the territorial and military crises of the seventh century produced a gradual reorganization from late Roman provincial structures toward the military-administrative arrangements characteristic of medieval Byzantium.

The reorganization changed the relationship among state, army, and countryside. Troops were increasingly stationed and supplied within Anatolian regions, while state taxation and logistics became more regionally organized, and the state's capacity to mobilize resources depended on those regional systems. These changes did not happen everywhere at once or under a single plan. Having established this transition, the chapter stops here, because Chapter 37 owns the fuller treatment of medieval Byzantine military organization, rural society, and recovery.

Section 36.10

Cities, Economy, Population, and Everyday Survival

A chapter that followed only emperors and battles would miss the experience of most people in the empire. Recurring plague outbreaks reduced the population, and war, raiding, and displacement added to the loss. Estimates of demographic decline are uncertain, and scholars give wide ranges, so students should treat any single figure with caution. The result was a smaller tax base and labor force. Long-distance trade changed as the Mediterranean was divided between Roman and Arab-ruled coasts, and as Egypt, Syria, and Africa ceased to supply the imperial economy.

Urban life changed, but not uniformly. Archaeology and inscriptional evidence indicate that many late antique cities in Anatolia and the Aegean contracted dramatically. Some monumental urban spaces were abandoned, built over, or converted to smaller fortified centers, and in some regions population shifted toward defensible hilltops and rural settlement. This is sometimes described as ruralization. It would be wrong, however, to speak of a universal collapse of cities. Some centers remained important as administrative, ecclesiastical, military, or commercial places. Thessalonica, Nicaea, and Ankara, among others, continued to matter, and regional variation was considerable. Coin finds and seals, which are valuable evidence for administration and exchange, show changing patterns of monetary circulation that historians interpret differently.

Constantinople was exceptional. Before the plague and the loss of Egypt it was one of the largest cities in the Mediterranean world, and although its population fell substantially, estimates of the extent are disputed, and it remained large by regional standards. The loss of Egypt changed provisioning. The regular Egyptian grain supply that underpinned the capital's traditional annona was cancelled around 618 as Persian occupation cut access to Egypt. This did not end all state provisioning or civic distributions. After the Arab conquest, Constantinople increasingly drew grain and other supplies from Thrace, Anatolia, and the Black Sea through a mixture of state procurement, institutional distribution, and market exchange. The palace, churches, workshops, harbors, and markets continued to function, and the city's role as imperial and religious center ensured that it kept attracting people and resources even as its scale shrank.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Communities in a Contracting Empire

A resident of Constantinople. Daily life centered on markets, workshops, churches, and the harbor. Residents experienced the threat of siege, shortages when supply routes were disrupted, and the shift from state grain distribution to dependence on markets. Processions, liturgies, and the walls themselves gave a collective sense of protection. Wealth and status varied widely, from palace officials and merchants to laborers and the poor.

An Anatolian farming community. Rural communities faced taxation, requisition of animals and supplies for the army, and the possibility of raiding as the Arab frontier approached. Some moved to fortified refuges or hilltop settlements. As armies were stationed in the district, the state's presence increased, with obligations for supply and service. Local church life continued, and the rhythm of planting and harvest remained the dominant pattern.

A Balkan community amid changing control. Imperial authority might be absent or intermittent, and communities negotiated with Avars, Slavic-speaking neighbors, or imperial officials as circumstances required. Some people took refuge in cities or islands, others remained and adapted. Hagiographical sources such as the Miracles of Saint Demetrius reveal religious life under siege, while archaeological evidence documents settlement shifts. These sources do not allow the reconstruction of individual stories, and this feature describes patterns, not named people.

Section 36.11

The Arab Sieges and the Defense of Constantinople

The caliphate developed naval power as it conquered the eastern Mediterranean coast, and Arab fleets challenged imperial control of the sea. The emperor Constans II (641–668) was defeated in a naval engagement off the Anatolian coast in the 650s, sometimes called the Battle of the Masts. He later moved his base to Sicily, where he was assassinated in 668, an episode that illustrates the empire's continuing western interests as well as the strains of the period.

Byzantine chronicle tradition, notably Theophanes, describes a seven-year Arab siege of Constantinople between 674 and 678. Modern scholarship is more cautious. Historians debate whether the evidence points to a continuous blockade or to seasonal naval operations and raids over several years, and Arabic sources describe the events differently, and one influential modern reconstruction relocates the principal operations to 667–669. The tradition also credits the use of an incendiary weapon later called Greek fire, said to have been introduced by an engineer named Kallinikos, a refugee from the Levant. The weapon, a flammable liquid projected from ships, was a valuable naval asset, but its exact composition is unknown, and it should not be treated as the sole reason Constantinople survived. In the wake of these operations the empire reached a settlement with the caliphate, and in 680 and 681 Constantine IV (668–685) faced a Bulgar group under Asparukh south of the Danube. Imperial forces were unsuccessful, and the empire came to recognize a Bulgar polity there, a development of lasting strategic importance that Chapter 39 will take up.

The following decades were politically turbulent. Justinian II (685–695, and again 705–711) pursued ambitious policies and was overthrown, mutilated, exiled, and restored. Between 695 and 717 a succession of emperors, among them Leontios, Tiberios III, Philippikos, Anastasios II, and Theodosios III, took power through coups and military revolt. This instability coincided with the loss of Carthage in 698, with continued Arab pressure, and with the Umayyad caliphate's preparation of a great expedition.

In 717 a commander named Leo, who had held the military command of the Anatolikon district, was acclaimed emperor as Leo III (717–741), and in the same year a large Arab army and fleet under Maslama, a member of the Umayyad family, besieged Constantinople. The siege continued through the winter of 717 and 718, which was unusually harsh. The defenders, according to Byzantine sources, used Greek fire against the Arab fleet, the walls held, and supplies and disease weakened the besiegers. Bulgar forces under Khan Tervel reportedly attacked Arab troops in Thrace, a reported intervention that illustrates how the Balkan strategic map had changed. The Arab forces withdrew in 718, suffering heavy losses on the retreat.

This siege demonstrates what the chapter's purpose calls the resilience of Constantinople. The city survived despite enormous external pressure, through its walls, its naval capacity, its administrative and logistical coordination, diplomacy, and good fortune. Later writers sometimes claimed that it saved Europe or Christianity, but such civilizational counterfactuals are unnecessary and unprovable. The defense was crucial to Byzantine survival, and that is a sufficient historical claim.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Empire Survive?

Long-term causes

Constantinople's geography and layered fortifications, the resources and manpower of Anatolia, a tradition of imperial taxation and bureaucracy, and a durable Roman and Christian political identity.

Short-term causes

Heraclius's strategy against Persia, naval control of the straits, military reorganization in Anatolia, diplomacy with steppe and Balkan powers, and the weaknesses of attackers who had to mount distant campaigns.

Immediate effects

The capital was not captured in 626, in the 670s, or in 717 and 718, and the empire kept a coherent core and political center despite losing most of its provinces.

Long-term consequences

A smaller, more compact, increasingly Greek-speaking and militarized state emerged, with new relations among army, countryside, and government, the basis for the medieval empire examined in Chapter 37.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains survival. Walls without a fleet, a fleet without revenue, or revenue without political legitimacy would not have sufficed, and the empire's enemies faced their own constraints, including Persian collapse and Umayyad political divisions.

Section 36.12

A Smaller Roman Empire

What changed between 565 and 718? Lost or transformed were most of the western reconquests, Syria, Palestine, Egypt, North Africa, effective control of much of the Balkan interior, the old provincial administrative structures, much of the empire's fiscal resources, and many late antique urban patterns. What persisted were the emperor, Constantinople, Roman identity, Christian imperial ideology, the bureaucracy, taxation, diplomacy, the army in reorganized form, Roman law, and Mediterranean connections. What was emerging was a more compact territorial core, an increasingly Greek administrative culture, new military organization, a new relationship between countryside, army, and state, and political structures that Chapter 37 will examine in mature form.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Empire in 565 and 718

What Changed

  • The empire shifted from a Mediterranean-wide state to a core centered on Anatolia, Constantinople, and the Aegean
  • Provincial government moved from separated civil and military authority toward combined regional commands
  • Greek became increasingly dominant in administration and ceremony
  • Population, urban scale, and revenue contracted, and long-distance exchange was reconfigured

What Remained Continuous

  • The emperor, the Roman name, and Constantinople as capital
  • Roman law, fiscal administration, and diplomatic practice
  • Christian imperial ideology and the close relation of emperor and church
  • Control of the straits and a strong fortified capital

Who experienced change most directly? Populations of the lost eastern provinces, frontier communities in the Balkans and Anatolia, and soldiers whose units were resettled. Who experienced greater continuity? Many residents of Constantinople and of districts that remained secure, whose churches, taxes, and markets changed more gradually. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that survival and transformation were not opposites.

Historical Debate

When Did Late Rome Become Medieval Byzantium?

The question: Historians use labels such as Late Antiquity and the Byzantine Middle Ages to organize the history of the eastern empire. Where, if anywhere, should the line between them fall?

Interpretation A: The seventh century was a decisive break. Some historians stress the scale of territorial loss, urban contraction, military reorganization, and the end of the late antique Mediterranean economy. On this view, the empire after the 640s was so different from the empire of Justinian that a new period must begin there.
Interpretation B: Gradual transformation from Late Antiquity. Others argue that many of the changes visible in the seventh century had earlier roots, in plague, fiscal strain, regional administration, and the growing role of Greek and Christian culture, and that change continued for generations afterward. Some also point to the idea of a long Late Antiquity extending well past the seventh century.
Interpretation C: Institutional continuity makes sharp breaks misleading. A third approach emphasizes the persistence of Roman law, imperial office, fiscal institutions, and identity, and suggests that the label “Byzantine” may obscure that continuity.

Evidence: All interpretations draw on the same coin and seal evidence, archaeology of cities and fortifications, chronicles, legal and administrative texts, and hagiography. They differ in which kinds of change they treat as decisive.

Why it matters: Period labels shape how students understand the relationship between Rome and Byzantium, and whether the eastern empire appears as a continuation or as a successor.

Student task: Choose two kinds of evidence from this chapter and explain how each might support one interpretation more than another, bearing in mind that period labels organize history, but contemporaries experienced change without knowing they had crossed from Late Antiquity into Byzantium.

The empire survived because it changed. That is the argument of this chapter, and it explains why the seventh century should be read not as the fall of Byzantium but as the transformation that made medieval Byzantine survival possible. The empire that survived 717 was no longer Justinian's Mediterranean empire. Chapter 37 examines the religious conflicts, military institutions, and social structures through which this smaller Roman state became the medieval Byzantine Empire.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the eastern Roman Empire survived the crises of the century and a half after Justinian's death in 565. It began by establishing the empire of 565 as a vast but strained Mediterranean state, with long frontiers, a recently reconquered West, severe fiscal strain, and a population affected by recurrent plague. The question was not whether Justinian had succeeded, but how difficult his enlarged empire would be to defend.

The chapter insisted that “Byzantine” is a modern label. The empire's inhabitants understood their state as Roman, and Constantinople as New Rome. Real changes occurred, including territorial contraction, the growing predominance of Greek, and military and urban transformation, yet no sharp date marks a transition from Rome to Byzantium, and Roman law, the imperial office, taxation, diplomacy, and political identity persisted.

In the West, the Lombard invasion of 568 ended unified imperial control of Italy and left a divided peninsula of Lombard realms and imperial enclaves around Ravenna, Rome, Naples, and Sicily. The last imperial positions in Iberia fell by about 624. In the Balkans, Avars and Slavic-speaking groups raided and settled, and imperial control receded inland, though cities and coasts remained and local populations did not simply vanish.

Under Maurice the empire tried to hold several fronts and developed exarchates in Ravenna and Carthage, which combined civil and military authority in exposed provinces. These were adaptations to distance and emergency, not the later themes. Maurice's overthrow in 602 launched the last great Roman-Persian war, an imperial struggle, not a religious one.

The war brought the empire to the brink. Persian forces took Jerusalem in 614 and Egypt in 619. Heraclius, who became emperor in 610, reorganized resources and carried the war into Persian territory, defeating the Sasanian regime by 628. Constantinople, meanwhile, withstood the pressure of 626 through walls, naval control, geography, and organization, though it was never impregnable.

The recovery was brief. Arab armies of the early caliphate took Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and later North Africa. The chapter stressed multiple interacting causes: exhausted imperial forces, fiscal strain, Arab mobilization, the collapse of Persia, and the difficulty of defending huge territories, with local religious divisions mattering but not being decisive.

The loss of the East produced a smaller empire centered on Anatolia and Constantinople. The relocation of field armies to Anatolian districts and the gradual change in provincial command began a long transformation from late Roman provinces toward the themes. The origins and chronology of that process remain debated, and the old view that Heraclius created the themes is no longer sustainable.

Demographic contraction, recurring plague, a smaller tax base, and urban change shaped everyday life, with great regional variation. Constantinople adapted to the loss of Egyptian grain and remained a city of exceptional size. Arab naval campaigns, the debated operations of 674 to 678, and the great siege of 717 and 718 tested the capital again, and Leo III's reign began with a defense that demonstrated its resilience.

The chapter ended by comparing 565 and 718. Much was lost or transformed, but the emperor, Constantinople, Roman identity, Christian imperial ideology, bureaucracy, taxation, diplomacy, and law endured. The empire survived because it changed, and Chapter 37 will examine the religious controversy, military organization, rural society, and recovery through which that smaller state became the medieval Byzantine Empire.

Mapping the Contraction and Survival of the Roman East

This project asks students to build a series of historical maps and a written commentary on territorial change and imperial survival between 565 and 718.

  1. Select reference maps. Use two or more historical atlases that depict the period, and note how they differ in showing frontiers and zones of control.
  2. Create three maps. Produce maps for c. 565, c. 628, and c. 700 that distinguish effective control, contested zones, and nominal claims.
  3. Collect evidence. Identify for each region at least one type of evidence, such as a chronicle, seal, inscription, papyrus, or archaeological site, and note its limitations.
  4. Compare sources. Compare a Greek, a Syriac or Armenian, and an Arabic source on one event, such as the capture of Jerusalem or the siege of Constantinople, noting dates and perspectives.
  5. Write a commentary. In 3 to 5 pages, explain what the maps show about contraction and survival, using careful language about uncertainty.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it prevents the history of Christian Europe from being told only from the Latin West. While Frankish, Lombard, Anglo-Saxon, and other western polities were consolidating, a Roman state continued to function in the East, with institutions that traced to antiquity, and it survived a catastrophe larger than any faced by contemporary western kingdoms.

The chapter also reveals a process that recurs in European history: survival through transformation. Territorial loss, fiscal strain, and military crisis did not simply destroy the empire but forced new arrangements. In the same way that Chapter 28 showed the Roman world transforming in the West, this chapter shows transformation in the East, and both challenge narratives of simple fall.

The legacies are considerable. Constantinople remained a great city and the seat of an empire, the Anatolian core and its military organization shaped the next centuries, and the Balkan and Black Sea frontiers created new relationships among Byzantines, Bulgars, and Slavic-speaking peoples, examined in Chapters 38 and 39. Relations with the caliphate, which began in warfare, would also include diplomacy, trade, and cultural exchange in later centuries.

For Volume II, the chapter establishes that medieval Christendom was plural from the start. The Latin and Greek Christian worlds developed along increasingly distinct institutional and theological paths, a process of separation that Chapter 40 will examine in detail. Part VI therefore begins with the smaller Roman empire that endured, and Chapter 37 turns to the conflicts and institutions through which it recovered.
