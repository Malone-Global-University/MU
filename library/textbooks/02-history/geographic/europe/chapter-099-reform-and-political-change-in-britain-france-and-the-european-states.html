Reform and Political Change in Britain, France, and the European States

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In May 1832 a crowd gathered outside Birmingham to demand a change in how Britain was governed, and the political establishment feared it without being overthrown by it. That tension, between pressure that threatened a political order and reform that preserved it, is the subject of this chapter.

On 7 May 1832, thousands of men and women assembled on Newhall Hill, on the edge of Birmingham, at a mass meeting organized by the Birmingham Political Union. Founded in 1830 by the banker and currency reformer Thomas Attwood, the Union had become one of the largest reform associations in Britain, and it united many middle-class employers with skilled workers around the demand for parliamentary reform. Estimates of the size of the crowd vary enormously, from tens of thousands to well over a hundred thousand, because the figures come largely from the Union's own supporters and from newspapers with strong opinions. The uncertainty is itself instructive. Historians cannot count the crowd precisely, but they can say with confidence that the meeting belonged to a national campaign whose scale alarmed ministers, peers, and magistrates alike.

The timing was dramatic. On the same day in London, the House of Lords carried a hostile procedural motion that threatened to wreck the government's Reform Bill, and within forty-eight hours the Whig prime minister, Earl Grey, had resigned after the king declined to guarantee the creation of enough new peers to overcome the Lords. For about a fortnight Britain faced what contemporaries called the Days of May. Yet the outcome was not a revolution. Grey returned to office, the Lords yielded under the threat of a mass creation of peers, and the Reform Act received royal assent on 7 June 1832. A movement that had mobilized crowds in Birmingham, Manchester, Bristol, and London achieved a constitutional change enacted by Parliament itself.

That result was both remarkable and limited. The Act redistributed seats, removed or reduced the representation of many tiny boroughs, and enfranchised additional property-owning men, so that the electorate of England and Wales grew by roughly half. But it left the great majority of adult men without a vote, excluded all women by explicitly using the word "male," retained open voting, and preserved a House of Lords with an effective veto. The broad popular movement for reform and the narrow electorate that finally resulted were not the same thing, and the gap between them drove British politics for the next generation, through Chartism and into the reforms of 1867 and 1872.

Chapter 98 examined the revolutions of 1820, 1830, and 1848 as moments of rupture. This chapter asks a parallel question about continuity and change: how did European governments alter their institutions between about 1815 and 1875, and why did reform sometimes achieve what revolution did not? France supplies the sharpest contrast with Britain. It passed through a restored monarchy, a July Monarchy, a republic, an authoritarian empire, and a new republic within sixty years, and it introduced universal male suffrage in 1848 without producing durable parliamentary democracy. Belgium, the Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, Switzerland, Prussia, the Habsburg monarchy, and Russia followed still other paths, from liberal constitutional monarchy to autocratic modernization.

The chapter also insists that political reform cannot be understood only through parliaments and electoral laws. In 1833 Britain abolished slavery in most of its colonies, but compensated slaveholders and imposed a period of apprenticeship on the formerly enslaved. In 1861 the Russian emperor emancipated the serfs while preserving autocracy. Religious minorities gained legal rights unevenly, and women, almost everywhere, remained excluded from national political participation even as men's rights expanded. Reform expanded rights for some populations while leaving others, often the majority of the population, outside the new political arrangements.

The evidence for these developments is unusually rich and varied. Statutes, constitutions, and parliamentary debates record the formal changes. Petitions, newspapers, pamphlets, and caricatures show how ordinary people argued about them. Electoral registers and censuses allow historians to estimate who could vote, though with the caution that figures for the electorate are often approximations. Diaries, letters, and court records reveal how enfranchisement, emancipation, and administrative reform were experienced by people far from capitals. Within Part XV, this chapter follows the discussion of ideology in Chapter 97 and the revolutions of Chapter 98, and it prepares the national unifications of Chapter 100 and the mass movements of Chapter 102. Within Volume IV, it shows how the political settlements left by the age of revolution were gradually reshaped without a single European model of democracy emerging.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish political reform from revolution and explain why reform could both preserve and transform established political orders.
  2. Compare absolute monarchy, constitutional monarchy, parliamentary monarchy, and republican government, and explain the difference between having a parliament and having parliamentary control of the executive.
  3. Trace the British reforms from Catholic emancipation in 1829 through the Acts of 1832 and 1867 and the Ballot Act of 1872, identifying who gained the vote and who did not.
  4. Analyze Chartism and the People's Charter as a campaign for democratic representation and explain its significance despite its immediate parliamentary failures.
  5. Explain why France moved among restored monarchy, July Monarchy, republic, empire, and renewed republic, and why universal male suffrage did not guarantee parliamentary government.
  6. Assess the distinct models of constitutional government in Belgium, the Netherlands, Iberia, Scandinavia, Switzerland, Prussia, and the Habsburg monarchy.
  7. Evaluate the Great Reforms of Alexander II as autocratic modernization rather than liberal parliamentary reform.
  8. Differentiate the prohibition of the slave trade, the abolition of slavery, and the emancipation of serfs, and analyze the continuing coercion that followed colonial emancipation.
  9. Interpret how religion, gender, property, and imperial status limited the citizenship created by nineteenth-century reform.
  10. Evaluate interpretations that explain reform through popular pressure, elite initiative, or a combination of both, using evidence from several states.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did some European governments reform their institutions under pressure while others experienced revolution, and how reliable is that distinction?
  2. Did reform represent a deliberate extension of political liberty, a strategy for preserving authority, or a contested combination of both?
  3. How could people without the vote influence government, and what limits did censorship, policing, and law place on that influence?
  4. Why did universal male suffrage in France coexist with authoritarian rule, while a far narrower British franchise coexisted with expanding parliamentary government?
  5. What was the difference between greater state capacity, more effective administration, and greater political accountability?
  6. How did similar demands for constitutional government produce different institutions in Belgium, Spain, Prussia, Switzerland, and the Habsburg lands?
  7. Why did emancipation, whether of serfs or of enslaved people, so often leave labor coercion and inequality in place?
  8. What does the history of these reforms suggest about the idea that European politics moved steadily toward democracy?

Historical Context

The political settlement of 1815, examined in Chapter 92, restored many dynasties and attempted to contain the forces unleashed by the French Revolution and Napoleon. It did not restore the Old Regime unchanged. France retained a written charter and a legal order shaped by the Revolution, the Netherlands and the German states were reorganized, and legal equality, codified law, and professional administration survived in many places that had been touched by French rule. Chapter 97 explained how liberals, conservatives, nationalists, and romantics offered competing visions of political legitimacy. This chapter follows those ideas into institutions.

Chapter 98 narrated the revolutions of 1820, 1830, and 1848. Those upheavals toppled the Bourbon king of France in 1830, created a new Belgian state, and in 1848 shook nearly every continental capital. Yet most of the revolutionary governments of 1848 collapsed within a year or two, and counterrevolution restored order in much of central Europe. What survived was often more modest than what revolutionaries had demanded, but it was not trivial. Constitutions, elected assemblies, and legal reforms emerged in the aftermath, sometimes granted by monarchs who wanted to prevent further upheaval. Revolution and reform were therefore connected: revolutionary threats altered what governments were willing to concede, and concessions shaped what later reformers could demand.

Economic and social change supplied the underlying pressure. The industrial transformation of Chapters 93 to 95 enlarged towns, strengthened commercial and professional classes, and created large urban working populations. Rising literacy, cheaper printing, and the railway and telegraph enlarged the public that followed politics. Older systems of representation, often based on land, privilege, or inherited corporate status, struggled to represent these new populations. At the same time, governments faced practical demands to manage cities, taxes, armies, courts, and poverty, and this administrative pressure drove reform even where no one intended political liberalization.

Students should use several cautions throughout the chapter. First, constitutional terminology is deceptive: a "parliament" could be a powerful institution or a largely advisory body, and a "constitution" could limit a ruler or merely describe the ruler's power. Second, figures for electorates are approximations drawn from contemporary registers and modern estimates, and proportions of the population enfranchised varied by how historians count adult men, residents, or inhabitants. Third, dates in the Russian Empire followed the Julian calendar, which lagged the Gregorian calendar used in western Europe by twelve days in the nineteenth century, and this chapter gives the Old Style date with the New Style equivalent when the difference matters. Finally, "Britain," "England," and "the United Kingdom" are not interchangeable: the major reforms discussed here applied differently in England and Wales, Scotland, and Ireland, and the chapter says so where the difference is significant.

Constitutional Government, Political Reform, and the Changing European State

The first four sections establish how reform operated, why governments faced pressure to change, and how constitutional institutions and public participation developed.

Section 99.1

From Revolution to Reform: European Political Change after 1815

Revolution and reform are both forms of political change, but they differ in method and in what they claim. A revolution seeks to replace a regime, often through insurrection, a breakdown of authority, or the creation of a new legitimacy. Reform changes institutions through procedures the existing order recognizes: legislation, royal concession, parliamentary negotiation, constitutional amendment, judicial decision, or administrative reorganization. The boundary between the two was never sharp. The French Revolution of 1830 produced a revised constitution, not a new republic, and British reformers in 1831 and 1832 used mass meetings and implicit threats of disorder to secure legislation that peers and ministers could still call lawful. Historians therefore treat them as points on a spectrum rather than as opposites.

The settlement of 1815 produced states that were uneasy with the political ideas of the previous generation. Conservative governments, most closely associated with Austria and Prussia and with their chief diplomat, Prince Metternich of Austria, prioritized order and dynastic legitimacy, and they policed universities, newspapers, and associations. Yet even these governments depended on administrations that had grown more systematic under Napoleonic and Enlightenment influences. In Britain, a parliamentary monarchy with powerful aristocratic landowners faced agitation over taxation, the Corn Laws, and religious inequality. In France, the Charter of 1814 created an arena in which royalists, liberals, and republicans contested the meaning of constitutional monarchy. Reform pressure emerged in different forms across all these settings.

Several forces pushed in the same direction. Expanding towns, commerce, and education created groups, such as manufacturers, merchants, lawyers, doctors, teachers, and skilled artisans, whose wealth and skills exceeded their political influence. Print culture allowed these groups to compare governments and criticize their own. Fiscal and military needs forced rulers to seek the cooperation of taxpayers and local notables. Religious minorities, from Catholics in Ireland to Jews in the German states, demanded equal civil standing. And the revolutions of 1830 and 1848 made rulers aware that refusal could be costly. These forces did not act uniformly. Liberal movements demanded constitutions and limited government, conservatives sometimes adopted reform to protect institutions they valued, popular movements demanded broader representation and economic relief, and governments pursued administrative efficiency for reasons that had little to do with liberty.

Reform could therefore protect an established order while transforming it. The British Whig leaders of 1832 explicitly argued that conceding a limited reform would prevent revolution by attaching the propertied middle classes to the constitution. Napoleon III used plebiscites to claim popular legitimacy while limiting opposition. Alexander II emancipated the serfs partly because defeat in the Crimean War exposed the weakness of the serf-based system. In each case, change altered how power operated even though it did not necessarily limit the ruler's authority.

This leads to the chapter's central problem. Was reform a deliberate extension of political liberty, a strategy for preserving authority, or a contested combination of both? The evidence supports the third answer in most cases. Reformers who sought liberty and governments who sought stability often supported the same measures for different reasons, and the resulting settlements bore the marks of both. The question is worth keeping in mind as the chapter turns from comparison to specific states.

Section 99.2

Constitutional Monarchy, Parliamentary Government, and the Distribution of Political Power

Nineteenth-century political vocabulary was often imprecise, so students need clear distinctions. In an absolute monarchy, the ruler held legislative, executive, and judicial authority and was not formally bound by an assembly or a written constitution, although custom, law, the church, and practical limits still constrained actual rule. In a constitutional monarchy, the ruler's powers were defined and limited by a constitution, charter, or fundamental law, and an elected or partly elected assembly participated in legislation or taxation. In a parliamentary monarchy, a further step had occurred: ministers depended on the confidence of the legislature, so that the monarch could not govern against a stable parliamentary majority. In a republic, executive authority derived from election or from a constitution without hereditary monarchy, though republics varied widely in how broadly they defined the people.

Historical Vocabulary
Parliamentary Government

Parliamentary government is a system in which the executive, usually a cabinet led by a prime minister, depends on the continuing support of a legislative majority and can be removed when it loses that support. In the nineteenth century, it developed gradually and unevenly. In Britain it emerged through convention, not a written constitutional text, so that by the 1860s ministers were widely expected to resign or seek a dissolution when defeated on major questions. In many continental states, written constitutions created elected legislatures long before ministers were responsible to them.

The term matters because a state could have a parliament without parliamentary government. Students should ask, in each case, who appointed ministers, who could dismiss them, and whether the legislature controlled taxation and the budget.

These distinctions help explain why constitutions differed so much in practice. The French Charter of 1814 was a charter granted by the king, not a constitution created by the people, and Louis XVIII retained the executive power and the right to appoint ministers. The Belgian Constitution of 1831 was drafted by a national congress and placed sovereignty in the nation, although the king retained significant authority. Prussia's constitution of 1850 was granted by the crown after revolution and reaction, and the king remained the commander of the army with wide authority over ministers. In Spain, successive constitutions alternated between emphasizing national sovereignty and sovereignty shared by Cortes and crown, depending on which political faction held power.

The key question for any system was the relationship between monarch and ministers. Where rulers appointed ministers independently, a legislature might criticize and delay but could not control policy. Where ministers increasingly depended on parliamentary support, the monarch's role narrowed toward ceremonial and symbolic functions, though the process was gradual and depended on personalities and party politics. In Britain, Queen Victoria retained considerable influence over appointments and policy, but the House of Commons and party leaders determined which ministries could survive. In Prussia, by contrast, the constitutional conflict of the early 1860s showed that a determined monarch and minister could govern without an approved budget, at least temporarily.

Bicameral legislatures also mattered. Most constitutional systems of the period divided the legislature into an upper chamber, often composed of hereditary nobles, appointed members, or the wealthiest taxpayers, and a lower chamber elected on a restricted franchise. The upper chamber could act as a conservative brake. The British House of Lords delayed the Reform Bill in 1831 and 1832, and the French Chamber of Peers and Prussian Herrenhaus played comparable moderating roles. Constitutional guarantees, such as freedom of the press, assembly, and religion, also varied widely and were often limited by laws regulating how those freedoms could be used.

Finally, constitutional arrangements could coexist with property-based suffrage, powerful monarchies, and substantial restrictions on civil liberties. A written constitution did not itself prove that power was limited, and the same constitutional text could be applied liberally or restrictively. This institutional vocabulary will serve in the British, French, and continental case studies that follow.

Section 99.3

Political Participation Beyond Parliament: Petitions, Newspapers, Associations, and Public Opinion

The vote was only one way of taking part in politics. In every state discussed in this chapter, most inhabitants had no vote, yet many of them petitioned, read newspapers, joined associations, attended meetings, and sought to influence officials. Historians therefore distinguish formal political rights, such as the right to vote and hold office, from broader political participation. The second category was always wider than the first, and the history of reform is partly the history of attempts to bring the two closer together.

Petitions were among the oldest and most flexible tools. In Britain, the right of subjects to petition Parliament was long recognized, and petition campaigns in the 1830s and 1840s used printed forms, local canvassing, and signature gathering to demonstrate scale. Signatories included many people who could not vote, and sometimes women, so that petitions could express opinions the franchise excluded. Public meetings, processions, and mass rallies added visible pressure, though governments regarded large gatherings with suspicion and magistrates sometimes banned or dispersed them. The Birmingham Political Union and similar associations combined these methods with newspapers and pamphlets to build national networks.

The press was central. Cheap newspapers, radical weeklies, and local papers circulated reports of debates and political arguments, and they also supplied a shared vocabulary. Governments tried to control this influence through taxes on newspapers, licensing, security deposits, and prosecutions. In Britain, the "taxes on knowledge," including the stamp duty on newspapers, were reduced and finally removed between 1836 and 1861. In France, press regulation tightened and loosened with each regime: the July Monarchy imposed restrictions after attempts on the king's life in 1835, the Second Republic briefly widened freedom, and the Second Empire's decree of 1852 imposed an authorization system and warnings that could suspend newspapers. In the German states and the Habsburg lands, censorship was routine before 1848 and returned in forms after it.

Voluntary associations, from religious societies and temperance groups to educational and philanthropic organizations, taught skills of organization that political movements could use. Chapels, churches, workplaces, reading rooms, and neighborhood networks supplied meeting places and communication. Literacy and urban concentration made this participation easier, though they did not guarantee it, and rural populations often had less access to the printed word and fewer opportunities to assemble. Law also shaped what was possible. Britain generally allowed associations to form but restricted some organizational forms, such as correspondence between branches. Prussia's laws on associations after 1850 banned women and minors from political organizations, and French law required authorization for associations above a small size.

Public opinion became a recognized political force without being institutionalized. Ministers cited it, opposition leaders invoked it, and newspapers claimed to speak for it, but no one could measure it. Historians treat claims about public opinion with caution: they came from those who could write and publish, and they often described an ideal public that suited the speaker. Still, the growth of extra-parliamentary participation matters because it prepared the ground for mass parties and labor organizations, which belong to Chapter 102. In this chapter, the point is narrower. Popular pressure helped shape reform even where the pressing population could not vote.

Section 99.4

Reform from Above and Pressure from Below: Administration, Local Government, and State Modernization

Many of the most consequential reforms of the period involved administration, not representation. Governments faced new tasks: policing growing towns, collecting taxes in monetary economies, standardizing laws, managing poverty, regulating public health, building roads and railways, and conscripting or training armies. They responded by creating professional civil services, reorganizing departments, codifying laws, and strengthening local government. These changes enlarged what governments could do, a quality historians call state capacity, and they often proceeded independently of any movement toward democracy.

Bureaucratic professionalism advanced unevenly. Prussia and France had long traditions of trained officials, and Britain expanded its administrative apparatus through reports, inspectors, and boards, notably in the 1830s and 1840s. The Northcote-Trevelyan report of 1854 recommended recruitment by examination, and competitive entry developed in the British civil service in the following decades. Legal standardization, whether through the continuing influence of the French civil code, Prussian codification, or reforms of courts, removed older local and privileged jurisdictions. Taxation grew more systematic, and censuses, registers, and statistics allowed governments to know their populations in new ways.

Local government sat at the center of the tension between central authority and local autonomy. In France, the centralized system of prefects, inherited from Napoleon, tied localities to the state. In Britain, local government rested on a patchwork of corporations, vestries, magistrates, and special bodies, which reformers replaced with elected councils in the 1830s. In Switzerland and Belgium, communes and cantons kept significant autonomy. In Russia, the zemstvos of 1864 created elected local institutions within an autocratic state. Each arrangement distributed power differently, and the choice between central control and local self-government became a persistent political question.

Reform came from different sources. Sometimes governing elites initiated change after investigations and reports, as with the British Poor Law inquiry that led to the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834. Sometimes petitions, protests, and electoral pressure forced action, as in the campaigns for the repeal of the Corn Laws and for factory regulation. Often the two combined: officials designed measures, but organized pressure determined which proposals could pass. Chapter 95 examined the social conditions that prompted many of these reforms, and the political consequences are the focus here.

Administrative modernization did not necessarily produce democracy. Napoleon III's France and Alexander II's Russia illustrate how a more capable state could be built under an executive that remained largely unaccountable. Students should keep three concepts apart: greater state capacity, which means the ability to collect taxes, enforce laws, and deliver services; more effective government, which means the ability to achieve stated goals; and greater political accountability, which means the ability of citizens or their representatives to hold rulers responsible. A state could gain the first two without the third. The sections that follow ask, for each state, who initiated reform, who benefited, who remained excluded, and what institutions actually changed.

Division II · Sections 99.5 to 99.8
Britain: Parliamentary Reform, Representation, and Constitutional Transformation

These sections trace Britain's movement from a restricted and unevenly distributed representation toward a substantially enlarged male electorate, emphasizing reform campaigns, Chartism, and the continued exclusion of important groups.

Section 99.5

Britain's Unreformed Political System, Catholic Emancipation, and the Demand for Change

After the Napoleonic Wars, the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland (formed by the Act of Union of 1800, effective 1801) was governed through a monarchy, a hereditary House of Lords, an elected House of Commons, and a cabinet of ministers who needed to command Commons support. The monarch still mattered, since the king chose ministers and could influence appointments, but practical power rested increasingly in the cabinet and in the ability to manage the Commons. This was a parliamentary system in formation, not a fixed constitution, and conventions evolved as political practice changed.

The electoral system, however, had not kept pace with demographic and economic change. Constituencies reflected medieval and early modern arrangements. Some boroughs with few or no inhabitants, popularly called rotten boroughs, still returned members, often because a landowner effectively controlled the seat, while the large industrial towns of Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds had no separate representation. Franchise qualifications varied from borough to borough and could rest on property, residence, guild membership, or ancient custom, and in county elections the principal qualification was a freehold worth forty shillings a year. Voting was open and public, which exposed tenants and dependents to pressure from landlords and employers. Electoral expense, influence, and patronage shaped many contests, and defenders of the system argued that it represented interests and property, not individuals. Critics saw corruption.

The place of Ireland and of religious dissent complicated the picture. The established churches of England and Ireland held privileges that others did not. Protestant Dissenters had been partly relieved by the repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828, but Catholics remained barred from sitting in Parliament and from most senior offices. In Ireland, where most of the population was Catholic, this exclusion carried a political and social weight beyond religious principle. Daniel O'Connell founded the Catholic Association in 1823 and built a mass membership by collecting a small subscription, the "Catholic rent," from large numbers of poor Catholics, often with the support of Catholic clergy. In 1828, O'Connell won the County Clare by-election even though, as a Catholic, he could not take his seat.

The Clare election forced a decision. The government of the Duke of Wellington, with Robert Peel as home secretary, concluded that continued refusal risked disorder in Ireland, and it passed the Roman Catholic Relief Act in 1829 (royal assent 13 April). The Act allowed Catholics to sit in Parliament and to hold most civil and military offices, though it excluded them from a small number of positions, including the regency, the lord chancellorship, and the lord lieutenancy of Ireland. At the same time, the government raised the qualification for the Irish county franchise from forty shillings to ten pounds, which disfranchised a large number of small Catholic freeholders. Catholic emancipation was thus a major concession, accompanied by a measure that reduced the electorate it had mobilized. It removed an important legal barrier without establishing full religious or political equality.

Emancipation also split the Tory party and encouraged reformers to think that substantial change was possible. By 1830, the demand for parliamentary reform had grown through radical clubs, middle-class political unions, and newspapers, and economic hardship and agricultural unrest added to the pressure. Local government, still based on unreformed corporations and parish vestries, would also come under scrutiny, preparing the Municipal Corporations Act of 1835 discussed in later sections.

Section 99.6

The Great Reform Act of 1832: Electoral Representation, Political Pressure, and Its Limits

The reform crisis began after the general election of 1830, which followed the death of George IV and coincided with the revolution in France. Wellington's government fell in November 1830 after he declared his opposition to reform, and the Whig leader Earl Grey formed a ministry committed to it. Lord John Russell introduced the first Reform Bill in March 1831. After it narrowly failed in committee, Grey obtained a dissolution, and the election of 1831 returned a strong reforming majority. The second bill passed the Commons but was rejected by the House of Lords in October 1831, provoking serious riots in Bristol, Nottingham, and elsewhere. A third bill passed the Commons in March 1832 and won a second reading in the Lords in April, but the Lords' hostile move of 7 May 1832 led to the crisis described in this chapter's opening.

Historical Vocabulary
Franchise and Representation

The franchise, or suffrage, is the right to vote. Representation concerns how voters, constituencies, and seats are connected to a legislature. These are separate questions. A system may enfranchise many men but distribute seats unequally, or it may have equal districts but restrict who can vote. The British debate of the 1830s combined both issues: reformers wanted to extend the franchise to more property-owning men and to redistribute seats from small boroughs to populous towns.

Historians also distinguish between formal enfranchisement and effective political influence, which depended on factors such as open voting, registration rules, patronage, and the cost of contesting elections.

The Representation of the People Act 1832 changed British parliamentary elections in several ways. It abolished the representation of fifty-six small boroughs entirely and reduced roughly thirty others to a single member, and it used the seats to create new constituencies, including new boroughs for Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, and other large towns, and to increase county representation. In the boroughs, it established a uniform franchise for occupiers of property valued at ten pounds a year, while preserving some older rights for existing voters. In the counties, it kept the forty-shilling freehold and added certain copyholders, long leaseholders, and tenants with higher annual values. It also introduced a system of voter registration. The electorate of England and Wales rose from roughly 435,000 to roughly 650,000, estimates which vary slightly among sources. Associated measures for Scotland and Ireland adjusted their representation, and the Scottish reform in particular enlarged a very small electorate by many times, though from a very low base.

The limits were as important as the changes. The Act did not introduce universal male suffrage, equal electoral districts, or the secret ballot. Because it specified "male persons," it also formally excluded women, who had not previously been explicitly barred in the same way, though in practice very few had voted. Most working-class men remained without the vote, and the ten-pound qualification favored shopkeepers, manufacturers, and the propertied middle class. The Act left significant aristocratic influence intact, since landowners continued to control many counties and small boroughs through tenants and patronage. Open voting continued, so that dependence on landlords and employers could still shape electoral outcomes. Reformers like Lord Grey had designed the measure to strengthen the constitution by admitting the propertied middle classes, not to democratize it.

The mobilization behind the Act showed that popular pressure could change a constitution through legal channels. Political unions, mass meetings, petitions, newspaper campaigns, and the credible fear of unrest persuaded peers and the king that refusal risked more than concession. The creation of a political settlement through Parliament itself, as opposed to revolution, became part of British political memory, though students should recognize that this memory was partly constructed by participants who wanted to present reform as orderly. It also produced disappointment among working-class supporters who found that the Act's benefits went to others, a disappointment that fed Chartism.

Section 99.7

Chartism, the People's Charter, and the Campaign for Democratic Representation

Chartism grew from the disillusionment of the 1830s. The Reform Act had failed to enfranchise most working men. The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 and its workhouse system angered many laboring people, and the commercial depression of the late 1830s and early 1840s intensified hardship. In 1838, William Lovett and Francis Place drafted the People's Charter in consultation with the London Working Men's Association, drawing on a wider radical reform network. Published in May 1838 as a proposed parliamentary bill, it supplied the common program behind successive Chartist petitions. The movement drew its strength from industrial towns of the north and Midlands, from London radicals, from Wales and Scotland, and from skilled and unskilled workers who saw political rights as a route to social change.

The Charter's six points were universal male suffrage, a secret ballot, annual parliamentary elections, equal electoral districts, payment of Members of Parliament, and abolition of the property qualification for candidates. Together these demands addressed both who could vote and how the system worked, and they would permit working men to elect and to become representatives. Several of the points were eventually adopted, though over a much longer period: the secret ballot in 1872, the abolition of the property qualification for candidates in 1858, payment of members in 1911, and male suffrage in stages from 1867 into the twentieth century. Annual parliaments were never adopted.

Chartism was a movement of organization and argument as well as protest. Its newspapers, especially the Northern Star, founded in 1837 by Feargus O'Connor, connected local groups and circulated speeches, news, and arguments. Local associations organized meetings, lecture series, and petitioning. Three great petitions were presented, in 1839, 1842, and 1848. The first carried more than a million signatures, the second gathered over three million according to Chartist counts, and the 1848 petition was claimed to carry nearly six million, though a parliamentary committee found a much lower and partly fraudulent total. Counting signatures is difficult, and both supporters and opponents had reasons to exaggerate or minimize. Parliament rejected each petition by large majorities.

The movement was divided over strategy. "Moral force" Chartists, associated with Lovett, emphasized education, argument, and peaceful pressure. "Physical force" rhetoric, associated with some speakers and most closely with certain local leaders, implied that violence might be justified if peaceful means failed. O'Connor, who dominated the movement's press, shifted between these positions and later promoted the Chartist Land Plan. Government response combined surveillance and arrests with limited concessions. Violence occurred, notably the Newport Rising in Wales in November 1839, in which Chartists marched on the town and troops fired on them, leaving more than twenty of the marchers dead, and in disturbances during the 1842 general strike. The great meeting at Kennington Common in London on 10 April 1848 was dispersed peacefully amid heavy security, and many historians treat it as a symbolic turning point.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Chartist Petition Campaign in an Industrial Town

Chartist politics was lived through local routines. A typical campaign relied on a local association that met in a hired room, a chapel, a public house, or the open air. Activists circulated sheets for signatures, held lectures, read newspapers aloud for people who could not read easily, and raised small subscriptions from working families. Sheets were then pasted together into the great rolls transported to London. Historians reconstruct these routines from surviving petitions, local newspapers, police and magistrates' reports, and the memoirs of participants, remembering that reports by magistrates and informers reflect hostile viewpoints.

Women took part in many ways, signing petitions, attending meetings, raising money, organizing boycotts of hostile shopkeepers, and forming female political associations in several towns, even though the Charter's suffrage demand applied only to men. Children attended Chartist Sunday schools in some places. For families living with irregular wages and unemployment, participation cost time and sometimes risk, because employers could dismiss known activists and authorities could prosecute organizers. The movement thus linked political demands with the practical circumstances of working-class households.

Chartism did not secure its program, and by the 1850s it had declined as economic conditions improved and leaders divided. Yet its legacy was significant. It trained activists, created a national political language of rights, and kept the question of democratic representation before the public. Some former Chartists later joined trade unions, cooperative societies, or reform leagues. The later history of organized mass labor politics belongs to Chapter 102, and the point here is that the Charter's demands, compared with later electoral laws, show how far reform proceeded and how much remained unfinished.

Section 99.8

The Second Reform Act of 1867, Municipal Government, and the Secret Ballot

After 1848, the pressure for further reform revived slowly. Economic growth, the strengthening of skilled workers' organizations, the Reform League and Reform Union of the mid-1860s, and the political attention generated by the American Civil War and the Italian and German unifications renewed argument about who deserved the vote. Within Parliament, leading politicians such as William Gladstone, who had moved from Conservatism toward Liberalism, favored a cautious extension. In 1866, Gladstone and Lord John Russell introduced a modest bill, which was defeated when a group of dissident Liberals joined the Conservatives. A mass reform demonstration in Hyde Park in July 1866, during which crowds broke down railings, intensified the sense of urgency.

The Conservative minority government of Lord Derby, with Benjamin Disraeli leading in the Commons, then introduced and carried a far wider measure. Historians debate whether this was a principled concession, a tactical maneuver to outflank the Liberals, or a response to popular agitation. The evidence suggests a mixture. Disraeli sought to keep his party in office and to claim reform for the Conservatives, and he accepted amendments from Liberals and radicals that widened the bill considerably. Derby himself described the measure as a leap in the dark, an acknowledgment that its consequences were uncertain.

The Representation of the People Act 1867 (for England and Wales) gave the vote in boroughs to all male householders who paid poor rates, and to lodgers occupying rooms worth at least ten pounds a year in unfurnished lodgings. In counties, it extended the vote to occupiers of property with a rateable value of twelve pounds. It also redistributed some seats from small boroughs to larger towns and counties. The electorate of England and Wales approximately doubled, to something in the order of two million, though precise totals depend on registration practice. A substantial share of urban working men, particularly skilled workers who were householders, gained the vote, while many agricultural laborers, lodgers below the qualification, and men without settled residence did not. Scottish and Irish reform followed in separate Acts in 1868, with different franchises and a more limited extension in Ireland.

The Ballot Act 1872 introduced secret voting in parliamentary and municipal elections, ending the open, public voting that had exposed electors to pressure. The secret ballot had been a Chartist demand, and its adoption reflected growing awareness that the enlarged electorate was vulnerable to landlords and employers. It did not end corruption immediately, but it removed the principal means of visible intimidation. The Corrupt Practices Act of 1883 later addressed bribery more directly.

Municipal government developed in parallel. The Municipal Corporations Act 1835 had replaced the old self-selecting corporations of 178 boroughs in England and Wales with elected councils chosen by ratepayers, and it required public accounts and regular elections. The reformed councils became important vehicles of local political life, and in the following decades they took on responsibilities for policing, sanitation, water, and urban improvement, as seen in the public health reforms discussed in Chapter 95. Further legislation extended and refined local government. These changes mattered because many people experienced government locally, and because municipal politics trained a generation of reformers.

Britain therefore expanded political participation substantially between 1832 and 1872 without establishing universal adult suffrage. By the end of this period, a majority of adult men in boroughs were enfranchised in practice, but no women, many poorer rural men, and large numbers of lodgers and servants remained excluded. The House of Lords retained its legislative role. The electorate had grown, the secret ballot existed, and parties were organizing to mobilize voters, developments that Chapter 102 will take further.

Division III · Sections 99.9 to 99.12
France: Constitutional Monarchy, Republicanism, Bonapartism, and the Third Republic

These sections examine France's changing political institutions after Napoleon, concentrating on the relationship between republican citizenship, constitutional monarchy, authoritarian government, and the establishment of a more durable republic.

Section 99.9

From the Bourbon Restoration to the July Monarchy: Constitutional Government and Restricted Representation

The Charter of 1814, issued by Louis XVIII as a grant from the crown, established the institutional framework of the Restoration. It created a Chamber of Peers appointed by the king and a Chamber of Deputies elected on a narrow property franchise, and it guaranteed equality before the law, religious toleration with Catholicism as the state religion, and certain individual liberties, including a limited freedom of the press. Only men who paid at least three hundred francs in direct taxes could vote, and a far higher qualification applied to candidates, so that the electorate numbered roughly ninety thousand to a hundred thousand in a country of about thirty million. The king alone proposed laws, appointed ministers, and commanded the army, and the Charter's preamble presented it as a royal gift, which later liberals regarded as a denial of popular sovereignty.

Politics in the Restoration revolved around competing visions. Ultraroyalists wanted to restore the privileges of nobility and church and viewed the Revolution's legal settlement with hostility. Constitutional royalists, including the doctrinaires, accepted the Charter as a compromise that protected property and order. Liberals sought a stronger parliament, press freedom, and wider rights, and republicans, a smaller group, hoped for the end of the monarchy. The accession of Charles X in 1824 strengthened ultraroyalist influence, and his appointment of the unpopular Prince de Polignac as minister, followed by the ordinances of July 1830 suspending press freedom and dissolving the Chamber, provoked the July Revolution narrated in Chapter 98.

The revolution of 1830 changed the constitution without creating a democratic republic. The Chamber of Deputies, dominated by liberal monarchists, revised the Charter and offered the crown to Louis-Philippe, the Duke of Orléans, who became "King of the French" by the will of the nation, not by hereditary right alone. The revised Charter removed the preamble's claim that the charter was a royal grant, abolished Catholicism's status as state religion in favor of recognizing it as the religion of most French people, gave deputies the right to propose laws alongside the king, and ended the hereditary peerage in 1831. The tax qualification for voting was lowered to two hundred francs, which approximately doubled the electorate, though it still included only a small fraction of adult men.

The July Monarchy (1830 to 1848) was governed largely by a propertied elite of landowners, officials, lawyers, and businessmen, and François Guizot, who dominated government from the 1840s, became its most identifiable figure. Its supporters argued that the regime represented capacity and property, and that political rights should be extended as citizens acquired education and wealth. Its critics, including republicans, Catholic legitimists, and increasingly liberal opponents, argued that a narrow electorate of around a quarter of a million, subject to government influence through patronage and the appointment of officials, made a mockery of representation. Repression of republican societies, press restrictions after 1835, and the crushing of insurrections in Lyon and Paris during the 1830s showed the regime's limits.

Demand for electoral reform grew during the 1840s. Proposals to lower the franchise qualification or to admit "capacities," such as professionals and educated men, were repeatedly rejected by Guizot's majorities. Opposition leaders organized a banquet campaign in 1847 and 1848 as a way of circumventing restrictions on political meetings, and the government's ban on a banquet in Paris in February 1848 was one of the triggers of revolution. This section's responsibility is the institutional framework and its limitations, while the events of the July and February Revolutions themselves belong to Chapter 98. What matters here is that the July Monarchy's constitutional government rested on a narrow political constituency and had few means of adapting to demands for wider participation.

Section 99.10

The Second Republic: Universal Male Suffrage, Republican Citizenship, and Constitutional Conflict

The Provisional Government proclaimed after the overthrow of Louis-Philippe in February 1848 moved quickly to establish a republic based on a broad understanding of citizenship. A decree of 5 March 1848 introduced universal male suffrage for men aged twenty-one and over, with a residence requirement of six months, and it expanded the electorate from a quarter of a million to around nine million. This was among the largest sudden enfranchisements in nineteenth-century Europe. Other measures abolished slavery in the colonies, which section 99.17 treats, and affirmed freedoms of the press and assembly, though the republican government remained divided between moderate republicans and those who sought social reform.

The first elections under the new suffrage, held on 23 April 1848, returned a Constituent Assembly with a moderate republican majority and many conservatives, including numerous monarchists who accepted the republic provisionally. The assembly's relationship with Paris radicals deteriorated, and the June Days of 1848, in which the government suppressed a working-class insurrection in Paris with heavy casualties, thousands of arrests, and extensive orders for transportation, left the republic divided between order and social reform. The orders must not be equated with completed overseas deportations: a later study identified approximately 459 people actually sent to Algeria in 1850 after administrative review and clemency. The political reaction after June moved the assembly toward more conservative positions, including limits on clubs and the press.

The Constitution of 4 November 1848 created a republic with a single legislative assembly of 750 members elected for three years by universal male suffrage, and a president elected by the same electorate for a four-year term who could not immediately be reelected. The president held executive power, appointed ministers, and commanded the armed forces, but the assembly could not be dissolved by him. The drafters, wary of both monarchy and Bonapartism, wanted to prevent either an overpowerful executive or an unstoppable assembly, and they had no mechanism for resolving conflicts between the two. Competing understandings of republican authority, one placing sovereignty in the assembly and the other in a president elected directly by the people, set the stage for the crisis that followed.

The presidential election of 10 December 1848 produced a decisive result. Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte, nephew of Napoleon I, won roughly three-quarters of the votes cast, far ahead of the moderate republican General Cavaignac and the socialist and radical candidates. His victory reflected the power of the Bonaparte name, the support of conservatives who saw him as a guarantor of order, and the votes of peasants and workers who had little trust in the republican leaders who had put down the June insurrection. Historians caution against reducing the vote to nostalgia, since it also expressed political choices in a rapidly expanded electorate.

The relationship between the president and the Legislative Assembly elected in May 1849, in which monarchists of various kinds held a majority, deteriorated. The assembly passed laws restricting clubs and the press and the Falloux Law of 1850 expanding church influence in education. Most consequential was the electoral law of 31 May 1850, which imposed a three-year residency requirement in a commune and effectively disfranchised roughly three million men, including many mobile workers, and was designed to reduce the influence of radical voters. Louis-Napoleon later denounced the law and demanded its repeal, positioning himself as defender of universal suffrage against an assembly that had curtailed it.

The Second Republic shows that formal expansion of political rights could coexist with continuing social inequality, administrative control, and political repression. Universal male suffrage was introduced by a revolutionary government and then restricted by a conservative assembly, while the executive built its authority by appealing to the same broad electorate. The republic's institutions did not collapse because voters rejected them but because their design and political divisions allowed a determined executive to seize power.

Section 99.11

Louis-Napoleon's Coup and the Second Empire: Authoritarian Government and Popular Sovereignty

The constitution barred Louis-Napoleon from standing for a second term in 1852, and the assembly refused to amend it. On 2 December 1851, the anniversary of Napoleon I's coronation and of Austerlitz, he dissolved the assembly, arrested leading opponents, restored universal male suffrage, and called a plebiscite. Resistance in Paris was crushed, and uprisings in parts of the south and center of France were suppressed, with thousands of arrests, imprisonments, and deportations. A plebiscite on 20 and 21 December 1851 approved his actions by an overwhelming margin, roughly seven and a half million to about 650,000, in conditions of intimidation and press control that make the figures difficult to interpret as a free expression of opinion, though many historians believe that real support existed. A new constitution of January 1852 gave the president ten-year powers, and after a further plebiscite in November 1852, he was proclaimed Emperor Napoleon III on 2 December 1852.

The Second Empire illustrates Bonapartism, a political system combining a centralized executive, appeals to popular sovereignty through plebiscites, public order, and administrative control. The emperor appointed ministers who were responsible to him alone. The Senate, whose members were chosen by the emperor, and the Council of State, which drafted legislation, held significant powers, while the elected Legislative Body (Corps législatif) could vote on laws and taxes but could not initiate bills and had a limited role in debate. Universal male suffrage continued, but the government used officially supported candidates, the influence of prefects, and control over municipal appointments to shape the outcome. The press decree of February 1852 imposed an authorization and warning system, and political associations and meetings were tightly restricted.

Prefects, the appointed heads of departments, were central. They supervised local officials, directed official candidates, influenced newspapers, and reported on local opinion. The regime also relied on the Catholic Church in the early years, on the support of conservative notables, and on the army. Opponents, including republicans, Orleanists, legitimists, and socialists, faced surveillance, exile, and exclusion from effective political activity. Victor Hugo's exile and his polemical writings against the regime are the best-known instance of a wider pattern.

The government also pursued economic and administrative modernization. Public works, including the rebuilding of Paris under Baron Haussmann, railway expansion, banking reform, and a commercial treaty with Britain in 1860, were associated with the regime's appeal. This chapter does not repeat the industrial history of Chapters 93 to 95. What matters politically is that the regime tried to base its legitimacy on prosperity, order, and national prestige as well as popular sovereignty, and that many citizens accepted it for those reasons.

The distinction between electoral participation and effective democratic accountability is central here. Millions of men voted in elections and plebiscites under the Second Empire. But the electorate could not remove the executive, the legislature could not control ministers, and opposition was restricted. The French experience therefore warns against equating the extent of suffrage with the degree of democracy. It also shows how authoritarian governments could claim popular legitimacy, a claim that later authoritarian movements would make in their own forms, though students should avoid treating the Second Empire as a simple precedent for them.

Section 99.12

The Liberal Empire, the Fall of Napoleon III, and the Constitutional Settlement of 1875

From about 1860, Napoleon III began to liberalize the regime. A decree of November 1860 allowed the Legislative Body to debate an annual address in reply to the emperor's speech and to publish its proceedings, and in 1867 deputies gained a right of interpellation, which allowed them to question ministers. Press and assembly laws of 1868 relaxed censorship and allowed public meetings under supervision. The reasons were various: foreign policy failures, including the unsuccessful Mexican intervention, a stronger opposition in the elections of 1863 and 1869, and the emperor's calculation that a more open regime could win broader support. Opposition candidates, including republicans, won seats in the larger cities, and new newspapers and meetings flourished.

In January 1870, Napoleon III appointed Émile Ollivier, a former republican turned moderate liberal, to lead a ministry, and a senatus-consultum of April 1870, approved by plebiscite on 8 May by roughly 7.3 million votes to about 1.5 million, created a more parliamentary system in which ministers were responsible to the legislature and the Senate became a second legislative chamber. The reforms did not remove the emperor's authority. He retained the right to appeal directly to the people by plebiscite and to be responsible to the nation, which left unclear who actually controlled the executive. The liberal empire was therefore a transitional form, and it did not have time to establish itself.

War ended it. France declared war on Prussia in July 1870, as the later section on German unification in Chapter 100 will describe in detail. Rapid German victories led to the capture of Napoleon III at Sedan on 2 September 1870, and the Parisian crowd and republican deputies proclaimed a republic on 4 September. The Government of National Defense continued the war, Paris endured a siege, and an armistice in January 1871 led to elections on 8 February 1871 for a National Assembly with the task of making peace. The assembly had a large monarchist majority, since voters prioritized peace, and it elected Adolphe Thiers as head of the executive, and the peace treaty was signed at Frankfurt in May 1871.

The Paris Commune, proclaimed in March 1871 after a confrontation over the National Guard's cannon, was a radical municipal government that challenged the assembly sitting at Versailles. It combined republicanism, demands for municipal autonomy, and social and labor measures. The government's troops suppressed it in the "Bloody Week" of 21 to 28 May 1871, with many thousands killed, and thousands more were imprisoned or deported. The repression and the fear of social revolution encouraged conservative republicans to present the republic as a regime of order, and it discredited the extreme left for years.

The Third Republic's institutions were created by compromise. Monarchists were divided between legitimists, who supported the Bourbon claimant, and Orleanists, who supported the Orléans line, and they failed to agree on a king. Marshal MacMahon became president in 1873, with a mandate to prepare a possible restoration. The constitutional laws of 1875, adopted in February and July after the Wallon amendment (30 January 1875) won approval by a single vote, established a president elected by the two chambers for seven years, a Senate chosen indirectly, and a Chamber of Deputies elected by universal male suffrage. The president retained significant powers, including dissolving the Chamber with the Senate's consent, and the laws did not explicitly declare a republic. The 1875 settlement must be explained as a contested compromise, not as the inevitable culmination of earlier reform, and its durability only became clear later, after the crisis of 16 May 1877 reduced the president's role.

The source of legitimacy shifted from royal grant to national sovereignty and plebiscitary appeal, and the electorate expanded dramatically in 1848, then fluctuated.

A centralized administration, the prefectoral system, the Napoleonic legal codes, and the exclusion of women from politics survived every regime change.

Men enfranchised in 1848, political activists subject to repression after 1851 and 1871, and officials who changed allegiance with each regime felt the changes most strongly.

Division IV · Sections 99.13 to 99.16
Constitutional and Administrative Reform across the European States

These sections compare reform beyond Britain and France, showing how political modernization followed different paths in western, northern, central, southern, and eastern Europe.

Section 99.13

Belgium and the Netherlands: Constitutional Monarchy and Ministerial Responsibility

Belgium emerged from the revolution of 1830 against Dutch rule, narrated in Chapter 98, and its National Congress adopted a constitution on 7 February 1831. The Congress chose Leopold of Saxe-Coburg as king, and he swore the oath to the constitution in July 1831. The text became a model for liberals elsewhere. It placed sovereignty in the nation, divided powers among king, legislature, and courts, created a bicameral parliament, and guaranteed freedom of religion, the press, education, and association. It also reflected the cooperation of Catholics and liberals who had united against Dutch policy, and the compromise on church and state it embodied shaped Belgian politics for generations.

Historical Vocabulary
Ministerial Responsibility

Ministerial responsibility is the principle that ministers, not the monarch, answer politically for government acts. Constitutional texts often declared the monarch inviolable and required a minister's countersignature on royal decisions, which made the minister accountable to parliament and left the monarch above criticism. The principle could have a narrow legal meaning, such as liability for violating the constitution, or a broader political meaning, in which ministers needed parliamentary confidence to remain in office.

The distinction matters because many constitutions mentioned ministerial responsibility without establishing the broader, parliamentary meaning in practice.

Belgium's political representation was nevertheless limited. Voting rights rested on payment of a minimum tax, with thresholds that varied by locality, and the electorate was initially only a few tens of thousands, roughly one percent of the population. The Senate was also chosen by voters on a property basis, with senators required to pay high taxes. Belgian liberalism was thus a liberalism of property and civil rights, not of democracy. Wider suffrage came later, in the 1890s, and belongs to Chapter 102. Within the constitution, however, the principle of ministerial accountability developed in practice through the 1830s and 1840s as the king chose ministers who could command parliamentary majorities, and Leopold I was an active but constitutionally cautious monarch.

The Netherlands followed a different path. The constitution of 1815 had created a monarchy with a strong crown and a States General of limited authority, and after Belgium's secession, King William I resisted change. A revision in 1840 had modest effects. The revolutions of 1848 elsewhere, and the fear of upheaval, convinced William II to accept a constitutional commission led by the liberal jurist Johan Rudolf Thorbecke. The constitutional revision of 1848 introduced direct election of the Second Chamber, indirect election of the First Chamber by provincial states, and, above all, ministerial responsibility: the king was declared inviolable, and ministers became accountable to the States General. Thorbecke formed the government in 1849 and introduced further reforms of provincial and municipal government.

The Dutch reform shows how revolutionary pressure abroad could produce peaceful constitutional change at home. The monarchy survived by accepting new principles. Yet representation remained restricted: the franchise depended on tax payments and varied by district, so that only a small proportion of adult men voted until later in the century. The political arguments of the following decades, notably the "school struggle" over state and religious education, led to later franchise extensions. Like Belgium, the Netherlands expanded its political institutions in ways that established parliamentary accountability while leaving most people without the vote.

Section 99.14

Constitutional Reform in Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, and Switzerland

Constitutional reform in these countries shows that similar demands produced different outcomes. In Spain, the liberal Constitution of 1812 had been suspended and restored several times, and during the regency for the young Isabella II, the progressives' Constitution of 1837 attempted a compromise between national sovereignty and the crown, with a bicameral Cortes and a limited franchise. The moderates, who dominated after 1844, replaced it with the Constitution of 1845, which emphasized sovereignty shared between the Cortes and the crown, strengthened the monarch, and restricted suffrage to a small property-owning minority. The Glorious Revolution of September 1868 drove Isabella II into exile, and the Constitution of 1869 proclaimed national sovereignty, guaranteed individual rights, and established a broad male suffrage, which was among the widest in Europe at the time. The experiment collapsed amid a monarchy under Amadeo of Savoy, a short-lived republic in 1873 to 1874, and the Bourbon restoration of 1874 and 1876, showing the fragility of constitutional change in a context of military intervention and factional conflict.

In Portugal, the Constitutional Charter of 1826, granted by Pedro IV, a monarch who held both the Brazilian imperial and the Portuguese crowns before abdicating the latter in favor of his daughter, established a system with a strong crown and an additional "moderating power." Civil war between liberals and supporters of Dom Miguel ended in 1834 with a liberal victory, and Maria II reigned under the Charter, but political conflict between supporters of the more radical Constitution of 1822 and 1838 and defenders of the Charter continued. The Charter was reestablished in 1842, and the Additional Act of 1852 revised it to introduce direct elections. Portuguese constitutional monarchy therefore developed through repeated conflict between liberal factions over the balance between crown and parliament.

Denmark transformed from absolute monarchy to constitutional government in 1848 and 1849. Under pressure from liberal and national movements, particularly the National Liberals, Frederick VII agreed to end absolutism, and the constitution of 5 June 1849 created a bicameral Rigsdag with a directly elected Folketing and an indirectly elected Landsting. Originally the same eligible male householder electorate voted for both chambers, but candidates for the Landsting faced higher age and income or tax qualifications. The constitution was revised in 1866 to give wealthy taxpayers privileged influence over the election of Landsting members, reflecting conservative reaction after the war of 1864 with Prussia and Austria. Danish politics thereafter centered on conflicts between farmers' parties in the Folketing and conservative governments supported by the Landsting.

Sweden ended its centuries-old Riksdag of the four Estates, composed of nobility, clergy, burghers, and peasants, in a reform decided by the Estates themselves in 1865 and enacted in the Riksdag Act of 1866, creating a bicameral parliament that first met in 1867. The First Chamber was elected indirectly by county councils and by the councils of major towns outside those counties, whose weighted municipal voting systems strongly favored property and wealth. The Second Chamber was directly elected by men meeting property or income requirements, so that only a modest share of adult men voted. The reform replaced estate privileges with property-based representation, which expanded the political role of rural landowners and townspeople, and it was a significant institutional change that nevertheless left the franchise narrow. Some women taxpayers had gained a municipal vote in 1862, one of the earliest such rights in Europe.

Switzerland combined federalism and democratic participation. After a short civil war in November 1847 between the Sonderbund of Catholic cantons and the federal Diet, the federal constitution of 1848 created a federal state with a two-chamber Federal Assembly, the National Council representing the population and the Council of States representing the cantons, and a collegial executive, the Federal Council. The cantons retained wide powers. The revision of 1874 strengthened federal authority over the army and law and introduced the optional referendum on federal laws, and it guaranteed freedom of religion. Citizenship was limited to men, and the restrictions on Jewish settlement were lifted only in 1866 and 1874. Switzerland shows a path in which reform came through federal compromise and direct democratic procedures, though it did not extend rights to women.

Section 99.15

Prussia and the Habsburg Monarchy: Constitutional Reform within Powerful Dynastic States

In Prussia and the Habsburg lands, constitutionalism developed within states where the dynasty, the army, and the bureaucracy remained strong. Prussia's king, Frederick William IV, had refused a constitution before 1848. Revolution in March 1848 forced concessions, and a constitution was imposed in December 1848 after the dissolution of the elected assembly. A revised constitution took effect on 31 January 1850. It created a two-chamber Landtag, a House of Representatives and a House of Lords (Herrenhaus), guaranteed certain civil rights, and recognized the legislature's role in taxation and law. The king, however, kept command of the army, appointed and dismissed ministers who were responsible to him, and could issue emergency decrees.

The most distinctive feature was the three-class franchise, introduced by decree in May 1849 for elections to the lower house. Voters were divided into three classes according to the amount of direct taxes they paid, so that each class, regardless of the number of voters it contained, chose one-third of the electors. A small group of wealthy taxpayers thus had the same weight as the far larger group of modest taxpayers. Voting was indirect and open, which exposed voters to pressure from employers and officials. The system gave disproportionate political weight to wealth, and it remained in force in Prussia until 1918. It is a clear example of a constitutional order that combined an elected assembly with limits on both the franchise and the assembly's real power.

The limits became visible in the constitutional conflict of 1860 to 1866. When King William I sought to enlarge and reorganize the army, the liberal majority in the lower house refused to approve the necessary funds. William appointed Otto von Bismarck minister-president in September 1862. Bismarck argued that the constitution contained a gap when the two chambers and the crown could not agree on a budget, and that the government could therefore continue to collect taxes and spend money without legislative approval. The government governed this way for several years, and in 1866, after military victory over Austria, the Landtag passed an Indemnity Bill retroactively approving the expenditures. The episode showed the limits of parliamentary authority over the Prussian executive, and its resolution through national success is part of the story told in Chapter 100.

The Habsburg monarchy experimented with several constitutional forms. After the revolutions of 1848, an imposed constitution of March 1849 was never put fully into effect, and in 1851 the emperor, Francis Joseph, ended constitutional experiments in what is known as neoabsolutism. Military defeat in 1859, financial strain, and national pressures led to new attempts. The October Diploma of 1860 promised a diet-based system with a greater role for provincial assemblies, and the February Patent of 1861 created a Reichsrat with a lower house chosen indirectly by provincial diets. Hungarian leaders rejected these arrangements, demanding the restoration of their own constitution.

The Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 resolved this problem by reorganizing the monarchy into two states under one ruler. Francis Joseph was crowned King of Hungary in June 1867, Hungary regained its constitution and parliament, and common ministries handled foreign affairs, defense, and related finances. The Austrian half, often called Cisleithania, received a set of fundamental laws in December 1867, the December Constitution, which established a bicameral Reichsrat, guaranteed civil rights including equality of nationalities and religious freedom, and set up an independent judiciary. The franchise, based on curiae representing landowners, towns, chambers of commerce, and rural communities, restricted voting heavily.

Constitutionalism in a multilingual and multinational imperial system faced particular difficulties. The Compromise satisfied Hungarian political elites, who then governed a kingdom containing large Romanian, Slovak, Serb, Croatian, and other populations whose language rights were more limited than the constitutional text might suggest. In the Austrian half, Czechs, Poles, Slovenes, Ukrainians, and others contended over language use, education, and representation. These conflicts, which belong to Chapter 106's analysis of the pre-1914 monarchy, show that constitutions did not resolve questions of nationality. The wars and diplomacy of unification are the subject of Chapter 100.

Section 99.16

Alexander II and Russia's Great Reforms: Emancipation, Local Government, and Autocratic Modernization

Russia's Great Reforms followed military defeat. The Crimean War (1853 to 1856), fought against Britain, France, the Ottoman Empire, and Sardinia, exposed weaknesses in Russia's army, finances, transport, and administration, and many officials concluded that serfdom and the institutions bound up with it limited the empire's strength. Alexander II, who became emperor in 1855, endorsed a program of change that his government implemented over the following two decades. The reforms were conceived and directed from above, by the emperor, his advisers, and committees of officials and nobles, though pressure from public discussion and peasant unrest also shaped them. This chapter gives dates in Old Style (Julian calendar) with New Style equivalents where useful, and it does not reproduce the fuller account in the separate Russian history textbook.

The emancipation manifesto of 19 February 1861 (3 March, New Style) ended the personal legal dependence of private serfs, who numbered more than twenty million people. Peasants gained legal personality: they could marry freely, own property, and sue in court. But the land settlement reflected compromise with landowners. Peasants received allotments of land, usually held collectively by village communes (the obshchina or mir), and in return owed redemption payments, spread over decades and financed by state credit to compensate landowners. Many peasants considered their allotments too small and the payments burdensome. A transitional period of obligations delayed full independence, and the commune's collective responsibility for taxes limited individual mobility. Emancipation was a transformation of legal status, but not of economic dependence.

The reform of local government in 1864 created zemstvos, elected assemblies at district and provincial levels, with responsibility for schools, roads, medical services, and local economic matters. Voting was organized by property and social estate, which gave nobles and wealthier landowners a large share of influence, but peasants also participated. The zemstvos developed a professional staff of teachers, doctors, and statisticians and became a notable arena of public life. The judicial reform of November 1864 introduced public court proceedings, an independent judiciary in principle, trained legal professionals, and jury trials in specified criminal cases. These changes brought elements of the rule of law into a system dominated by arbitrary administrative power, though political offenses were often removed from the new courts.

Military reform, led by War Minister Dmitry Milyutin, shortened service terms and improved training, culminating in the general conscription statute of 1874, which replaced the earlier recruitment system, drawn largely from peasants and townspeople for very long service, with a universal obligation, though with numerous exemptions, particularly for the educated. Education reform expanded schooling at several levels, and censorship was partly relaxed in the early years of the reign.

These reforms transformed institutions without establishing parliamentary government. Russia had no national legislature, and the emperor remained an autocrat. After an attempt on his life in 1866 and the Polish revolt of 1863, the government became more cautious, and revolutionary terrorism in the 1870s, which culminated in Alexander II's assassination in 1881, provoked further reaction. The historical significance of the reforms is contested. Some historians treat them as a decisive modernizing turning point, others emphasize their incompleteness and the persistence of social and legal inequalities. Students should also distinguish carefully between the emancipation of serfs, who were legally subject to landowners within the empire, and the abolition of chattel slavery in overseas colonies, which section 99.17 examines, since the legal status, economic setting, and political context of each differed.

Section 99.17

The Abolition of Slavery and the Contradictions of European Liberal Reform

European states abolished slavery in their colonies at different times and in different ways, and the process was tied closely to metropolitan political change. Students should first distinguish the prohibition of the slave trade from the abolition of slavery itself. Britain outlawed its Atlantic slave trade in 1807, and other states followed over the next decades, but the institution of slavery continued in many colonies for decades afterward. Chapter 70 examined Atlantic slavery as part of the European economy, and this section concerns its legal ending and the politics surrounding it.

Britain's Slavery Abolition Act, passed in August 1833 and effective from 1 August 1834, ended slavery in most British colonies, though not in territories then administered by the East India Company, Ceylon, and St Helena. The Act authorized £20 million in compensation, which was paid to slaveholders, not to the enslaved, and it established an apprenticeship system under which formerly enslaved adults remained bound to their former owners for unpaid or partly paid labor for up to six years. In practice, apprenticeship generated widespread conflict, accusations of abuse, and strong public opposition in Britain, and colonial legislatures ended it early, by August 1838. Abolitionist campaigning in Britain, which combined religious networks, petitions, and speakers, was an important influence. So too was resistance by the enslaved, notably the Jamaican uprising of 1831 to 1832, led by Samuel Sharpe, which persuaded many in Britain that slavery could not be maintained without increasing violence.

In France, slavery had been abolished in 1794 and restored by Napoleon in 1802, after which the Haitian Revolution had created an independent state. The Second Republic's decree of 27 April 1848, drafted under the influence of Victor Schoelcher, abolished slavery in the French colonies, and it was a significant part of the republic's claim to extend liberty. The law also provided compensation for slaveholders, and the formerly enslaved received citizenship rights, including the franchise, in the colonies of Martinique, Guadeloupe, and Réunion, though conditions in colonial society limited their effective use. The Dutch abolished slavery on 1 July 1863 in Suriname and the Caribbean colonies, paying compensation to owners, and in Suriname, they imposed a ten-year period of state supervision during which formerly enslaved laborers were obliged to work on contract, a system that ended in 1873. Denmark ended slavery in its Caribbean colonies in 1848, after an uprising of the enslaved on St Croix forced the governor to proclaim emancipation.

Multiple Perspectives

Abolition in the British Caribbean, 1833 to 1838

Note: the perspectives below summarize documented positions held by groups in this period, and they do not carry equal power, moral claim, or responsibility for the system of slavery.

Abolitionists in Britain: Many campaigners, often motivated by religious conviction, regarded slavery as sinful and pressed Parliament through petitions and meetings. Many also accepted gradualism and compensation as the price of passing legislation, and some assumed that freed people would need supervision.
Enslaved and formerly enslaved people: They sought freedom, control over their own time, family life, and access to land and wages. They resisted through uprisings such as that in Jamaica in 1831 to 1832, through everyday refusals to work, and, after 1834, through negotiating hours and pay under apprenticeship and leaving plantations when possible. They were not merely recipients of reform but political actors who shaped it.
Colonial officials and the imperial government: Officials sought to maintain order, protect production, and manage a transition. They designed apprenticeship partly to guarantee labor and prevent social collapse, and special magistrates sent to the colonies often reported abuses while enforcing labor discipline.
Plantation proprietors: Owners argued that emancipation threatened their property and economic survival. Many received compensation, and many invested it, some in Britain, and they pressed for labor controls, and later for indentured migrants, to replace the labor they had lost.

Emancipation legislation did not create equal political, social, or economic conditions. Formerly enslaved people often remained dependent on the same planters for land and employment, faced labor laws that restricted mobility, and encountered racial hierarchies in law and custom. Colonial governments introduced indentured labor from India and elsewhere, and vagrancy and contract laws limited freedom. In metropolitan Europe, abolition was often celebrated as a triumph of liberal principle, yet it was accompanied by compensation for owners, and the colonial subjects of European states generally did not enjoy the political rights of citizens in the metropole. Abolition could thus represent a major legal transformation without establishing equality, a pattern that illustrates the contradictions of European liberalism: a commitment to liberty and legal equality coexisted with imperial hierarchies and coerced labor. The experiences of the formerly enslaved are best recovered from court records, missionary reports, petitions, and the later testimonies and oral traditions of descendants, remembering that official records reflect the viewpoint of administrators.

Section 99.18

Religious Equality, Minority Rights, and the Changing Boundaries of Citizenship

Legal and political equality for religious minorities advanced throughout the period, though unevenly. In Britain and Ireland, the repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts in 1828 and Catholic emancipation in 1829 removed major barriers for Protestant Dissenters and Catholics. Further steps followed: the disestablishment of the Church of Ireland in 1869 and the opening of Oxford and Cambridge to non-Anglicans in 1871. These changes narrowed, without eliminating, the privileged position of the Church of England, and Catholics, particularly in Ireland, continued to confront social and economic disadvantages. Emancipation removed formal disabilities and did not guarantee equal treatment.

Jewish emancipation proceeded through different legal routes in different states. Revolutionary France had granted Jews citizenship in 1790 and 1791, and under Napoleon the state organized Jewish communities through consistories while imposing some restrictions. In the German states, the emancipation of Jews was gradual and often reversible: Prussia's edict of 1812 granted citizenship with limitations that were maintained or reinterpreted afterward, the revolutions of 1848 brought declarations of equality that were partly rolled back, and the North German Confederation passed a law in 1869 removing religious restrictions on civil and political rights, which extended to the German Empire in 1871. In the Habsburg monarchy, the December Constitution of 1867 guaranteed equal civil and political rights regardless of religion, and Hungary adopted legal equality for Jews in 1867. In Britain, Jews could not sit in Parliament until the Jewish Relief Act of 1858 changed the oath, after Lionel de Rothschild had been repeatedly elected for the City of London. Belgium's constitution guaranteed religious equality from 1831. Students should recognize that legal emancipation seldom ended social prejudice, exclusion from certain professions, and violence, and the history of antisemitism and racial nationalism that intensified in the later nineteenth century belongs to Chapter 103.

The relationship between state religion, education, public office, and political participation varied. In France, the Concordat of 1801 continued to govern relations between state and Catholic Church, while Protestants and Jews were also officially recognized and their clergy supported by the state. Conflicts over church influence in education, such as the Falloux Law of 1850, shaped French politics. In Belgium, the liberal and Catholic alliance of 1830 gave way to open party conflict over schools. In the Habsburg and Ottoman lands, religious communities held distinct legal status, and reform involved renegotiating that status. Within the Ottoman Empire, the Tanzimat reforms of 1839 and 1856 promised equality before the law to Muslim and non-Muslim subjects, though implementation was uneven and contested.

The problem of citizenship in multinational states and colonial systems pushed beyond religion. In the Habsburg monarchy, equality of nationalities in the constitution coexisted with unequal use of languages in administration and education. In European colonies, subjects were governed under different laws than citizens, a distinction that became explicit in later colonial legal systems. Equality before the law did not necessarily guarantee electoral participation, public acceptance, or equal access to state institutions, since property qualifications, informal discrimination, and unequal enforcement limited the practical effect of legal change.

Section 99.19

Women, Civil Rights, Education, Property, and the Limits of Political Reform

Women were excluded from national parliamentary voting in every European state during this period, yet their position in law and public life was not uniform. Students should distinguish several things: formal political citizenship, which included the right to vote and hold office; civil legal capacity, which concerned whether a woman could make contracts, own property, and appear in court; access to education; property rights within marriage; and participation in public organizations. A woman might be excluded from the first and still take part in the last, and legal reform could move some of these dimensions while leaving others unchanged.

Women's involvement in reform movements was extensive. They signed petitions, attended meetings, and organized female associations in Chartist towns. They were central to British abolitionism, forming ladies' antislavery societies and sustaining boycotts of slave-grown sugar. They worked in religious and philanthropic reform, in educational initiatives, in local associations, and as writers and journalists. In France, women participated in the clubs and press of 1848, but the republican government and the assembly refused to extend the vote, and laws in 1848 and 1850 restricted women's political activity. Jeanne Deroin's attempt to stand as a candidate in 1849 was rejected. In Prussia, the law on associations from 1850 barred women from political organizations.

Legal capacity differed by state. The Napoleonic Code, which influenced Belgium, parts of Italy, the Rhineland, and other territories, placed married women under their husbands' authority and limited their control over property and legal acts. English common law, under the doctrine of coverture, similarly merged a married woman's legal identity into her husband's. Early reforms in Britain gave limited relief: the Married Women's Property Act of 1870 allowed married women to keep their own earnings and certain inheritances, and a broader Act in 1882 expanded their rights over property. Unmarried women ratepayers gained the vote in British municipal elections in 1869. Sweden extended a municipal vote to some women taxpayers in 1862. These measures were limited but significant as precedents.

Education was an important area of change. Girls' secondary schools, women's colleges in Britain, such as Girton in 1869, and expanded teacher training widened opportunities for some women, and in France, Minister Victor Duruy encouraged public courses for girls in the 1860s. Access remained restricted by class, religion, and region, and the professions mostly remained closed.

John Stuart Mill was the most prominent male advocate of women's political equality in this period. Elected to Parliament in 1865, he presented a petition for women's suffrage, signed by about fifteen hundred women, in June 1866, and in May 1867 he proposed an amendment to the Reform Bill to replace "man" with "person," which was defeated by a substantial majority but gained a significant minority of votes. His book The Subjection of Women was published in 1869. His arguments, and the organizations that followed, mark a beginning, not a conclusion. Nineteenth-century reforms could expand men's political participation while leaving women without national voting rights, because the dominant understandings of citizenship tied political capacity to property, military obligation, and household headship in ways that excluded women. The later mass suffrage organizations and feminist movements belong to Chapter 102, and this section has concerned their legal and institutional foundations.

Section 99.20

Reform, Citizenship, and the Transformation of European Political Institutions

A comparison of the cases shows both common patterns and striking differences. British reform extended parliamentary representation in stages through legislation negotiated within Parliament, while the monarchy and Lords adapted without being eliminated. France experienced repeated regime change, with suffrage expansions and contractions and an executive that at times overrode representative institutions. Continental constitutional monarchies, from Belgium and the Netherlands to Denmark, Sweden, Prussia, and Austria, adopted varying combinations of elected assemblies, restricted franchises, and royal prerogatives. Switzerland built federal democracy, Russia combined sweeping legal reform with autocracy, and colonial emancipation changed legal status while preserving coercive and hierarchical relations.

Several relationships recur. Economic modernization created new social groups that demanded recognition and new administrative tasks that required more capable governments. Political mobilization, through petitions, newspapers, associations, and sometimes riots, shaped what governments considered necessary or prudent. Revolutionary threats, whether realized as in 1830 and 1848 or merely feared as in Britain in 1831 and 1832, influenced the timing and form of concessions. War, as in the Crimean War and the Franco-Prussian War, exposed weaknesses and opened or closed opportunities. And government initiative, whether liberal, conservative, or authoritarian, designed many of the institutional changes.

The chapter has insisted on several distinctions. The expansion of voting rights differed from the extension of civil liberties, legal equality, and substantive political influence. A broad franchise under Napoleon III did not amount to democratic accountability, while a narrower franchise in Britain accompanied growing parliamentary control of ministers. Governments introduced reforms for reasons ranging from reducing revolutionary pressure to preserving authority and modernizing administration. Some changes proved lasting: parliamentary government in Britain, the Belgian and Dutch constitutional systems, Swiss federal institutions, and, with delay, the French republic. Others remained incomplete, such as the franchises of Prussia and Sweden, reversible, as in the French law of 1850 and Spain's collapse after 1869, or limited to particular populations, as in colonial emancipation.

Historical Debate

Did Reform Result Mainly from Popular Pressure or from Elite Initiative?

The question: Historians disagree about how much weight to give to pressure from below and to the choices of governing elites in explaining nineteenth-century political reform, whether in Britain's reform acts, French constitutional change, Russian emancipation, or colonial abolition.

Interpretation A: Popular pressure was decisive. Scholars emphasizing this view point to the political unions of 1830 to 1832, the Chartist petitions, the Reform League demonstrations of 1866 to 1867, the uprisings of the enslaved, and peasant unrest in Russia. They argue that elites conceded only when the cost of refusal rose, and that organized movements shaped the agenda even when they failed immediately.
Interpretation B: Elites shaped and controlled reform. Scholars emphasizing this view point to the design of the 1832 Act to detach the middle classes from radical politics, to Disraeli's party calculations in 1867, to the bureaucratic origins of Russian emancipation and the administrative reforms of many states, and to the compensation paid to slaveholders. They argue that governments chose reforms that preserved their authority and often limited what protesters obtained.

Many historians now favor an interpretation combining both, in which elites responded to pressure but determined the form of concessions, and in which movements and governments influenced each other.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on parliamentary debates, ministerial correspondence, petitions, newspapers, police and magistrates' reports, party records, and the testimony of participants, each of which reflects its authors' interests.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the role of ordinary people in political change, and whether reform should be regarded as a gift from above, a victory from below, or the outcome of bargaining.

Cause and Effect

The Reform of Political Institutions, 1815 to 1875

Long-term causes

Urban and commercial growth, rising literacy and printing, the legacy of the French Revolution and Napoleonic administration, religious and national pluralism, and the diffusion of liberal and constitutional ideas.

Short-term causes

Economic crises, the revolutions of 1830 and 1848, the Irish mobilization of the 1820s, military defeat in the Crimean and Franco-Prussian wars, party competition, and organized campaigns such as the political unions and the Chartists.

Event or transformation

Constitutions, enlarged and redistributed electorates, new local government, legal and administrative reform, and emancipation of serfs and enslaved people.

Immediate effects

Wider but still restricted political participation, new party alignments, stronger legislatures in some states, stronger executives in others, and continuing inequality in property, religion, gender, and imperial status.

Long-term consequences

Institutional foundations for later mass politics, uneven models of parliamentary and authoritarian government, and a legal vocabulary of rights that later movements used to demand more.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains reform. Economic change, mobilization, revolution, war, bargaining, and government initiative interacted differently in each state, and outcomes were contingent, not inevitable.

Nineteenth-century reform transformed European states without creating a single European model of democracy or citizenship.

Chapter Summary

Between 1815 and 1875, European governments and societies reshaped their political institutions through a combination of legislation, concession, revolution, war, and administrative reorganization. This chapter distinguished reform from revolution while showing that the two were connected: revolutionary threats altered what governments were willing to concede, and concessions shaped what later reformers could demand. Reform could preserve an established order while transforming it, and the chapter's central problem was whether reform extended liberty, preserved authority, or did both in contested combination.

Constitutional vocabulary required care. A constitution or parliament did not necessarily limit a ruler, and parliamentary government, in which ministers depended on legislative confidence, developed unevenly. Political participation extended well beyond the franchise, through petitions, newspapers, associations, and public meetings, and governments responded with a mixture of concession, censorship, and policing. Administrative modernization increased state capacity in many countries without increasing accountability.

Britain expanded representation through a sequence of measures. Catholic emancipation in 1829 removed major religious barriers while narrowing the Irish county franchise. The Great Reform Act of 1832 redistributed seats and enlarged the property-qualified electorate, but it excluded most men and all women. Chartism, with its six-point Charter, mobilized working-class demands for democratic representation, and though its petitions failed, its program influenced later reform. The Second Reform Act of 1867 roughly doubled the electorate, and the Ballot Act of 1872 introduced secret voting, while municipal reform created elected local councils.

France passed through the Charter of the Restoration, the narrow July Monarchy, the Second Republic with universal male suffrage, the plebiscitary authoritarianism of the Second Empire, and a republic founded in 1870 and given institutional form by the compromise laws of 1875. The French case showed that a broad franchise did not guarantee parliamentary democracy, and that executive power, administrative centralization, and political conflict over the location of sovereignty were as important as the size of the electorate.

Elsewhere, constitutional monarchy took varied forms. Belgium and the Netherlands established ministerial responsibility within restricted franchises. Spain and Portugal alternated among competing constitutional models amid factional and military conflict. Denmark and Sweden replaced absolutism and estate-based representation with bicameral parliaments, and Switzerland created a federal state with direct democratic procedures. Prussia combined a legislature with a three-class franchise and royal control of the army, while the Habsburg monarchy reorganized itself through the Compromise of 1867 into a dual structure containing many nationalities.

Russia under Alexander II carried out far-reaching reforms, including the emancipation of the serfs in 1861, elected zemstvos and new courts in 1864, and universal conscription in 1874, but retained autocracy. The reforms transformed institutions and legal status without creating a parliament, and emancipation of serfs within the empire should be distinguished from the abolition of overseas slavery.

Abolition in British, French, Danish, and Dutch colonies was a major legal transformation shaped by abolitionist campaigns and by the resistance and political action of enslaved people. Yet it involved compensation to owners, apprenticeship or state supervision, indentured labor, and continuing racial and colonial hierarchy. Religious minorities gained legal rights unevenly, and Jews and Catholics in several states moved toward legal equality, while social prejudice persisted. Women were excluded from national political participation everywhere, though they took part in many movements and gained limited property and municipal rights.

The historical debate over whether popular pressure or elite initiative produced reform is best answered by a combination, in which movements and governments influenced each other and elites controlled the form of concessions. The comparison of states showed no single path to democracy, and some reforms were reversed or limited to particular populations. The chapter's conclusion is that nineteenth-century reform transformed European states, extended important rights, and created institutions on which later movements built, without resolving fundamental inequalities of property, gender, religion, political participation, and imperial authority.

The right to vote, defined in this period by property, tax payment, residence, sex, and sometimes religion.
Constituency
The electoral district or body of voters represented by a member of a legislature.
Rotten borough
A British parliamentary constituency with very few voters or inhabitants that nevertheless returned members, often under the influence of a patron.
Redistribution
The reallocation of legislative seats among constituencies, as in 1832 and 1867, to reflect population change.
Constitutional monarchy
A monarchy whose powers are defined and limited by a constitution and an assembly with a role in lawmaking or taxation.
Parliamentary government
A system in which the executive depends on a legislative majority and can be removed by it.
Ministerial responsibility
The principle that ministers, not the monarch, answer to the legislature for government acts.
Bicameralism
A legislature of two chambers, often an elected lower house and an upper house of nobles, appointees, or wealthy electors.
Charter
A constitutional document granted by a ruler, such as the French Charter of 1814, as distinct from one drafted in the name of the nation.
Property qualification
A requirement of wealth, rental value, or tax payment for voting or holding office.
Plebiscite
A direct popular vote on a ruler's action or constitutional change, used by Napoleon III to claim popular legitimacy.
Bonapartism
A political system combining strong executive authority, plebiscitary appeals to the people, public order, and administrative control.
Prefect
An appointed head of a French department who supervised local government and, under the Second Empire, elections.
Secret ballot
Voting in private, introduced in Britain in 1872, which reduced pressure from landlords and employers.
Chartism
The British working-class movement for the six points of the People's Charter, active chiefly from 1838 to 1858.
Municipal corporation
A borough's governing body; the Act of 1835 replaced many unreformed corporations with elected councils.
Federalism
A system dividing powers between central and regional governments, as in Switzerland after 1848.
Three-class franchise
The Prussian system weighting votes by tax payment so that a few wealthy voters equaled many poorer ones.
Zemstvo
An elected district or provincial assembly created in Russia in 1864 to manage local services.
Emancipation
The legal release of a dependent group from bondage, applied to Russian serfs in 1861 and, in broader usage, to enslaved people and religious minorities.
Abolition
The legal ending of slavery itself, distinct from the prohibition of the slave trade.
Apprenticeship
The British transitional system of 1834 to 1838 requiring formerly enslaved people to work for former owners.
Citizenship
Legal membership in a political community, carrying rights and duties that differed by sex, religion, property, and colonial status.
Civil rights
Legal rights to property, contract, legal process, and religious practice, distinct from political rights such as voting.
Estates
Legally defined social orders such as nobility, clergy, burghers, and peasants, as in the Swedish Riksdag before 1866.
Autocracy
Government by a ruler with unrestricted authority, as in Russia, where reform occurred without a legislature.
Compromise of 1867
The agreement creating Austria-Hungary as two states with one ruler and common ministries.
Coverture
The legal doctrine in English law merging a married woman's legal identity into her husband's.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how political change in nineteenth-century Europe often occurred without a decisive revolutionary break and still altered the foundations of government. The institutions examined here, parliaments, electoral systems, local councils, courts, and legal codes, shaped the political life of later generations, and they supplied the framework within which mass parties, trade unions, and women's movements would grow.

It also reveals that reform was never a single process. Britain, France, the continental constitutional monarchies, Russia, and the colonial empires followed different paths because of different social structures, dynastic traditions, wars, and relations between rulers and ruled. The chapter has avoided treating the British route as a model for all Europe, or treating the French or Russian experience as deviations from it.

The chapter connects regions that conventional narratives often separate. Dutch, Belgian, and Swiss constitutionalism, Iberian instability, Scandinavian parliamentary reform, central European dynastic constitutionalism, and Russian autocratic modernization, along with the overseas emancipations carried out by European states, belong to one European history in which institutions, ideas, and pressures circulated. It also shows that the history of rights inside Europe cannot be separated from empire and slavery, a theme that Volume IV develops further in its treatment of imperialism.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the argument of Volume IV by showing that the age of revolution had institutional consequences that extended long after 1848. Industrialization, nationalism, and imperial expansion changed the societies these institutions governed, and the unresolved questions of property, gender, religion, and imperial authority returned with greater force in the decades that followed. The next chapter turns to the wars and diplomacy that unified Italy and Germany and redrew the political map of central Europe.
