Portuguese and Dutch Commercial Empires

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

When Vasco da Gama's ships anchored off Calicut in 1498, they entered not an unknown ocean but one of the busiest commercial zones in the world. The two empires examined in this chapter, one built by the Portuguese Crown and the other by a chartered company of Dutch investors, would spend the next three centuries trying, with very uneven success, to bend that zone to their advantage.

In May 1498 a small Portuguese fleet reached the Malabar coast of India, where the port of Calicut was ruled by a Hindu sovereign known as the Zamorin and frequented by Muslim, Hindu, Jewish, and Christian merchants whose networks reached East Africa, the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, Gujarat, Bengal, the Malay world, and China. The Portuguese had spent most of a century learning to sail down the African coast, as Chapter 68 explained. What they had found at the end of that effort was not a vacant ocean waiting for European enterprise but a long established system of seasonal navigation, credit, brokerage, and political bargaining in which Asian and African merchants, rulers, and shipowners already knew very well what the pepper, cloves, textiles, gold, and porcelain of the region were worth.

Little more than a century later, in March 1602, the States General of the Dutch Republic granted a charter to the United East India Company, the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, usually abbreviated VOC. Its capital was raised through public subscription, its shares could be traded, and its charter allowed it to negotiate treaties, maintain fortresses and armed forces, and wage war in the name of the Republic east of the Cape of Good Hope. In 1621 its forces carried out a campaign of conquest and mass killing in the Banda Islands to secure control of nutmeg and mace, and in 1619 they had destroyed the Javanese town of Jayakarta and founded Batavia on its site. These episodes show the double character of the commercial empires studied here: they were enterprises that bought and sold goods, and they were also organizations that built forts, collected tolls, enforced monopolies, and killed, enslaved, and displaced people to protect their profits.

The central argument of this chapter is that Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires emerged from attempts to control strategic maritime connections and profitable exchanges within trading systems that long predated European expansion. Neither empire exercised uncontested authority across the Indian Ocean. Their influence rested on a limited number of ports and fortifications, on naval force that was powerful at particular places and moments, on treaties and agreements with Asian sovereigns, on local intermediaries and allies, and on the labor of many people who were never consulted about the enterprise. Where either empire overreached, Asian states and merchants frequently resisted, bargained, or simply sailed around it.

The two empires were also different from each other. The Estado da Índia, the Portuguese State of India, was tied to a royal administration, a crusading and dynastic ideology, and a Crown monopoly that private traders constantly penetrated. The VOC was a corporation, financed by a broad investing public in Amsterdam and other Dutch towns, which exercised many of the powers of a state in Asia while remaining answerable to directors in the Republic. Comparing them clarifies what was distinctive about each, and it also shows how often they borrowed methods from Asian precedent and from one another.

This chapter is also a chapter in European history, not only in the history of Asia and Africa. The spices, textiles, porcelain, tea, and silver that moved through these networks, the shipping and insurance and credit that financed them, and the knowledge of coasts, plants, languages, and peoples that returned to Lisbon and Amsterdam all altered European commerce and public life. The chapter therefore follows both directions of the connection, while reserving the cumulative social and cultural transformation of Europe for Chapter 73.

Historians reconstruct this subject from an unusual range of evidence: Portuguese royal letters and the records of the Casa da Índia, the immense VOC archives in the Netherlands and Indonesia, Malay, Sinhalese, Persian, Arabic, Japanese, and Chinese chronicles and administrative texts, merchant accounts, shipwrecks, forts, cemeteries, coins, and the surviving trade goods themselves. Many of the best known European sources were written by officials with reasons to magnify their own achievements, so this chapter repeatedly asks what the sources reveal, whose voices they omit, and how Asian and African documents change the picture. The chapter belongs to Part XI, which follows Portugal, Spain, and the Atlantic through the Americas and Atlantic slavery in Chapters 68 to 70 and will turn to British and French expansion in Chapter 72.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the principal African, Arab, Persian, Indian, Southeast Asian, and Chinese commercial networks of the Indian Ocean world before and during Portuguese and Dutch expansion.
  2. Explain how monsoon navigation, ports, and strategic straits shaped commerce and the strategies of European newcomers.
  3. Trace the formation of the Portuguese Estado da Índia from 1498 through the conquests of Goa, Malacca, and Hormuz, and assess the limits of Portuguese control.
  4. Analyze the cartaz system, fortified ports, and Crown trading institutions as attempts to regulate Asian commerce, distinguishing claims from actual capacity.
  5. Explain the organization, financing, and delegated powers of the Dutch East India Company, and trace its expansion through Batavia, the Moluccas, Sri Lanka, Taiwan, Japan, and the Cape.
  6. Evaluate the relationship between commerce and coercion, including naval warfare, monopoly enforcement, conquest, slavery, and the violence of 1619 and 1621.
  7. Compare the Portuguese and Dutch systems in institutions, finance, military power, private trade, and relations with Asian rulers.
  8. Assess the agency of Asian and African rulers, merchants, intermediaries, and laboring populations and the limits they imposed on European commercial power.
  9. Interpret Portuguese, Dutch, and Asian sources, identifying perspective, purpose, and silence.
  10. Connect maritime empire to European finance, shipping, imported commodities, and global commercial interdependence without treating the Indian Ocean as an extension of Europe.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did Portuguese ambitions shift from seeking access to trade toward attempting to control it, and why did that attempt succeed only partially?
  3. How did a Crown administration and a chartered company differ as instruments of overseas empire, and how much did the difference matter in practice?

Historical Context

Three earlier developments set the stage. The first was the long commercial connection between Europe and Asia. Spices, silks, and other goods reached the Mediterranean through Red Sea and Persian Gulf routes controlled by Muslim merchants, the Mamluk sultanate of Egypt, and, in the final stage, Italian traders, above all the Venetians, as Chapter 43 described. European demand for pepper, cloves, nutmeg, cinnamon, and ginger was real, but the sums involved should not be exaggerated: the spice trade was a high value luxury sector, not the foundation of European consumption. Its importance lay in its profitability, in the political rivalries it fed, and in the way a few commodities came to symbolize access to Asian wealth.

The second development was Iberian expansion. Portuguese exploration of the African coast and Atlantic islands, covered in Chapter 68, combined commercial motives, dynastic ambition, and a crusading ideology shaped by the long Iberian struggle against Muslim powers, discussed in Chapter 47. The Crown of Portugal developed methods that it carried into the Indian Ocean: royal licensing, fortified trading posts called feitorias, armed ships, and the claim that sea routes could be reserved by papal and royal authority. Spanish activity in the Americas, treated in Chapters 69 and 70, produced silver that would later flow through Manila and Macau into Chinese markets and become a crucial medium of Asian trade.

The third development was the political and economic transformation of the Low Countries. The Dutch Revolt against Habsburg rule, examined in Chapter 66, drove capital and merchants from Antwerp and other southern towns toward the northern provinces and made Amsterdam a major financial and commercial center. Dutch merchants had earlier carried Asian goods from Lisbon to northern Europe, and the interruption of that trade by the Spanish Crown after Portugal passed under Philip II's rule in 1580 gave them a powerful incentive to go to Asia themselves. Chapter 77 will return to the Dutch Republic as a political system; this chapter concentrates on its overseas commercial institutions.

The Asian side of the story must be kept in view throughout. In 1498 the Indian Ocean was bordered by powerful and dynamic states, including the Mamluk Sultanate, the Safavid and later Ottoman empires, the sultanates of Gujarat and the Deccan, the Vijayanagara empire and its successors, the kingdoms of Malabar, the Malay sultanate of Malacca, Javanese and Sumatran polities, Ayutthaya, Ming China, and later Mughal India, the Tokugawa shogunate, and Qing China. Swahili city states along the East African coast and Omani and Hadrami merchant networks added further layers. Europeans entered this world as one group of participants among many, and for much of the period covered here they were not the most powerful.

The evidence demands the same breadth. Portuguese chroniclers such as João de Barros, Fernão Lopes de Castanheda, and Gaspar Correia, and royal correspondence preserved in Lisbon, offer detailed narratives from a Portuguese point of view. The VOC left one of the largest archives of any early modern organization, including the Generale Missiven, the regular letters sent by the Governor-General and Council in Batavia to the directors in the Netherlands. Asian perspectives survive in works such as the Malay chronicle Sejarah Melayu, the Arabic text Tuhfat al-Mujahidin written in Malabar in the later sixteenth century to urge resistance to the Portuguese, Sinhalese chronicles, Persian and Gujarati histories, Chinese gazetteers and memorials, and Japanese administrative records. Archaeology, including shipwrecks and harbor excavations, supplies a further independent check. Almost every source type has a bias, and the chapter treats the reconciliation of these sources as part of the subject.

Section 71.1

The Indian Ocean before Portuguese and Dutch Expansion

The Indian Ocean was not discovered by Europeans. For well over a thousand years before 1498, ships had linked the coasts of East Africa, Arabia, the Persian Gulf, western and eastern India, Sri Lanka, the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java, and southern China. This was less a single system than a set of overlapping circuits, each tied to the next by ports where goods and merchants changed hands. East African towns exported gold from the interior through Sofala, along with ivory and enslaved people. Arab and Persian merchants carried horses, pearls, and textiles, and sailors from the Hadramawt and Oman were prominent in the western ocean. Gujarati merchants from Cambay, Surat, and Diu were among the most active in the whole region, shipping cotton cloth west to Arabia and Africa and east to Southeast Asia.

Tamil and Telugu merchants and weavers along the Coromandel coast supplied textiles for Southeast Asia, where printed and painted Indian cloth was exchanged for pepper, cloves, nutmeg, and other products. The Malabar coast produced pepper in large quantity, and Malay and Javanese traders moved the fine spices of the eastern islands toward the great entrepôt of Malacca. Chinese ships, including the state sponsored voyages of the early fifteenth century associated with Zheng He, had reached as far as East Africa. After the Ming court ended such voyages, private Chinese merchants, often operating in defiance of or in negotiation with imperial prohibitions, remained active in Southeast Asian ports.

The commerce was integrated in the sense that prices, credit, and information moved across its ports, and a disturbance at one point was felt at others. It was also religiously and culturally plural. Islam had spread along the trade routes, carried by merchants and by Sufi teachers, and Muslim communities were influential in Swahili towns, Gujarat, Malabar, and Malacca. Hindu, Jain, Buddhist, Jewish, and Christian merchants were equally present in many ports, and the ocean belonged to no one civilization.

Evidence for this world is varied. The Cairo Geniza documents illuminate earlier medieval Mediterranean and Indian trade, Ibn Battuta and other travelers described ports, chronicles survive from the Swahili coast and Southeast Asia, and Chinese records and archaeological finds of Asian ceramics along the African coast show how far goods traveled. Europeans learned about this world slowly, and Italian merchants had long obtained its goods through Egyptian and Levantine intermediaries, as Chapter 43 explained.

Three features of this older system deserve emphasis because they conditioned everything that followed. First, its commerce was polycentric. No single ruler or religious establishment governed the ocean, and the principal ports competed for merchants by offering predictable customs, security, and warehousing. Second, it moved bulk goods as well as luxuries. Cotton cloth, rice, grain, timber, salt, and horses crossed the ocean in large quantities, and the gold of the Zimbabwe plateau, the textiles of Gujarat and the Coromandel coast, and the pepper of Malabar were exchanged through chains of intermediaries. The spices that fascinated Europeans were a conspicuous part of a much larger exchange of everyday commodities. Third, political authority was layered. A port ruler might owe tribute to a more powerful neighbor while taxing foreign merchants on his own account, and merchants carried their own law, languages, and credit across these layers.

The Chinese presence illustrates the point. After the great voyages of the early fifteenth century ended, the Ming court restricted official overseas contact, yet Chinese junks, many owned by Fujian and Guangdong merchants, remained prominent in Southeast Asian ports, and settled Chinese communities served as brokers and shipowners. When the Portuguese reached these waters they met a commercial order that included Chinese, Javanese, Malay, Indian, Persian, and Arab participants with long experience of one another.

Section 71.2

Monsoons, Maritime Routes, and the Geography of Asian Commerce

The rhythm of Indian Ocean commerce was set by the monsoon. During the northern summer, from roughly June to September, the southwest monsoon carried ships from East Africa and Arabia toward India and, farther east, toward Southeast Asia. In winter the northeast monsoon reversed the flow, allowing ships to return west. Timing was crucial: a ship that missed its season might wait months in port. This seasonality shaped the size and rhythm of markets, the arrival of merchant fleets, the working of credit, and the calendars of rulers who collected customs at the height of the trading season.

Southeast Asian waters had their own patterns, with the Strait of Malacca and the Sunda Strait serving as passages between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Sailors used experienced pilots, knowledge of coasts and stars, and instruments such as the kamal for measuring stellar altitude. Ships ranged from Arab and Indian dhows with lateen sails, often stitched rather than nailed, to large Southeast Asian vessels known to Portuguese writers as jongs and to Chinese junks with watertight compartments. Portuguese observers sometimes recorded surprise at the size of the vessels they met.

Geography created chokepoints. The Bab el-Mandeb at the mouth of the Red Sea, the Strait of Hormuz at the entrance to the Persian Gulf, the Palk Strait and southern Indian coast, the Strait of Malacca, and the Sunda Strait all concentrated traffic. Control of such points promised customs revenue and political leverage, which is why both the Portuguese and the Dutch fixed upon them. But the Indian Ocean was large and its alternatives many. When one strait or port was controlled, merchants could shift to another, which is why the geography that made chokepoints attractive also limited the effectiveness of controlling them.

This section does not repeat the Atlantic navigation history of Chapter 68, but the European voyages to Asia need brief mention. Portuguese fleets typically left Lisbon in spring, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, and reached India in the autumn, then returned in the following months. The Dutch later adopted a route that used the strong westerly winds south of the Indian Ocean before turning north toward the Sunda Strait, a route associated with the navigator Hendrik Brouwer in 1611 and a key to Batavia's location. European ships therefore had to adapt to the same winds that governed Asian shipping.

Navigation was a body of practical knowledge as well as a matter of winds. Pilots and shipmasters transmitted sailing directions, star lore, and knowledge of reefs and harbors, and several written pilot guides circulated in Arabic and other languages. The Portuguese depended on this knowledge, using Asian and East African pilots on important voyages, and later Dutch skippers likewise learned from Asian sailors and from printed Portuguese material such as Linschoten's Itinerario. Seasonal ports of call mattered. Ships bound for Malacca from India typically waited for the right wind, and the port filled with merchants who stayed for months, which created the markets, warehouses, and lodging that gave entrepôts their character.

Risk was shared in familiar ways. Merchants divided cargoes among several ships, took partners, and borrowed against voyages, and ports developed customary rules for jettison, salvage, and the responsibilities of shipmasters. The Malay world possessed codified maritime customs, associated with Malacca, which regulated the duties of captain and crew and the sharing of cargo space. The tropical sea also imposed hazards that no merchant could eliminate, including cyclones in the Bay of Bengal, uncertain winds in the Sunda region, and piracy. Portuguese and Dutch naval power should be understood against this background: it could make a particular strait unsafe for rivals, but a ship sailing at the right season along a coast with friendly harbors had alternatives.

Section 71.3

Asian Port Cities, Rulers, Merchants, and Trading Institutions

The great ports were run by rulers who understood commerce as a source of revenue and prestige. Calicut, under the Zamorin, hosted Muslim merchants of Arab, Persian, and local Mappila origin and a Hindu merchant elite, and the ruler depended on the pepper trade. Cambay and other ports of Gujarat were governed under the Gujarat sultanate and exported textiles and indigo. Aden controlled access to the Red Sea, Hormuz served the Persian Gulf and the Iranian interior, and Malacca, founded around 1400 and a Muslim sultanate by the fifteenth century, became the central entrepôt of Southeast Asia, where merchants from every direction met.

Port administration was elaborate. In Malacca, as described by the Portuguese apothecary Tomé Pires, there were officials called shahbandars, each responsible for merchants of particular origins, such as Gujarat, Tamil and Bengali regions, Java, and China. They supervised weights, customs, disputes, and the supply of warehouses. Customs duties, gifts to the ruler, and restrictions on trade created a predictable framework. Merchants who accepted these rules received protection, and rulers who protected them were rewarded with revenue.

Credit and partnership arrangements were highly developed. Merchants used bills of exchange, brokers, agency relationships, and partnerships in which investors supplied capital and traveling partners supplied labor and risk, forms known under Islamic commercial law and in Hindu and Jain practice. Merchant diasporas, including Gujarati Banias, Tamil Chettis, Arab and Persian traders, Jewish merchants, and Chinese communities in Southeast Asian ports, provided trusted networks across political borders. This sophistication matters because later claims that Europeans introduced modern commerce to Asia cannot withstand evidence of what already existed.

A feitoria, or factory, in the Portuguese sense was a trading post staffed by royal agents and merchants. The idea resembled Asian merchant quarters that foreign communities had long maintained, and the Europeans were entering a world accustomed to such arrangements.

Malacca illustrates how a port could function as an institution. Its sultans drew revenue from customs, from rents on warehouses, and from gifts, and the court relied on powerful merchant officials who managed the port's relations with foreign communities. Merchants from Gujarat formed one of the largest groups, buying spices and Chinese goods in exchange for Indian cloth, while Chinese, Javanese, Tamil, Bengali, and Burmese merchants maintained their own quarters. Disputes among merchants were handled by officials familiar with their customs, which reduced friction and encouraged long term investment.

On the Malabar coast the political arrangement was different but comparable. The Zamorin of Calicut presided over a port in which Mappila Muslim merchants, many of them descendants of Arab traders and local converts, controlled much of the shipping and a Hindu ruling elite received customs, while Cochin, Cannanore, and other ports under different rulers offered alternatives. This competition gave pepper producers and merchants bargaining power. Jewish and Christian communities, including the Saint Thomas Christians and the Jewish community of Cochin, held established roles in trade and administration. In the Gulf, Hormuz taxed trade between India, Iran, and Arabia, and its rulers, though sometimes subordinate to larger Persian or Arab powers, were adept at balancing their neighbors.

Credit instruments moved capital without the physical movement of silver. A merchant in one port could pay a partner in another by bill, and brokers specialized in connecting buyers and sellers. Because such arrangements rested on reputation and kinship, they favored established diasporas and made it difficult for a newcomer without local partners to compete. The Portuguese and Dutch, in different ways, both attempted to solve this problem by acquiring local partners, intermediaries, or monopolies.

Historical Vocabulary
Feitoria (Factory)

A feitoria was a fortified or unfortified trading post maintained in a foreign port by an agent called a factor. The word came into English as factory, but it did not originally mean a manufacturing building. Portuguese feitorias on the African coast and in Asia served as warehouses, offices, and sometimes garrisons, and the Dutch and English later used the same term.

Feitorias mattered because they show how European enterprise often began as a privilege negotiated from a local ruler, not as conquest. The permission to hold a feitoria depended on the host, and many feitorias existed for decades without sovereign control over the surrounding territory.

Section 71.4

The Portuguese Arrival and the Transformation of Maritime Competition

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in May 1498 after a voyage that had followed the African coast and crossed the Arabian Sea with the help of local pilots from the East African coast. The Zamorin received the Portuguese, but negotiations failed. The goods the Portuguese carried were not those in demand, and Muslim merchants who already dominated the pepper trade saw no reason to welcome a rival whose king demanded exclusive access. Da Gama returned to Lisbon in 1499 with samples of spices and the knowledge that a sea route existed.

The second fleet, under Pedro Álvares Cabral in 1500, carried more men and more guns. Its negotiations at Calicut collapsed in violence, and the Portuguese factory was attacked and its occupants killed. Cabral bombarded the city and then established relations with Cochin, whose ruler wanted to reduce his dependence on Calicut. In 1502 da Gama returned with a larger fleet and behaved with deliberate brutality, including the attack on the pilgrim ship Meri off the Malabar coast, in which many passengers were killed. The Portuguese were learning that violence could create leverage in a region where they lacked other advantages.

These episodes show a transition. The initial aim of Manuel I of Portugal was to obtain access to Asian goods and diplomatic alliance against Muslim rivals, in a spirit that mixed commercial and crusading aims. The king's titles later claimed lordship over the conquest, navigation, and commerce of the Indian Ocean. But what began as a search for access quickly became an attempt to control trade, by intercepting ships bound for the Red Sea and compelling merchants to deal with Portuguese forts and officials.

Two points need emphasis. First, the Portuguese had few goods that Asians wanted, so they relied on silver, copper, coral, and eventually on the carrying trade within Asia. Second, their claims of exclusive maritime sovereignty were without precedent in the Indian Ocean, where earlier powers had taxed and protected trade without attempting to close seas to others. Local rulers and merchants understood the novelty and objected to it, and the next division shows how the Portuguese tried to enforce it.

The motives for the Portuguese voyages were mixed, and distinguishing them avoids simplistic accounts. The Portuguese Crown wished to obtain pepper and other goods at lower cost, to weaken Muslim competitors, and to find Christian allies in the East, including the legendary priest king Prester John, a figure of medieval European imagination whom contemporaries located in Ethiopia or India. The crusading frame, discussed for the earlier medieval period in Chapter 47, gave violence a religious justification, and Manuel I proclaimed himself lord of the conquest, navigation, and commerce of the Indian Ocean regions. Such titles expressed aspiration, not control.

The first Portuguese expeditions were also small. A fleet of a few ships and a few hundred men could not impose its will on a large port without local allies, and the Portuguese quickly learned to exploit divisions. Cochin's ruler gave them shelter and a factory, Cannanore did the same, and Malabar's rival kings had reasons to accept a new naval power that could harm Calicut. Calicut and its Mappila merchants, for their part, organized resistance, and in the later sixteenth century the Kunjali Marakkar family of Muslim naval commanders, nominally allied with the Zamorin, attacked Portuguese shipping on the Malabar coast until the Portuguese and the Zamorin destroyed the last Kunjali Marakkar stronghold in 1600. The contest for Malabar therefore shaped the Portuguese system from its beginnings.

Division II

Portugal and the Formation of the Estado da Índia

This division follows the Portuguese attempt to build a network of fortified ports and naval power, and the institutions that connected Asia to Lisbon.

Section 71.5

Francisco de Almeida, Portuguese Alliances, and the Battle of Diu

In 1505 Manuel I appointed Francisco de Almeida as the first viceroy of India, with orders to build fortresses on the East African and Indian coasts and to protect Portuguese trade. Almeida established forts at Kilwa and Anjediva and at Cannanore, strengthened the alliance with Cochin, and became involved in the politics of Malabar, where the Zamorin of Calicut remained hostile. The Portuguese position in these years depended heavily on local alliances, since the Cochin ruler had his own reasons for opposing Calicut.

The principal external threat was the Mamluk sultanate, whose revenue from the Red Sea trade was threatened by the Portuguese. With support from Gujarat, whose ports and shipping were affected, and with Venetian interest in the outcome, the Mamluks sent a fleet to the Indian Ocean. In 1508 it defeated a Portuguese squadron at Chaul, where Almeida's son Lourenço was killed. Almeida responded by assembling forces for revenge.

At Diu, off the coast of Gujarat, in February 1509, Almeida destroyed the combined fleet in an engagement that depended on Portuguese naval artillery, the maneuverability of their ships, and the coordination problems of an alliance of Mamluk, Gujarati, and other commanders. Diu is often described as one of the decisive battles of naval history, but the claim needs qualification. It confirmed Portuguese ability to defeat major fleets in open water for the next several decades. It did not give Portugal command of the whole ocean, and Gujarat continued to be a major commercial power. It also did not end Mamluk and later Ottoman interest, for Ottoman fleets operated in the Red Sea and Indian Ocean in the following decades and besieged Diu unsuccessfully in 1538, and Gujarati forces with Ottoman support besieged it again in 1546.

Almeida favored what is sometimes called a policy of controlling the sea rather than building numerous land fortresses, arguing that naval strength could secure Portuguese commerce more cheaply. His successor Albuquerque disagreed, and the debate between maritime and territorial approaches ran through Portuguese Asian policy for the next century.

The administrative form of the new Portuguese state emerged in these years. The viceroy represented the Crown and commanded the fleet, while captains commanded fortresses, factors managed trade and accounts, and royal secretaries and treasury officials kept records. Almeida's instructions limited the number of forts and favored the construction of ships, and during his term the Portuguese established fortresses in East Africa and on the Indian coast chiefly to protect watering, trade, and factories. The shipyards of Cochin and Goa later became important, supplied with teak and other timber from the Malabar coast, because ships built in India could be repaired and replaced without the expense of Lisbon.

Almeida's policy faced criticism from Albuquerque and his supporters, and when Albuquerque arrived with a commission to succeed him in 1509, Almeida initially refused to hand over authority, and the dispute was resolved only after the Diu victory and the arrival of an important Portuguese noble, the marshal Fernando Coutinho, in late 1509. The episode shows how personal rivalry and uncertain command structures weakened Portuguese administration. It also shows the importance of naval victory in giving a governor legitimacy.

Naval logistics were demanding. Fleets from Lisbon brought troops, artillery, iron, and silver, but supplies and manpower for the Asian fleet largely came from local sources, including Indian shipbuilders and sailors, enslaved crews, and soldiers recruited in Asia. A policy of control by sea required ships that could remain on station for months, and the Portuguese never had enough. The distinction between the dramatic victory at Diu and the long, expensive work of patrol is central to understanding the system.

Section 71.6

Afonso de Albuquerque and the Conquest of Goa

Afonso de Albuquerque became governor in 1509 and held the post until his death in December 1515. He believed that the Portuguese needed permanent bases that controlled strategic sites, because ships alone, without secure harbors for repair and supply, could not maintain the system. Goa, an island port on the western coast ruled by the sultanate of Bijapur, was attractive because of its harbor, its position between Gujarat and Malabar, and its access to horses imported for Deccan armies.

Albuquerque seized Goa in February 1510, assisted by a local Hindu leader, Timoji, who was hostile to Bijapur. The Bijapur forces retook the town in May, and Albuquerque recaptured it in November 1510 in an assault accompanied by heavy killing of the Muslim inhabitants. Portuguese sources report this violence, and later historians have treated it as deliberate terror directed against a population that the Portuguese saw as enemies. The Hindu inhabitants were treated more leniently, and Albuquerque preserved aspects of local village institutions and revenue collection, which provided income and stability.

The conquest created something new: a permanent Portuguese political center in Asia with its own mint, shipyard, and administration. Albuquerque encouraged Portuguese soldiers to marry local women, creating a settler population called casados, partly to anchor the city and partly because Portuguese women rarely traveled to Asia. This policy produced a distinctive Luso-Asian society. It was also an act of conquest that displaced and transformed the lives of the city's inhabitants. Muslim merchants fled or were killed, Hindu brokers and landholders adapted, and Christian conversion began almost immediately and would increase under later governors.

Albuquerque's vision should not be reduced to personal ambition. His letters to Manuel I reflect strategic reasoning, crusading imagery, and administrative detail. He also expected to cut Muslim commerce in the Red Sea and in Gujarat, though he never succeeded in closing the Red Sea.

Albuquerque's government of Goa illustrates both continuity and innovation. He retained many local arrangements, including village communities with hereditary landholding families, which collected revenue and managed irrigation and common land, and which provided the Portuguese with a stable income. The Portuguese kept tax assessments set by the former Bijapur administration for a time, employed local Hindu officials, and relied on Hindu brokers and financiers. At the same time, the administration introduced Portuguese law, a Portuguese captain, a church, and a mint, issuing coins that circulated in Asia and Europe.

Goa's relations with regional powers also mattered. Bijapur remained hostile and threatened the city on several occasions, but the Hindu empire of Vijayanagara, whose army required war horses, welcomed access to the horses imported through Portuguese ports and signed trade agreements with the Portuguese, a mutually beneficial arrangement that helped Goa survive. The Portuguese were thus a participant in the politics of peninsular India, not an isolated enclave.

The consequences for inhabitants were uneven. The violence of the 1510 assault killed many Muslims, and the social order of the city was transformed as Muslim merchants left or were displaced and Hindu groups adapted. The policy of encouraging marriage with local women changed the demography. Over the following decades the conversion pressure, the destruction of Hindu temples, and restrictions on public religious practice altered the religious landscape of the islands of Goa, while neighboring territories under Bijapur and later the Marathas remained distinct. Goa thus became a place where Portuguese ambition, local adaptation, and religious coercion were concentrated.

Section 71.7

Malacca, Hormuz, and the Struggle for Strategic Maritime Passages

In 1511 Albuquerque led a fleet to Malacca, whose sultan, Mahmud Shah, governed one of the wealthiest ports in Asia. The Portuguese had been in Malacca briefly in 1509 under Diogo Lopes de Sequeira, with a hostile reception, and Albuquerque came with the intention to take the city. After a siege that lasted into August, he captured it and began building a fortress, A Famosa, using stone from local buildings, including the mosque. The sultan withdrew to Bintan, and his heirs eventually established what became the Sultanate of Johor.

The importance of Malacca in Portuguese thinking is visible in the writing of Pires, whose Suma Oriental is examined below. But the results of conquest were mixed. Many Asian merchants, unwilling to accept Portuguese control and tolls, moved their business to other ports, including Aceh in northern Sumatra, Patani, Johor, and Banten on Java. Malacca remained important, but the trade that had flourished under Malay rule was not fully recovered, and Portuguese Malacca spent much of its history under pressure from Aceh and Johor.

Hormuz at the mouth of the Persian Gulf was a smaller island but an important trading point. After an earlier attempt in 1507 failed, Albuquerque returned and by 1515 the ruler of Hormuz had accepted Portuguese control, with a fortress and customs collected jointly. The relationship was complicated by Safavid Persia's rise. In 1622, Safavid forces assisted by English ships captured Hormuz, ending Portuguese control.

The comparison of Malacca and Hormuz shows a recurrent problem. Chokepoints were valuable but difficult to hold, and control of a chokepoint did not equal control of the commerce that passed through it. Merchants could change routes, rulers could resist, and the Portuguese lacked the manpower to dominate large hinterlands.

The capture of Malacca was a more difficult operation than Portuguese chronicles sometimes suggest. The city had a large population and a defended bridge across the river, and Albuquerque's force was small, perhaps one thousand or so men. The attack succeeded after a prolonged assault, and Albuquerque then ordered the construction of a stone fortress, using materials from local buildings. The Portuguese took considerable plunder, and Albuquerque attempted to regulate the commerce of the city by appointing a Portuguese captain and a factor, and by inviting Chinese and Indian merchants to remain.

The long term results were mixed. Aceh, in northern Sumatra, rose as a rival port with a strong naval force and a Muslim identity that attracted merchants avoiding Portuguese tolls, and it attacked Malacca repeatedly, including major assaults in the later sixteenth century and in 1629, when a large Acehnese fleet suffered heavy losses. Johor, the successor of the old sultanate, alternated between fighting the Portuguese and negotiating with them. Javanese shipping also declined at Malacca, and merchants from Java, Gujarat, and elsewhere found alternative routes through the Sunda Strait and Banten. Malacca thus remained a customs post and fortress of considerable importance, but the fear that its conquest would divert the trade of Asia to Portuguese hands proved exaggerated.

Hormuz, by contrast, was controlled through an arrangement with a local ruler who remained nominally on the throne while the Portuguese held the fortress and collected a share of customs. Its revenue was substantial, but the arrangement also made Portuguese control depend on the good will of Persian and Arab neighbors. The Safavid shah Abbas I, who wanted to gain control of Gulf trade, accepted English naval help in 1622 and captured the island, after which commerce shifted to the mainland port of Bandar Abbas. The Portuguese had held a strategic point for more than a century, but they never controlled the whole Gulf trade.

Section 71.8

East Africa, the Red Sea, and the Persian Gulf

The Portuguese presence in East Africa began with the pursuit of gold and the need for provisioning stations on the way to India. In 1505 an expedition under Almeida attacked and plundered Kilwa and Mombasa and established forts in Kilwa and Sofala, and Mozambique Island became a key waystation, with a fort built from 1507. The Portuguese hoped to capture the gold trade of the Zimbabwe plateau, which reached the coast at Sofala, but in practice they depended on African and Swahili middlemen and on long supply chains. The Kilwa fort was abandoned within a few years because of its expense.

Swahili cities such as Mombasa, Malindi, and Zanzibar followed varied policies, and rivalries among them gave the Portuguese allies. Malindi allied with Portugal against Mombasa, which was sacked more than once. In 1593 the Portuguese built Fort Jesus at Mombasa, a massive fortification. The Swahili elite did not disappear, and trade with the interior and with Arabia continued. From the mid seventeenth century Omani rulers expanded their maritime power, retaking Muscat in 1650 and besieging Fort Jesus until its capture in 1698. The outcome showed that Portuguese power depended on the inability of rivals to concentrate force.

In the Red Sea, Albuquerque's attack on Aden failed in 1513, and the Portuguese could not close the sea. Spice cargoes continued to reach Egypt, and by the 1540s the Red Sea route was again carrying large quantities of pepper. The Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517 changed the political frame: the Ottomans fielded a fleet at Suez and sent expeditions against Portuguese positions, notably the failed siege of Diu in 1538. Portuguese intervention in Ethiopia in the early 1540s, when a small force under Cristóvão da Gama assisted the Christian ruler against the Adal sultanate, showed how Portuguese policy linked crusading ideology to regional alliances.

Persian Gulf politics were similarly layered. Portugal controlled Hormuz and later Muscat, but Safavid Persia, the Ottomans, and Arab rulers all contested the region. The Portuguese position was therefore a collection of strongpoints within a larger system that they could influence but not command.

Portuguese actions in East Africa illustrate the difference between raiding and governing. The expeditions of 1505 plundered Kilwa and Mombasa and demanded tribute, and the Portuguese built a fort at Kilwa that was abandoned within a few years because it was costly to maintain and did not produce the expected revenue. Sofala, where gold from the interior arrived, was more valuable, but the interior trade was controlled by African rulers and by Swahili and Muslim merchants. The Portuguese obtained a share but did not control the sources, and in the following decades they tried to establish a presence on the Zambezi, an effort that later produced the prazo estates, land grants worked by African communities and led by Portuguese or Luso-African landholders.

Swahili towns responded in diverse ways. Some, such as Malindi, accepted Portuguese protection in return for support against rivals. Others, such as Mombasa, resisted and suffered sacks and occupation. The Swahili chronicles of Kilwa and other towns preserve local memories of dynastic politics and of Portuguese intrusion, and archaeology shows continued occupation and building. The Portuguese system in East Africa was therefore a network of forts and alliances more than a territorial state.

In the northern ocean, the Ottoman Empire became the principal rival after 1517. The Ottomans captured Aden in 1538, sent a fleet to besiege Diu in the same year, and in the early 1550s a fleet under Piri Reis raided Muscat and Hormuz, though it did not dislodge the Portuguese. These expeditions were costly for the Ottomans, who had other priorities in the Mediterranean and Europe, and they show that a Portuguese naval monopoly was never uncontested. Portuguese defenders also depended on local allies in the Gulf, and the later Omani conquest of Muscat in 1650 showed how swiftly the situation could change once a local power gathered strength.

Section 71.9

Fortresses, Naval Power, and the Cartaz System

The cartaz was the instrument by which the Portuguese tried to turn naval power into revenue. Asian ships sailing in waters the Portuguese claimed were expected to obtain a pass at a Portuguese fortress, naming the ship, its destination, and its cargo, and to call at Portuguese customs houses. Ships without passes could be seized. The system recalls earlier Indian Ocean practices of taxing and protecting commerce, and Portuguese officials presented it as a form of protection. To Asian merchants it also represented a new kind of demand, since it assumed exclusive authority over an open sea.

Portuguese power rested on fortified ports such as Goa, Cochin, Colombo, Malacca, Hormuz, Diu, Mozambique, and Macau, and on galleons, galleys, and smaller vessels armed with artillery. The tactical advantage in naval warfare was real in the early sixteenth century. But the number of ships was always small relative to the size of the ocean, and fleets had to be concentrated against threats. The Portuguese could police particular routes at particular times, such as the passage to the Red Sea, but could not prevent smuggling, and Asian merchants evaded passes through local connections, bribery, and alternative ports.

Historians have debated how effective and how burdensome the system was. Some stress the violence of seizures and the extraction of tolls. Others show that many Asian ships continued trading with passes and that rulers such as the Gujarati sultans negotiated terms. The honest assessment is that the cartaz gave the Portuguese leverage and revenue in some regions at some times, while falling well short of a total monopoly.

Private traders played a large part. Portuguese captains, officials, and settlers carried goods on their own account, often in partnership with Asian merchants and Asian rulers, and some operated outside the Estado da Índia's jurisdiction. The line between Crown commerce and private commerce was porous, and corruption was a continual complaint.

Enforcement depended on organized squadrons. The Portuguese maintained patrol fleets in particular waters, such as off the Malabar coast and Gujarat, and at the Red Sea approaches during certain seasons, and the Crown issued orders to captains to seize unlicensed vessels. These forces were funded by customs income and by Crown revenue, and they were always short of ships. The cost of maintaining a fort or squadron had to be balanced against the revenue it could generate, and where revenue was insufficient officers supplemented it by private trade and extortion, which in turn generated resentment among Asian merchants.

The relationship with Gujarat shows the combination of conflict and bargaining. After the Gujarat sultan Bahadur Shah faced pressure from the Mughal emperor Humayun in 1534, he agreed to cede Bassein to Portugal and to permit a fort at Diu, built from 1535, in return for military help. The agreement was soon followed by the sultan's death in 1537 in a dispute with the Portuguese and by renewed conflict, yet Gujarati merchants continued to trade with and through Portuguese ports, and the sultanate obtained cartazes for its ships. Similar mixed relationships can be seen elsewhere. Some Asian rulers, including those of Cochin, accepted Portuguese rules as part of an alliance, while others, such as Calicut, rejected them, and many ordinary merchants complied when it was cheaper to pay than to evade.

The cartaz also extended to the Mughal Empire. Under Akbar and his successors, Mughal pilgrim ships to the Red Sea and Mughal merchants at Surat carried Portuguese passes at times, and negotiated exemptions, because the emperors valued the pilgrimage and the textile trade and could not match Portuguese naval power at sea. The Mughals preferred diplomacy to a naval contest, and their restraint illustrates how an effective land power could tolerate a limited maritime pretension.

Historical Vocabulary
Cartaz

The cartaz, from an Arabic word for paper, was a pass issued by Portuguese authorities that allowed a ship to sail within waters Portugal claimed to control. It specified the ship, its owner, its route, and the goods allowed.

The cartaz mattered because it expressed the Portuguese ambition to regulate Indian Ocean commerce, and because its uneven enforcement shows the gap between claims of sovereignty and actual capacity. It should not be mistaken for a system of effective monopoly, since many ships traded without it or obtained it and traded as they wished.

Section 71.10

The Carreira da Índia, Lisbon, and the Royal Spice Trade

The sea route between Lisbon and Goa, the Carreira da Índia, was the lifeline of the Estado da Índia. Every year, usually in March or April, a fleet of large ships called naus or carracks left Lisbon, rounded the Cape, and reached Goa in the autumn. Return fleets left India in winter. A voyage took roughly six months each way, and ships carried soldiers, officials, missionaries, convicts, and merchandise. Disease, storms, shipwreck, and overloading took a heavy toll, and accounts of wrecks, collected in the seventeenth century as the História Trágico-Marítima, were widely read in Portugal. Modern estimates of losses differ and vary by period, but the voyage was perilous.

In Lisbon the Casa da Índia, established in the early sixteenth century, organized the trade. It received the cargoes, controlled the Crown's monopoly on pepper and other spices, collected duties, and arranged sales. The Crown contracted with private merchants, including Italian, German, and Flemish houses, to buy the goods, and for several decades Antwerp served as the principal northern market for Portuguese spices, a connection that tied Portuguese Asia to the commercial networks examined in Chapter 43.

The royal monopoly was expensive. Ships, forts, garrisons, and salaries cost a great deal, and income depended on shipments arriving and selling at a profit. The Crown borrowed heavily, and its debts and several suspensions of payment, particularly in the middle and later sixteenth century, indicate the strain. The spice trade also was not a pure Crown affair. Portuguese captains and officials carried private cargo, Asian merchants shipped goods within Asia, and Asian rulers received share of the profits.

The importance of the Portuguese system for Europe was substantial but should be put in proportion. Pepper and other spices arrived in Lisbon in large quantities and supplied a significant share of European demand, but the Red Sea and Levantine route remained active, and Venice recovered a considerable part of its trade by the middle of the sixteenth century. The Portuguese had altered the routes of European supply without gaining the monopoly that the Crown desired.

Lisbon's institutions were part of the Portuguese system. The Casa da Índia occupied buildings beside the Tagus and recorded the arrival, valuation, and sale of goods, while the shipyards of the Ribeira das Naus built and equipped the fleets. The Crown sold much of its pepper under contracts to merchant syndicates, and from the middle of the sixteenth century it increasingly leased the trade to contractors, a sign of financial pressure. Foreign merchants, including Italian, German, and Flemish houses, invested in Portuguese expeditions from the early years, and for several decades the Portuguese Crown maintained a factory at Antwerp, from which pepper and other goods reached northern Europe, until the factory was reduced in the middle of the century.

Ship design and loading illustrate the constraints. The carracks of the Carreira were large, slow, and heavily laden with cargo, passengers, and artillery, and overloading with private goods increased the risk of wreck. The ships left Lisbon too late in some years, missing the monsoon window, and had to winter in East Africa or return to Lisbon. Losses of ships and people were a continual drain, and the crew were often poorly paid or pressed into service.

The Crown tried to limit private trade by reserving cargo space and prohibiting certain goods, but captains, officers, and passengers received allowances for private freight, which grew into a large share of the cargo. This private trade, together with corruption and diversion of customs, reduced royal income. Historians therefore describe the Carreira as a system that tied together royal ambition, private enterprise, and Asian supply in a way that made it vulnerable. It was nevertheless a remarkable organizational achievement, sustained for more than a century over a distance of thousands of kilometers.

Historical Debate

Did Portuguese Maritime Power Create an Effective Commercial Monopoly in the Indian Ocean?

The question: Did the Portuguese Crown, through fortresses, naval power, and the cartaz, succeed in establishing a monopoly over the spice trade between Asia and Europe, or merely a powerful presence within a trading system that continued to function in its own way?

Interpretation A: Some earlier historians, drawing mainly on Portuguese chronicles and official correspondence, described the Estado da Índia as a successful attempt to impose a maritime monopoly, pointing to Portuguese naval victories, the capture of key ports, and the diversion of large quantities of spices to Lisbon.
Interpretation B: Many later scholars, using Asian, Dutch, and Venetian sources as well as Portuguese ones, argue that Portugal never achieved an effective monopoly. They point to the continued flow of spices through the Red Sea, the persistence of Asian merchant networks, the porous nature of cartaz enforcement, the importance of private trade, and the rise of competing ports such as Aceh. Portuguese power, in this view, was real but concentrated in particular places and was most effective when it cooperated with local interests.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on Portuguese customs records, Venetian reports of spice arrivals, Asian chronicles, and archaeological evidence of trade goods. The disagreement concerns how to weigh claims by Portuguese officials against evidence of what actually happened.

Why it matters: The question determines how historians assess European power in early modern Asia: as a decisive break with earlier patterns or as one force within a larger and persistent system.

This division explains how Portuguese commercial power functioned within diverse Asian political and cultural environments, distinguishing commercial access from sovereignty.

Section 71.11

Goa as an Administrative, Commercial, and Cultural Center

Goa became the capital of the Estado da Índia and the seat of a viceroy, with councils, a high court (the Relação, established in 1544), a municipal council, a royal treasury, a mint, a hospital, and the charitable brotherhood of the Misericórdia. Royal officials supervised customs, shipbuilding, and the collection of revenue from the surrounding territories. Revenue came from customs, from the land tax on rural districts governed through village communities, and from the Crown's trading profits. The governors and viceroys were often nobles who held three year terms and had ample opportunity for private profit.

Urban society was layered. At the top stood the officials and nobles who came from Portugal, below them the casados, the settled married Portuguese men and their families, and the soldiers, who were numerous and often poor. A large population of Hindu merchants, brokers, and artisans, Konkani speakers, Muslims, Jews, and converted Christians lived and worked in or near the city. Enslaved men and women from Africa, India, and Southeast Asia performed domestic and manual labor. Foreign visitors, such as the Dutchman Jan Huyghen van Linschoten, who lived in Goa in the 1580s, described a city of wealth and display alongside poverty and disease. His account, published in 1596, became a manual for later Dutch expeditions, an irony that shows how information flowed.

The distinction between official and private commerce was central. The Crown reserved certain goods and routes for itself, such as pepper, but allowed other trade through licenses and concessions, including voyages to particular destinations granted to officials as rewards. Private Portuguese traders, known as country traders in later writing, handled much of the commerce within Asia, often in partnership with Asian merchants, and their activities extended to Bengal, the Coromandel coast, Southeast Asia, and Japan. Indian Hindu financiers and brokers supplied credit and expertise.

Goa was therefore a Luso-Asian city. Portuguese institutions provided the framework, but its commerce and population depended on Asian participants, and its culture combined Iberian Catholic and Konkani elements. It was also a place where the costs of conquest remained visible: Hindu temples on the islands were destroyed or abandoned during the sixteenth century, and conversion pressure was sustained.

Goa's administration shows how a royal state operated at a distance. The viceroy presided over a council, and other officials handled finance, justice, and military affairs, including a chief auditor, a treasurer, and judges of the high court. The municipal council of Goa, modeled on that of Lisbon and endowed with privileges, represented the settler community, which used it to petition the Crown and challenge viceroys. The Misericórdia provided charity, burial, and loans, and the church hierarchy under the archbishop of Goa, an office created in 1557 when Goa became a metropolitan see, shaped urban life.

Revenue and trade were closely connected. Customs on goods entering Goa, tolls on the horse trade, revenue from rural districts, and the profits of voyages formed the basis of finance. The Crown also sold or granted the right to command voyages to Malacca, Japan, and other destinations, which served as rewards to nobles and officers, who then profited by trading privately or selling the right to merchants. This system turned public office into a form of private property and encouraged short term extraction.

Private Portuguese traders, many of whom had left royal service or married locally, built networks around the Bay of Bengal, the Coromandel coast, and Southeast Asia that operated with little Crown supervision. They lived in ports such as São Tomé de Meliapor and Nagapattinam, and traded under the protection of local rulers who valued their ships and their access to Portuguese markets. This Luso-Asian trade, sometimes called the shadow empire of the Estado da Índia, often outlasted the formal Crown structures.

Section 71.12

Sri Lanka, Cinnamon, and Regional Political Rivalries

Sri Lanka was the source of the finest cinnamon, and its coastal polities were divided. At the beginning of the sixteenth century the principal kingdom on the southwest coast was Kotte, with others in Jaffna in the north and in the central highlands, where the kingdom of Kandy emerged. The Portuguese arrived in 1505 and secured a commercial agreement with Kotte, building a fortified factory at Colombo in 1518 as part of their growing presence.

The island's politics shaped Portuguese involvement. Kotte was weakened by internal division, and the breakaway kingdom of Sitawaka, under Mayadunne and then his son Rajasinha I, became a formidable adversary, winning major victories over Portuguese forces and their Sinhalese allies in the 1560s and 1570s. The Portuguese tied themselves to Kotte's kings, notably Dharmapala, who converted to Christianity and in 1580 bequeathed his kingdom to the Portuguese Crown. The legal and political meaning of this transfer was contested by Sinhalese rivals and the Portuguese had to fight to enforce it.

Kandy, in the interior highlands, remained a durable independent state, repeatedly defeating Portuguese invasions, as at Danture in 1594 and Randeniwela in 1630. The Jaffna kingdom in the north was conquered by Portugal in 1619. Portuguese rule along the coast gave them cinnamon, which was gathered by specialized groups of laborers and delivered as obligations to the rulers, and relied on local soldiers called lascarins.

Sri Lanka shows the limits of the Estado da Índia. Portuguese control was effective along parts of the coast but fragile inland, and cinnamon production depended on the cooperation of local elites and the labor of caste groups. The Kandyan kings later invited the Dutch to help expel the Portuguese, with consequences examined in Section 71.21.

The political history of Sri Lanka in the sixteenth century was complex, and the Portuguese were a participant, not the main actor. Kotte's ruler Bhuvanekabahu VII sought Portuguese support in the 1520s and 1530s to maintain his authority against rivals, and the royal lineage was weakened by disputes in which his brothers established the breakaway state of Sitawaka. Under Mayadunne and Rajasinha I, Sitawaka became an effective military power, defeating Portuguese and Kotte forces in the 1560s and 1570s and pressing on Colombo, though it never took the fortress. Rajasinha's death in 1593 opened the way for a Portuguese resurgence under local allies and encouraged the growth of Kandy as the main independent power of the island.

Kandy was a kingdom of mountains and forests that was difficult to invade. Its rulers, notably Vimaladharmasuriya I, who ruled from 1592, and his successors, maintained independence, strengthened Buddhist institutions, and used negotiating skill. Portuguese expeditions into the highlands in 1594 and 1630, led by captains who expected to take the kingdom, were destroyed by ambush and attrition, and their commanders were killed. Defeat at Randeniwela in 1630 ended serious Portuguese hopes of conquering the interior.

Cinnamon production on the coast depended on the labor of specialized communities that peeled bark and delivered it as a service obligation, and on the cooperation of local chiefs. The Portuguese established a system of land registers and obligations, relied on local troops called lascarins, and sent Franciscan and later Jesuit missionaries to convert local elites. The result was a coastal society with Catholic communities, a Portuguese influenced elite, and a rural population that continued older practices. It also left a legacy of resentment that Kandy used to its advantage in 1638, when it invited the Dutch.

Section 71.13

Portuguese Merchants and the Spice Islands

The clove and nutmeg trade lay at the eastern end of the system. Cloves grew on a few islands of the Moluccas, notably Ternate, Tidore, Bacan, and Makian, and nutmeg and mace on the Banda Islands. Before the Portuguese, Javanese, Malay, Chinese, and Gujarati traders carried these goods to Malacca and beyond. After the conquest of Malacca in 1511, Portuguese expeditions under António de Abreu reached Banda, and Francisco Serrão reached the Moluccas, where he became an adviser to the sultan of Ternate.

The Portuguese established a fort on Ternate in 1522, but their position was never secure. Ternate and Tidore were rival sultanates whose rulers sought allies and trade, and they used the Portuguese against each other. Spanish ships arrived from the east, from the Magellan expedition in 1521 and later Spanish voyages from Mexico, and Tidore allied with Spain. The competition between Spain and Portugal was nominally resolved by the Treaty of Zaragoza of 1529, in which Spain sold its claims to the Moluccas for a payment, but local rulers had their own interests.

Relations between the Portuguese and Ternate deteriorated. In 1570 Portuguese officials killed Sultan Hairun, and his son Babullah organized a movement against them, besieging their fort and expelling them in 1575. The Portuguese shifted their activity to Ambon and Tidore and found that Muslim merchants from Java, Makassar, and elsewhere continued to supply cloves. Jesuit missions reached Ambon, creating Christian communities that would later become subjects of the VOC.

This episode shows several things: the Portuguese could not impose their monopoly in the Spice Islands, Asian rulers controlled access, and violence by European officials could provoke effective resistance. It also prepared the ground for the Dutch, who found local rulers who resented Portuguese behavior and were ready to bargain with a new power.

The Moluccas were politically organized in a way the Portuguese found difficult to understand. Ternate and Tidore were rival sultanates that each claimed authority over neighboring islands and over clove production, and the rulers controlled harvest, purchase, and sale. The Portuguese, entering in 1512, could gain access only by negotiating with these rulers, and the Portuguese captains who held the fort at Ternate attempted to control prices and the clove harvest. They also became involved in succession disputes and local rivalries, in which their reputation for arrogance and violence grew.

The killing of Sultan Hairun in 1570, committed by the Portuguese captain at a meeting that Hairun had accepted in good faith, transformed Ternate's politics. His son Babullah led a long campaign, besieging the fort, and in 1575 the Portuguese surrendered and departed. Under Babullah, Ternate expanded its influence over a large area of eastern Indonesia and reorganized trade with merchants from Java and Sulawesi. This was a major reversal for the Portuguese, and shows that a small garrison with a monopoly ambition could be defeated by a coalition of local forces.

Spanish activity added to the competition. After the Spanish settled in the Philippines in 1565, Manila became a center from which Spain could support Tidore, and Spain maintained a fort on Tidore for several decades, including a period from 1606 when it also occupied a fort on Ternate, until withdrawing in 1663. Portuguese and Spanish crowns were united after 1580, but their local agents remained rivals. On Ambon, Portuguese Jesuits baptized large numbers of people, and the Christian community that emerged there would later become subject to Dutch rule.

Section 71.14

Macau, Ming China, and the Portuguese China Trade

Portuguese contact with China began in the early sixteenth century and was marked by hostility. After Pires's embassy failed in 1517 to 1521, clashes occurred in 1521 and 1522, and Ming officials restricted foreign merchants. Portuguese traders then operated at sites along the Fujian and Zhejiang coasts in cooperation with Chinese merchants engaged in illicit private trade, until Chinese forces suppressed the settlement at Liampo in the 1540s.

Around the middle of the 1550s Portuguese merchants obtained permission to reside and trade at Macau, on a peninsula in the Pearl River estuary. The exact terms are uncertain, but the settlement was authorized by Guangdong officials and the Portuguese paid ground rent and customs. Macau was never conquered. Chinese officials administered the surrounding territory, maintained a barrier gate, and reserved judicial and fiscal control, while the Portuguese organized a municipal council, the Senado, and a Jesuit college. The Portuguese sovereignty claimed in later centuries has to be distinguished from the sixteenth century arrangement.

The commercial logic of Macau lay in connecting Chinese silk and porcelain with Japanese silver. Chinese authorities banned direct trade with Japan in this period, so Portuguese ships carried Chinese goods to Nagasaki and Japanese silver back to China. Merchants also exchanged goods with Goa, Malacca, and Manila, where Spanish American silver arrived from the 1570s, and Chinese demand for silver absorbed much of what flowed into the region.

Macau thus depended on Chinese tolerance. Officials in Canton and the imperial court could and sometimes did restrict supplies, and Portuguese privileges lasted as long as the arrangement suited both sides. The settlement endured through the Dutch wars, the end of the Japan trade in 1639, and the transformations of the seventeenth century, partly because it served Chinese as well as Portuguese interests.

The Macau arrangement was a bargain reached in a bureaucratic setting. The Portuguese were allowed to build houses and a church, while Chinese authorities in Guangdong collected rent and customs, controlled the entry of goods, and administered the surrounding territory. A barrier gate separated Macau from the mainland, a Chinese magistrate supervised relations with the settlement, and Portuguese merchants went to Canton for periodic trading fairs to buy silk and porcelain. The Portuguese council ran the town, and the bishop and Jesuit college provided a religious and educational center, which also served as a base for missions to China and Japan.

This relationship rested on mutual advantage but also on Chinese suspicion. Authorities at times restricted supplies to the settlement, and the Portuguese were careful not to challenge Chinese sovereignty. The Ming dynasty's fall in 1644 and the Qing conquest brought new uncertainties, but the Qing, too, found Macau useful and allowed the Portuguese to remain. Macau therefore illustrates how European commercial access could rest on Asian sufferance.

The commodity exchange connected several economies. Chinese silk, gold, and porcelain went to Japan and Goa, Japanese silver moved to China, and Indian and Southeast Asian goods reached Macau, which served as a link in a long chain. When the Japan voyage ended in 1639 and the Dutch captured Malacca in 1641, Macau suffered a serious commercial decline and survived by adapting, including through trade with Manila, Timor, and Southeast Asia.

Section 71.15

Portugal, Japan, Nagasaki, and Jesuit Commercial Networks

Portuguese men reached Japan in 1543, when a ship blown off course reached Tanegashima and the Japanese rapidly adopted firearms, which were soon manufactured locally. Francis Xavier of the Society of Jesus arrived in 1549, beginning a Christian mission that grew in southern Japan. The Jesuits depended on the Portuguese trading ship, the annual ship from Macau, to finance and supply their mission, and they sometimes acted as intermediaries between traders and Japanese lords.

Japanese daimyo competed for the trade, which brought silk, firearms, and other goods, and some converted to Christianity. The port of Nagasaki was developed in 1571 by Ōmura Sumitada, a convert, and for a time the Jesuits exercised substantial control there. The trade in silk for silver was very profitable. But as the Japanese state reunified under Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and then the Tokugawa, Christianity was increasingly seen as a political threat. Hideyoshi issued an order expelling missionaries in 1587 that was unevenly enforced, and Tokugawa authorities banned Christianity in 1614.

The final break came after the Shimabara rebellion of 1637 to 1638, a revolt of impoverished peasants, many of them Christian, which the shogunate suppressed with heavy killing. In 1639 Portuguese ships were barred from Japan, and in 1640 an embassy sent from Macau to seek reversal was refused and most of its members were executed. The shogunate maintained trade with the Dutch, Chinese, Koreans, and others under strict supervision, as Section 71.25 describes.

This history shows the limits of European leverage. Japan was a well armed and politically sophisticated country that regulated foreign contact according to its own priorities. The Portuguese trade lasted only as long as the Japanese authorities permitted it, and its end was a decision made in Edo, not a result of Dutch competition alone.

The Japan trade was organized around an annual voyage under a captain major appointed by the Crown, who held a monopoly over the ship from Macau, and who combined commercial and diplomatic responsibilities. The trade depended on Chinese silk, which the Japanese wanted in large quantities, and Japanese silver, which Chinese merchants valued, and it provided profit for the Portuguese and revenue for the Jesuit mission. Japanese authorities sought to control the silk trade by establishing a collective purchasing system, introduced in the early seventeenth century, which fixed prices and reduced Portuguese bargaining power.

The role of the Jesuits shows the entanglement of religion and commerce. They interpreted for traders and officials, received a share of silk profits to finance their mission, and in some places exercised political influence, as at Nagasaki. This role aroused suspicion among Japanese rulers who feared that a foreign religion might provide a basis for rebellion or foreign intervention. The destruction by Japanese forces of a Portuguese ship, the Nossa Senhora da Graça, in Nagasaki harbor in 1610 after a dispute shows that Japanese authorities were willing to confront the Portuguese when they wished.

The Dutch, who had arrived in 1600 on the ship Liefde and who obtained trading rights in 1609, benefited from the shogunate's concerns about Catholic missions. Yet the shogunate's decision to exclude the Portuguese arose primarily from internal considerations: the suppression of Christianity, the regulation of overseas contact, and the fear of rebellion after Shimabara. The shogunate was prepared to risk the loss of Portuguese silk imports, which the Dutch and Chinese could partly replace.

Section 71.16

Missionaries, Migrants, Families, and the Social World of Portuguese Asia

Religion was woven into the Portuguese empire through the padroado, the royal patronage by which the Crown supported missions and appointed bishops in return for responsibility for the church's spread. Jesuits, Franciscans, Dominicans, and Augustinians established missions from East Africa to Japan, running schools, hospitals, and printing presses, and writing grammars of local languages. Conversion took many forms. In Goa it was accompanied by laws restricting Hindu rites and by the destruction of temples. In other places missionaries adapted to local custom, and Christian communities arose among fishing groups on the Indian coast and in the Moluccas. The older Christian communities of Kerala, the Saint Thomas Christians, were pressed to accept Roman authority, notably at the Synod of Diamper in 1599.

The Goa Inquisition, established in 1560, investigated Catholics suspected of heresy, including New Christians of Jewish descent, converts accused of relapsing into Hindu or Muslim practice, and others. It was an instrument of religious and social control that operated for more than two centuries, and the records, though incompletely preserved, show the fear it created. Jewish, Muslim, and Hindu communities lived under varying degrees of tolerance and restriction.

Social life produced new communities. Casados, mixed families, and Portuguese speakers formed groups in Goa, Cochin, Malacca, Hughli in Bengal, and elsewhere. On Timor and Flores a mixed population known as Topasses developed, speaking a Portuguese creole and acting as autonomous traders and warriors. In Malacca and other ports, creole languages survive. Enslaved people, from Africa and across Asia, were an essential part of this society and were bought, sold, and employed in households, shipyards, and fields.

Women's experience is difficult to recover but important. Portuguese settlement relied on local women, who married or lived with Portuguese men, and orphans and mixed children were part of institutions sponsored by the Crown and the Misericórdia. Some women acquired wealth and influence, while others had little choice.

Missionary methods varied. The Jesuit visitor Alessandro Valignano argued that missionaries in Japan and China should adapt to local customs and learn local languages, and Jesuits such as Matteo Ricci, who prepared in Macau before entering China, studied Chinese learning and presented European science at the imperial court. In Goa and parts of India, by contrast, conversion could involve legal pressure and the destruction of temples. The Franciscans, Dominicans, and Augustinians competed for fields of mission, and disputes about methods and jurisdiction, including quarrels between the Portuguese Crown and the papacy over the padroado, recurred for centuries.

The Portuguese presence created new communities in many places. In Malacca, Kristang speakers preserved a creole that persists in small communities, in Sri Lanka a Portuguese influenced elite and Christian communities remained after the Dutch conquest, and in Bengal Portuguese settlements at Hughli and Chittagong included traders and adventurers who sometimes served local rulers as mercenaries. The best known example is Filipe de Brito e Nicote, who held Syriam in Burma from 1599 until 1613. Some of these adventurers acted with the approval of local rulers and with little reference to Goa, which illustrates the independence of Luso-Asian private actors.

Women's experience included the group of orphan girls sent from Portugal by the Crown to marry settlers, local Christian women, and enslaved women. Mixed families passed on property and language, and the church regulated marriage. Enslaved people from East Africa, India, and Southeast Asia worked in households, ships, and workshops, and their legal and religious status was shaped by baptism and by the claims of owners. The Inquisition at Goa investigated New Christians and converts, and its prosecutions, in the context of a society that depended on both Hindu and Muslim merchants, show the contradiction between religious ideology and economic need.

The Dutch Republic and the Rise of the VOC

Section 71.17

The Dutch Republic, Maritime Capitalism, and Overseas Ambition

By the 1590s the northern Netherlands, in revolt against Philip II since 1568 and organized from 1579 as the Union of Utrecht, had become a major commercial region, as Chapter 66 described. Dutch merchants controlled much of the Baltic grain trade, shipped herring and salt, built efficient cargo vessels, including the fluyt, a ship designed for low crew costs and large capacity, and carried goods across northern Europe. Amsterdam, with its merchants, insurers, and credit facilities, was becoming a market for commodities and information. The fall of Antwerp to Spanish forces in 1585 and the closure of the Scheldt drove southern merchants and capital north.

Dutch ships had long distributed Asian goods bought in Lisbon, and this trade was disrupted when Philip II, after taking the Portuguese throne in 1580, embargoed Dutch shipping in Iberian ports. The Dutch had good reasons to seek the goods at their source. They also gained knowledge of Asian routes from Linschoten's Itinerario and from the cartographer Petrus Plancius, and from Dutch sailors who had worked on Portuguese ships. A first expedition, led by Cornelis de Houtman, reached Banten on Java in 1596 after a difficult voyage that cost many lives. It brought back modest profits but proved a route.

The relationship between European conflict and overseas expansion was direct. The Dutch were at war with Spain and its Portuguese dependency, and attacks on Portuguese Asia were an extension of that war. Dutch investors, many of them refugees from the south, brought expertise in finance and shipping and a willingness to risk capital in speculative ventures. But the Dutch Republic was a federal state with limited central authority, and it needed an institution that could coordinate numerous competing groups and exercise power overseas.

Several of these elements, a shipbuilding industry, access to capital, information about Asia, and a political incentive to attack Portugal, were already in place by the end of the sixteenth century. None determined that the Dutch would build an empire. They also might have remained a collection of rival merchant syndicates, and the next section explains why they did not.

The Dutch overseas drive cannot be separated from the legal and political questions raised by the Revolt. The Republic had no recognized king, and the provinces had never agreed on a single foreign policy, yet merchants and officials argued that the Dutch had a right to trade with Asians and to resist Iberian claims to a monopoly. In 1603 a company fleet commander seized a Portuguese carrack, the Santa Catarina, in the Strait of Singapore, and the controversy over whether the capture was lawful prompted the jurist Hugo Grotius to write a treatise defending the right to use force against those who obstruct trade. A part of that work, published in 1609 as Mare Liberum, argued that the sea was open to all nations. The argument served Dutch interests, though Dutch companies themselves pursued monopolies on land and at particular ports, a contradiction that critics noted. Chapter 77 returns to the Republic's political institutions.

Dutch commercial strength also depended on specific institutions. Amsterdam's exchange provided a market for commodities and shares, its insurance underwriters spread risk, and notaries registered contracts. The Wisselbank, founded in 1609, offered secure payment settlement. Shipbuilders in the Zaan region and at Hoorn produced ships with economical designs, and the large Baltic trade ensured that Dutch seamen and shipyards were experienced and numerous. Protestant refugees from the south, including skilled merchants and craftsmen, added capital and expertise.

Section 71.18

The Founding of the Dutch East India Company, 1602

After the first voyage of 1595 to 1597, Dutch merchants founded numerous companies, called voorcompagnieën, that sent more than sixty ships to Asia in the following years. They competed in buying spices in Asia and in selling them in Europe, driving up purchase prices and driving down sale prices. The leading statesman of Holland, Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, pressed for a merger, which would also create an instrument of war against Portugal and Spain.

The charter granted by the States General on 20 March 1602 created the Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie for an initial term of twenty-one years. It gave the company a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and west of the Strait of Magellan. It also authorized the company to make treaties with Asian rulers, build fortifications, appoint governors and judges, maintain armed forces, and wage defensive war, powers normally reserved to sovereigns. The company was organized in six chambers, based in Amsterdam, Middelburg in Zeeland, Delft, Rotterdam, Hoorn, and Enkhuizen, and governed by a board of seventeen directors, the Heeren XVII, drawn from the chambers, with Amsterdam holding the largest share.

The capital raised was about 6.4 million guilders, subscribed by many investors, including merchants, artisans, and widows. Shares could be bought and sold, and a secondary market in VOC shares developed on the Amsterdam exchange. Investors were originally promised the return of their money after ten years, but the company's capital soon became permanent, and shareholders could only exit by selling their shares, a feature that made the VOC resemble a modern joint stock corporation. The directors, however, retained wide discretion, and shareholders had little say, which led to complaints that the company was run in the interests of insiders.

In Asia the company was led by a Governor-General and a Council of the Indies, resident in Asia from 1609 and at Batavia from 1619. The distance between directors in the Netherlands and officials in Asia, a round trip taking well over a year, meant that men on the spot made many decisions.

The company's governance was shaped by the compromise among towns. Amsterdam provided about half of the capital and the largest number of directors, Zeeland about a quarter, and the smaller chambers the remainder. Each chamber had local directors who fitted out ships, stored goods, and organized sales, and the board of seventeen decided the general policy, including the number and timing of fleets. The arrangement protected local interests but made decision making slow. Directors were drawn from the merchant elite, and the principal investors, the hoofdparticipanten, held strong influence, while ordinary shareholders had no vote on ordinary business.

Shareholder discontent appeared early. Investors complained about the lack of accounts and about the postponement of repayments, and a pamphlet campaign led by Isaac Le Maire, a former director who had speculated against the company's shares in 1609, accused the directors of mismanagement. The States General confirmed the arrangement and allowed the charter to be renewed in 1623 after negotiation, a result that suggests how much the state depended on the company and how little it interfered. The company's capital was made permanent, so that investors could not withdraw funds, and the shares became a market security whose price reflected expectations of future dividends.

Staff were organized in ranks, from assistants and bookkeepers to merchants of various grades, and the company employed soldiers, sailors, artisans, and clergy. Employees swore an oath to the company and the States General, and the company maintained its own courts and laws in Asia. In the early years, dividends were sometimes paid in kind, including pepper and cloves, and the first decades produced large profits for some investors but also required heavy expenditure on forts and ships. These details matter because they show that the VOC was a public enterprise in a legal sense as well as a private one, governed by officials who combined commercial and governmental roles.

Historical Vocabulary
Heeren XVII and the Chartered Company

A chartered company was a business organization created by a government charter that granted privileges, usually a monopoly, and sometimes governing powers. The Heeren XVII, or Seventeen Gentlemen, were the directors of the VOC, representing the six chambers, who set policy and appointed senior officials.

The VOC mattered because it combined features that were later separated: it was a joint stock corporation open to public investment, a trading monopoly, and an organization with powers of war and diplomacy. Modern equivalents are misleading, since the VOC was not a private company in the contemporary sense and not simply an arm of the Dutch government.

Section 71.19

Jan Pieterszoon Coen, Jayakarta, and the Establishment of Batavia

Jan Pieterszoon Coen, born in Hoorn, joined the company as a young merchant and became Director General and then Governor-General, serving from 1618 to 1623 and again from 1627 until his death in 1629. He argued that commerce in Asia required armed force and fortified bases, writing to the directors that the Company could not profit without the protection of its own weapons. This view reflected his experience in a region where Portuguese, English, and Asian competitors all used force.

The VOC first used Banten on western Java as its base, but Banten's rulers controlled the pepper market and its customs, and the English also had a factory there. A rival base was established at Jayakarta, a town on the north coast, under its local ruler, Prince Wijayakrama, who allowed both the Dutch and the English a presence but played them against each other and against Banten. In 1618 and 1619 tension rose as Jayakarta's ruler considered an alliance with the English, and the Dutch fort was besieged. Coen returned with a fleet in May 1619, relieved the fort, and destroyed Jayakarta, burning the town and driving out its ruler and population.

On the site he founded Batavia, named for the Batavi, whom the Dutch regarded as ancestors, and built a fortified town on a canal grid reminiscent of Dutch towns. Chinese merchants and laborers were encouraged to settle, and the city developed into a hub with Dutch company servants, free burghers, Chinese, Javanese, Balinese, South Asians, and a large population of enslaved people from across the region. Freed Christian slaves were called Mardijkers. Batavia's rulers governed through officials such as the Chinese captain who managed relations with that community.

Batavia was attacked by the sultan of Mataram, Agung, in 1628 and 1629, in sieges that failed but cost many lives. Coen died in the city during the second siege in September 1629, probably of dysentery; the precise diagnosis is not securely established. The city showed how the VOC could found a colonial center on the ruin of an Asian one, and also how dependent it was on negotiations with the Javanese powers around it.

Coen's early career shows the alliance of commerce and violence in company policy. He served the company as a young merchant in Banten and Sumatra and rose to senior rank, and he developed the view that the company must secure bases from which it could exclude competitors. He wrote influential reports to the directors arguing that the company should concentrate its resources, populate its main base with settlers, and use the profits of intra Asian trade to finance purchases. His successors followed some of these ideas and rejected others, but the principle of a fortified Asian center was settled by his actions.

The fall of Jayakarta involved the English as well as the Javanese. The local ruler, Pangeran Jayawikarta, had allowed both the Dutch and the English to build trading posts, and in 1618 a dispute led to open conflict, in which an English fleet under Thomas Dale, newly arrived, fought both the Dutch and the Javanese at different times. Coen returned in May 1619 and, after the destruction of the town, a treaty of cooperation between the English and Dutch companies, concluded in July 1619 under pressure from their governments, briefly aligned the two companies. After the destruction of Jayakarta, the English retired to Banten, and the Dutch built the new city.

Batavia's population was built deliberately. Coen encouraged Chinese settlement, including through Chinese leaders such as the first Chinese captain, Souw Beng Kong, and arranged the capture and transport of populations from places the company attacked, including coastal Fujian and the Banda Islands. The city's structure reflected distinct legal categories: company servants, free burghers, Chinese, Mardijkers, and enslaved people, each with different rights and obligations. The city council included burgher representatives, though the Governor-General controlled the fortress and the army. The settlement became the center of the company's administration, trade, and justice, and a place where the company's power was most visible.

Section 71.20

Ambon, Banda, and the Violent Enforcement of Spice Monopolies

The VOC sought to gain control of the sources of the most valuable spices, cloves in the Moluccas and nutmeg and mace in Banda. In 1605 it took the Portuguese fort on Ambon, and thereafter it forced local rulers to agree to sell cloves only to the company. Enforcing this required naval patrols, called hongi expeditions, the term for local fleets of war canoes that the VOC used to suppress smuggling and to destroy unauthorized clove trees. Over the following decades, clove cultivation was concentrated on Ambon and nearby islands under VOC control, and trees elsewhere were uprooted.

The Banda Islands were small, produced the world's only nutmeg and mace, and were governed by local elites, the orang kaya, who traded with Javanese, Malay, English, and other merchants. The Dutch demanded exclusive contracts. The Bandanese resisted, and relations deteriorated after the killing of the Dutch commander Pieter Verhoeven in 1609. English traders maintained a contested foothold on Run, while the VOC had seized Ai in 1616. Continued Bandanese trading with English and other merchants sharpened Dutch determination to enforce exclusive contracts.

In 1621 Coen led a large expedition to Banda, with a fleet and several thousand men, including Japanese mercenaries. The Dutch attacked, burned villages, and killed many inhabitants. Dutch records say that forty four orang kaya were executed after a trial. Many Bandanese were killed in the fighting, died of hunger and exposure, or were enslaved and shipped to Batavia, while others fled to nearby islands. Estimates of the pre conquest population are uncertain and often placed at around fifteen thousand, and the surviving population on the islands afterward is estimated at under a thousand. Scholarly estimates of deaths vary, and exact numbers cannot be established. The Dutch National Archives and many historians describe the events as genocide.

The islands were divided into plantations, called perken, assigned to Dutch planters, the perkeniers, who worked them with enslaved labor brought from elsewhere. The VOC seized Run in 1621, and the island remained contested before the Treaty of Breda in 1667 confirmed the Dutch retention of Run while England retained New Netherland, including New Amsterdam. The treaty was a wider peace settlement, not a literal island-for-Manhattan barter. This was a commercial triumph for the VOC achieved through the destruction of a society.

The company used different methods in different islands. On Ambon, it combined a clove monopoly with contracts with local rulers, regular hongi expeditions that destroyed trees on unauthorized islands, and a system of forced deliveries. The policy of extirpation, formalized in the mid seventeenth century, aimed to limit clove cultivation to specified islands, so that the company could control supply and prices, and it was enforced by sending armed fleets to cut down trees. Villages that had grown cloves for local rulers or for Javanese and Malay merchants lost their livelihood, and the loss of independence was accompanied by repeated rebellions. In 1623 the company's officials on Ambon tortured and executed a group of English, Japanese, and Portuguese men accused of conspiracy, an episode known as the Amboyna affair, which long poisoned Anglo-Dutch relations.

On Banda, Coen planned the 1621 operation carefully. Forces landed on Neira and then on other islands, and the Dutch burned settlements, destroyed boats, and killed or captured resisting fighters. The Japanese soldiers in company service played a role in the execution of the orang kaya. Hundreds of surviving Bandanese were shipped to Batavia as slaves, and others fled to the Kei Islands, Seram, and other nearby places, where Bandanese communities still exist. The perken system gave planters plots on condition that they sold nutmeg and mace to the company at fixed prices, and the labor shortage created by the conquest was met by importing enslaved people from other parts of Asia.

Historians have long debated how to classify the violence. The term genocide did not exist in 1621, but it is used by many modern scholars and by the Dutch National Archives to describe the deliberate destruction of a people. The evidence from company records shows planning, intent to remove or eliminate the Bandanese from the main islands, and extensive killing and enslavement. Other scholars emphasize the uncertainties in population figures and the role of disease and flight, while agreeing on the central responsibility of the company's leaders. The Bandanese, whose oral traditions record the events, are an essential part of the evidence.

Section 71.21

The Dutch Capture of Malacca and Expansion into Sri Lanka

After establishing Batavia, the VOC turned to Portuguese positions that blocked its routes. Malacca, controlling the strait, had been weakened by Aceh's attacks and by the migration of merchants, but remained a strong fortress. The Dutch blockaded it for years, and in 1640 they joined the sultan of Johor in a siege, which ended in January 1641 when the fortress fell after months of hunger and disease among defenders. The Portuguese defender surrendered and the Dutch took possession. Johor, a partner in the capture, expected a share of influence, and the Dutch had to bargain with it.

Malacca under the VOC did not recover its previous role as the main entrepôt, since Batavia served that function and many Asian merchants traded at other ports. The Dutch used it as a fortified checkpoint and as a base for relations with Malay states.

In Sri Lanka the VOC made an alliance with the kingdom of Kandy, whose king, Rajasinha II, had asked for help against the Portuguese. A Dutch fleet arrived on the east coast in 1638, and the Dutch took Galle in 1640 and Negombo the same year. Disputes over control of the cinnamon coast soon divided the allies. The siege of Colombo lasted from 1655 to 1656 and ended with its capture after severe hardship, and Jaffna fell in 1658, ending Portuguese rule on the island.

The Dutch did not hand the coast to Kandy. They kept it, developing a cinnamon monopoly based on traditional labor obligations, and Kandy remained independent in the interior until 1815, when it was taken by the British. The campaign shows how Asian alliances helped the VOC in the first stage and how the VOC then pursued its own aims. It also illustrates a recurrent pattern: the Dutch used Asian rulers against the Portuguese, and Asian rulers used the Dutch against the Portuguese, and each side ended with a view of the other as a troublesome partner.

The siege of Malacca in 1640 and 1641 combined European and Asian forces. The Dutch fleet blockaded the harbor, the troops of Johor besieged the land side, and the defenders suffered from hunger and disease. Dutch and Johorean forces had different aims: Johor wished to restore its influence in the strait, while the VOC wished to remove a Portuguese rival and secure access. The Dutch afterwards kept the city, restricted Johor's influence, and developed Malacca as a minor port. The Malays and Portuguese Eurasian communities adapted, and the Malay states continued to operate their own trade through other ports.

In Sri Lanka, the Dutch negotiated a treaty with Rajasinha II in 1638 in which the Dutch were to be reimbursed for their expenses by cinnamon and other goods, and in which Kandy hoped to receive the captured forts. The Dutch declined to hand over the forts, citing unpaid debts, and Kandy withdrew its cooperation. The conquest of Colombo in 1656 and of Jaffna in 1658 was completed with Dutch forces alone, and the VOC held the coastal districts, with Kandy cut off from the sea. Dutch policy toward the population differed from the Portuguese. The Reformed Church became the official church, Catholic priests were expelled and Catholic worship was restricted, while Buddhism was tolerated in a limited way. The company introduced Roman Dutch law, and the descendants of Dutch and local families formed a community known as Burghers.

The VOC's cinnamon monopoly relied on the existing labor system, administered through local headmen and a hierarchical structure of service obligations. Efforts to cultivate cinnamon in company gardens were later expanded, and conflicts with Kandy over trade routes and borders continued. The Dutch period shows both the effectiveness of the VOC in eliminating a European rival and its inability to subdue the interior kingdom.

Section 71.22

Java, Makassar, and the Expansion of VOC Political Power

On Java the VOC encountered two powerful sultanates. Banten, in the west, was a major pepper port and a rival of Batavia, and Mataram in central and eastern Java had a large agrarian base and military force. After the failed sieges of Batavia in 1628 to 1629, Mataram remained a menace for decades. The VOC pursued blockades, treaties, and eventually intervention in succession disputes. In 1674 a rebellion led by Trunajaya, with Makassarese allies, threatened Mataram, and the VOC intervened on behalf of Amangkurat I's successor, receiving concessions of revenue and territory as payment. Later treaties, notably in 1705, gave the company control of areas on Java's north coast and the Priangan highlands, which would become important sources of coffee in the eighteenth century.

In 1682 and 1683 the VOC intervened in a dispute within Banten between the sultan, Ageng Tirtayasa, and his son, Sultan Haji, and in 1684 forced a treaty giving the company a pepper monopoly and effective control of the port.

Makassar in South Sulawesi was a major port, open to traders of many nations, including Portuguese, English, Danes, and Malays, and the sultanate of Gowa-Tallo defended its policy of free trade against VOC attempts to enforce a spice monopoly. A VOC fleet under Cornelis Speelman, allied with Arung Palakka, a Bugis prince from Bone who had been a rebel against Gowa, attacked in 1666. The Treaty of Bungaya in November 1667 imposed harsh commercial and political terms on Gowa, including restrictions on rival European trade, but did not end the war. Fighting resumed, and VOC and Bugis allied forces captured the principal Gowa stronghold at Somba Opu in June 1669. The fort at Makassar was renamed Rotterdam.

These actions altered the company's character. A trading organization that had sought forts and monopolies now collected taxes, appointed rulers, and governed populations in parts of Java and Sulawesi, though it still depended on local elites and allies. Historians describe this as the creeping transformation of the VOC into a territorial power in particular regions, not a conquest of the archipelago, since many islands and sultanates remained independent.

The sequence of the Makassar war is essential for understanding its significance. In 1660 Gowa defeated Bone, an ally of the VOC, and drove Arung Palakka into exile, and he eventually reached Batavia and became an ally of the company. In 1666 Cornelis Speelman led the expedition, joined by forces from Bone, Ambon, Buton, and other places, which attacked Gowa. The Treaty of Bungaya, signed in November 1667 by Sultan Hasanuddin, forced Gowa to expel other European merchants, grant the company a trade monopoly, give up Bone and other conquests, and pay indemnities. Gowa soon resumed resistance, and in 1668 and 1669 the VOC and its allies besieged and took the fortress at Somba Opu, after which Makassar was effectively subjugated. Arung Palakka became the dominant ruler of southern Sulawesi, a client of the company.

The consequences were wide. Makassar's open port, which had attracted merchants from Southeast Asia, Europe, and India, lost this role as its trade was channeled through the company, and many Makassarese left for Java, Siam, and elsewhere. Some of them joined anti Dutch forces in Java, including those of Trunajaya in the 1670s. The war shows how the VOC used local alliances, such as that with Arung Palakka, to achieve ends that its own forces could not.

The company's methods in Java and Sulawesi followed a pattern. It signed treaties that required rulers to deliver commodities at fixed prices, to forbid trade with other Europeans, and sometimes to cede territory and revenue, and it stationed residents and garrisons to enforce compliance. These arrangements gave the company authority without full responsibility for administration, and left local rulers with considerable autonomy in internal affairs. In this way, the VOC became a political power with real power over certain areas, but not a unified colonial state.

Historical Debate

Was the VOC Principally a Trading Corporation, an Imperial State, or an Institution Combining Both Functions?

The question: Was the VOC mainly a business concerned with profit, a state that used commerce as a source of revenue, or a hybrid institution that defies either description?

Interpretation A: Some historians emphasize the company's commercial character: its aim was dividends, its officials were judged by profits, and its use of force served trade. Its monopoly, treaties, and fortresses were tools to secure goods at low cost. On this view the VOC is best understood as a business that used political means to reduce risk.
Interpretation B: Others argue that the VOC functioned as a state in Asia. It maintained armies and navies, made war and peace, administered justice and collected taxes in several regions, and governed populations. The company's territorial governance, they argue, shows that its commercial and political roles were inseparable, and that the Dutch state delegated imperial power.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the charter of 1602, company correspondence, financial records showing the proportion of income from trade versus taxation and tribute, and descriptions of governance in Batavia, Ambon, Sri Lanka, and Java. The mixture varied by place and time, with territorial governance more developed in some areas than others.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians describe the origins of corporate and state power and how they interpret later European colonialism, including whether the company was a precursor of nineteenth century state empires.

Section 71.23

Indian Textiles, Pepper, and Dutch Trade in South Asia

Indian cotton textiles were the key to the VOC's success in the spice islands. Spice producers in Southeast Asia wanted cloth, and Indian weavers produced it in styles and qualities suited to different markets. The company established factories on the Coromandel coast at Pulicat, where it built Fort Geldria in 1613, at Masulipatnam, and later at Nagapattinam, and in Bengal at Hughli and Kasimbazar, in Gujarat at Surat, and on the Malabar coast, where it captured Cochin from the Portuguese in 1663. It purchased pepper in Malabar, saltpeter and silk in Bengal, and textiles on the Coromandel coast.

Textile purchasing required engagement with Indian production and politics. The company advanced money to weavers through Indian brokers and merchants, a system that tied artisans into long chains of credit. Local rulers, such as the kingdom of Golconda, the Nayaks of southern India, and the Mughal Empire, controlled access and set customs and privileges. The company relied on farmans and agreements from these rulers and had to respect Indian merchants who dominated local markets, including Chettis, Muslim merchants, and Armenian and Gujarati traders.

By the middle of the seventeenth century Indian cottons also became an important item of the European trade, and the VOC and its English competitors sold them in the Netherlands and England, a development with consequences for European tastes and industries that Chapter 85 and Chapter 93 will examine.

The VOC in South Asia was therefore a commercial participant that had to adapt to Indian markets, which were far larger than anything the Dutch could dominate. It was one of several European and Asian merchants in the field, and its position depended on negotiation, credit, and the maintenance of factories by permission of rulers.

Textile procurement was an Indian industry before it was a Dutch one. On the Coromandel coast, weavers and dyers in villages near ports such as Pulicat and Masulipatnam produced cloth for export, and the company advanced money to them through Indian brokers and merchants. The cloth was graded and priced according to the markets for which it was destined, with different qualities for Java, Sumatra, Siam, and Europe, and the quality was regulated by negotiation. Because cloth had to be paid for in advance, the company's local servants spent much time managing the credit chain and negotiating with brokers and officials. The system tied Dutch commerce to the fortunes of regions that were subject to famine, as in Gujarat in the early 1630s, and to warfare among Indian powers.

In Bengal the company bought raw silk, cotton cloth, and saltpeter, which was needed for gunpowder in Europe and Asia, and opium, which it sold in the archipelago. The company's factories at Hughli, Kasimbazar, and Patna depended on Mughal permission and the goodwill of provincial officials. On the Malabar coast, the capture of Cochin in 1663 gave the company access to pepper through treaties with local rulers, and its commander, Hendrik van Rheede, sponsored the compilation of the Hortus Malabaricus, a botanical work that relied on the expertise of local physicians and scholars, among them the Ezhava physician Itty Achuthan. The work illustrates how European knowledge production relied on Indian collaborators.

The importance of textiles in the VOC's trade grew over time. The company discovered that its European sales of Indian cloth could be profitable, and by the later seventeenth century textiles formed a major share of imports from India. This growth, together with the competition of the English company, led to public debates in Europe about the effect of imported cottons on domestic industries, which Chapters 85 and 93 take up.

Section 71.24

Taiwan, China, and Dutch Commerce in East Asia

Ming China restricted foreign trade, and the VOC wanted a base from which to deal with Chinese merchants. In 1622 the Dutch occupied the Penghu Islands (Pescadores) and were forced by Ming forces to withdraw in 1624, relocating to Taiwan, where they built Fort Zeelandia at Anping. The island was then home to Austronesian communities, notably the Siraya, with Chinese and Japanese traders on the coast. Spain also established posts in the north from 1626 until the Dutch expelled them in 1642.

The Dutch colony combined a trading base with an administration over indigenous villages. The company taxed villagers, regulated the deerskin trade, and sent Protestant missionaries and schoolteachers, who produced the first written texts in Siraya. It encouraged Chinese settlers to cultivate rice and sugar, and the Chinese population grew. Taiwan connected Chinese silk and porcelain, Japanese silver, and Southeast Asian goods. A Chinese uprising in 1652 was suppressed with heavy casualties, revealing the tensions of colonial governance.

The Ming collapse in 1644 and the Qing conquest transformed the situation. Zheng Chenggong, known in Europe as Koxinga, whose father Zheng Zhilong was a powerful maritime merchant, remained loyal to the Ming cause and commanded a fleet. Pressed by the Qing, he looked to Taiwan as a base. In 1661 he landed with a large force, and after a siege of Fort Zeelandia, the Dutch governor Frederick Coyett surrendered in February 1662. The Dutch were expelled.

Taiwan shows both the extent and the limits of Dutch power. The VOC created an administration over indigenous communities and Chinese settlers, but it was overwhelmed by a regional power that could mobilize a larger army. The Siraya, whose lands changed hands several times, experienced the Dutch, the Zheng regime, and later the Qing in succession.

Dutch Taiwan combined a trading post with an experiment in governing an indigenous population. The company convened assemblies of village headmen, called landdagen, to confirm Dutch authority, assigned its Protestant ministers to learn local languages and establish schools, and leased the right to collect trade taxes to Chinese contractors. The deerskin trade, in which indigenous hunters took deer on lands the company controlled, formed a main source of exports, and the company encouraged Han Chinese settlers to farm rice and sugar. Chinese migration from Fujian grew rapidly, and by the 1650s Chinese numbered in the tens of thousands, though precise estimates are uncertain. The company's taxes and monopolies produced tension, and in 1652 a large revolt led by Guo Huaiyi was crushed with the help of indigenous allies.

The relationship between the VOC and the Zheng family was crucial. Zheng Zhilong, a powerful merchant and naval commander who began as an intermediary for the Dutch, served the Ming and later switched to the Qing, and his son Koxinga continued to resist. Koxinga's fleet and army were far larger than the Dutch garrison, and after landing in 1661, he besieged Fort Zeelandia, whose defenders surrendered after many months. The Dutch afterwards tried to ally with the Qing against the Zheng, and held Keelung in northern Taiwan until 1668, but they never recovered the island.

The Dutch period in Taiwan left a legacy in written Siraya texts and in descriptions of local society, and it also shows the limitations of company power. The VOC could not defend its position against a regional naval power, and it depended on the cooperation of Chinese merchants whose interests did not coincide with its own.

Section 71.25

The VOC, Japan, and Restricted Trade at Nagasaki

The VOC established a factory at Hirado in 1609 with permission from the Tokugawa shogunate, and traded Chinese silk, Southeast Asian goods, and later products for Japanese silver. The Dutch were Protestants, and they argued that unlike the Portuguese they had no missionary purposes. This claim was useful and largely true, and they assisted the shogunate in 1637 and 1638 by bombarding the Shimabara rebels from the sea.

After Portuguese ships were excluded in 1639, the Dutch were ordered in 1641 to move their operations from Hirado to Dejima, a small artificial island in Nagasaki harbor, where they were confined under supervision. They were forbidden to leave, to bring families, or to practice Christianity in public, and their ships were inspected. The company accepted these terms because the Japanese trade was profitable, and it sent an annual delegation to the shogun's court at Edo, a journey that gave Japanese officials and scholars access to Dutch knowledge.

The Dutch exported silver, copper, and camphor, until Japan restricted silver exports in the 1660s, and imported Chinese silk, sugar, and Southeast Asian products. Chinese merchants also traded at Nagasaki in greater numbers than the Dutch. The shogunate regulated other contact through Tsushima with Korea, Satsuma with the Ryukyu Kingdom, and Matsumae with the Ainu, so the Dutch were one of several permitted channels.

Dejima became a source of European knowledge in Japan, known as Dutch learning (rangaku), in medicine, astronomy, and geography, and a source of information about Japan for Europeans, including the writings of the German physician Engelbert Kaempfer, who stayed in 1690 to 1692. The relationship was restricted, but it was useful to both sides.

Dejima was a compromise. The island, small and artificial, had been built in the 1630s to house Portuguese merchants, and the Dutch were moved there to occupy the space left empty by their expulsion. The company paid rent to the Nagasaki merchants who owned it, and the Dutch staff, numbering only a small group, lived under constant observation by Japanese officials and interpreters, a hereditary group of translators. Ships were inspected on arrival, books were examined, and the Dutch were required to provide annual reports on world events, called fūsetsugaki, which gave the shogunate a source of foreign news.

The relationship was costly and limited. The company paid for annual journeys to Edo, the capital, with gifts for the shogun and officials, which allowed some personal contact between Dutch and Japanese scholars and physicians. After 1668 Japan restricted silver exports and later regulated copper exports, which reduced the profit of the company's Japanese trade. Chinese merchants, who were allowed to trade at Nagasaki in larger numbers, supplied silk and other goods, and remained the dominant foreign merchants there.

The contacts at Dejima, however, were intellectually significant. Japanese scholars studied European medicine, astronomy, and geography through the Dutch language, and European visitors, including Kaempfer and later the Swedish botanist Carl Peter Thunberg, who visited in the 1770s, wrote about Japan, a country the Dutch described but could not enter freely. Dejima shows an Asian state setting terms and a European company accepting them in order to preserve profits and access.

Section 71.26

The Cape of Good Hope and the Company's African Supply Network

In 1652 the VOC sent Jan van Riebeeck to establish a station at Table Bay on the southwestern tip of Africa. Its purpose was to provide fresh water, vegetables, and meat to company ships on the long voyage, and the company did not initially intend to create a settlement colony. But the need for food led it to release company servants to farm as free burghers from 1657, and settlement spread.

The land was home to Khoikhoi pastoralists, divided into groups with their own leaders, as well as San hunter gatherers. Early relations involved trade, in which the Khoikhoi supplied cattle and sheep, but conflict arose over land and grazing, and wars occurred in 1659 to 1660 and 1673 to 1677. The Dutch seized land, restricted Khoikhoi movement, and drew many into labor on colonial farms. A smallpox epidemic in 1713, carried by a Dutch ship, devastated the Khoikhoi of the region.

Slavery was foundational. Because the company discouraged the use of Khoikhoi labor and wished to avoid enslaving local people, it imported slaves from Indonesia, India, Madagascar, and East Africa. These enslaved people worked on wheat and wine farms, in the company's lodge, and in households. Political exiles from the VOC's Asian territories, such as the Muslim leader Sheikh Yusuf of Makassar, who arrived in 1694, contributed to a Muslim community in the Cape. European settlers, including Dutch, Germans, and French Huguenots from the 1680s, pushed inland as stock farmers.

The Cape thus tied Africa to the Indian Ocean system in a way that was permanent. It is also a case where a supply station became a colony of settlement, with consequences that extended far beyond the company's end.

The Cape station was founded in a region of existing political and economic networks. Khoikhoi groups such as the Cochoqua and Peninsulars grazed their cattle in the area, and interpreters such as Autshumato, who had earlier dealt with English and Dutch ships, played important roles in early trade. The company wanted cattle and sheep from the Khoikhoi but did not pay much, and the Khoikhoi were reluctant to sell animals that were central to their wealth. Conflicts over grazing land and cattle led to war in 1659, and the settlement expanded after the company decided that farming by free burghers could supply food more cheaply.

Expansion brought new settlements at Stellenbosch from 1679 and Drakenstein, where French Huguenots arrived after 1688, and an increasing demand for labor. Landholding arrangements evolved as settlement expanded: alongside grants and other tenure forms, the Company formalized a loan-place system for grazing land in 1714, under which settlers paid for permission to use land without acquiring outright ownership. Settlers also held enslaved workers. The enslaved population grew, supplied by the company's slave trade from Madagascar, East Africa, India, and Indonesia, and by the 1700s enslaved people were a majority of the colony's agricultural workforce in the western districts. Khoikhoi were pushed off land, subjected to labor demands, and decimated by the smallpox epidemic of 1713. The colony thus became a settler society with a hierarchy defined by status, religion, and legal category.

The Cape's importance to the VOC lay in its function as a provisioning port, but its social consequences were far larger. It connected the Indian Ocean slave trade with the settlement of southern Africa, brought Muslim exiles and Indonesian craftsmen to the colony, and began a pattern of land seizure and labor coercion that would continue under later governments.

Everyday Life and Social History

Comparative Experiences in Goa, Batavia, and an Asian Port beyond European Control

Evidence for ordinary life in these ports is uneven, but comparison is possible. In Goa, a married Portuguese settler, a casado, might hold a house, work as a petty trader or officer, and depend on enslaved servants. His wife was frequently of Indian or mixed origin. Religious life centered on parish churches, confraternities, and processions, and the Inquisition cast a shadow over households with Jewish or Hindu ties. Hindu brokers and merchants ran much of the commerce and lived in their own quarters, while many soldiers lived in poverty and disease. Mortality among new arrivals from Portugal was high.

In Batavia, company servants, many of them from the German states, Scandinavia, and the Low Countries, lived under strict hierarchies and high mortality from malaria and dysentery. The wealthy built canal houses, kept enslaved domestic servants, and adopted Asian foods and dress, while the Chinese community ran much of the retail trade, sugar milling, and tax farming. Enslaved people made up a large share of the population and came from Bali, Sulawesi, India, and elsewhere. Moral regulation, church attendance, and legal distinctions of status structured daily life. The massacre of 1740 revealed how fragile relations among communities were.

A major Asian port outside European control, such as Aceh, Makassar before 1667, or Ayutthaya, offers a contrast. Merchants from many origins lived in separate quarters, supervised by officials like the shahbandar, and paid customs to local rulers. Ordinary sailors, laborers, and artisans moved between ports, and a mixture of religious practices coexisted. European residents, where present, lived as guests, subject to local law and custom. The contrast with Goa and Batavia lies in who held legal authority: in those cities Europeans set the rules for others, while in independent ports they accepted the rules of others.

Section 71.27

Intra-Asian Trade, Commercial Logistics, and the VOC's Maritime System

The VOC's profits were never based solely on shipping spices to Europe. A large part of its operations consisted of intra-Asian trade, sometimes called country trade, in which company ships and officials carried goods between Asian ports. Japanese silver and copper paid for Indian textiles, Indian textiles paid for spices and pepper in the archipelago, Chinese silk and porcelain went to Japan and Europe, tin from the Malay Peninsula and Bangka moved through Batavia, sugar from Java and Taiwan served several markets, and Persian silk and Ceylon cinnamon were sold in different directions. Batavia served as a collecting point, a warehouse, and a port of transshipment.

The system required elaborate logistics. Ships from the Netherlands, called retourschepen, sailed in fleets and returned with cargoes, the outward voyage to Batavia commonly taking around eight months and involving large crews. Warehouses, shipyards, arsenals, and hospitals in Batavia and other posts supported the fleet. Company bookkeeping recorded accounts, prices, wages, and stocks, and the Generale Missiven provided a regular record of policy. Credit and remittance systems moved funds between Asian posts and the Netherlands, since silver sent from the Republic and silver from Japan supplied the needed currency.

Staffing was multinational. The company recruited thousands of men from the Dutch Republic and also from the German states, Scandinavia, and elsewhere, and a large share of VOC sailors and soldiers were not Dutch. Asian crews, enslaved workers, and local soldiers made up a large part of the actual workforce. Mortality among Europeans, especially in Batavia and on the voyage, was very high.

The multinational network offered advantages: knowledge, flexibility, and access to multiple markets. It also had costs, including the expense of garrisons, the tendency of officials to engage in private trade, and the weight of administration. Understanding the system as a whole is crucial for judging the VOC's profits and its later difficulties.

The records of the company are central to knowing how it worked. Each factory kept daily registers of events, called dagregisters, the Governor-General and Council in Batavia sent annual reports to the Netherlands, and the accounts of trade, prices, and stocks are preserved in large quantities. These records enable historians to reconstruct which goods were sold where and at what prices, though they reflect the company's perspective and leave out private trade and Asian commerce.

Asian trade dynamics were as important as European demand. Japanese silver and copper provided the means to buy Indian textiles, and textiles bought pepper and spices in Indonesia, but changes in any link could disrupt the chain. When Japan restricted silver exports or when Indian harvests failed, the company had to adjust purchases and supplies. Tin from the Malay Peninsula, secured by treaties with Perak and other states, was shipped to India and China, sugar from Java and Taiwan went to Japan and Persia, and in the later seventeenth century coffee from Mocha and Java was added.

The cost structure was heavy. Forts, ships, warehouses, hospitals, and salaries accounted for much of the expense, and mortality among Europeans, especially in Batavia, which had a reputation for fever, forced continuous recruitment. Employees engaged in private trade, which increased their income but reduced the company's. The same multinational character that allowed the VOC to recruit labor and capital across Europe also made management difficult and expensive, especially in the eighteenth century. For these reasons, historians increasingly treat the VOC as an organization whose profits depended on a complex balance of commodities, markets, and costs, not on spices alone.

Section 71.28

Portuguese-Dutch Rivalry and the Iberian Union, 1580–1640

In 1578 King Sebastian of Portugal died in battle in Morocco without a direct heir, and after the brief reign of his great uncle Cardinal Henry, Philip II of Spain claimed the Portuguese throne, which he secured through military force and negotiations with the Cortes at Tomar in 1581. The union of the crowns, which lasted until 1640, was a dynastic union, with Portugal keeping its own laws, institutions, and empire in principle. Chapter 75 examines the Habsburg composite monarchy more fully.

For Portuguese Asia the effect was to make its territories targets in the Dutch Revolt. Spain's embargo on Dutch ships in Iberian ports gave Dutch merchants a reason to go directly to Asia. Dutch attacks on Portuguese holdings followed, including the capture of the fort at Ambon in 1605, unsuccessful assaults on Mozambique and Macau in 1607 to 1608 and 1622, and campaigns in Brazil, West Africa, and Asia. Portuguese forces, stretched across the globe and supported by a Crown with many commitments, could not defend everything. The loss of Hormuz in 1622 to Persian and English forces reduced Portuguese revenue.

In December 1640 Portuguese nobles overthrew Spanish rule and acclaimed John IV of Braganza as king, the Restoration. A truce with the Dutch followed in 1641, but it did not apply fully in Asia, where the VOC took Malacca in January 1641 and continued attacks in Sri Lanka and Malabar. Portugal lost Cochin and Cannanore in 1663. A peace treaty in 1661 recognized Dutch gains and Portugal's compensation, although Portugal retained Goa, Daman, Diu, Macau, Timor, and Mozambique.

The rivalry therefore combined European war and Asian opportunity. The Dutch succeeded where they used naval power against isolated fortresses and where local alliances aided them, and failed where they met effective local resistance, as in Macau and Mozambique.

The Iberian Union was not a complete merger. The assembly at Tomar in 1581 confirmed Portuguese laws and offices, and Philip II promised that the Portuguese empire would be administered by Portuguese officials. In practice, Portuguese Asia was affected by the priorities of Madrid, including wars with England and the Dutch, which brought attacks on Portuguese shipping and the loss of ships and revenue. The Dutch war on Portuguese possessions was therefore both an extension of the Revolt and an assault on a weak target.

The Twelve Years' Truce between Spain and the Dutch Republic from 1609 to 1621 was only partly observed in Asia, where the Dutch maintained that the truce did not apply and continued to attack. After the truce ended, Dutch forces made major attacks in the Atlantic and the Indian Ocean. They blockaded Goa on several occasions in the 1600s and later, and captured Portuguese outposts in Ceylon, Malabar, and elsewhere. The Portuguese Restoration of 1640 was followed by a truce in Europe in 1641, but the VOC continued operations in Asia until its position was recognized. A treaty in 1661 acknowledged Dutch gains in return for a payment from Portugal, and ended the formal war.

The conflict contributed to the Portuguese decline in some regions, but it also reflected local conditions. Dutch success depended on Portuguese weakness, but also on local allies such as Johor, Kandy, and the rulers of Malabar, who had reasons to weaken the Portuguese. The outcome was an Asia in which the Portuguese retained certain places and the Dutch dominated others. Chapter 75 treats the Habsburg system within which this Portuguese weakness occurred.

Section 71.29

The Dutch West India Company and the Atlantic Dimension of Dutch Empire

The Dutch West India Company, founded in 1621 when the truce with Spain ended, was the Atlantic counterpart to the VOC. It had a monopoly over Dutch trade in West Africa and the Americas and powers of war and governance. Its structure was similar, with chambers and a board of directors, but it relied more on privateering and conquest, and was less consistently profitable.

The WIC captured Salvador in Brazil in 1624 and held it briefly, then took Pernambuco in 1630 and governed Dutch Brazil, with Recife as its center, until its expulsion in 1654, under governors such as Johan Maurits of Nassau from 1637 to 1644. It seized Elmina on the Gold Coast from Portugal in 1637, took Luanda in 1641, and established stations on Caribbean islands such as Curaçao. In North America, the company founded New Netherland, with its center at New Amsterdam, which England took in 1664.

The distinction between WIC and VOC is significant. The VOC operated in a world of powerful Asian states and made profits from trade within Asia. The WIC operated in regions where European colonization and slavery were central, and its fortunes depended on sugar, the slave trade, and privateering. The WIC was reorganized after financial difficulties in 1674. This section does not repeat Chapter 70's analysis of Atlantic slavery, but notes that Dutch commerce linked Atlantic and Asian systems, with merchants, ships, and capital moving between them and with parallel practices of coerced labor.

The Dutch West India Company deserves attention because it highlights the different environments of the two Dutch overseas systems. Its early successes included privateering, most spectacularly the capture of the Spanish silver fleet at Matanzas Bay in Cuba in 1628 by Piet Heyn, which brought large profits. But the company's major conquest, Dutch Brazil, drew large expenditure and depended on sugar and the slave trade, and its loss in 1654 was costly. The WIC also held forts on the West African coast and traded in enslaved people, commodities, and goods, subjects covered in Chapter 70. By the 1670s its finances were in trouble and it was reorganized, while the VOC, with a different system of profits, remained stronger for much longer.

Section 71.30

English, French, and Asian Competition for Maritime Commerce

The English East India Company, chartered in 1600, entered the Asian trade with fewer resources than the VOC. It established a factory at Surat in Gujarat after defeating a Portuguese fleet at Swally in 1612, and secured trade privileges from the Mughal emperor Jahangir, through the embassy of Sir Thomas Roe from 1615 to 1618. It also competed with the Dutch in the spice islands, until the execution of English merchants at Ambon in 1623, known as the Amboyna affair, became a lasting source of English grievance and led the company to concentrate on India.

English holdings in India grew from the 1639 grant at Madras and the establishment of Fort St George from 1640, Bombay, which came to England in 1661 as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza and was transferred to the company in 1668, and Calcutta in 1690. The French Compagnie des Indes orientales was founded in 1664 under Colbert's patronage and established Pondicherry in 1674. Danish, Swedish, and other companies also took part. These companies fought with each other in Europe and Asia, and Chapter 72 examines British and French expansion more fully.

It is crucial that Asian powers set the terms. The Mughal Empire controlled India's major textile centers and ports and allowed European traders under conditions. When Mughal forces expelled the Portuguese from Hughli in 1632 they showed that Europeans could be removed. The Safavids, Ottomans, and the sultanate of Oman under the Ya'rubid dynasty each pursued their own aims in the Gulf and Red Sea, Aceh and Makassar bargained for position, Siam and Vietnam dealt with Europeans on their own terms, and the Qing and Tokugawa regimes regulated trade strictly.

The competition among European companies could lead to cooperation or to conflict, and alliances shifted. Asian rulers used the rivalry to extract better terms, and merchants of many origins used European ships and passes when it suited them.

The English East India Company began as a series of separate voyages, each financed by subscription, and only after the middle of the seventeenth century was it organized as a permanent joint stock. Its early position in the spice trade was weak compared to the Dutch, and its focus on India reflected both its defeat in the archipelago and the attraction of Indian textiles. The conflict in 1686 to 1690 between the company and the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, in which the English were driven from several ports, shows that European companies were not yet dominant in India. The French company, a state sponsored enterprise established under Colbert, faced financial difficulties but founded Pondicherry as a base. The Danish company obtained Tranquebar from the ruler of Thanjavur in 1620.

Asian powers continued to determine outcomes. The Mughal Empire expelled the Portuguese from Hughli in 1632 after a siege, Aceh's sultans used European competition to maintain their position in Sumatra, Siam's court under King Narai in the later seventeenth century played Dutch, French, and English agents against one another, and Ottoman rule in Yemen shaped the coffee trade from Mocha. Omani forces, under the Ya'rubid dynasty, established a maritime empire that took Portuguese possessions on the East African coast. The companies were important participants but were far from alone in determining the outcome. Chapter 72 follows the British and French story further.

Section 71.31

Commerce, Credit, Coercion, and Labor in Imperial Trading Systems

Finance shaped both empires. The Portuguese Crown financed the Carreira da Índia from customs revenue, loans from merchants in Lisbon and Antwerp, and sales of spices, but its debt rose. The VOC was financed by share capital, short term loans, and bonds sold in the Netherlands, and it paid dividends that attracted new investment. Its accounts allowed investors to follow the company, though less transparently than ideals of the period required. Asian commercial finance, with bills of exchange, partnership, and brokerage, continued to run in parallel, and Europeans relied on it.

Private trade and corruption blurred the line between company and individual. Portuguese captains and officials engaged in private commerce, and VOC employees, despite prohibitions, traded privately on a large scale, supplementing their salaries. Asian intermediaries, including brokers, interpreters, Chinese captains, and Indian merchants, held considerable influence, because only they could manage relations with local markets.

Coercion supported commerce. The Portuguese used naval seizure and tolls, and the VOC used contracts imposed through force, forced deliveries of spices, blockades, and destruction of unauthorized crops. Labor was often coerced. The VOC and its servants bought and sold enslaved people from Arakan, Bengal, the Coromandel coast, Bali, Sulawesi, and Madagascar, and employed them in Batavia, the Cape, Ceylon, and the spice plantations. The Portuguese enslaved people from East Africa and across Asia. Estimates of numbers are disputed, but there is no doubt that slavery was integral to both systems, even though it differed in scale from Atlantic plantation slavery.

The relationship between profit and violence was intimate. The VOC's greatest profits in the early seventeenth century came from spice monopolies enforced by force, and Coen's argument that commerce required war was acted upon. Not all profits depended on violence, since much of the company's trade was ordinary exchange, but the violent episodes were not accidents. They reflected a strategy in which delegated sovereignty was used to reduce competition.

The VOC's finances combined long term capital, borrowing, and sales revenue. The company shipped silver and gold from Europe to Asia in large amounts in the seventeenth century to pay for goods, and financed some operations by local revenue, including taxes from territories and profits from intra Asian trade. It raised additional capital by issuing bonds in the Netherlands, and its credit depended on the confidence of Amsterdam investors. The Portuguese Crown, by contrast, borrowed on weaker terms and had less ability to spread risk across a wide investing public.

Labor conditions in the system were harsh. Sailors and soldiers were often recruited in Dutch ports by agents who advanced wages and held recruits in debt, and many died of disease. Asian workers served as sailors, laborers, and soldiers, and enslaved people performed domestic and heavy labor. The company also obtained labor by obligations imposed on subject populations, such as the clove deliveries in Ambon, the cinnamon peelers in Sri Lanka, and in the eighteenth century the forced coffee deliveries in the Priangan highlands. Estimates of the number of enslaved people brought to VOC settlements range widely, and one reconstruction suggests roughly 660,000 to 1,135,000 arrivals over the company's life, based on partial records and demographic reasoning. Not all were carried on company ships; many were moved by private traders and by Asian networks.

The Portuguese used a similar range of labor systems. They employed enslaved Africans and Asians on ships and in households, relied on obligations from subject communities such as the cinnamon peelers of Sri Lanka, and hired mercenaries. Both empires thus drew labor from coercion and compulsion as well as wages, and violence was an instrument for establishing and maintaining the labor supply.

Section 71.32

Portuguese Persistence, VOC Difficulties, and the Transformation of Commercial Empire

The decline of Portuguese power in some regions did not mean the disappearance of Portuguese commerce. Goa, Daman, Diu, Macau, Timor, and Mozambique remained Portuguese, and private Luso-Asian merchants continued to trade in Bengal, Southeast Asia, and the Indian Ocean, often under the protection of local rulers. The Portuguese also built a new Atlantic center of gravity in Brazil, where gold discoveries in the 1690s changed the focus of the empire. Goa itself declined as a commercial center but survived as a religious and administrative one.

The VOC reached its greatest relative strength in the mid seventeenth century, with control of the spice trade, a network of forts, and large dividends. Problems accumulated in the eighteenth century. European demand shifted from spices to Asian textiles, tea, coffee, and porcelain, which were more difficult to monopolize, and the English and French companies competed vigorously. The company's administrative costs, garrisons, and corruption weighed on its finances, and dividends were sometimes paid from borrowing rather than profit. It tried new products, including coffee from Java, grown under forced delivery systems, and tea purchased from China at Canton.

Wars hurt the company. The Fourth Anglo-Dutch War of 1780 to 1784 destroyed much of its shipping and led to the loss of some stations to Britain. Internal conflicts in the Republic, culminating in the Batavian Revolution of 1795, led to the nationalization of company debts, and the charter expired. The VOC was dissolved on 31 December 1799, and its possessions passed to the Dutch state. The massacre of Chinese residents in Batavia in October 1740, in which thousands were killed, shows the violent side of eighteenth century colonial society.

The pattern is neither Portuguese failure nor Dutch triumph. Portugal retained footholds through adaptation and local partnership, while the VOC's success proved vulnerable to changing markets, competition, and financial strain.

The VOC's financial problems were visible in the eighteenth century. Dividends were maintained at high levels in some years even when profits did not justify them, partly to sustain share prices, and the company borrowed to cover shortfalls. Administrative costs rose, the Asian garrisons were expensive, and corruption among officials reduced the company's income. Changing consumer tastes in Europe reduced the relative importance of spices and increased the importance of tea, textiles, and coffee, where the VOC had less control. In the 1730s the company bought tea from Chinese merchants at Canton, and the English company overtook it in the trade.

Colonial society also faced strains. In October 1740 a rumor of a Chinese uprising and the company's attempt to deport Chinese residents led to a massacre in Batavia in which thousands of Chinese residents were killed, and the survivors faced further hardship, an episode that was followed by a war in central Java. The sugar industry, run mostly by Chinese entrepreneurs, suffered. The Fourth Anglo-Dutch War of 1780 to 1784 and the French Revolutionary wars delivered the final blow, and in 1795 the Batavian Republic took over the company's debts and soon after established a committee to oversee its affairs, until the charter expired and the company was dissolved on 31 December 1799.

Portuguese Asia, meanwhile, remained smaller and poorer but not extinct. Goa remained a viceregal seat, Macau developed trade with Canton in the eighteenth century, Timor and the Topasses preserved a Portuguese cultural presence, and Mozambique's economy turned increasingly toward the slave trade. The Marquis of Pombal's reforms of the mid eighteenth century attempted to strengthen Portuguese administration, but the center of the Portuguese empire had shifted to Brazil. The story of both empires in the eighteenth century is therefore one of adaptation to changing conditions, not simple decline.

Section 71.33

Comparing the Portuguese Estado da Índia and the Dutch VOC

The two empires shared aims but differed in structure. The Estado da Índia was a royal enterprise, headed by a viceroy or governor appointed by the Crown, with fortresses, officials, and a royal monopoly on certain goods. Its ideology joined crusade, dynastic prestige, and commerce, and its church was bound to the Crown through the padroado. The VOC was a corporation whose directors answered to shareholders and the States General, and whose goals were defined mostly in terms of profit, though its powers were quasi sovereign.

Finance differed. The Portuguese Crown relied on royal revenue and loans and faced repeated shortages. The VOC drew on a broad base of investors and the Amsterdam capital market and had a permanent capital stock, which gave it staying power in its first century. Military power also differed. The Portuguese had an early advantage in artillery ships and fortified ports, but few men. The VOC mobilized larger fleets and, in the seventeenth century, more soldiers, though it also suffered heavy losses to disease.

Private trade was important to both. Portuguese private traders handled much of the commerce within Asia, often outside Crown control, while VOC servants traded privately in violation of company rules. Territorial authority varied. Goa and parts of its surrounding territory, and Sri Lanka's coast, were governed directly, as were Batavia and parts of Java, Ambon, and Banda for the VOC. Elsewhere both relied on treaties and permissions.

The comparison should not be oversimplified. Portuguese Asia contained many men acting independently, and the VOC's governors exercised extensive government authority. The empires also resembled each other in their reliance on Asian intermediaries, their use of coerced labor, and their need to bargain with local rulers.

Several institutional contrasts deserve more emphasis. The Estado da Índia relied on appointed nobles and officials who served limited terms, often with a mix of public duty and private profit, and its law and administration were those of the Portuguese Crown, adapted to circumstances. The VOC's officials were employees, ranked in a hierarchy, with oaths and salaries, who answered to directors in the Netherlands, and its law combined company ordinances and Roman Dutch law. Portuguese society in Asia was marked by an emphasis on conversion and by the integration of local people into a Catholic framework, while the VOC separated its commercial purposes from missionary activity, except where Protestant clergy served its settlers and converts.

These contrasts should not hide the similarities. Both empires used force to compel compliance, both relied on forts and fleets, both depended on local people for labor and information, and both sought to impose monopolies in high value goods and then found that trade within Asia was more important and more difficult to control than they had expected. Both also experienced the problem of principal and agent: officials far from the center acted on their own interests. The Portuguese response was to rotate officials, the Dutch to establish a hierarchy of audit, and neither solution was fully effective.

The different financial structures explain much of the difference in resilience. The VOC's access to capital markets gave it larger fleets and a greater capacity for sustained war in the seventeenth century. The Portuguese Crown's fiscal weakness, along with its diversion of resources to Brazil and Europe, left Asia with limited support. But the VOC's dependence on dividends also created pressure to pay investors, a pressure that later weakened its finances.

Section 71.34

Asian and African Agency and the Limits of European Commercial Empire

Asian and African actors shaped the empires at every stage. The Zamorin resisted Portuguese claims for decades. The Gujarati sultanate fought, negotiated, and traded with Portuguese ships. Hindu merchants in Goa and Muslim merchants in Malacca dealt with new authorities and adapted. The kingdom of Kandy used the Dutch against the Portuguese and then resisted the Dutch. Johor allied with the Dutch against Portugal in 1641 and fought for its own advantage. Arung Palakka of Bone used the VOC to defeat Gowa and win power. Koxinga expelled the Dutch from Taiwan.

Rulers also controlled the terms of trade. Ming and Qing officials regulated Macau and Canton, the Tokugawa shogunate confined European merchants, and the Mughals granted and revoked privileges. Omani forces took Muscat and Mombasa from the Portuguese, and Safavid forces took Hormuz. Asian merchants chose whether to take European passes and which ships to use.

Laborers and intermediaries mattered as much as rulers. Brokers, interpreters, pilots, soldiers, sailors, artisans, and enslaved people supplied the labor and knowledge on which the Europeans relied. Women who married Portuguese or Dutch men were vital in building communities. Resistance took the form of revolt, flight, sabotage, and legal claims.

The limits of European power are therefore clear. Fleets could win battles, but could not occupy the Indian Ocean. Companies could capture ports, but could not supply goods without local producers and merchants. The question for historians is not whether Asians had agency, but how the structure of power constrained what they could do, and where they could shape outcomes.

The evidence of agency is wide. In India, Mughal and regional authorities decided which Europeans could trade and where, and Indian merchants and bankers financed and brokered much of European commerce. In Southeast Asia, rulers such as the sultans of Aceh, Banten, Mataram, Johor, and Gowa pursued their own interests, sometimes allying with the Dutch and sometimes resisting them, and the Bugis and Makassarese, in particular, spread through the archipelago as traders and warriors. In Sri Lanka, the Kandyan kings maintained independence for centuries. In East Africa, Swahili and Omani actors decided the fate of Portuguese forts.

Agency also existed among those with less power. Enslaved people escaped, formed communities, and used legal and religious institutions to gain freedom. Soldiers and sailors deserted or defected to local rulers, as did some Portuguese and Dutch men, who sometimes served Asian rulers as gunners or advisers. Local interpreters, clerks, brokers, and pilots exercised power by controlling information, and women in mixed families transmitted languages, customs, and property. Resistance took many forms, including revolt, evasion of tolls, smuggling, and refusal to cooperate, and it affected company profits.

These forms of agency shaped the limits of European power, but they should not be romanticized. Agency operated within structures of coercion that were often brutal, and many people's choices were severely constrained. Historians therefore try to recognize both the capacity of Asian and African actors to shape events and the violence and exploitation that framed their lives.

Section 71.35

Maritime Networks, Global Commodities, and the Changing European Economy

The goods that moved from Asia to Europe changed over time. In the sixteenth century, pepper and spices dominated. In the seventeenth century, the Dutch expanded imports of cloves, nutmeg, and cinnamon, along with Indian textiles, silk, saltpeter, and porcelain. In the eighteenth century, tea, coffee, and textiles became more important than spices. Asian goods influenced European taste, domestic furnishings, and fashion, and Chinese porcelain inspired Delft pottery.

Silver and capital flowed in the other direction. Japanese silver, and later American silver carried by Spanish and Dutch routes, financed purchases in Asia. European demand for Asian goods, and Asian demand for silver, linked continents. Ships, shipbuilding, insurance, bookkeeping, and the Amsterdam exchange became part of this system, and the joint stock company and tradable shares developed as institutions for pooling risk, though they were not invented only for this purpose.

Knowledge also moved. Navigators, cartographers, naturalists, and scholars collected information about coasts, plants, languages, and peoples. The Dutch botanist Georg Eberhard Rumphius studied the plants of Ambon, and VOC mapmakers contributed to atlases. Missionaries and officials produced dictionaries and descriptions. These works served commerce and curiosity and fed European science, discussed in Chapters 81 and 82, though they relied on local informants whose contribution was often unacknowledged.

Chapter 73 will address the broader transformation of European society, including consumption, finance, and politics. For this chapter the point is narrower: Portuguese and Dutch maritime networks helped create a more interdependent global commercial system, with Europe as an important but not controlling participant, and with consequences for Asian and African societies that were often severe.

The economic effects on Europe need careful statement. Asian trade was important for Amsterdam and Lisbon, but it was not the largest part of Dutch or Portuguese commerce. The Dutch economy relied heavily on the Baltic grain trade, fisheries, shipping services, and European manufacturing, and the VOC was an important but not dominant sector, though its shares and dividends were significant to the Amsterdam capital market. For Portugal, the spice trade provided large revenue to the Crown but did not produce a durable commercial economy, partly because the Crown spent revenue on wars and court expenses and partly because foreign merchants handled the distribution.

The commodities, however, affected European consumption. Pepper and cloves became common in elite kitchens, Chinese porcelain reached Dutch and Portuguese households and inspired local imitations, Indian cottons influenced fashions, and tea and coffee, which grew in importance in the eighteenth century, shaped sociability. These changes were part of the broader development of consumer markets that Chapter 85 examines. The effect on manufacturing, including the debate over whether imported textiles stimulated domestic industry, will be examined in Chapter 93.

The institutional legacy also mattered. The joint stock company, the market in shares, the use of bills and bonds, and the maritime insurance system were refined in connection with Asian trade, though they drew on earlier Italian and Asian practices. The accumulation of geographical knowledge, from maps to botanical catalogues, supported navigation and science. These institutions and ideas would be used by the British and French and by later states. The lesson is not that Europe was transformed by Asia alone but that overseas commerce was one of several interacting causes of change.

Section 71.36

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

The chapter has traced two commercial empires through a changing Afro-Eurasian world. Portugal entered an Indian Ocean already linked by monsoon routes, port cities, merchant diasporas, and credit, and tried to turn naval power and fortresses into control over trade. It created the Estado da Índia, with Goa as capital and a chain of forts from Mozambique to Macau, but its monopoly was never effective, its manpower was small, and its relations with Asian states were often negotiated.

The Dutch entered after 1595, driven by the Revolt and by the Spanish embargo on Dutch trade, and organized the VOC in 1602 as a joint stock company with delegated sovereign powers. It built Batavia, enforced spice monopolies through violence, captured Malacca and Sri Lanka, traded across Asia, and founded a station at the Cape. It also faced limits, as in Taiwan, Japan, and India.

Both empires depended on Asian and African societies, and both used coercion and enslaved labor. Portuguese networks persisted after the Dutch advance, and the VOC declined in the eighteenth century and was dissolved in 1799. The central historical lesson is that early modern European commercial empire was real, violent, and consequential, but also partial, contingent, and dependent. Chapter 72 follows the British and French, whose later empires built on this foundation.

Three interpretive conclusions emerge. First, the history of Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires cannot be written from European sources alone, and each major argument in this chapter has depended on reading Portuguese and Dutch records against Asian, African, and archaeological evidence. Second, power in the early modern Indian Ocean was always relational. Fortresses, fleets, and charters mattered, but only as they combined with alliances, markets, labor, and local political conditions. Third, commerce and coercion were inseparable, but not in a uniform way: the Portuguese Crown, the VOC, private traders, and Asian rulers each used force and bargaining differently, and the consequences for particular communities ranged from profitable partnership to destruction. Students should carry these conclusions into Chapter 72, where English and French companies encounter a world already reshaped by these earlier systems.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how Portugal and the Dutch Republic established extensive commercial networks in the Indian Ocean and beyond, how those networks worked, and how Asian, African, and European societies shaped them. It began from the premise that the Indian Ocean was an old and sophisticated commercial world. East African, Arab, Persian, Gujarati, Tamil, Malay, and Chinese merchants had built overlapping circuits tied together by the monsoons, by port cities such as Calicut, Cambay, Aden, Hormuz, and Malacca, and by institutions of credit, brokerage, and customs administration. The Portuguese and Dutch entered this world as newcomers.

The Portuguese reached Calicut in 1498 and soon moved from seeking access to attempting control. Under Almeida and Albuquerque they won the naval victory at Diu in 1509, took Goa in 1510 and Malacca in 1511, and secured Hormuz by 1515. They built fortified ports along the East African and Indian coasts and tried to regulate shipping through the cartaz. Their purpose combined commerce, dynastic ambition, and crusade, and their methods included alliances and violence.

The Estado da Índia, governed from Goa, extended to Sri Lanka, the Moluccas, Macau, and Japan, but it was never a unified empire. Goa and parts of the surrounding territory were governed directly, while Macau and Nagasaki involved privileges negotiated with Chinese and Japanese authorities. The Carreira da Índia and the Casa da Índia linked Asia to Lisbon, but the Crown's monopoly was expensive, was widely evaded, and did not eliminate the Red Sea route or Asian merchants. Portuguese society in Asia was a Luso-Asian one, shaped by casados, mixed families, missionaries, the Inquisition, and enslaved labor.

The Dutch Republic's commercial economy, the Revolt, and the Iberian Union led to the formation of the VOC in 1602. Its charter gave it a monopoly, shareholders, and powers of war and diplomacy, and its directors, the Heeren XVII, governed from the Netherlands. Under Coen, the company destroyed Jayakarta, founded Batavia, and conquered the Banda Islands in 1621 in an episode of mass killing and forced displacement, which the Dutch National Archives and many historians call genocide. It enforced monopolies in the Moluccas, took Malacca in 1641, helped end Portuguese rule in Sri Lanka by 1658, and extended political power in Java and Sulawesi.

The VOC was not only an exporter of spices to Europe. It traded within Asia, linking Japanese silver and copper, Indian textiles, Southeast Asian spices and tin, and Chinese goods. It established factories in India, a colony in Taiwan until 1662, a restricted post at Dejima, and a station at the Cape that became a settler colony based on slavery and the dispossession of the Khoikhoi. The company's multinational workforce, high mortality, and reliance on Asian intermediaries were basic features.

Competition among the empires was shaped by European politics. The Iberian Union exposed Portuguese Asia to Dutch attack, the Portuguese Restoration of 1640 changed the diplomatic context but not the Asian war, and the Dutch West India Company extended Dutch empire to the Atlantic. English and French companies challenged the Dutch, and Asian powers such as the Mughals, Safavids, Omanis, Aceh, Tokugawa Japan, and Qing China set limits.

Finance and coercion were bound together. Portugal relied on royal revenue and loans, and the VOC on share capital and bonds. Private trade, corruption, and coerced labor were embedded in both systems, and violence was used to enforce monopoly and discipline labor. Historians debate whether Portugal achieved an effective monopoly and whether the VOC was a trading corporation or a state, and the most persuasive answers stress the variety of practice by place and period.

Continuity and change were uneven. Asian trade continued, and most merchants and states experienced less transformation than European sources suggest, but particular places, such as the Banda Islands, Ambon, coastal Sri Lanka, and conquered ports, experienced devastating change. Portuguese networks persisted through Goa, Macau, Timor, Mozambique, and Luso-Asian private traders, while the VOC faced rising debt, competition, and the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War before its dissolution in 1799.

The chapter's central argument is that Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires emerged from attempts to control strategic connections and profitable exchanges within long established systems. They were distinct institutions, the one royal and the other corporate, but both depended on ports, alliances, intermediaries, and labor, and both met strong resistance. Their consequences for Europe included new commodities, financial and organizational innovations, and growing global interdependence, which Chapter 73 will treat in a wider social context.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 71 matters because it shows that European overseas expansion was not a single wave of conquest but a collection of attempts to enter, tax, and sometimes dominate complex commercial systems. It reveals the possibilities and limits of early modern state power. Portuguese kings and Dutch merchants had real advantages in shipbuilding, artillery, and finance, but they relied on alliances, intermediaries, and Asian markets, and they met resistance from states that could close ports or expel them.

The chapter also reveals how commerce and violence were intertwined. The destruction of Jayakarta, the conquest of Banda, the displacement of merchants, and the expansion of slavery were not departures from commerce but tools of particular commercial strategies. Recognizing this does not require ignoring the creativity of Dutch finance, the skill of navigators, or the cultural exchange that occurred. It requires holding these facts together, which is a central task of this textbook.

Within Volume III, the chapter connects Atlantic expansion, studied in Chapters 68 to 70, with the building of states in Part XII. It shows how Portugal's Crown and the Dutch Republic used overseas enterprise to compete in a European system shaped by the Habsburg wars, and how chartered companies became a model that British and French rivals would adapt. The VOC's combination of corporate finance and public authority anticipated debates about the relations of business and government in later centuries, though without any simple line of descent.

Many consequences extended beyond the chapter. Portuguese and Luso-Asian communities, Dutch colonial institutions in Indonesia and Sri Lanka, the Cape's settler society, and the memory of Banda influenced the later colonial world, as Chapter 105 and Chapter 128 will show. Chapter 72 now turns to British and French overseas expansion, which inherited the competitive environment created by the Portuguese and Dutch and then reshaped it, and Chapter 73 will examine how all of these empires changed European societies.
