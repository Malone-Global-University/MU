Italian and German Unification and the Remaking of Central Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 18 January 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, a palace built to glorify the kings of France, the king of Prussia was proclaimed German Emperor while Paris, a few kilometers away, was still under siege. A new national state had been announced in the capital of the power it had just defeated. Yet the unification of Germany, like that of Italy, was less the fulfillment of a single national destiny than the outcome of wars, bargains, constitutions, and decisions that could have gone otherwise.

Consider the setting of that January day. German princes and senior officers, many of them in the field uniforms of a campaign still in progress, gathered in the great gallery of Louis XIV's palace, which the German army was using as headquarters and hospital. The ceremony was organized by the Prussian military and political leadership and attended by a gathering of princes and soldiers rather than by elected representatives of the German people. William I, king of Prussia, was reportedly uneasy about the imperial title, which he regarded as diminishing the Prussian crown he valued more highly. The painting best known to later generations, by Anton von Werner, was produced in versions that date mostly from the 1880s. It is a retrospective representation, composed to give the event a particular political meaning, and it is therefore evidence about how the Empire wished to be remembered as much as about what occurred on the day.

The contrast with Italy is instructive and forms one of the chapter's governing themes. Ten years earlier, on 17 March 1861, a parliament dominated by Piedmontese institutions had proclaimed Victor Emmanuel II king of Italy, a kingdom that at that moment lacked Venice and Rome and included territories whose populations had little common political experience. In Italy, a constitutional monarchy of modest size, a diplomatist of exceptional skill, a revolutionary general with a popular following, and a French emperor with his own agenda combined to create a state. In Germany, a militarily powerful monarchy, a minister of conservative convictions who did not hesitate to employ nationalist sentiment, and three short wars produced an empire. In both cases, the state came first in practical terms, and the nation that was supposed to have created it had to be assembled afterward.

This chapter traces how those two states came into being between the failed revolutions of 1848 and 1849 and the early 1870s, why their paths differed, and how Prussia's victory over Austria in 1866 forced the Habsburg monarchy to reinvent itself as Austria-Hungary in 1867. It is the culminating chapter of Part XV. It draws on the ideologies of liberalism, conservatism, and nationalism examined in Chapter 97, the revolutionary movements of 1848 examined in Chapter 98, and the constitutional reforms discussed in Chapter 99, and it asks how ideas and popular movements were converted into territorial states and international consequences. Its governing analytical distinction is between national unification as a political ideal and national unification as a concrete process of building states and exercising power.

The chapter also rejects two simplifications. The first is inevitability: unification did not follow automatically from the spread of nationalist ideas or industrial growth. The decision of Napoleon III to halt the war in 1859, the success of Garibaldi's improbable landing in Sicily, the speed of Austria's collapse in 1866, the editing of a telegram at Ems, and the willingness of the southern German monarchs to accept a Prussian-led empire were all moments at which different outcomes were conceivable. The second is the cult of the great statesman. Cavour, Garibaldi, and Bismarck were extraordinary actors, but they worked through armies, parliaments, bureaucracies, banks, railway networks, newspapers, dynasties, and foreign governments whose interests they could manipulate but not command.

The evidence for these events is unusually rich and unusually politicized. Contemporary evidence includes diplomatic dispatches, parliamentary debates, constitutional texts, plebiscite returns, military records, newspapers, and caricatures. Against these stand memoirs written after success, official histories that treated unification as a story of national resurrection, and paintings and monuments created to sustain the legitimacy of the new states. Historians use the first kind of material to test the second. The primary source window in section 100.15 asks students to do exactly that with the Ems Dispatch, comparing an original diplomatic telegram with the version Bismarck released to the press.

Finally, the chapter treats central Europe as a region remade, not simply as a stage for two national triumphs. Austria's defeat transformed the Habsburg monarchy into a dual state with two constitutional systems, a shared dynasty, and a population of many nationalities, many of whom were dissatisfied with the settlement. The new Germany and Italy, for their part, were themselves composed of regions, confessions, and language communities whose integration was incomplete. The diplomatic order of Europe, built at Vienna in 1815 and shaken by the Crimean War, was replaced by a system in which a unified Germany stood at the center of the continent. The consequences, including the annexation of Alsace and part of Lorraine, will carry the narrative directly into the imperial rivalries and alliance systems of Part XVI.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Italian national aspirations survived the defeats of 1848 and 1849, and why Piedmont-Sardinia became the principal center of Italian moderate nationalism.
  2. Analyze Cavour's combination of economic modernization, parliamentary politics, and diplomacy, and distinguish his aims from those of Mazzini and Garibaldi.
  3. Trace the sequence from Plombières and the war of 1859 through the annexations, the Expedition of the Thousand, and the proclamation of the Kingdom of Italy in 1861.
  4. Assess the role of foreign powers, especially France, in the Italian process, and explain why Venetia and Rome were acquired only in 1866 and 1870.
  5. Describe the political, economic, and military conditions that strengthened Prussia in the German Confederation, including the Zollverein, army reform, and the constitutional conflict.
  6. Evaluate Bismarck's methods and the limits of describing them as a single plan, with reference to the Danish War, the Austro-Prussian War, and the Franco-Prussian War.
  7. Compare the North German Confederation of 1867 with the German Empire of 1871 in terms of federalism, suffrage, and executive authority.
  8. Explain the Compromise of 1867 and the structure of Austria-Hungary, and analyze its consequences for the monarchy's nationalities.
  9. Interpret competing historical explanations of unification as a popular national movement, a state-led achievement, or a combination of both, using primary and secondary evidence.
  10. Connect the unifications to the transformation of the European balance of power, the Franco-German relationship, and the transition to the age of empire.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did nationalism, which was powerful as an idea in 1848, achieve political success only when it was taken up by established states and armies?
  2. How did the political and economic strengths of Piedmont-Sardinia and Prussia differ from those of their rivals, and why did those strengths matter?
  3. In what ways did foreign governments, not only national leaders, determine the shape and timing of unification?
  4. What role did war play in creating national states, and what did military victory leave unresolved?
  5. How legitimate were plebiscites, constitutions, and parliaments as expressions of popular consent in the new states, and who was excluded from them?
  6. Why did the unifications of Italy and Germany follow different paths, and how far were those differences produced by contingency rather than structure?
  7. Why did the creation of national states fail to resolve questions of national belonging in Austria-Hungary, Italy, Germany, and the borderlands between them?
  8. How did the emergence of a unified Germany alter the balance of power, and why should that change not be reduced to a single cause of later conflict?

Historical Context

The territorial settlement made at the Congress of Vienna in 1815, discussed in Chapter 92, rearranged central Europe without erasing the political fragmentation that Napoleon's wars had begun to reduce. In Italy, the peninsula was divided among the Kingdom of Sardinia, which included Piedmont, Savoy, Nice, Liguria, and the island of Sardinia; the Kingdom of Lombardy-Venetia, a possession of the Austrian emperor; the Duchies of Parma, Modena, and Tuscany, the latter two ruled by Habsburg-connected dynasties; the Papal States, extending across central Italy; and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, ruled by a Bourbon dynasty from Naples. The name "Italy" was in 1815, as the Austrian statesman Metternich is said to have remarked, chiefly a geographic expression, and the Austrian presence in Lombardy-Venetia and its influence over central Italian rulers made the peninsula a field of Habsburg power.

In the German lands the Vienna settlement created the German Confederation, a loose league of sovereign monarchies and free cities that numbered roughly thirty-nine members at its founding. Austria presided over its Federal Diet at Frankfurt, and both Austria and Prussia belonged to it only partly, since substantial portions of their territories lay outside its boundaries. The Confederation was designed to defend its members against external attack and internal revolution, not to build a nation. Within it, Prussia grew economically stronger, particularly after the creation of the Zollverein customs union in 1834, while Austria remained the leading dynastic and diplomatic power with a vast multinational empire to govern.

The revolutions of 1848 and 1849, examined in Chapter 98, were the great test of national movements. In Italy, revolutions in Milan, Venice, Rome, and elsewhere, and the war of Piedmont against Austria, ended in defeat. In Germany, the Frankfurt National Assembly drafted a constitution and offered the imperial crown to the king of Prussia, who refused it in 1849. Armies, not parliaments, restored the old order. The central lesson that many observers drew was that national unity could not be created by revolutionary declaration alone. Leaders on both sides of the Alps, whether liberals or conservatives, concluded that power, including armies, fiscal resources, and diplomacy, was the instrument that had been missing.

The international order that had contained these ambitions also weakened in the 1850s. The Crimean War of 1853 to 1856 broke the cooperation among the conservative eastern powers that had sustained the post-1815 system. Austria had antagonized Russia by remaining neutral without supporting its former benefactor, and Russia, the principal guarantor of conservative order in central Europe, withdrew inward to consider reform. France, under Napoleon III, sought to revise the settlement of 1815 and to enhance its prestige. Britain, preoccupied with empire and commerce, was reluctant to intervene on the continent. These shifts created the diplomatic room in which Cavour and Bismarck maneuvered.

Economic and social change provided the material framework. Railways, telegraphs, and expanding banks increased the speed of communication, mobilization, and commerce, as Chapters 93 to 95 have shown. Industrial growth in Prussia's Rhineland, Westphalia, and Silesia, the commercial agriculture of the Po Valley, and the growth of a literate urban public that read newspapers all contributed. But none of these developments automatically produced states. They altered the resources available to governments and the expectations of educated publics, and what governments did with those resources depended on political choices.

Students should also recognize the kinds of evidence at issue. Contemporary evidence includes treaties, ministerial correspondence, parliamentary debates, plebiscite returns, and the press. Official statistics, such as census figures and electoral rolls, are valuable but often incomplete, particularly for illiterate and rural populations whose political attitudes rarely appear in writing. Memoirs by Bismarck, Garibaldi, and others are essential but were composed with an eye to reputation. National historiography of the later nineteenth century, written in the service of new states, often presented unification as a teleological story, and twentieth-century scholarship has worked to separate that story from the evidence.

Division I · The Italian Question · Section 100.1

Italy after 1848: Fragmented States, Failed Revolutions, and the Survival of Nationalism

After 1849 the political geography of the Italian peninsula looked much as it had in 1815. The Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont, with its capital at Turin, was the only substantial Italian state ruled by an Italian dynasty, the House of Savoy, and the only one that retained a constitution. Lombardy and Venetia, the richest agricultural and commercial regions of the north, were governed directly by Austria, which maintained a large garrison and a system of fortresses known as the Quadrilateral. The duchies of Parma, Modena, and Tuscany were small states whose rulers relied on Austrian protection. The Papal States, ruled by Pope Pius IX in Rome, stretched from the Po Valley to the Roman countryside and were propped up after 1849 by French troops in Rome. In the south, the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, ruled by Ferdinand II and, from 1859, Francis II, was the largest Italian state by territory and population, with its capital at Naples and a Sicilian population whose relations with Naples had long been difficult.

The revolutions of 1848 and 1849 had produced several distinct political projects, and their failure did not discredit all of them equally. Giuseppe Mazzini, whose Young Italy movement had advocated a unified republic created by popular insurrection, had played a central role in the short-lived Roman Republic of 1849, defended by Garibaldi's volunteers until French troops took Rome in July. Moderate liberals, many of them Piedmontese, favored a constitutional monarchy under the House of Savoy, and thinkers such as Vincenzo Gioberti had earlier imagined a federation of Italian princes under the presidency of the pope. Federalists such as Carlo Cattaneo, by contrast, envisioned a decentralized union that would preserve regional autonomy. Others remained loyal to dynastic rule, believing that stability, not national unity, best served their regions and their own interests.

Defeat did not eliminate nationalism for several reasons. The military defeats of 1848 and 1849 were widely attributed to the shortcomings of individual leaders, to the divisions of the movement, and to the intervention of foreign powers rather than to the weakness of the national idea itself. The experience of insurrection had created a body of memories, martyrs, and symbols that sustained a patriotic culture. Political exiles, who settled in Turin, Paris, London, and elsewhere, kept national questions alive in newspapers, pamphlets, and correspondence. Patriotic associations, clandestine networks, reading societies, theater audiences, and opera, in which composers such as Verdi were sometimes read as vehicles for national feeling, extended a sense of common concern among an educated minority. Memory mattered too: the Roman Republic of 1849 became a powerful republican reference point even though it lasted only months.

It is important not to overstate the reach of this culture. At the time of unification the great majority of the population of the peninsula, perhaps three quarters, was illiterate, and many people spoke regional dialects as their everyday language, with standard Italian understood and used by only a small minority. The national movement was strongest among urban professionals, students, landowners, and military officers; peasants and many urban workers were more likely to be attached to local communities, religious practice, and the practical question of land and taxes. Political nationalism was thus a minority commitment whose significance depended on the actions of states and armies that adopted it.

Historical Vocabulary
Risorgimento

Risorgimento, literally "resurgence" or "rising again," is the name given to the long process, usually dated from the late eighteenth century or 1815 to 1861 or 1870, by which the Italian peninsula was transformed into a unified state. The term emerged in the cultural and political writing of the period and became the standard label in national historiography after unification. It carries a built-in interpretation, since "rising again" implies a pre-existing Italian nation that had been dormant and now revived.

Modern historians use the term with caution. The Risorgimento was not a single movement but a series of overlapping and sometimes competing projects, republican, monarchical, federalist, and Catholic liberal, whose supporters disagreed over the form, methods, and territory of the future state. It was also not a uniform popular awakening: its participants were drawn disproportionately from educated and propertied groups, and many Italians were indifferent or opposed. Nevertheless, the term remains useful as a label for a real historical process, as long as it does not imply that unification was a predestined result.

One of the persistent questions of the period, to which Chapter 97 and the historians of the Risorgimento have both contributed, is how political ideals relate to political outcomes. The Risorgimento as a cultural movement produced a language of national feeling, and as a political movement produced the failed revolutions of 1848. But the creation of the Kingdom of Italy depended on the actions of a state, Piedmont-Sardinia, that had not been the center of the revolutionary movement and on the diplomacy of a minister whose views differed sharply from Mazzini's.

Division I · The Italian Question · Section 100.2

Piedmont-Sardinia after 1849: Victor Emmanuel II and the Constitutional Alternative

On 23 March 1849, the Piedmontese army was defeated by the Austrian forces of Field Marshal Radetzky at Novara. King Charles Albert, who had granted the Statuto Albertino in March 1848 and had led his kingdom into war against Austria, abdicated in favor of his son Victor Emmanuel II. The new king signed an armistice with Austria and faced a difficult situation: a defeated army, a large indemnity, an inflamed parliament, and Austrian pressure to abandon constitutional government. Victor Emmanuel refused to repeal the constitution. Whatever his private views of parliamentary politics, which were by most accounts unenthusiastic, the Statuto remained in force, and this decision gave Piedmont a distinct identity among Italian states.

The Statuto Albertino was not a democratic document. It established a hereditary monarchy with an appointed Senate and an elected Chamber of Deputies, but the king retained significant powers, including command of the army, the conduct of foreign policy, and the appointment of ministers. The franchise was limited by property and tax qualifications to a small proportion of adult men, perhaps two percent of the population or less, so that the political nation remained a narrow elite. Nonetheless, a functioning parliament, a free press by the standards of the peninsula, and an independent judiciary offered liberals elsewhere in Italy a point of reference, and the Statuto later became the constitution of the Kingdom of Italy.

Under Massimo d'Azeglio, who served as prime minister from 1849 to 1852, the government pursued a program of moderate liberal reform. The Siccardi Laws of 1850 abolished the special ecclesiastical courts and the right of sanctuary in churches, reducing privileges the Church had enjoyed in Piedmont. These measures provoked conflict with the Papacy and with conservative Catholics, and the subsequent arrest of the Archbishop of Turin in 1850 intensified the confrontation. Church and state relations would remain an axis of Italian political conflict long after unification. The government also pursued commercial treaties, reorganization of the finances, and military reforms to rebuild the army after the defeat, work that helped establish the administrative capacity and credibility on which later action depended.

Piedmont's position also reflected its exposed circumstances. Its government had to repay an indemnity to Austria, maintain an army sufficient to deter attack, and carry on a diplomacy that could secure outside support without provoking another war it could not win. The kingdom was small, with a population of around five million, and its leaders were conscious that it could not defeat Austria alone. The question was whether a constitutional kingdom of this size could attract the support of Italian nationalists who had previously been republican or federalist.

Over the 1850s many did. The National Society, founded in 1857 by Giorgio Pallavicino, Daniele Manin, and Giuseppe La Farina, brought together former republicans and moderates around a program of unification under the House of Savoy, and Garibaldi joined it. This shift reflected disillusion with Mazzinian insurrection, which had repeatedly failed, and a pragmatic judgment that only an established state with an army could challenge Austria. It should not be read as a conversion of republicans to monarchism. Many accepted the arrangement as a tactical necessity, retaining their earlier convictions, and the tension between these visions would return after 1860.

Count Cavour: Economic Modernization, Parliamentary Leadership, and National Strategy

Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, was born in Turin in 1810 into a landowning aristocratic family. He did not fit the profile of a romantic nationalist. A landowner and agricultural improver who invested in new methods, fertilizers, and canals, he was also a businessman with interests in banking, steamships, and railways, and had traveled extensively in France and Britain, where he studied parliamentary government and commercial development. He first served in the Piedmontese government as minister of agriculture and commerce, and later of finance, in the d'Azeglio ministry, and in November 1852 he became prime minister, a post he held, with an interruption in 1859 to 1860, until his death in 1861.

His program in the 1850s centered on making Piedmont economically stronger and politically more credible. His government concluded trade agreements with Britain, France, Belgium, and other states that lowered tariffs and promoted exports. It supported railway construction, which linked Turin with Genoa and the Ticino frontier and expanded the kingdom's commercial and military mobility, and it supported banking and credit institutions, including the National Bank, which was the ancestor of the later Bank of Italy. It improved public finances and increased taxation, though at the price of growing public debt, and promoted agricultural development in Piedmont and Lombardy. Cavour believed that economic growth and administrative efficiency were necessary conditions for foreign policy ambitions, since a state without resources could not wage war or negotiate with confidence.

In parliamentary politics, Cavour built a majority through an alignment known as the connubio, or "marriage," of 1852, in which his center-right supporters allied with the center-left led by Urbano Rattazzi. The coalition isolated both the clerical right and the republican left and gave the government a stable parliamentary base. Critics at the time accused him of opportunism and of weakening party discipline, and historians have debated whether it represented a creative parliamentary practice or the beginnings of a habit of political management, later called transformism, that would shape Italian politics. In any case, it enabled him to pass reforms and to govern effectively, even in conflicts with the Church and the crown.

The national dimension of his thought developed gradually. In his early career Cavour was more concerned with Piedmont's reform and prestige than with unification of the peninsula, and he was skeptical of both Mazzini's conspiracies and the idea of an Italy created by popular insurrection. He regarded revolutionaries as a danger to ordered liberty and an obstacle to the support of foreign governments, whose cooperation he considered indispensable. By the later 1850s he had come to see that Piedmont could expand its territory only by challenging Austrian control of northern Italy, and that the national movement, domesticated within a monarchy, could supply the political energy for such a campaign. Historians who describe him as following a precise blueprint for the unification of all Italy overstate the evidence. His aims in 1858 and 1859 were the expansion of Piedmont into an Italian kingdom of the north, and the later extension to the south went well beyond what he had planned.

The link between domestic capacity and diplomacy was central. A state with a functioning parliament, a reformed army, a stable currency, and railway connections could attract credit, make itself useful to a great power, and credibly threaten a rival. Cavour's reputation among the liberal governments of Britain and France, which regarded Piedmont as a model of constitutional progress, was a diplomatic asset that more reactionary Italian states could not match. This is why the story of Italian unification begins in Turin's ministries and railway offices as much as on its battlefields.

The Crimean War, Napoleon III, and the Plombières Understanding of 1858

In January 1855 Cavour took the bold decision to join Britain and France in the Crimean War, sending an expeditionary force of about 18,000 men to the Crimea. The decision was controversial at home, since Piedmont had no obvious interest in the Black Sea, and the campaign cost lives to disease as well as combat, but it achieved its purpose. Piedmontese troops fought creditably at the Tchernaya in August 1855, and at the Congress of Paris in 1856 Cavour was able to raise the condition of Italy before the assembled powers, speaking critically of Austrian occupation and papal misgovernment. No concrete result followed, but the Italian question had become a matter of European diplomacy, and Cavour had established personal relationships with the British and French representatives.

Napoleon III was the decisive figure. The nephew of Napoleon I had himself been involved in Italian conspiracies as a young man, and his sympathy for Italian nationalism was probably sincere, but it coexisted with calculations of state. He wished to revise the settlement of 1815, to enhance French prestige, to weaken Austria, and to extend French influence in Italy. At the same time he had to consider French Catholic opinion, which strongly supported the papacy, and French troops had been in Rome since 1849 to protect the pope. He did not want a strong unitary Italian state on France's southeastern border that could become a rival. His preferred solution was an Italian confederation of states under the nominal presidency of the pope, with a strengthened northern kingdom allied to France.

The attempted assassination of Napoleon III on 14 January 1858 by the Italian revolutionary Felice Orsini, who threw bombs at the imperial carriage outside the Paris Opera, killing and wounding many bystanders, had complex effects. It alarmed the French government about Italian conspiracies and led to repressive measures, but Orsini's letters from prison appealing to the emperor to help Italy were published, and they seem to have strengthened Napoleon's sense that action on Italy was necessary to prevent further violence. Historians disagree about how much weight to give this episode, and it is better regarded as one factor among several than as the cause of the French decision.

In July 1858, Cavour met Napoleon III secretly at the spa town of Plombières in the Vosges. No formal treaty was signed on the spot, and the principal evidence is Cavour's own report and later correspondence, but the understanding as reconstructed by historians is clear in outline. France would support Piedmont in a war against Austria, provided Austria could be presented as the aggressor. The peninsula would then be reorganized into a Kingdom of Upper Italy under the House of Savoy, including Lombardy, Venetia, and the duchies, a central Italian kingdom, the Papal States reduced to the area around Rome, and the Kingdom of Naples; these states would form a confederation under the pope's presidency. In return, France would receive Savoy and probably Nice, and a marriage was arranged between Napoleon's cousin, Prince Napoleon, and Victor Emmanuel's daughter Clotilde, which took place in January 1859.

The arrangement shows why Italian unification depended on the European balance of power. French assistance was not unconditional, nor was it directed at a unified Italy. The plan at Plombières envisaged a reorganization of the peninsula that did not correspond to the single kingdom established in 1861, and what emerged later owed as much to events that exceeded the plan as to the plan itself. Cavour accepted the terms, including the cession of Savoy, the ancestral home of the royal dynasty, because French military power was indispensable. The relationship between the small power and the great one was one of mutual use, in which each hoped to benefit and neither controlled the other.

The War of 1859: Magenta, Solferino, and the Limits of French Assistance

In the early months of 1859 Piedmont mobilized and Cavour sought to provoke Austria into war while keeping the diplomatic initiative. Britain and Russia proposed a congress to defuse the crisis, and Napoleon III briefly wavered. Austria, believing it could end the Piedmontese threat decisively, sent an ultimatum on 23 April demanding that Piedmont disarm within three days. Cavour rejected it, and Austrian troops crossed the Ticino on 29 April. Austria had appeared as the aggressor, which was what the arrangement at Plombières required, and French forces entered Italy to support Piedmont. The war was fought under the personal command of Napoleon III and Victor Emmanuel II, with Garibaldi leading a volunteer corps, the Cacciatori delle Alpi, in the foothills of the Alps.

The principal battles show the character of mid-nineteenth-century warfare. At Magenta on 4 June, French and Piedmontese forces defeated the Austrians after a confused engagement in which both sides suffered heavy casualties and command was poorly coordinated. The Austrians withdrew from Milan, which the allied armies entered on 8 June. The decisive battle was fought on 24 June near Lake Garda at Solferino and San Martino, where armies totaling perhaps 270,000 men engaged along a front of many kilometers. The French defeated the Austrians at Solferino, and the Piedmontese held against Austrian forces at San Martino after heavy fighting. Both Napoleon III and the Austrian emperor Franz Joseph were present on the field, an occasion often described as the last on which two reigning monarchs personally directed opposing armies in a major European battle.

The human costs were large, and the medical services were inadequate. Casualties for the day were many thousands killed and wounded, and thousands of injured men lay for days in nearby towns, especially in Castiglione, with little organized care. The Swiss businessman Henry Dunant, who passed through the area, was so moved that he published A Memory of Solferino in 1862, proposing neutral volunteer relief societies. His proposal led to the founding of the International Committee of the Red Cross in 1863 and the Geneva Convention of 1864. Solferino thus stands at the origin of modern humanitarian law, a reminder that industrializing warfare, with its railways, rifled weapons, and mass armies, generated new problems that societies attempted to regulate.

Napoleon III's decision to end the war was abrupt. On 11 July 1859, without consulting Piedmont, he met Franz Joseph at Villafranca and agreed to an armistice. Austria would cede Lombardy, but not through direct transfer to Piedmont: it would hand it to France, which would pass it to Victor Emmanuel. Venetia, including the fortresses of the Quadrilateral, would remain Austrian, and the rulers of Tuscany, Modena, and Parma would be restored. An Italian confederation presided over by the pope was envisaged. The final settlement was formalized in the Treaty of Zurich in November 1859.

The reasons for Napoleon's decision were several. The casualties had shocked him, the Austrian fortresses in Venetia would require an expensive siege, and Prussia was mobilizing troops on the Rhine, raising the specter of a wider war. French Catholics were disturbed by the likelihood that the papal territories would be endangered, and the revolutionary tendencies of the Italian movement, especially in central Italy, were troubling to him. In addition, his own plans for an Italian confederation did not require the destruction of Austrian power in the peninsula. He had achieved his principal aims and could present himself as a pacifier.

For Cavour, the armistice was a disaster. He believed the whole purpose of the war, the expulsion of Austria from northern Italy, had been abandoned. After a bitter confrontation with Victor Emmanuel, who accepted Villafranca as unavoidable, Cavour resigned on 13 July. The outcome illustrates the dependence of the Italian movement on its French ally. The Piedmontese army had fought bravely but could not have prevailed alone, and the great power that had made victory possible also determined its limits. Yet the war had unleashed forces that neither Napoleon nor Cavour could easily contain, as the events in central Italy were soon to demonstrate.

Central Italian Annexations, Plebiscites, and the Transfer of Nice and Savoy

While the war was being fought, the rulers of Tuscany, Parma, and Modena fled their capitals as the Austrian military protection on which they relied collapsed. In Tuscany, the grand duke Leopold II left Florence in April 1859 and a provisional government was established that placed its military forces at the disposal of the Piedmontese war effort. In Parma and Modena similar provisional governments emerged, and in the Romagna and the northern provinces of the Papal States, known as the Legations, a revolt against papal government took place after Austrian troops withdrew. The political elites of these regions, composed largely of landowners, lawyers, and officials who were liberal and moderate, were not insurgents seeking social revolution but men who saw a chance to secure order, administrative modernization, and security from a return of reactionary rulers by union with Piedmont.

The Villafranca settlement, which would have restored the dukes, was thus resisted from below, and Piedmontese agents, notably the commissioners dispatched by the government and the National Society, encouraged that resistance. Napoleon III, who had no army with which to impose the restoration and was unwilling to authorize Austrian intervention, retreated. His ministers and the British government, led by Lord Palmerston and Lord John Russell, favored letting the Italians choose their own government. This combination of local initiative, diplomatic opportunity, and French reluctance to use force made the annexations possible.

Cavour returned to office in January 1860 and negotiated the diplomatic terms for the annexations. The price, agreed in the Treaty of Turin of 24 March 1860, was the cession to France of Savoy and Nice, which Napoleon III had been promised at Plombières in return for the Italian war, though the terms had not been fulfilled because Venetia had not been liberated. Cavour judged the sacrifice worth the acquisition of Tuscany, Emilia, and Romagna, whose population exceeded that of Savoy and Nice combined and whose territory made Piedmont a considerable state in the center of the peninsula.

The legitimacy of these changes was tested through plebiscites. In March 1860, voters in Tuscany and Emilia, which included Parma, Modena, and the Legations, were asked to choose between union with the constitutional monarchy of Victor Emmanuel and a separate kingdom. In April a vote was held in Nice and Savoy on union with France. The results were overwhelmingly in favor of annexation, often reported with more than ninety-five percent in favor. These plebiscites asserted the principle that territory should not be transferred without the consent of its inhabitants, a principle with a long future in European and global politics, but historians have emphasized their limitations. Voting was open in many places, so social pressure and administrative influence on voters could be strong. Women were excluded, the framing of the questions limited choices, and the outcomes were largely predetermined by the political conditions in which they occurred. They are best understood as instruments of legitimation more than as measurements of free popular opinion.

The transfer of Nice and Savoy provoked genuine controversy. Savoy was the ancestral homeland of the House of Savoy, and Nice, the birthplace of Garibaldi, whose family and local connections were there. Garibaldi, who had been elected deputy for Nice in the Piedmontese parliament, denounced the cession angrily in the Chamber and spoke of its injustice, and he never forgave Cavour. The episode also shows a gap between national claims and diplomatic reality. Although nationalists spoke of the right of peoples to choose their state, many borders were in fact determined by bargaining among governments, with plebiscites staged to ratify what had already been agreed. Both elements, popular claim and diplomatic bargain, were present, and neither alone explains the outcome.

Garibaldi and the Expedition of the Thousand: Sicily, Naples, and the Meeting at Teano

Giuseppe Garibaldi was born in Nice in 1807 and had spent years as a sailor and as a guerrilla leader in South America, fighting in Brazil and Uruguay, before returning to Italy in 1848. A republican by background and an admirer of Mazzini, he had defended the Roman Republic in 1849 and escaped after its fall. By the late 1850s he had made a pragmatic accommodation with Piedmont, joining the National Society and accepting the slogan "Italy and Victor Emmanuel," but he remained a revolutionary at heart, impatient with Cavour's caution and dismayed by the loss of Nice. He was also a charismatic figure whose reputation as a heroic fighter drew volunteers from across the peninsula and admirers from abroad, especially in Britain.

In April 1860 an insurrection began in Palermo, which was suppressed but sparked resistance in the Sicilian countryside. Garibaldi, urged on by Sicilian exiles and by republican associates such as Francesco Crispi, decided to lead an expedition. Cavour's role remains debated. The Piedmontese government did not authorize the venture but did not prevent it, and some arms and money were quietly provided by the National Society. Cavour was concerned that a failed expedition might embarrass Piedmont, but was also aware that stopping it might provoke the left. On the night of 5 to 6 May about a thousand volunteers, drawn heavily from the urban middle classes, including lawyers, students, and artisans, embarked at Quarto near Genoa on two steamships. They landed at Marsala in western Sicily on 11 May, protected, as it happened, by the presence of British warships.

The campaign was unexpectedly successful. At Calatafimi on 15 May, Garibaldi's force defeated a larger Bourbon army, and Sicilian volunteers, known as the picciotti, joined it in growing numbers. After an assault that captured Palermo at the end of May, Garibaldi proclaimed himself dictator of Sicily in the name of Victor Emmanuel and won another victory at Milazzo in July. In August he crossed to the mainland, and as the Bourbon administration collapsed, with the army unwilling or unable to resist, he entered Naples on 7 September 1860, as King Francis II withdrew to the fortress of Gaeta. The speed of the collapse owed much to Garibaldi's skill and daring, but also to the weaknesses of the Bourbon state, divided and demoralized, and to the willingness of many officers and officials to change sides or give way.

The southern populations did not participate uniformly. Urban liberals and professionals in Palermo and Naples tended to welcome liberation and national union. Landowners were divided, some seeing in the new regime a guarantee of order and others fearing social upheaval. Peasants were largely concerned with land, taxes, and the use of common lands, and many hoped that Garibaldi's arrival would bring redistribution. His decrees abolishing the hated grist tax and promising land to those who had fought for him raised expectations. When the expectations were not fulfilled, disillusion followed. At Bronte in eastern Sicily in August 1860, peasants who rose against landowners were put down by Garibaldi's lieutenant Nino Bixio, who ordered summary executions. The episode illustrates the conflict between revolutionary rhetoric and the preservation of property that characterized the new regime.

Multiple Perspectives

The Making of the South, 1860: Four Viewpoints

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified by historians. They are not quotations from any single named person, and individuals within each group held varied opinions.

Garibaldi and the volunteer movement: The expedition was a patriotic and revolutionary enterprise. Its leaders sought to liberate the south from Bourbon tyranny and bring it into a united Italy, using popular energy, volunteer enthusiasm, and speed. They accepted the authority of Victor Emmanuel as a symbol, but feared that a slow, diplomatic approach would delay liberation and leave Rome and Venice under foreign control. For many volunteers, unification was also a moral cause, and its social promises mattered.
Cavour and the Piedmontese government: The south offered an opportunity but also a threat. A revolutionary advance toward Rome might provoke French intervention and destroy the diplomatic framework that had secured the annexations in the center. A republican or radical regime in Naples would also undermine the moderate, monarchical settlement Cavour favored. His strategy was to bring the south under Piedmontese authority quickly, using the army and plebiscites to establish a constitutional kingdom before others could shape the outcome.
Southern peasants and landowners: Peasants hoped for relief from taxation, access to common lands, and land redistribution, and they judged the new authorities by whether those hopes were met. Landowners, many of whom had resented Bourbon policies, welcomed a government that would guarantee property and order, and some rapidly shifted loyalty to the new regime. The two groups thus experienced the same events as promise and threat in opposite combinations, which helps explain violence such as that at Bronte.
Defenders of Bourbon and papal authority: For loyal Bourbon officials and soldiers, and for many Catholics who regarded the pope's temporal power as essential to his spiritual independence, the invasion was a violation of legitimate government and of the Church. Pius IX's supporters, including volunteers from Catholic Europe, fought in the papal army, and for some southern clergy and rural communities, the new regime meant intrusion by a secular state. Their loyalty has often been dismissed in national histories, but it shaped resistance after 1860.

Cavour's fear of a revolutionary march on Rome, which French troops still protected, led to a bold decision. In September 1860, Piedmontese forces entered the Papal States, defeating the papal army at Castelfidardo on 18 September, and took Marche and Umbria, but not Rome and its surrounding province. Napoleon III, who was kept informed, did not intervene, and Cavour thus placed the Piedmontese army between Garibaldi and the papal capital. Garibaldi's volunteers defeated the Bourbon army at the Volturno on 1 October, and plebiscites held on 21 October in the south and in the Marche and Umbria returned large majorities for union, subject to the same limitations discussed earlier. On 26 October, near Teano, Garibaldi met Victor Emmanuel II and hailed him as king of Italy. Shortly afterward, Garibaldi laid down his dictatorship and retired to the island of Caprera, refusing a reward.

Teano should not be treated as a simple creation of national unity. It symbolized the transfer of political authority from a revolutionary leader to a monarchy and the absorption of the southern conquests into a Piedmontese framework. Garibaldi's volunteer army was dissolved or incorporated into the regular army, with many officers excluded, and the republican and democratic hopes that had accompanied the expedition were left unsatisfied. Whether southern unification was primarily a popular revolutionary achievement or a territorial incorporation directed by Piedmont is a question that historians have answered differently, and the evidence suggests that it was both: an improbable revolutionary success, made permanent by the intervention of the Piedmontese state.

Division II · The Making of the Kingdom of Italy · Section 100.8

The Kingdom of Italy, 1861–1871: Venetia, Rome, and the Problems of State Building

On 17 March 1861 the parliament in Turin, elected by a restricted electorate from the annexed territories, proclaimed Victor Emmanuel II king of Italy "by the grace of God and the will of the nation." He retained the title Victor Emmanuel II, as if the new kingdom were a continuation of Piedmont, a choice that expressed the dynastic nature of the process. The new state had a population of about twenty-two million, but it lacked Venetia, still Austrian, and Rome, still ruled by the pope under French protection. Cavour, who regarded Rome as the necessary capital, died on 6 June 1861 at fifty, leaving his program unfinished and his successors lacking his authority.

The task of integrating the peninsula was enormous. The government chose to extend the Piedmontese laws, administrative structure, and institutions to the whole kingdom, rather than adopt a federal or regional system, partly out of fear of centrifugal tendencies. Prefects appointed from Turin governed provinces, a uniform system of taxation and conscription was introduced, and the Piedmontese legal codes and tariff policy were applied. These measures were efficient from the standpoint of the central government but they overrode local traditions and institutions, imposed unfamiliar taxes, and removed young men from the countryside for military service, which was deeply unpopular, especially in the south. The franchise remained narrow, at about two percent of the population, which meant that most Italians had no direct voice in the state said to represent them.

The consequences were most severe in the south. Between 1861 and about 1865, large areas of the former Kingdom of the Two Sicilies experienced a violent conflict that the authorities described as brigandage. It involved armed bands, sometimes composed of former soldiers, peasants, and criminals, some of whom had links to Bourbon and papal supporters, and who attacked landowners, officials, and troops. The government responded with a massive military deployment, which involved a large proportion of the army, and with emergency legislation, notably the Pica Law of 1863, that authorized military tribunals and summary punishment. Casualty estimates are disputed, and historians disagree on whether to regard the conflict principally as organized political resistance, a social revolt, or a form of civil war, but it is clear that it expressed deep discontent with the new regime and that its repression left lasting bitterness.

Foreign policy remained central. The remaining objectives were Venetia and Rome, and Italian governments could not act without regard to Austria and France. Garibaldi's attempt in 1862 to march on Rome from Sicily was halted by Italian troops at Aspromonte, where he was wounded. In September 1864, Italy and France agreed to the September Convention, under which France would withdraw its troops from Rome within two years while Italy pledged to respect papal territory and moved its capital from Turin to Florence, which was done in 1865. Many in Italy interpreted the move as a step away from Rome, while others saw it as an acknowledgement that the capital issue could be postponed.

Venetia came through Italy's alliance with Prussia in 1866. In April, Bismarck and the Italian government concluded a secret treaty promising Italy Venetia if it joined a war against Austria. The Italian campaign was unimpressive: Italian forces were defeated at Custoza on 24 June and the Italian fleet was beaten by an Austrian squadron at Lissa in the Adriatic on 20 July. Yet Prussia's victory at Königgrätz, examined in section 100.13, gave Italy what its armies had not won. Austria ceded Venetia to Napoleon III, who passed it to Italy, a diplomatic maneuver that spared Austrian pride, and a plebiscite in October 1866 returned an overwhelming vote for union. The episode again showed Italian dependence on other powers, and many Italians resented being given Venetia as a gift, which weakened the sense of a national triumph.

Rome was taken in 1870 as a consequence of French defeat. In 1867 Garibaldi had again attempted to take Rome, but French and papal forces defeated his volunteers at Mentana in November. The Vatican Council of 1870 defined the dogma of papal infallibility, further hardening Catholic positions. When the Franco-Prussian War broke out in July 1870, Napoleon III withdrew his garrison, and after Sedan the Italian government decided to act. Its troops entered Rome through a breach in the walls at Porta Pia on 20 September 1870 after a brief resistance ordered by the pope. A plebiscite on 2 October registered an overwhelming vote for union with the kingdom, and the transfer of the capital to Rome was completed in 1871. The Law of Guarantees, passed in May 1871, granted the pope sovereign honors, the Vatican and Lateran palaces, and an annual income, but Pius IX rejected it, refused to recognize the kingdom, and considered himself a prisoner in the Vatican. Catholics were later discouraged from taking part in national elections, and the Roman Question remained unresolved until the Lateran Treaties of 1929. Rome's Jewish community, confined to a ghetto under papal rule, was emancipated after 1870, a reminder that the new state's liberal legislation had consequences for religious minorities.

Division III · The German Question · Section 100.9

Germany after 1848: The German Confederation, the Zollverein, and Competing Visions of Unity

The Frankfurt National Assembly, elected in 1848 to write a constitution for a united Germany, produced a document in March 1849 that proposed a federal empire with an hereditary emperor, a parliament elected on a broad male franchise, and a guarantee of basic rights. When a delegation offered the imperial crown to King Frederick William IV of Prussia in April 1849, he refused, reportedly scorning a crown offered by a parliament as a gift from the gutter that did not come from the princes. The Assembly dissolved under military pressure, and uprisings in Saxony, the Palatinate, and Baden were crushed, in many cases by Prussian troops. Prussia then attempted to create a smaller union of German states, the Erfurt Union, but under Austrian pressure and Russian support for Austria, the king abandoned it in the Punctation of Olmütz in November 1850, and the German Confederation was restored.

The Confederation was a league of sovereign states, not a federal state. Its Federal Diet at Frankfurt, presided over by Austria, consisted of delegates instructed by their governments, and it could act only through the consensus of its members. The rivalry between Austria and Prussia dominated its politics. Austria sought to preserve its leading position and its multinational empire, while Prussia, a mixed state with Polish, Catholic, and Protestant populations, sought equality or leadership. The middle states, notably Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg, and Hanover, sought to preserve their independence and played the two great powers against each other, a position known as the "Third Germany." The persistence of dynastic loyalties and local identities among the thirty-odd member states is an essential part of the story, since German unification meant reducing the autonomy of many established governments.

Historical Vocabulary
Großdeutsch and Kleindeutsch: German Unification Alternatives

Großdeutsch ("greater German") solutions would have united the German lands including the German-speaking territories of the Habsburg monarchy under some form of Austrian leadership or shared leadership. Kleindeutsch ("smaller German") solutions would have excluded Austria and created a state led by Prussia from the other German states. The terms emerged in the debates of 1848 and 1849 and remained central until 1866.

The Großdeutsch idea faced a fundamental problem: the Habsburg monarchy included large non-German populations, including Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Italians, and South Slavs, and Austria could not subordinate its whole empire to a German state without destroying its own cohesion. A Kleindeutsch solution avoided that problem but meant dividing the German-speaking peoples between two states. The outcome of 1866 and 1871 was therefore not the realization of a single national program, but the victory of one alternative through war, a victory that left millions of German speakers in Austria outside the new Empire.

Economic integration complicated the picture. The Zollverein, founded in 1834 under Prussian leadership, eliminated internal customs among most member states and created a common external tariff. By the 1850s it included most German states except Austria, Hamburg, and Bremen. It stimulated trade and encouraged railway building, and in doing so gave Prussia economic leadership in a Germany that excluded Austria. Austria's attempts to join or to create a rival customs union were unsuccessful. Prussia's industrial base in the Rhineland, Westphalia, and Silesia, with its coal, iron, and engineering, grew rapidly, as discussed in Chapter 94. When Prussia concluded a trade treaty with France in 1862, which Austria and several German states opposed because it embodied free trade principles, the Zollverein states largely accepted it, confirming Prussian economic influence.

But economic integration did not mean political unification. Merchants and manufacturers in Bavaria, Württemberg, and Saxony valued the common market, but that did not necessarily make them supporters of Prussian political dominance. Catholic populations in southern Germany and the Rhineland were wary of a Protestant-led Prussia, and the monarchs of the middle states had their own interests. The liberal nationalist movement continued to exist, finding expression in the Nationalverein, founded in 1859 and drawing on professionals, businessmen, and civil servants, which supported a Kleindeutsch solution under Prussian leadership with a liberal constitution. It was a significant sign of national feeling in the bourgeois public sphere, as were gymnastic, choral, and shooting festivals that brought together participants from different states. Yet the Nationalverein lacked the means to impose its program, and its leaders hoped that Prussia would adopt liberalism, a hope that Bismarck would disappoint. The failure of 1848, in short, left both a national idea and an unanswered question of power.

Prussia's Monarchy, Army Reforms, and the Constitutional Conflict

Prussia in 1861 was a constitutional monarchy of a limited kind. The constitution of 1850, imposed by the crown after the revolutions, established a bicameral parliament, with an upper house, the House of Lords, dominated by the nobility, and a lower House of Representatives elected by a three-class franchise that weighted votes according to tax payments, so that a small group of wealthy taxpayers chose one third of the electors, and the majority of poorer voters another third. Voting was public and indirect. The king retained command of the army, control of foreign policy, and the power to appoint and dismiss ministers, who were responsible to him, not to parliament. The parliament had a right to approve budgets, but the constitution was ambiguous on what should happen if the legislature and the crown disagreed, an ambiguity that mattered greatly after 1862.

William I, who succeeded his brother in January 1861 after serving as regent since 1858, was a conservative soldier who considered the army the foundation of the monarchy. He believed that the Prussian military system, which had performed poorly in the mobilization of 1859, needed reform. Albrecht von Roon, appointed war minister in 1859, and the army leadership proposed to expand the standing army by increasing annual recruitment, to extend the term of active service to three years, and to reduce the role of the Landwehr, the reserve militia that was associated with the liberal tradition of the citizen-soldier and the wars of liberation of 1813. These changes would make the army larger and more reliable as an instrument of the crown, and more expensive, and they would shift the military establishment away from a force connected to civil society.

At the same time, the army's operational capacities were being transformed. Helmuth von Moltke, chief of the general staff from 1857, developed a system of planning, rapid mobilization, and coordinated movement using railways and the telegraph. He emphasized decentralized command, in which subordinate commanders were given objectives and the freedom to achieve them, and the training of staff officers in a common doctrine. The Prussian infantry was equipped with the Dreyse needle gun, a breech-loading rifle that could be loaded and fired more rapidly, from a prone position, than muzzle-loading rifles. The technology and doctrine did not guarantee victory, but they gave Prussia significant advantages in speed of mobilization and concentration that would be demonstrated in 1866 and 1870.

The liberal majority in the House of Representatives, led by the Progressive Party founded in 1861, supported national unification and a strong army, but objected to the expansion on constitutional grounds. They preferred a two-year term of service and a continued role for the Landwehr, and they insisted on parliament's right to control the budget. The conflict intensified in 1860 to 1862, as the House refused to approve military funds, and the king considered abdication. The dispute was thus not just about army organization but about whether Prussia would develop toward parliamentary government or remain a monarchy in which the crown controlled the military and the executive. The elections of 1861 and 1862 returned increasingly strong liberal majorities, showing that the Prussian electorate, even under the three-class system, was willing to oppose the crown.

In September 1862, William, confronted by a deadlocked parliament, summoned Otto von Bismarck, whom Roon had recommended. The constitutional conflict made the unification of Germany a question of political authority in Prussia as well as relations among states. The king and his advisers were determined to preserve the monarchy's control of the army, and the liberal majority was equally determined to establish the principle of parliamentary budget authority. The resolution that followed, in which the government governed without a legally approved budget for four years, and later sought indemnity from parliament after the military victory of 1866, shows how external success could resolve a domestic conflict.

Otto von Bismarck: State Power, Diplomacy, and the Politics of Unification

Otto von Bismarck was born in 1815 into a family of Junker landowners in Brandenburg. He studied law, entered the civil service briefly, and managed family estates before entering politics as an ultraconservative defender of the monarchy and the landed aristocracy during the revolution of 1848, when he opposed the liberal movement. His diplomatic career began in 1851 when he was appointed Prussian envoy to the Federal Diet in Frankfurt, where he experienced at first hand Austrian efforts to dominate the Confederation and came to regard Austria as an obstacle to Prussian interests. He later served as ambassador to Russia, from 1859, and briefly to France, in 1862, where he gained knowledge of the personalities and calculations of the powers whose actions would affect Prussia.

When he became minister-president on 23 September 1862, he was widely distrusted by liberals and mistrusted by many conservatives. A week later, speaking to the budget committee of the House of Representatives, he proposed to the deputies that the great questions of the time would not be decided by speeches and majority votes, as had been the error of 1848 and 1849, but by iron and blood. The remark, popularly rendered "blood and iron," was received with horror by liberals and has often been taken as a program of militarism. In context, it was an argument within the budget dispute, aimed at asserting that Prussia must strengthen its power, and that the crown's military policy could not be dictated by a parliamentary majority. The edited versions and recollections of the speech differ, and students should consult the documentary record rather than the slogan.

Bismarck's approach to German unification was distinctive. He regarded the national question as a matter of state interest, whereas many nationalists regarded Prussia as a means to achieve the nation. He sought to enhance Prussian power, and he was prepared to use nationalism where it served that purpose, as, for example, in his decision to introduce universal male suffrage for the North German Confederation, which he believed would mobilize conservative rural voters against the liberal middle classes. He also showed great flexibility: he cooperated with Austria in 1864 and fought it in 1866, he courted liberals after 1866, and he combined threats with offers of compromise. The concept of Realpolitik, a term originally coined by the liberal writer Ludwig von Rochau in 1853 to describe politics grounded in the realities of power, is often applied to his methods.

Historians caution against reading the process as a master plan. Bismarck himself, in his memoirs written after his dismissal, later presented his policy as consistent and farsighted, but contemporary evidence suggests that he improvised, seized opportunities, kept several options open, and was at times uncertain of the outcome. He compared the statesman to a person who walks through a forest and must wait for the moment to grasp the passing hem of God's cloak, an image that conveys the importance of timing and contingency. The wars of 1864, 1866, and 1870 had different causes and outcomes, and it is a retrospective simplification to treat them as stages of one program. What Bismarck did possess was a clear sense of Prussian interests, tactical dexterity, and the ability to use the tools of the state, including the army, the diplomatic service, and the press.

The relationship with liberals was complex. Many liberals opposed him in 1862 and the following years because he governed without a lawful budget and censored the press, and the Progressive Party remained in opposition. Yet as national success came, a growing number faced a choice between constitutional principle and national unity. After the victory of 1866, the Prussian parliament passed an indemnity bill retroactively approving the unauthorized expenditure, and the National Liberal Party emerged, supporting Bismarck's national policy while seeking liberal constitutional developments. This division within German liberalism, between those who gave priority to national unification under Prussian leadership and those who insisted on constitutional rights first, affected German politics for decades. It also illustrates a central theme of this chapter: the unification of Germany was achieved by the state and conservative leaders, whose methods many liberal nationalists rejected, even while they benefited from the result.

The Schleswig-Holstein Question and the Danish War of 1864

The Schleswig-Holstein question was so intricate that it has been said that only three people understood it, one of whom died, one of whom went mad, and one of whom forgot it, a quip generally attributed to the British statesman Lord Palmerston. The duchies of Schleswig and Holstein were linked to the Danish crown through the king of Denmark, who ruled them as duke. Holstein, with a German-speaking population, was a member of the German Confederation. Schleswig, with a mixed Danish and German population, was not, and was claimed by Danish nationalists as part of the Danish kingdom and by German nationalists as inseparably linked to Holstein. Both duchies were governed under distinctive legal arrangements, which included the principle, claimed by German nationalists, that the two were "forever undivided." The duchies also had a succession law different from that of the Danish kingdom, which created a potential dynastic crisis when the Danish royal line approached extinction.

After the revolutions of 1848 produced a first war over the duchies, the great powers settled the matter in the London Protocol of 1852, which recognized Prince Christian of Glücksburg as heir to the entire Danish monarchy, including the duchies, with the promise that Denmark would not integrate Schleswig more closely into the Danish state than Holstein. The crisis erupted in November 1863, when Christian IX succeeded, and the Danish parliament approved a constitution that bound Schleswig more closely to Denmark. German nationalists denounced this as a violation of the 1852 settlement, and the Federal Diet took action in Holstein. A rival claimant, Frederick of Augustenburg, was supported by many German liberals who hoped to create a new, independent state of Schleswig-Holstein within Germany.

Bismarck chose to act in cooperation with Austria rather than through the Confederation. Using the argument that Denmark had violated the London Protocol, he and the Austrian government delivered an ultimatum, and in early 1864 Austrian and Prussian forces invaded Schleswig. The Danish army made a stand at the fortifications of Dybbøl, which fell to a Prussian assault on 18 April 1864 after a siege in which Prussian artillery played a decisive part. The war was brief. After an armistice and a failed conference in London, hostilities resumed, and Denmark was forced to accept the Peace of Vienna of 30 October 1864, in which the king renounced Schleswig, Holstein, and Lauenburg to Austria and Prussia jointly. The loss deeply shaped Danish national memory and contributed to the smaller, more homogeneous Danish state that would later emerge. The war also showed the great powers' reluctance to intervene: Britain was unwilling to act alone, France did not support Denmark, and Russia remained aloof.

The outcome created a problem of joint rule. Bismarck, who did not support the Augustenburg claim, wanted to annex the duchies to Prussia, but Austria was reluctant to see Prussia grow. The Convention of Gastein, signed in August 1865, temporarily divided administration: Prussia would govern Schleswig and Austria Holstein, while Lauenburg was sold to Prussia, and the arrangement was recognized as a pragmatic postponement. It also meant that Holstein, a German Confederation territory, was administered by Austria in a territory surrounded by Prussian-controlled land, a situation guaranteed to provoke friction.

For Bismarck, the Danish war and its aftermath offered opportunities. It enabled him to enhance Prussian military prestige and popularity, to demonstrate that Prussia could act for German national interests, and to place Austria in a position where its administration of Holstein could be challenged. It also showed that the great powers would not necessarily prevent changes to the settlement of 1815 by force. A conflict that began in a dispute over the status of two duchies and the rights of a Danish king thus became the occasion for the greater contest between Austria and Prussia over leadership in Germany. It is a good example of how a regional conflict involving languages, dynasties, and legal claims could be turned to advantage by a state seeking to change the balance of power, and of how nationalist claims could be used by governments whose motives were not simply national.

The Austro-Prussian War of 1866: Königgrätz and the Exclusion of Austria

Relations between Austria and Prussia deteriorated through 1865 and early 1866 as Bismarck used the administration of the duchies to press Austria. Prussia protested Austrian toleration of agitation for Augustenburg in Holstein, and in the Federal Diet Prussia proposed a reform of the Confederation that would have excluded Austria and included a national parliament elected by universal male suffrage, a proposal intended to appeal to German nationalists and to isolate Vienna. Bismarck's tactical use of a democratic franchise for a conservative state's purposes startled contemporaries, and it showed his willingness to use the language of national reform against Austria.

His diplomatic preparation was careful. In October 1865 he met Napoleon III at Biarritz, and though no formal agreement resulted, he gained the impression that France would remain neutral, probably hoping for compensation on the Rhine, and Bismarck encouraged that hope without commitment. Russia, grateful for Prussian cooperation against the Polish insurgents in 1863 and resentful of Austria since the Crimean War, was disposed toward benevolent neutrality. Italy, eager for Venetia, signed a treaty of alliance with Prussia on 8 April 1866. Britain, preoccupied elsewhere, did not intervene. Austria, in contrast, found itself isolated from the major powers, supported mainly by the middle German states of Bavaria, Saxony, Hanover, Württemberg, and Hesse, which feared Prussian expansion.

War came in June 1866. After Prussian troops entered Holstein, Austria asked the Federal Diet on 11 June to mobilize against Prussia, and the motion was carried on 14 June. Prussia declared the Confederation dissolved and invaded Hanover, Saxony, and Hesse-Kassel. The Prussian army, deployed by railways in three main armies under Moltke's coordination, advanced into Bohemia and converged on the Austrian army under Ludwig von Benedek. The decisive battle took place on 3 July 1866 near the Bohemian fortress town of Königgrätz, known in some sources as Sadowa. Roughly 200,000 Austrians and Saxons faced about the same number of Prussians. The battle was hard-fought and its outcome uncertain for some hours, until the arrival of the Second Army on the Austrian flank turned the engagement into a decisive victory. Austrian losses, including killed, wounded, and prisoners, were far heavier than Prussian, and the Austrian army was forced to retreat. Technological factors, notably the needle gun, were important, but so were the speed of the Prussian concentration, the staff organization, and Austrian command weaknesses. In the south, Prussian forces also defeated Bavarian and other forces in smaller engagements.

Bismarck's most significant act was in the peace settlement. King William and the generals wanted to march on Vienna and to impose territorial penalties on Austria, but Bismarck insisted on moderation, partly to avoid French intervention and partly because he wanted to leave open the possibility of future cooperation with Austria. The preliminary armistice at Nikolsburg on 26 July and the Treaty of Prague of 23 August 1866 dissolved the German Confederation, excluded Austria from German affairs, and agreed to Prussia's reorganization of Germany north of the River Main. Austria lost no territory to Prussia, but paid a modest indemnity and ceded Venetia, via Napoleon III, to Italy. Prussia annexed Schleswig and Holstein, the Kingdom of Hanover, the Electorate of Hesse-Kassel, the Duchy of Nassau, and the free city of Frankfurt, thereby gaining about four million inhabitants and joining its eastern and western provinces into a contiguous territory. Saxony, though a defeated enemy, was left intact but joined the new Prussian-led confederation. The southern states of Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, and the southern part of Hesse-Darmstadt remained independent, but signed secret military alliances with Prussia.

The North German Confederation of 1867: Federalism, Suffrage, and Prussian Dominance

The North German Confederation, created in 1866 and 1867 by treaties among Prussia and the other north German states, combined about twenty-two states under Prussian leadership. Its constitution, drafted largely by Bismarck and approved by a constituent parliament elected by universal male suffrage, came into effect on 1 July 1867. It was a federal state with a single military command, a common foreign policy, and a unified customs and commercial system. The king of Prussia held the hereditary presidency of the Confederation, and thus commanded its armed forces and conducted its foreign affairs. The Prussian army, with its structure, was extended to the other member states.

The institutions combined elements of federalism, representative government, and monarchical authority. The Bundesrat, or Federal Council, consisted of delegates instructed by the governments of the member states and had legislative functions, with Prussia holding a large share of votes. The Reichstag was elected by direct, secret, and equal suffrage for men, which was among the most democratic franchises in Europe at the time. The federal chancellor, a post created for Bismarck, was appointed by the president and was responsible to him, not to the Reichstag. The Reichstag could pass legislation and share control of the budget, but it could neither appoint nor dismiss the chancellor. Military expenditures were fixed for a period, limiting parliament's control of the army, a principle that resolved the Prussian constitutional conflict in the crown's favor.

The suffrage was broad but qualified. The electoral law, of 1869 in its final form, enfranchised men aged twenty-five and over, excluded women and men under twenty-five, and excluded certain categories such as those receiving poor relief and soldiers on active service. It is therefore more accurate to speak of broad male suffrage than of universal suffrage. Even so, its introduction on the national level was striking in a state that kept the three-class franchise in Prussia itself. Bismarck's motives were probably several: he believed that rural and conservative voters would support the monarchy, he wished to appeal to the national movement, and he calculated that a democratic franchise would undermine the liberal middle-class opposition. The result was a notable combination of mass electoral politics with a government not dependent on parliamentary confidence.

The existing states retained substantial autonomy. They kept their dynasties, their administrations, their courts, and responsibility for matters such as education and local government. The Confederation's powers expanded gradually, notably in commercial law, economic regulation, and the legal system, and the liberals, particularly the National Liberals, supported the creation of common institutions. In the Reichstag they cooperated with Bismarck on measures such as a common commercial code, uniform weights and measures, and freedom of movement and occupation, which broke down obstacles to the national market. Their support for national unification and economic liberalization coexisted with their disappointment over the limits of parliamentary power. A federal law of July 1869 also removed legal disabilities based on religious confession, including those affecting Jews, so that the Confederation ended legal discrimination on grounds of religion in civil and political rights, a step later extended to the Empire.

The southern states posed the major question. Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, and southern Hesse were joined to the north through the Zollverein, which was reorganized after 1867 with a customs parliament, and through secret military agreements. But the elections to the customs parliament in 1868 showed that many southern voters, especially Catholics, particularists, and democrats, were unenthusiastic about Prussian leadership. Bismarck, aware of this, did not press the southern states to join immediately, preferring to wait for circumstances. The Luxembourg crisis of 1867, when French attempts to purchase the Grand Duchy from the king of the Netherlands provoked a nationalist outcry in Germany, demonstrated that foreign pressure could stir German feeling. The question was how to bring the south into a federation whose structures had been designed by and for Prussia.

The North German Confederation thus reconciled national representation with strong monarchical executive authority by distinguishing between participation and control. The Reichstag represented the nation and shaped legislation, but the executive remained in the hands of the Prussian crown, army, and chancellor. This arrangement was later carried into the Empire of 1871, as examined in the following section.

The Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871: The Spanish Candidacy, Ems Dispatch, and Military Victory

After 1866, relations between France and Prussia were tense. Napoleon III had expected the Austro-Prussian war to be long and to give France the role of arbiter, and had been disappointed by Prussia's quick victory and by the lack of compensation he sought on the Rhine. French public opinion, aroused by the phrase "revenge for Sadowa," saw Prussian power as a threat, while the emperor, whose regime faced growing domestic opposition and who was in poor health, felt under pressure to reassert French prestige. The failed attempt to obtain Luxembourg in 1867 further embittered relations. In Germany, the success of 1866 and the growth of national sentiment encouraged the view that France was an obstacle to completing unification.

The immediate occasion was the vacant Spanish throne. After a revolution in 1868 deposed Queen Isabella II, the Spanish government, led by General Prim, sought a new monarch and in 1870 offered the crown to Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, a Catholic member of a south German branch of the Prussian royal house. France regarded the prospect of a Hohenzollern on the Spanish throne as an intolerable encirclement. Bismarck supported the candidacy, probably for diplomatic advantage, and he and the Prussian general staff may have hoped that it would provoke a crisis in which France could be placed in the wrong, though historians disagree about how far he intended war. When the candidacy was announced in early July, the French government, led by the foreign minister the Duke of Gramont, responded with strong threats. On 12 July, Leopold's father withdrew his son's candidacy, a diplomatic victory for France that might have ended the crisis.

French leaders, however, pressed further. On 13 July, Count Vincent Benedetti, the French ambassador, approached King William, who was taking the waters at Bad Ems, and asked him to guarantee that the candidacy would never be revived. William refused, politely but firmly, and subsequently declined to receive the ambassador again on the matter. The king's aide, Heinrich Abeken, sent a telegram reporting the incident to Bismarck in Berlin, who was dining with Roon and Moltke. Bismarck shortened the text and released it to the press and to Prussian embassies, in a form that suggested a more abrupt exchange. The edited version was widely read in Germany as a humiliation of the king and in France as an insult to the ambassador.

The southern German states, rather than staying neutral as the French had hoped, honored their alliances and mobilized alongside the North German Confederation, aided by the perception that France was the aggressor. The German armies, directed by Moltke, assembled rapidly by rail, numbering over a million men with reserves, while the French, whose mobilization was slower and less organized, advanced with smaller forces and lacked adequate supplies and planning. The French rifle, the chassepot, was in many respects superior to the Prussian needle gun, but German artillery, organization, and operational coordination proved decisive.

In August, German armies won a series of battles in Alsace and Lorraine, including Wörth, Spicheren, and Gravelotte-St. Privat, and one French army under Marshal Bazaine was besieged in the fortress of Metz. The other, under Marshal MacMahon, with Napoleon III accompanying it, was surrounded at Sedan, where on 1 and 2 September more than 100,000 French soldiers were captured, together with the emperor. When news reached Paris, the Second Empire collapsed, and on 4 September a republic was proclaimed and a Government of National Defense formed. The war then entered a second phase. The new government, led by Léon Gambetta, who left Paris by balloon, raised new armies in the provinces, but they were poorly trained and equipped. Metz capitulated in October, and German forces besieged Paris from 19 September. The siege, which lasted until an armistice on 28 January 1871, caused severe hardship: food and fuel were scarce, the population ate horses, dogs, and zoo animals, disease spread, and German artillery bombarded the city in January. Irregular French fighters, the franc-tireurs, harassed German forces, and the German army responded harshly with reprisals against villages and civilians, a pattern that left bitter memories.

The war accelerated the incorporation of the southern states. German victories, the sense of shared struggle, and the mobilization of national sentiment created strong pressure for union, and in November 1870 treaties were signed by Baden and Hesse and then by Bavaria and Württemberg, which negotiated special rights concerning their armies, post, and railways. The conflict that had begun as a diplomatic confrontation over a candidacy had thus become the final catalyst for German unification. Whether Bismarck had planned it that way, or whether events carried him beyond his expectations, remains a matter of interpretation, but the result was unmistakable.

The German Empire of 1871: Versailles, Constitutional Authority, and the New European Power

The negotiations in Versailles in late 1870 were delicate. The southern monarchs had to be persuaded to accept an imperial title for the king of Prussia without a sense that they were surrendering their sovereignty. Bismarck offered concessions, including reserved rights for Bavaria and Württemberg, and, according to the evidence, used a financial subsidy from the Prussian treasury to persuade King Ludwig II of Bavaria to write a letter proposing that William assume the imperial title, a letter that Bismarck drafted. William himself was reluctant: he cherished his Prussian crown and was uneasy about a title that seemed to depend on the offers of princes and on liberal or revolutionary associations. The compromise was that he would be "German Emperor," not "Emperor of Germany," a distinction that preserved the sovereignty of the other princes.

The proclamation on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors was a carefully staged assertion of dynastic and military power. It was the anniversary of the coronation of the first king of Prussia in 1701. The ceremony was attended by princes, generals, and delegations from regiments, and the proclamation was made by William I, with Bismarck reading the text. The Reichstag of the North German Confederation had adopted resolutions asking William to accept the crown, but representatives of the people were not central to the event, and that absence was noted by liberals. The ceremony should be understood as symbolic, creating a new political identity, not as a legal act. The constitutional establishment of the Empire required the treaties with the southern states, the revision of the Confederation's constitution, and its approval by the Reichstag, steps completed in the spring. The new constitution took effect on 4 May 1871 after passage by the Reichstag in April.

The Empire was a federation of twenty-five states: twenty-two monarchies and three free cities, plus the imperial territory of Alsace-Lorraine, which was directly administered. The emperor was the king of Prussia, who commanded the armed forces and appointed the chancellor, who was also usually the Prussian minister-president. The Bundesrat represented the governments of the states, with Prussia holding 17 of 58 votes, enough to block constitutional amendments. The Reichstag was elected by the same broad male suffrage as in the North German Confederation. The Bavarian and Württemberg armies retained a degree of autonomy in peacetime, but were placed under imperial command in war. The Empire's structure thus combined federal and monarchical elements, with Prussia dominant in size, population, and military power.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the North German Confederation (1867) to the German Empire (1871)

What Changed

  • The territory expanded south of the Main to include Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, and southern Hesse, adding Catholic and particularist populations
  • The presidency of the Confederation became the hereditary imperial title of the Prussian king, with new symbolic weight
  • Alsace-Lorraine, acquired by conquest, entered the Empire as a territory without the status of a state
  • The southern monarchies negotiated special rights over their armies, post, railways, and some taxes

What Stayed the Same

  • The constitutional framework of Bundesrat, Reichstag, and chancellor, which was carried over with modest changes
  • Broad male suffrage, with a chancellor responsible to the monarch rather than to parliament
  • Prussian dominance in votes, army, and administration, and Bismarck's role as chancellor
  • The retained sovereignty and internal administration of the member states

Who experienced change most directly? Southern Germans, who now belonged to a larger state, and Alsatians and Lorrainers, who were transferred without a plebiscite. Who experienced more continuity? Northerners already living in the Confederation, and the Prussian officials and officers whose institutions expanded. Why the pattern matters: The Empire was less a new creation than an extension of the structures of 1867, which explains both its efficiency and the limits on its parliamentary development.

The peace with France was concluded in the Treaty of Frankfurt on 10 May 1871, after an armistice in January and a preliminary peace in February, while the Paris Commune was in revolt against the French government. France ceded Alsace and part of Lorraine, including the fortress city of Metz, and paid an indemnity of five billion francs, with German troops remaining in parts of France until payment, which was completed by 1873. The annexation of territory, urged by the German military and supported by nationalist opinion, was contrary to the wishes of the inhabitants, many of whom protested, and many chose to leave for France in the following years. Bismarck was reportedly less enthusiastic about the annexations than the generals, but accepted them. It was a decision that deeply embittered French opinion.

What kind of state emerged in 1871? It was a powerful national state, with a rapidly growing industrial economy, the most effective army on the continent, a large population, and a central position in Europe. It was also a federation with monarchical executive authority and an elected parliament that could not control the chancellor. It faced tensions between national unity and the particularism of its states, between Protestant and Catholic populations, and between the aristocratic, military traditions of Prussia and the liberal, commercial interests of the middle class. Within a few years the Kulturkampf, a conflict between the Reich and the Catholic Church, would test those tensions. The Empire's creation transformed the European balance of power, as section 100.20 explains.

The Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 and the Creation of the Dual Monarchy

The Habsburg monarchy had been under strain for a decade before 1866. It had lost Lombardy in 1859, suffered financial difficulties, and tried a series of constitutional experiments, the October Diploma of 1860 and the February Patent of 1861, that failed to satisfy either centralizers or the national and historic communities of the empire. Hungary, in particular, had never accepted the abolition of its constitution after the revolution of 1848 and 1849, and a passive resistance led by moderate leaders such as Ferenc Deák had kept the question open. Defeat at Königgrätz and the loss of Venetia exposed the weakness of the existing system. Emperor Franz Joseph, who had ruled since 1848, concluded that the monarchy could survive as a great power only if it settled its relationship with Hungary.

The negotiations involved Franz Joseph and his ministers, notably Friedrich Ferdinand von Beust, the Saxon statesman who became Austrian foreign minister and chancellor in 1866 and 1867, and Hungarian leaders led by Deák and Count Gyula Andrássy. The outcome, the Compromise of 1867, known by its German name as the Ausgleich, was enacted by the Hungarian parliament in the spring and was completed by the coronation of Franz Joseph as king of Hungary in Buda on 8 June 1867, with Andrássy as the first Hungarian prime minister of the restored constitution. The Habsburg monarchy became Austria-Hungary, officially the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy.

Historical Vocabulary
Ausgleich and the Dual Monarchy

Ausgleich, German for "settlement" or "compromise," refers to the agreement of 1867 between the Habsburg crown and the Hungarian political leadership that created the Dual Monarchy, a state with two largely self-governing halves joined by a common ruler and certain common institutions. The Hungarian term is kiegyezés. The Austrian half is often called Cisleithania, from the River Leitha that approximated part of the boundary, and the Hungarian half Transleithania, though these names were not official designations, and the Austrian half was formally known as the kingdoms and lands represented in the Reichsrat.

The term "dual" can mislead. Austria-Hungary was neither a unitary state nor a simple personal union of two independent kingdoms, since it had common ministries and a shared army. It also should not be equated with an alliance of two nations, since neither half was ethnically uniform, and most of the monarchy's population belonged to neither of the two dominant groups.

The structure was complex. The monarch, Emperor of Austria and King of Hungary, remained the central figure and retained authority over foreign policy and the army. There were three common ministries, for foreign affairs, war, and the finances required for those functions, responsible to the monarch, and common affairs were overseen by the Delegations, committees of sixty members each drawn from the two parliaments. Other matters, including internal administration, justice, education, and most economic policy, were separate. A customs and trade union and the proportion of common expenses paid by each half, the quota, were to be renegotiated every ten years, and these negotiations became a recurring source of political tension. Hungary received its own parliament, government, and administration and, with the incorporation of Transylvania and the recognition of its historic constitution, control of a territory that included large non-Magyar populations.

In the Austrian half, the December Constitution of 1867 established a bicameral Reichsrat, an upper house of nobles and appointees and a lower house elected by a curial franchise that favored property holders. It also contained a fundamental law on citizens' rights, including equality before the law, freedom of conscience, and a provision that all nationalities enjoyed equal rights and that the state recognized the equal right of each language in schools, administration, and public life. The same year, laws removed legal restrictions on Jews in both halves of the monarchy, a significant step in the political emancipation of Habsburg Jews, who became prominent in commerce, the professions, and culture in Vienna, Budapest, and Prague, while antisemitism remained a political force.

Why did the Habsburg monarchy reorganize rather than dissolve? Reasons include the dynasty's will to survive, the interests of the Hungarian nobility in preserving their position within a great power, the lack of a more attractive alternative, and the fear on both sides that failure would open the door to disintegration or to Prussian or Russian pressure. For the Hungarian elite, the Compromise secured autonomy and a dominant position over the Kingdom's non-Magyar populations. For the dynasty, it preserved a great-power role. But other groups, notably the Czechs, who hoped for a similar settlement for the Bohemian crown lands, and the Slavic populations of Hungary, were not included. The Compromise therefore stabilized the monarchy at the price of leaving unresolved the claims of its other nationalities, a problem that the next section addresses.

The Nationalities of Central Europe: Germans, Magyars, Czechs, Croats, Poles, and Other Communities

The population of the Habsburg monarchy, roughly thirty-five million in the late 1860s by contemporary estimates, was among the most diverse in Europe, and no single nationality formed a majority. German speakers, concentrated in the Alpine lands, Bohemia, Moravia, and in urban centers, dominated the administration and economy of the Austrian half. Magyars were the largest group in Hungary, though they formed less than half of the Kingdom's population by many estimates, alongside Romanians, mostly in Transylvania, Slovaks, Serbs, Croats, Germans, Ruthenians, and Jews. In the Austrian half there were also Czechs in Bohemia and Moravia, Poles in Galicia, Ruthenians (Ukrainians) in eastern Galicia and Bukovina, Slovenes in Carniola and neighboring provinces, Italians in Trentino and the Adriatic, and others. Language, religion, and region overlapped in complicated ways, and many people had multiple loyalties, to the dynasty, locality, church, and language community, that did not map neatly onto the national categories later imposed by historians and politicians. Census data, which asked about the language of daily use, were themselves politicized, since the categories determined the allocation of schools and offices.

Political arrangements after 1867 favored some communities over others. Czech leaders, including František Palacký and František Rieger, had demanded recognition of the historic rights of the Bohemian crown, which would have given Bohemia a status similar to Hungary's. They boycotted the Reichsrat in the late 1860s, and in 1871 a ministry under Count Hohenwart prepared the Fundamental Articles that would have recognized Bohemian autonomy, but opposition from Germans and Hungarians led the emperor to withdraw support. In Galicia, by contrast, Polish elites obtained substantial autonomy in the late 1860s and 1870s, including Polish as the language of administration and instruction and a regional diet, and they became influential in Vienna, though this autonomy left the Ruthenian majority in eastern Galicia in a subordinate position. Polish loyalty to Habsburg rule contrasted with the experiences of Poles in the Russian and Prussian partitions, where policies of Russification and Germanization were harsher, a reminder that Poland's divided territories experienced different political environments.

Within the Hungarian half, the Hungarian Nationalities Law of 1868 defined all citizens as members of a single Hungarian political nation, while permitting some use of other languages in local administration, schools, and churches. Representatives of the Slovaks, Romanians, and Serbs argued that this denied them collective rights, and the narrow franchise, which favored the Magyar gentry and urban middle classes, limited their representation. Croatia received a separate arrangement in the Croatian-Hungarian Settlement, or Nagodba, of 1868, which gave Croatia-Slavonia autonomy in internal administration, justice, education, and religion, a diet in Zagreb, and representation in the Hungarian parliament, but left finances and many other matters under Budapest, and the ban, or governor, was appointed by the crown on the recommendation of the Hungarian government. Croatian nationalists disagreed about whether to accept the Settlement, whether to seek greater autonomy, or whether to promote a broader South Slav union. The question of Bosnia and the Serbs of southern Hungary, and broader South Slav claims, would loom large in Chapter 107.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Multilingual Community in the New Central Europe

Note: the following is an analytical reconstruction of patterns documented by historians across several regions, not the history of a single named town.

Consider a mid-sized town in Bohemia, Galicia, or Transylvania in the years after 1867, whose inhabitants spoke several languages. The administration used the official language of the province, which might differ from the language of the majority of the town's inhabitants. Schools were a crucial arena of conflict: the language of instruction, the choice of teachers, and the funding of institutions determined which community's children would be educated in which language, and therefore which language would offer access to the civil service, university, and professions. In Austria, the constitutional guarantee of linguistic equality had to be turned into practice by local decisions, and it produced continuous disputes. Local officials, judges, and postal and railway employees might be required to know more than one language, and bilingualism often brought advantages in commerce and public life.

Conscription also shaped life. In both halves of the monarchy, universal military service was introduced in 1868 after the shock of 1866, with common army units trained in regimental languages but using German as the language of command and service. Young men from villages encountered other nationalities, learned army German, and returned with new experiences. For many peasant families, service meant the loss of labor for several years. Jewish communities, newly emancipated, participated in commerce, schools, and public life and often adopted the dominant language of their environment, German in Bohemia and Vienna and Hungarian in Budapest, while Yiddish remained the everyday language of many Jews in Galicia. Women's experience varied by class and region but was shaped by legal restrictions on political participation and by the unequal access to schooling.

These experiences did not automatically create national hostility. Many families lived with mixed marriages, trade across language lines, and multiple identities. But as states expanded education and administration, the choices that individuals and communities made about language and schooling became politically significant, and national movements used these institutions to build their own constituencies. The state's expanded role in everyday life thus contributed to the politicization of national identity.

It is important to compare the Habsburg situation with the new national states. Italy and Germany also incorporated minorities and regional differences: Germans in the Empire included Poles in Posen and West Prussia, Danes in North Schleswig, Lithuanians in East Prussia, and, after 1871, French-speaking and German-speaking Alsatians and Lorrainers, as well as Catholics and Jews within a Protestant-dominated state. Italy included Albanian, Slovene, German, Greek, and French-speaking communities and a population in which standard Italian was a minority language, and it faced the difficulty of integrating regions with different economic and social histories. The unifications of Italy and Germany therefore did not settle Europe's nationalities question, but transformed it by creating states with national ideologies and by leaving substantial populations outside their borders, notably Germans in Austria and Italian speakers in Trentino, Trieste, and Istria, a territorial aspiration later labeled irredentism. The creation of national states intensified some disputes over sovereignty and belonging, because the principle that state and nation should coincide could not be fully realized in lands where populations were intermixed.

Comparing Italian and German Unification: Popular Nationalism, Monarchy, Diplomacy, and War

The two unifications are best understood through comparison, which clarifies what they shared and how they differed. Both were achieved under the leadership of a monarchy that was a constitutional state, the House of Savoy in Piedmont and the Hohenzollerns in Prussia. Both involved a statesman, Cavour and Bismarck, who directed the foreign policy and domestic politics of a state and who regarded revolutionary movements with suspicion while using nationalist feeling for state purposes. Both relied on armed force and exploited the weakness of Austria. And both resulted in states whose institutions reflected the dominant partner: Italy extended Piedmontese law and administration, while the German Empire was shaped around Prussian institutions and leadership. Mazzini and Garibaldi, in Italy, represent a popular and revolutionary tradition that had no exact equivalent in Germany, where the 1848 liberal movement had been defeated and whose heirs largely accepted Prussian leadership after 1866.

There were also major differences. In Italy, foreign powers provided the decisive military assistance: French troops in 1859 and, indirectly, Prussian victory in 1866 and French defeat in 1870. Italy's own armies were often unimpressive, but popular volunteers under Garibaldi achieved the dramatic conquest of the south. In Germany, Prussia relied on its own military power, which defeated Denmark, Austria, and France in sequence, and on the diplomatic management of potential rivals, Russia, France, and Britain. Italy's unification created a unitary state, with a centralized administration and a narrow franchise, while Germany's produced a federation in which states kept considerable powers, but with an elected national parliament chosen by broad male suffrage. Italy's major internal fault lines were the Roman Question and the north-south divide. Germany's included the relationship between Prussia and the other states, the Catholic minority, and the unresolved tension between the monarchical executive and the Reichstag. Plebiscites, used extensively in Italy, played little part in the German process, where the legitimacy of unification rested on princes' agreements, parliamentary resolutions, and military success.

Contingency remained central in both cases. At Villafranca, Napoleon III's decision to make peace shaped the outcome in Italy, while Garibaldi's improbable success in Sicily transformed the situation in the south. In Germany, Austria's rapid collapse at Königgrätz, the lack of French intervention in 1866, the escalation at Ems, and the willingness of the southern states to join the Empire were not foregone conclusions. The compositions of the final states depended on such contingent decisions, and different decisions might have led to a federation of north Germany only, a looser Italian confederation, or a very different relationship between Austria and Germany.

Historical Debate

Were Italy and Germany Unified by Nationalist Movements or by the Strategic Decisions of States?

The question: To what extent were the unifications the result of popular national movements that expressed the aspirations of the peoples concerned, and to what extent were they the result of the policies of established states and ruling elites that used nationalism for their own purposes?

Interpretation A: Popular nationalism as a driving force. Older national historiography, and some later scholars, emphasized the nationalist movements, with their cultural associations, volunteers, publicists, and martyrs, as the decisive force, the expression of a developing national consciousness. In this view, Garibaldi's volunteers, the plebiscites, the Nationalverein, and the nationalist enthusiasm of 1870 show that the creation of national states responded to widespread popular feeling, which governments could not ignore.
Interpretation B: State-led consolidation. Many historians emphasize that unification was carried out by established states, with armies, bureaucracies, and diplomats, and that popular nationalism was a minority commitment, especially among the educated and propertied. In this view, the decisive events were wars and diplomatic bargains, and the states used nationalism to legitimize decisions made for reasons of dynastic interest and power politics. Studies of the Risorgimento such as those of Lucy Riall and Christopher Duggan, which students should consult directly, attend to the political elites and state institutions while also analyzing the cultural and popular dimensions of the movement.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on selected evidence. Supporters of the first point to volunteer movements, the growth of nationalist associations and press, large festivals, and plebiscite returns. Supporters of the second point to the dependence on foreign armies and diplomacy, the narrow franchise, the limits of popular literacy and participation, the manipulation of plebiscites, and the evident preference of Cavour and Bismarck for state control. Neither view is sufficient alone. Most scholars today emphasize the interaction: nationalism created a political environment that states could exploit, while state power supplied the means to realize aspirations that movements could not achieve alone.

Why it matters: The answer affects how we assess the legitimacy of the new states, the extent of national integration, and the later claims of governments to speak for the nation. If unification was primarily popular, the new states can be seen as expressions of national will. If primarily state-led, the nation had to be created afterward, a task that continued for decades.

The integration problems after unification were also different. In Italy, the state's response to southern resistance and Catholic opposition revealed the gap between legal unity and social integration. In Germany, the Empire's federal structure moderated some regional tensions, but the Kulturkampf and the treatment of Poles and Alsatians showed that the state's national identity was exclusive in some respects. Both states had to build national institutions, schools, and symbols, while addressing the political demands of workers and other groups that were excluded from the original settlements, matters that will be examined in Chapter 102.

The Remaking of Central Europe: New National States and a Transformed Balance of Power

By 1871 the political order established at Vienna in 1815 had been profoundly altered. The Concert of Europe, which had relied on cooperation among the conservative eastern powers and on the legitimacy of dynastic arrangements, had been shaken by the Crimean War and then overturned by wars that changed borders without the consent of all the great powers. Between 1859 and 1871, Austria lost its position in Italy and Germany, France lost its predominance on the continent, a new Italian kingdom appeared, and a German Empire became the strongest military and, increasingly, industrial power on the continent. The British statesman Benjamin Disraeli, speaking in the House of Commons early in 1871, is reported to have described the German war as a greater political event than the French Revolution, because it had destroyed the balance of power; students should treat the exact wording with caution, but the judgment reflects what many contemporaries felt.

The relationships among the powers changed. Germany, in the center of the continent, with a rapidly growing population, a large and efficient army, and rich coal and iron resources in the Ruhr, Silesia, and Lorraine, was now the dominant power. France, defeated and humiliated, sought recovery and faced a prolonged domestic struggle over the form of its republic, as later chapters show. Austria-Hungary, excluded from Germany, turned its attention toward southeastern Europe and the Balkans, a shift marked by Andrássy's appointment as joint foreign minister in 1871. Russia, which had remained benevolently neutral, took advantage of the situation to repudiate the neutralization of the Black Sea imposed in 1856, an action accepted by the powers at the London Conference of 1871. Britain, still the dominant naval and commercial power, remained wary of continental commitments. Italy, a new but weaker power, sought recognition as a great power and was ambivalent about its relations with both Germany and France, and resentful of the unfinished boundaries to its northeast.

French resentment over Alsace-Lorraine is a significant element of the longer history of Franco-German rivalry, but it should not be treated as the single cause of later conflicts. Bismarck, after 1871, regarded Germany as a satisfied power and sought to preserve the settlement by isolating France and maintaining good relations with Austria-Hungary and Russia, as in the Three Emperors' League of 1873, which will be examined in Chapter 107. The subsequent alliance systems, imperial rivalries, arms races, and nationalist crises, discussed in Chapters 104, 106, and 107, involved many factors beyond the territorial question, and it would be a teleological error to treat the Franco-Prussian War as the first act of a predestined sequence leading to 1914.

Cause and Effect

Military Victory, Alliances, and a New European Balance

Long-term causes

The post-1815 settlement left Italy and Germany politically divided; economic integration and railway growth strengthened Prussia and Piedmont; liberal and national movements spread among educated publics; and the failure of 1848 persuaded leaders that power, not declarations, was required.

Short-term causes

The Crimean War broke the conservative alliance among the eastern powers; Napoleon III sought to revise 1815; Prussian army reform and the needle gun and rail mobilization gave Prussia an operational advantage; and Cavour and Bismarck exploited opportunities with skill.

Event or transformation

The wars of 1859, 1864, 1866, and 1870 to 1871, with the diplomacy that surrounded them, produced the Kingdom of Italy, the German Empire, and Austria-Hungary, and the replacement of the Vienna system with a new alignment of powers.

Immediate effects

Austria's exclusion from Italy and Germany; the loss of Alsace and part of Lorraine by France; Rome and the Roman Question; Russian revision of the Black Sea clauses; and a German-centered diplomatic system under Bismarck.

Long-term consequences

A powerful and industrializing Germany at the center of Europe; French desire to recover standing and territory; Habsburg concentration on the Balkans; unfinished national integration in Italy and Germany; and persistent minority and nationality disputes.

Historical complexity

No single factor produced the outcome. Armies, alliances, economic resources, domestic politics, and personal decisions interacted, and later events, from imperial rivalry to the alliance systems, cannot be derived from 1871 alone.

Historical complexity: Similar structural conditions, including economic growth and national sentiment, existed elsewhere without producing unification. Outcomes depended on the decisions of actors operating within those conditions.

The new national states did not eliminate regional loyalties, linguistic diversity, minority populations, or political disagreements. Bavaria and Sicily retained distinct identities; Catholics, Poles, and socialists challenged the German settlement; and in Italy the south, the Church, and the excluded majority raised enduring questions. Nationalism itself had been demonstrated as a powerful political force, but also as one that could be used by governments for differing purposes, and its triumph in two states made it an increasingly central concern in the Habsburg and Ottoman lands, the Balkans, and the overseas empires. This chapter's developments connect directly to Part XVI: the industrial expansion of Chapter 101, the mass politics of Chapter 102, the racial and nationalist thought of Chapter 103, the imperial expansion of Chapter 104, and the alliance system and Balkan crises of Chapter 107 all unfold in the political space the unifications created. Central Europe was remade, but the great questions of belonging, authority, and security remained open.

Chapter Summary

Between 1848 and 1871 the political geography of central and southern Europe was transformed. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861 and extended to Venetia in 1866 and Rome in 1870, the German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles in 1871 after three wars, and the Habsburg monarchy was reorganized as Austria-Hungary in 1867. These changes demonstrated the political power of nationalism, but they also demonstrated that nationalism achieved success through states, armies, diplomacy, and bargains, and that the settlements left major questions unresolved.

The starting point was failure. The revolutions of 1848 and 1849 showed that national unity could not be achieved by revolutionary declaration alone. In Italy, the defeat of Piedmont at Novara and of the Roman Republic left the peninsula divided, yet the survival of a national culture among exiles, publicists, and associations, and the survival of the Statuto Albertino in Piedmont, kept the national question alive. In Germany, the refusal of the Prussian king to accept the crown offered by Frankfurt and the restoration of the Confederation showed that the established dynasties retained control.

In Italy, Piedmont-Sardinia became the center of moderate nationalism because of its constitutional government, its administrative modernization, and the diplomacy of Cavour. Cavour combined economic development, parliamentary coalition building, and international engagement, joining the Crimean War to bring the Italian question before the powers and making a secret agreement with Napoleon III at Plombières in 1858. His own aims in 1858 were an enlarged northern kingdom, not a unified Italian state, and the later extension of the process exceeded his plan.

The war of 1859, with the battles of Magenta and Solferino, secured Lombardy, but Napoleon III's armistice at Villafranca left Venetia under Austrian rule, demonstrating the limits of French assistance. Local initiative in central Italy, together with diplomatic bargaining and plebiscites, led to annexations in 1860, at the price of Nice and Savoy. Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand then overthrew Bourbon rule in Sicily and Naples, and Cavour's decision to send the Piedmontese army through the Papal States prevented a revolutionary advance on Rome and brought the south under the monarchy. The meeting at Teano symbolized the transfer of authority but did not reconcile the competing visions within the national movement.

The Kingdom of Italy faced the task of integration. The extension of Piedmontese institutions, a narrow franchise, taxation, conscription, and the brutal repression of the conflict in the south, along with the unresolved Roman Question, showed that political unification was not national integration. Venetia came in 1866 through the alliance with Prussia, despite Italian military defeats, and Rome in 1870 after the French defeat. The papacy's rejection of the settlement left a lasting division between the Church and the state.

In Germany, the Zollverein and economic growth strengthened Prussia, but political unification depended on power. The army reforms of Roon and Moltke, the constitutional conflict over the military budget, and the appointment of Bismarck in 1862 set the stage. Bismarck's policy was not a fixed plan but a flexible use of diplomacy, war, and political compromise, directed toward the interests of the Prussian monarchy. The Danish war of 1864 gave Prussia and Austria the duchies, and the problem of their administration created the occasion for the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, in which Königgrätz decided the leadership of Germany, dissolved the Confederation, and led to the North German Confederation, with its combination of broad male suffrage and a chancellor responsible to the crown.

The Franco-Prussian War completed the process. The Spanish candidacy, French overreach, and Bismarck's editing of the Ems Dispatch brought about war in July 1870, and German victories at Sedan and elsewhere, followed by the siege of Paris, led to the fall of Napoleon III and the accession of the southern states. The Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871 at Versailles, established constitutionally in the spring, and confirmed by the Treaty of Frankfurt, which transferred Alsace and part of Lorraine and imposed a heavy indemnity. The Empire was a powerful federal state under Prussian dominance, with an elected Reichstag but a monarchical executive.

Austria's defeat led to the Compromise of 1867, which created a Dual Monarchy of two largely self-governing halves with a common monarch and common ministries for foreign affairs, war, and finance. The settlement stabilized the monarchy but privileged Germans and Magyars, leaving Czechs, Slovaks, Romanians, Serbs, Ruthenians, and others dissatisfied, and gave Poles in Galicia and Croats in Hungary only partial autonomy. The Habsburg experience, like those of Italy and Germany, shows that national states and multinational empires faced the problem of populations that did not fit national boundaries.

The comparison of Italy and Germany reveals shared features, including monarchical leadership, the use of nationalism by statesmen, and the exploitation of Austria's weakness, and differences in the roles of foreign assistance, popular volunteers, federalism, and plebiscites. Historians debate whether unification was primarily a popular movement or a state-led achievement, and the best answer lies in the interaction between a minority nationalism and the power of states.

The consequences for Europe were profound. A powerful Germany stood at the center of the continent, France had lost territory and prestige, Austria-Hungary turned toward the Balkans, Russia exploited the situation to revise the Black Sea settlement, and Britain remained aloof. The settlement did not create a stable peace by itself; its tensions, including Alsace-Lorraine, the nationalities question, the Roman Question, and the rise of mass politics, carried into the age of empire and alliances examined in Part XVI.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an idea became a state, and what was lost and left unresolved in the process. The unifications of Italy and Germany, and the reorganization of the Habsburg monarchy, were not simply the triumph of national feeling. They were the products of armies, diplomatic bargains, constitutional compromises, and dynastic interests, in which popular movements played real but unequal parts. Recognizing this prevents the error of treating the national state as the natural endpoint of European history.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. The wars of 1859 to 1871 were fought with railways, the telegraph, rifled weapons, and mass mobilization, techniques made possible by the industrial developments of earlier chapters. Their outcomes depended on the shifting relationships among the great powers, which broke the post-1815 order and created a new one centered on a powerful Germany. The settlements transformed the position of France, Austria-Hungary, Russia, and Britain, and set the diplomatic context for the age of empire.

Continuities survived beyond the chapter. The conflict over the relationship between the monarchy and parliament, the tension between regional and national loyalties, the place of the Catholic Church in public life, and the status of minorities remained central to Italian, German, and Habsburg politics for decades. Alsace-Lorraine, Trentino and Trieste, and the nationalities of the Dual Monarchy became enduring sources of dispute, though none of these inevitably led to the crises of the early twentieth century.

Within Volume IV, the chapter brings together the Enlightenment's political ideas, the revolutionary legacy, and the industrial transformation by showing how they were converted into state power. It closes Part XV and leads into Part XVI, which examines the Second Industrial Revolution, mass politics, racial thought, imperial expansion, and the alliance system. Students should carry from this chapter a habit of asking how ideals are turned into institutions, who benefits, who is excluded, and how contingent decisions shape what later appears inevitable.
