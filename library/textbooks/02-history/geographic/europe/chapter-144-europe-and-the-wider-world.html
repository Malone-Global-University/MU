Europe and the Wider World

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Europe began this textbook as a question, and it ends as one. After one hundred and forty three chapters, the evidence shows a region that was never sealed off from the world, never culturally uniform, and never possessed of a boundary on which all of its inhabitants agreed. What Europe meant depended on who was speaking, in which century, and in relation to whom.

In the fifth century BCE, the Greek historian Herodotus, writing about the known world, admitted that he could not understand why the earth had been divided into three parts, Europe, Asia, and Libya, each named for a woman, and he noted that no one could say with confidence where the dividing lines lay or even whether the sea surrounded all three. His puzzlement is instructive. The earliest surviving Greek discussion of Europe as a named division of the world already treated the name as a convention whose origins were obscure and whose borders were uncertain. The myth that later explained the name told of Europa, a princess carried across the sea from the Phoenician coast of the eastern Mediterranean, and modern scholars still debate the word's etymology. Even in its mythology, the continent's name pointed toward the east and the sea rather than toward a self contained European homeland.

This chapter returns to the question that Chapter 1, What Is Europe? , placed at the head of the textbook, and it does so with the evidence of the intervening chapters in hand. Chapter 1 distinguished Europe as a geographic concept, a cultural idea, and a political identity, and warned that none of the three had fixed borders. Chapter 2 then described the peninsulas, seas, rivers, mountains, plains, and climates of the physical landmass. The present chapter asks what happened to those definitions and that landscape across several thousand years of history, how European societies were shaped by their connections with Africa, Asia, the Americas, and the oceans, how some European states came to exercise extraordinary influence in the wider world, and how that influence was transformed after 1914 and again after 1945.

The chapter advances five arguments, developed across five divisions. First, Europe was geographically connected and internally diverse from the beginning, and no single political, religious, or linguistic boundary has ever enclosed all European societies. Second, European development cannot be explained from within Europe alone, because exchange, migration, conquest, commerce, and intellectual transmission with other regions were part of how European societies formed. Third, European global predominance, where it existed, was historically contingent, uneven among European states, and dependent on resources, markets, knowledge, and labor drawn from outside the continent, often by coercion. Fourth, the erosion of that predominance after the world wars did not end European history or European influence, but redirected them through new institutions, new migrations, and new relationships. Fifth, European identity remains a contested historical question that no single institution, religion, language, or nation can settle.

The chapter draws on kinds of evidence that differ widely in character. Historical maps show how Europeans and non-Europeans represented the world, and the changing placement of the Europe and Asia boundary on those maps is itself evidence. Treaties, charters, and declarations record how political actors defined legitimacy and sovereignty. Merchant ledgers, customs records, and shipping registers document commodities and labor. Archaeology and environmental science reconstruct exchanges that no document recorded. Scholarly works, museum collections, monuments, and school curricula show how later generations remembered and reshaped the past. Each of these sources reflects a perspective, and the chapter repeatedly asks which perspectives are present and which are missing.

The chapter belongs to Part XXI, European Historical Patterns and Legacies , and to Volume VI, Cold War, Integration, and Modern Europe . Chapters 140 through 143 examined, in turn, religion and secularization, war and state formation, empire and migration, and class, gender, family, and labor. Chapter 144 does not repeat those syntheses. It uses them to reconsider Europe itself as a changing category, positioned within a world that shaped it as much as it was shaped by it. Because it is the last numbered chapter of the textbook, it also carries the work's concluding interpretation, although the separate Volume VI concluding essay remains a distinct component of the series.

Two cautions govern the chapter. The first is against teleology, the habit of reading history as a march toward a predetermined result. European integration, European decline, European exceptionalism, and European universalism are all outcomes and interpretations that had to be made, and they were contingent at every stage. The second concerns the present. The chapter treats developments through November 20, 2026, as its information cutoff, distinguishes settled historical events from ongoing developments, and does not predict outcomes. Where recent events are mentioned, they appear as historical conditions that students may examine, not as conclusions already written.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Europe must be studied as a historical category whose meanings and boundaries changed, not as a timeless civilization.
  2. Distinguish Europe as a geographical expression, a cultural idea, a religious community, a system of states, and a political project.
  3. Trace the connections of ancient and medieval European societies with Mediterranean, African, Asian, and Eurasian networks of exchange.
  4. Analyze how maritime expansion, conquest, and colonial systems transformed both European and non-European societies.
  5. Connect global commodities, ecological exchange, and knowledge circulation to ordinary life and intellectual development inside Europe.
  6. Evaluate competing explanations for European industrialization and the Great Divergence, including the strengths and limits of each.
  7. Assess how the world wars, the Cold War, and decolonization changed Europe's position in the international order.
  8. Compare the UN Charter, the Schuman Declaration, and the Bandung communiqué as different postwar visions of international order.
  9. Distinguish European integration, the Council of Europe, NATO, and the European Union as separate institutions with different functions.
  10. Interpret how memory, historiography, museums, and public controversy shape the writing and teaching of European history.
  11. Synthesize the findings of Chapters 1 through 143 into a historically grounded answer to the question of what Europe has meant.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who has defined Europe at different moments, for what purposes, and which populations and territories did each definition include or exclude?
  2. In what ways did Europe's geographical position produce connection, and why is it misleading to treat that geography as the cause of particular historical outcomes?
  3. How should historians weigh internal European developments against external stimuli, such as trade, migration, conquest, and knowledge from other regions, when explaining European change?
  4. Why did a small number of European states exercise disproportionate global influence between roughly 1750 and 1914, and why did that predominance prove temporary?
  5. How did Enlightenment claims about universal rights coexist with slavery, colonial rule, and racial classification, and how did colonized peoples use those claims?
  6. What is the difference between the relative decline of European power and the disappearance of European influence, and what evidence distinguishes them?
  7. How did decolonization and postcolonial migration make the history of empire part of the domestic history of European societies?
  8. Why do the questions that historians ask about Europe change, and what do museums, monuments, and school curricula reveal about those changes?

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established the three registers in which this textbook uses the word Europe. As a geographic term, Europe designates the western peninsula of Eurasia, with an eastern limit that is conventional rather than natural. As a cultural idea, it has been associated at different times with Christendom, with classical heritage, with Enlightenment reason, and with civilization, each of which was claimed by some groups and denied to others. As a political identity, it has referred to systems of states, to dynastic networks, to colonial metropoles, and, since the middle of the twentieth century, to institutions of cooperation. Chapter 2 supplied the physical setting, emphasizing a long and indented coastline, navigable rivers, mountain systems that functioned as both barriers and corridors, open plains, and a steppe corridor into Central Asia, and it warned against geographic determinism.

The chapters between those two openings traced the societies that inhabited that setting. Volume I followed the settlement of the continent, the Greek and Roman worlds, and the transformation of the Roman Mediterranean. Volume II examined Latin Christendom, Byzantium, the Orthodox and Slavic worlds, and the economic and intellectual expansion of the High Middle Ages, followed by the crises of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Volume III covered the Renaissance, the Reformation, maritime expansion, and the dynastic and fiscal military states that competed within a developing balance of power. Volume IV moved through the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment, the revolutions that ended the Old Regime, industrialization, nationalism, and the New Imperialism. Volume V treated the two world wars, the interwar crisis, occupation, and the Holocaust. Volume VI followed the division of Europe, decolonization, social transformation, the end of communist rule, European integration, and the contested security order of the twenty first century.

Chapters 140 through 143 then reorganized this narrative around recurring structures. Chapter 140 examined Christianity, religious pluralism, and secularization. Chapter 141 followed the relationship among war, states, law, and sovereignty. Chapter 142 connected empire, colonialism, migration, and diaspora across Roman, medieval, dynastic, maritime, and postcolonial forms. Chapter 143 synthesized class, gender, family, labor, and social change. Chapter 144 adds a different kind of reflection. Those four chapters asked what patterns recur within European history. This chapter asks what the history of Europe reveals about the category of Europe, and about its relationship to the wider world.

Students should recognize that this final reconsideration depends on methods developed across the textbook. Historians now draw on archaeology, environmental science, economic data, colonial and postcolonial archives, oral histories, and comparative and global approaches in addition to the diplomatic, political, and intellectual sources that long dominated European historiography. A chapter that reconsiders Europe's global position must therefore also reconsider the sources through which that position has been described, because European, colonial, and postcolonial authors have disagreed sharply about what the evidence shows and whose experience counts as evidence.

What Is Europe? Geography, Historical Identity, and the Problem of Definition

The word Europe entered the historical record as a geographical label used by Greek authors, and its earliest uses were vague. Early Greek writers sometimes applied it to the lands north of the Aegean, and by the fifth century BCE it had become one of three named divisions of the known world. Herodotus doubted the symmetry of the scheme, and later geographers, among them Strabo, whose description of Europe's indented coastline Chapter 2 examined, treated the dividing rivers, the Tanais (the Don) in the north and the Nile or the Red Sea in the south, as matters of convention. The ancient world therefore left the later tradition with a word, a mythology, and a boundary problem, but not with a settled European identity. People in the Roman Empire identified themselves by city, province, citizenship, and, increasingly, religion. Few if any thought of themselves as Europeans.

The word acquired new meanings as political and religious circumstances changed. In the Carolingian period, court writers occasionally used it for the realm over which Charlemagne ruled, and a poem composed around 800 praised the king as a father of Europe. Such usage was rhetorical and attached to a particular dynasty. For most of the Middle Ages, the more common frame of reference was Christendom, a religious community that stretched beyond the geographic peninsula, included Christian communities in the Near East and Africa, and divided internally between Latin and Orthodox traditions, as Chapters 38 through 41 explained. After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, humanist writers such as Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini, later Pope Pius II, began to use Europe in a way that overlapped with Christendom and expressed anxiety about Ottoman power. The term nevertheless remained secondary to confessional and dynastic identities until the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

In the early modern period, Europe took on a third meaning, a system of competing states bound by diplomacy, dynastic marriage, and the idea of a balance of power. Eighteenth century writers referred to the equilibrium of Europe, and the treaties examined in Chapters 67 and 80 established principles that governed relations among sovereigns who were frequently at war. Enlightenment authors in turn used Europe to denote a community of letters and of reason, the Republic of Letters discussed in Chapter 82, while increasingly ranking Europe above other regions in hierarchies of civilization. The geographical boundary was fixed in its modern conventional form in this period: the Urals in the east, which writers such as Vasily Tatishchev and Philip Johan von Strahlenberg promoted in the early eighteenth century, in part to integrate the expanding Russian Empire into a European frame.

Several recurring ambiguities remain visible. Russia has been described as a European power, as an Asian one, and as a distinct civilization, depending on the writer and the decade. The Ottoman Empire controlled much of southeastern Europe for centuries and participated in European diplomacy, yet it has often been described as external to Europe, and the status of modern Turkey remains debated. The Caucasus lies on a boundary that conventions place differently. The Mediterranean, treated in Chapter 2 as a connecting sea, has repeatedly been imagined as a border between Europe and Africa or between Christian and Muslim worlds, even though its coasts shared commercial, linguistic, and political ties across more than two millennia. Each of these cases shows the same principle. Europe's limits were determined by choices, and those choices served particular purposes.

The central analytical rule of this textbook follows from that evidence. Europe is a historical category whose meanings changed, not an unchanging civilization. A geographic reading of Europe, a religious reading, a state system reading, a civilizational reading, and an institutional reading each describe some period or community well and others poorly. Using any of them as the universal definition requires omitting evidence, such as the Jews and Muslims who lived in Europe for centuries, the Orthodox societies of the east, the Mediterranean connections with Africa, or the colonial territories ruled from European capitals. This chapter therefore treats Europe as an object of historical analysis whose definitions are themselves among the evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Europe, Civilization, Region, and Cultural Identity

Europe is used in this textbook for the western portion of Eurasia, for ideas about that portion, and for political institutions that have borne its name, and the chapter always specifies which meaning is in play. Civilization was a concept whose modern meaning emerged in the eighteenth century and was later used to rank societies on an assumed scale of development, and it was often used to justify imperial rule, so modern historians use it carefully and usually prefer more specific terms. A region is an analytical area defined by shared physical, economic, political, or cultural features, such as the Baltic or the Balkans, whose boundaries depend on the question being asked. Cultural identity refers to the sense of belonging that people attach to language, religion, locality, or community, and it is plural, layered, and changeable. Students should avoid assuming that a shared geographic location produced a shared cultural identity.

Europe's Geographical Position: Barriers, Routes, and Connections

Chapter 2 described a peninsula of Eurasia with an unusually long and irregular coastline, and the later chapters repeatedly confirmed that the physical setting mattered. Seas shaped commerce and warfare. The Mediterranean connected Greek, Phoenician, Carthaginian, Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and Italian commercial worlds. The North Sea and Baltic supported the networks of the Hanseatic towns and the grain and timber trades that supplied western Europe. The Black Sea tied the Balkans and Anatolia to the steppe and to Caucasian and Central Asian routes. The Atlantic, which for most of antiquity and the Middle Ages formed Europe's western edge, became after the fifteenth century the foundation of transoceanic commerce.

Rivers and overland corridors performed similar functions. The Rhine and Danube served as a Roman imperial frontier, a medieval trade artery, and a corridor of migration and war. The Rhône and Rhine linked the Mediterranean to northern Europe. The Dnieper, Don, and Volga connected the forests of eastern Europe to the Black and Caspian Seas. The North European and East European Plains offered movement across great distances with few natural obstacles, and the Pannonian Basin lay at the meeting point between steppe pastoralists and settled societies. Mountains such as the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians, and Balkan ranges channeled movement into passes, produced distinctive regional cultures, and shaped the strategies of armies, but they never provided absolute cultural boundaries. Alpine passes carried more trade and pilgrimage than many lowland routes.

The comparison between maritime and continental connections is a useful analytical exercise. Water transport was typically cheaper for bulk goods before the railway, which favored coastal and riverine regions, and it allowed distant ports to maintain closer ties with one another than with their own hinterlands. Overland routes, by contrast, supported the movement of herders, armies, pilgrims, and caravans, and they connected Europe to Central Asia more continuously than the sea connected it to the Americas before 1492. Both patterns were always present. A strictly maritime or strictly continental interpretation of European history therefore misrepresents the evidence, a point reinforced by the fact that the great oceanic expansions of the fifteenth century were partly efforts to bypass or compete with overland and Mediterranean trade routes dominated by other powers.

The Baltic demonstrates that the same sea connected societies in several distinct historical ways. Viking routes joined Scandinavian ports to the British Isles, the Frankish realms, and the rivers of the eastern Baltic and Rus. Later, German-speaking merchants and the Hanseatic towns coordinated exchange in grain, fish, timber, and cloth, but the commercial privileges of a city did not eliminate local rulers or competing trading communities. In the early modern period, Swedish, Polish-Lithuanian, Danish, and Russian rulers fought to control ports and customs revenue. By the nineteenth century, railways and steamships changed the relationship between inland producers and maritime markets. These successive systems used much of the same physical geography but gave different political and economic meanings to it. A map of coastlines alone could not explain their changing fortunes.

The Black Sea provides an equally important counterexample to histories organized solely around the Atlantic. Greek settlements, Byzantine commerce, the trade of Genoese and Venetian merchants, Ottoman rule, Crimean Tatar networks, and later Russian expansion connected its shores to the Balkans, Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Eurasian steppe. Peoples and goods moved through these systems under different conditions, including free commerce, tribute, military mobilization, and enslavement. Describing the Black Sea as the edge of Europe misses its historical function as an intersection of political orders. The geographical question for historians is therefore not simply where Europe's border lies, but which routes and institutions made distant communities interdependent in particular periods.

Students should also remember the limits of geographic explanation, which Chapter 2 developed under the heading of environmental possibilism. The same coastline hosted a large trading empire in one century and a poor fishing community in another, and the same river served as a frontier or a highway according to the political circumstances. Physical geography established conditions, and human institutions, technologies, and choices determined which of the possibilities were realized. The eastern limit of Europe makes the point particularly clearly. Contemporary scientific and administrative bodies, such as the European Environment Agency, continue to describe the eastern geographical limit as partly conventional, which is consistent with the long history of disagreement traced in the previous section.

Ancient Europe within Mediterranean, African, and Eurasian Worlds

The early chapters of this textbook showed that European societies formed through movement and contact. Chapter 3 followed the migrations of Paleolithic populations into a landscape continuous with Africa and Asia. Chapter 4 traced the spread of farming from Southwest Asia across the continent, a process that involved the movement of both people and techniques. Chapters 5 and 6 documented Bronze Age and Iron Age exchange networks that moved tin, copper, amber, salt, and finished goods across long distances, and they cautioned against projecting later ethnic or national labels onto Celtic, Germanic, Italic, Illyrian, Thracian, Iberian, Baltic, and other societies. These populations were neither isolated nor uniform, and they were linked to the Mediterranean and to the steppe through trade and migration.

The Greek world of Part II developed within the eastern Mediterranean. Minoan and Mycenaean palaces interacted with Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant, and Greek writing, religion, and craft production drew on Near Eastern models, a debt that Greeks themselves sometimes recognized. Greek colonization from the eighth century BCE established cities from the Black Sea coast to southern Italy, Sicily, Gaul, and the Libyan coast, and Greek settlers traded and fought with Phoenician, Carthaginian, Etruscan, Scythian, Thracian, Celtic, and Iberian neighbors. The Greek poleis also confronted the Persian Empire, which stretched from Anatolia to Central Asia and forms part of the context of the Greco Persian conflicts. Alexander's campaigns then created Hellenistic kingdoms in Egypt and western Asia, and the intellectual life of Alexandria and Antioch was part of Greek history as much as Athens was.

Rome extended this pattern. A city on the Tiber rose to rule an empire that surrounded the Mediterranean and included provinces in Europe, North Africa, and western Asia. The Roman Empire was never a European empire in the modern sense. Its center of economic and demographic weight lay substantially in the eastern Mediterranean, its Egyptian grain supplied the capital, and its emperors included men from Iberia, Africa, Syria, and the Balkans. Roman law, Latin, urban institutions, and roads were exported to Gaul, Britain, and the Danube lands, while religious traditions from Egypt, Anatolia, Persia, and Judea, including Christianity, spread westward through Roman networks. For Rome, the Mediterranean was an internal sea, and the European north was one frontier among several.

The Roman example also raises the problem of assigning modern continents to ancient institutions. Cities in Roman Africa, Syria, and Asia Minor participated in the same political and intellectual networks as those in Italy and Gaul, while populations beyond the Rhine and Danube traded with, resisted, and sometimes served the empire. Roman citizens could hold offices far from their places of birth, and the extension of citizenship to most free inhabitants in 212 CE did not erase inequalities of class, slavery, locality, or privilege. Historians should therefore resist treating Rome as the political ancestor of one later continent to the exclusion of the Mediterranean world it actually governed. The Roman past became a European inheritance through selective later appropriation, not because the ancient empire possessed a European national identity.

Greek and Roman writers did not consider themselves the founders of a European civilization. Later societies attributed that role to them. Medieval kingdoms, Renaissance humanists, Enlightenment reformers, and nineteenth century nationalists each reinterpreted Greek philosophy, Roman law, Latin literature, and imperial ideology to serve their own political and cultural goals, as Chapters 57 and 60 showed for the Renaissance. The Roman inheritance was selective, contested, and plural, and it reached medieval Europeans through the Byzantine, Islamic, and Latin Christian worlds. Treating the ancient Mediterranean as the origin of an already European civilization therefore obscures both what ancient societies actually were and how later Europeans made them into ancestors.

Division I · Europe as Geography, Idea, and Historical Crossroads Section 144.4

Medieval Europes: Religious Plurality, Trade, and Eurasian Exchange

The plural in the section title is deliberate. Medieval Europe contained Latin Christian kingdoms and principalities, the Byzantine Empire and its Orthodox neighbors, Muslim polities in Iberia and Sicily, Jewish communities in most regions, pagan societies in the Baltic and parts of the north until their conversion or conquest, and steppe peoples along the eastern frontier. Chapters 29 through 55 traced the construction of Latin Christendom, but they also treated Byzantium, Bulgaria, Serbia, and Kievan Rus as central, and they described the Great Schism as a gradual institutional and cultural separation, not a single event. Medieval Christendom was geographically uneven and doctrinally and liturgically divided, and no individual authority, whether pope, emperor, or patriarch, commanded all Christians.

The coexistence of religions was a defining feature, though it was not always peaceful and its conditions varied widely. Al-Andalus, established in the early eighth century, endured in diminishing forms until 1492, and its cities supported Muslim, Jewish, and Christian communities who shared languages and crafts while living under unequal legal regimes. Norman Sicily combined Latin, Greek, and Arabic administration. Jewish communities in the Rhineland, Iberia, Italy, and later Poland and Lithuania developed distinctive legal and scholarly traditions and played roles in commerce and finance, yet they also faced restrictions, violence, and expulsions, including from England in 1290 and from Spain in 1492. Muslims lived under Christian rule in Iberia, Sicily, and later in parts of eastern Europe, and Ottoman expansion in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries brought Muslim rule and settlement to the Balkans, as Chapter 41 described. The history of Christian and Muslim relations included war, as Chapter 47 showed for the crusading movement, and it also included diplomacy, commerce, intermarriage, conversion, and intellectual exchange.

Trade tied these societies into Eurasian networks. Italian maritime cities, Venice and Genoa above all, built commercial empires in the eastern Mediterranean and the Black Sea, purchasing spices, silk, and other goods that arrived through Islamic and Byzantine intermediaries. The Mongol conquests of the thirteenth century created a political space across much of Eurasia within which merchants, envoys, and missionaries moved with unusual security, and Europeans who traveled to the Mongol courts and to China recorded observations that influenced later geographical ideas. The same connections carried disease. Most scholars accept that the plague pandemic that reached Europe in 1346 to 1347, treated in Chapter 50, moved along trade routes from the Black Sea region, a reminder that connection brought hazards as well as profits.

These contacts were not confined to famous travelers or scholarly books. Mediterranean and Black Sea ports depended on translators, sailors, pilgrims, brokers, and diasporic communities with knowledge of multiple languages and legal systems. Jewish commercial documents preserved in the Cairo Geniza illuminate merchants whose relatives and agents linked North Africa, Egypt, Iberia, and the Indian Ocean. The same routes that carried expensive textiles and spices also carried ordinary goods, letters, and information about prices and political risks. These records remind historians that connectivity was maintained by families and working communities as much as by kings and merchants celebrated in chronicles. The daily mechanisms of exchange help explain why religious and political boundaries often remained porous even during periods of warfare and persecution.

Knowledge also moved. Chapter 48 described the universities and scholastic learning of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, which depended on the translation into Latin of Greek and Arabic works, often undertaken in Toledo, Sicily, and southern Italy with the help of Jewish, Muslim, and Eastern Christian scholars. Arabic writers had preserved, commented upon, and extended Greek philosophy, medicine, mathematics, and astronomy, and the Hindu Arabic numerals reached Latin Europe through works such as Leonardo Fibonacci's Liber abaci of 1202. Paper, produced in China and transmitted through the Islamic world, became established in Iberia and Italy. Historians distinguish carefully among transmission, in which a text or technique moved intact, adaptation, in which it was altered for new use, and independent development, in which similar ideas arose separately, and the evidence supports all three. The argument of this section rejects both an isolated medieval Europe and a uniform medieval Christendom.

Historical Vocabulary
Christendom, Cultural Exchange, and Historical Identity

Christendom denotes the community of Christians, and especially the political and institutional world in which Christianity shaped law and public life, but it was never a single unified polity. Latin Christendom and Orthodox Christendom had different liturgical languages, ecclesiastical structures, and political cultures, and Christian communities also existed in the Near East, Egypt, and Ethiopia. Cultural exchange refers to the movement and reinterpretation of ideas, goods, techniques, and practices between societies, and it is rarely symmetrical, because the parties differ in power and in what they choose to adopt. Historical identity is the sense of belonging that communities construct, often with reference to a remembered past, and historians treat it as constructed and changing. Students should not equate medieval Christendom with modern Europe, since the two had different boundaries and different members.

Division II · Global Encounters and the Transformation of European Societies Section 144.5

Maritime Expansion and the Reorientation of European Connections

The voyages of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, introduced in Chapter 68, changed the geography of European commerce and power. Portuguese expeditions moved down the African coast after the capture of Ceuta in 1415, drew on Mediterranean and Arab navigational knowledge, and reached the Indian Ocean when Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa in 1488 and Vasco da Gama arrived at Calicut in 1498. Castilian sponsorship of Christopher Columbus produced the voyage of 1492 and its sequel, and the expedition begun by Ferdinand Magellan and completed after his death by Juan Sebastián Elcano accomplished the first circumnavigation in 1519 to 1522. The Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494, which divided prospective claims between Portugal and Castile, expressed European assumptions that other peoples' lands could be allocated by agreement among Europeans, and the treaty bound no one else.

European maritime expansion did not begin global trade. The Indian Ocean had long supported a network of exchange among East Africa, Arabia, Persia, India, Southeast Asia, and China, carried by Arab, Persian, Gujarati, Malay, Chinese, and other merchants, and it included large cosmopolitan ports such as Calicut, Malacca, and Kilwa. The Portuguese, with armed ships and fortified bases, entered this system and sought to control parts of it, but they never dominated it, and they depended on local rulers, intermediaries, and financiers. The Ming and Qing empires, the Mughal Empire, the Ottoman Empire, and Safavid Persia remained far stronger than any European state in Asia. The Tokugawa regime in Japan confined European contact to controlled channels such as Dejima at Nagasaki. Until the eighteenth century, European merchants typically traded on terms set by Asian powers and paid for Asian goods largely with American silver.

That silver illustrates the integration of the new routes. The discovery of Potosí in the Andes in 1545 and of Mexican mines supplied bullion that moved to Spain, then onward to northern Europe and Asia, and the Manila galleons that began regular crossings of the Pacific in 1565 brought it to China, where silver was in demand. The Dutch United East India Company, established in 1602, and the English East India Company, chartered in 1600, were organized as joint stock institutions that combined commerce, finance, and armed force, as Chapter 71 described. These institutions, together with Atlantic ports such as Seville, Lisbon, Antwerp, Amsterdam, London, Bordeaux, and Nantes, changed the geography of European prosperity. Mediterranean centers did not collapse, but Atlantic ports gained relative importance, and states with Atlantic access acquired new sources of revenue and strategic rivalries over trade and territory, which fed into the wars treated in Chapter 80.

The commercial importance of the Indian Ocean requires careful chronological comparison. In the sixteenth century, Portuguese fleets could seize vessels, levy duties, and hold fortified ports, yet they lacked the manpower or resources to replace the trading networks that supplied Asian markets. Indian merchants, Gujarati shipowners, Arab and Persian traders, and rulers at coastal ports continued to determine much of what moved and at what price. In subsequent centuries, Dutch and English companies negotiated rights, fought rivals, and gradually acquired greater military and fiscal powers in certain territories. The transition from buying goods in established Asian markets to administering large areas of land was a historical development rather than a characteristic of all European commerce from its beginnings. It depended on specific regional struggles and alliances, and political outcomes were neither uniform nor predetermined.

The reorientation was gradual and uneven. Central and eastern European societies, including the Habsburg lands, Poland Lithuania, and Muscovy, remained connected primarily by land and river, and the Baltic grain and timber trade retained great importance. The Ottoman Empire continued to control the eastern Mediterranean routes into the seventeenth century. The effects of oceanic expansion within Europe were therefore mediated by region, social group, and political structure, and they shaped different European societies in different ways and at different times.

Conquest, Colonial Encounters, and the Unequal Formation of a Connected World

Chapters 69 through 73 examined the conquest of the Americas, Atlantic slavery, and the commercial and settler empires of Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, Britain, and France. This section assesses their significance for the international relationships within which Europe developed, leaving the analysis of empire and diaspora as historical systems to Chapter 142. The conquests of the sixteenth century in Mexico and Peru combined military force, alliances with Indigenous groups hostile to the dominant powers, and the catastrophic spread of Old World diseases among populations without prior exposure. Hernán Cortés's campaign against the Mexica, completed with the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521, relied heavily on Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous allies. Spanish authority in the Andes after the capture of the Inca ruler Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532 rested on exploitation of existing structures of tribute and labor as well as on violence.

Colonization imposed labor systems that rearranged populations. The encomienda and the mita extracted Indigenous labor, and Atlantic plantation agriculture, first in Portuguese Brazil and the Caribbean and then across the Americas, relied on the forced transportation of enslaved Africans. Modern estimates, which scholars refine as new records are analyzed, indicate that approximately 12.5 million Africans were embarked on transatlantic slave ships between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries, of whom approximately 10.7 million disembarked, mainly in the Americas. These reconstructed totals exclude the many who died before embarkation and distinguish departures from survivors of the crossing. European states, merchants, insurers, and financiers organized and profited from this trade, and African polities, merchants, and rulers participated, on terms that varied and that were shaped by European demand. The system therefore connected Europe, Africa, and the Americas, and the human cost was immense and permanent.

Claims of sovereignty must be distinguished from the political reality they described. European crowns and companies asserted title over territories on the basis of discovery, papal grants, treaties among themselves, or conquest, but in practice authority was frequently limited to forts, ports, and coastal enclaves, as along much of the African coast and in the trading posts of South and Southeast Asia, and in the interior it often depended on cooperation with local rulers. European colonizers relied on interpreters, intermediaries, soldiers, officials, and merchants drawn from local societies, and they confronted continuing resistance, including the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, the long Mapuche resistance in Chile, Maroon communities in Jamaica, Suriname, and Brazil, and many other movements. Indigenous, African, and Asian communities were historical actors who bargained, adapted, resisted, and sometimes shaped the terms of colonial rule.

The political consequences of European encounters also varied according to local conditions. On the Atlantic seaboard, settler expansion often produced sustained struggles over Indigenous land and political authority, with treaties, military campaigns, missionary institutions, and colonial legal systems defining unequal relationships. Elsewhere, European merchants initially depended on permission to trade from powerful sovereigns. Neither situation can be captured by treating a European map claim as proof of effective rule. Resistance also had different forms: direct military opposition, communities established by people escaping slavery, legal petitions, selective alliances, and the maintenance of local religious and political institutions. A global interpretation must account for these actions because they influenced the size, costs, and durability of imperial projects. Europeans made demands, but they seldom determined the terms of encounter alone.

For Europe, the important consequence was that its relations with the rest of the world became structurally unequal in specific zones and periods, while they remained reciprocal and often unequal in the opposite direction elsewhere. The silver, sugar, tobacco, and slave trade transformed finance and consumption at home. At the same time, Europeans continued to treat the Ottoman, Mughal, Chinese, and Japanese states as powers to be negotiated with. The formation of a connected world was therefore asymmetrical and regionally differentiated, and historians avoid describing the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries as a uniform era of European domination.

Multiple Perspectives

Global Encounters Seen from Different Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions based on patterns documented in many sources, not quotations from named individuals. The groups did not have equal power, and the descriptions do not imply moral equivalence.

From the perspective of an Iberian or Dutch crown official and merchant: Overseas expansion promised revenue, strategic advantage over rivals, and, for the Iberian monarchies, the extension of Christianity. Officials sought to convert claims into regular income, but they struggled with distance, local resistance, shortages of manpower, and corruption among their own agents.
From the perspective of an Indigenous community under colonial rule in the Americas: The arrival of colonizers meant epidemics, demands for tribute and labor, religious conversion campaigns, and displacement. Communities negotiated through the colonial courts, preserved languages and ritual practice, formed alliances, and in some cases rebelled, and many adapted European institutions to their own ends.
From the perspective of an enslaved African and of an African coastal ruler: For the enslaved, capture, forced transport, and plantation labor meant the destruction of families and communities and the construction of new ones under coercion, along with continual resistance in the form of flight, sabotage, and revolt. For African rulers and merchants who sold captives, the trade offered goods, weapons, and political advantage, although the demand it generated also intensified warfare and insecurity across wide regions.
From the perspective of an Asian imperial court and its merchants: Mughal, Qing, Safavid, and Japanese authorities generally treated Europeans as one set of foreign merchants among others. They granted trading privileges under conditions they defined, and they were often more concerned with internal and neighboring challenges than with the newcomers.

Commodities, Ecology, Consumption, and the Remaking of Everyday Life

Global exchange reached ordinary European households through food, drink, clothing, and work. The term Columbian Exchange describes the transfer of plants, animals, microbes, and people across the Atlantic after 1492. Potatoes and maize, domesticated in the Americas, eventually became staples in parts of Europe, although acceptance was slow and uneven. The potato, which can yield more calories per acre than many grain crops in poor soils, supported population growth in Ireland, Flanders, Prussia, and Russia in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and Ireland's dependence on it, together with the political and economic structure of landholding, made the potato blight of the 1840s catastrophic, an episode of famine and emigration that belongs to the nineteenth century history treated in Volume IV. Tomatoes, peppers, beans, and turkeys, meanwhile, entered European kitchens and farms more gradually, and each followed its own timing.

Consumer goods linked households to plantation labor. Sugar, once a luxury, became a common sweetener, and its consumption rose strongly in Britain across the eighteenth century, with tea and coffee as common vehicles. Tobacco, introduced from the Americas, created new trades and social customs. Coffeehouses, which first spread in the Ottoman world, became centers of news and commerce in London, Amsterdam, Vienna, and Paris and helped sustain the public sphere that Chapter 82 described. Cotton textiles, long produced and exported by India, were prized in Europe, and protective measures such as the English Calico Acts of 1700 and 1721 attempted to shield domestic producers. Later, British mills depended on raw cotton grown by enslaved people in the Americas, so the clothing of European workers was tied to coerced labor across the Atlantic.

Historians caution against exaggerating the speed or uniformity of these changes. A new commodity did not transform every society at once. Households in the Dutch Republic or England, with commercial agriculture and high levels of urbanization, incorporated imported goods earlier and more thoroughly than villages in the interior of Poland, Hungary, or the Balkans. Regional diets, fashion, and trading networks differed, and the evidence, which includes probate inventories, shop records, customs registers, and household accounts, shows significant variation among social ranks. The wealthy often adopted new goods first, though imitations and cheaper substitutes extended their reach.

The incorporation of new foods into European agriculture makes the gradual nature of exchange clear. Maize spread through some Mediterranean and Balkan farming systems, while the potato became important in parts of northern and eastern Europe only after a long history of local experimentation, adaptation, and resistance to unfamiliar crops. These plants affected food security and population change differently depending on climate, landholding, market access, and government policy. The same commodity could be a staple for a rural household, an object of urban trade, or a taxable product for a state. Ecological exchange therefore cannot be separated from social structures. It transformed possibilities for production and consumption, but institutions determined who could use those possibilities and who bore their risks.

Taken together, these changes show how global history can be observed in a European kitchen or workplace. A family in a northern European port might drink Caribbean sugared tea from porcelain made in China or imitated from Chinese models, wear cotton processed in Britain from American fiber, and work in a trade financed by colonial credit. Demand for such goods also shaped labor within Europe, encouraging households to increase market work in order to afford them, a development some historians have called an industrious revolution. The remaking of everyday life was thus both a European development and a global one.

Everyday Life and Social History

Global Commodities in European Households

In an eighteenth century western European town, a middle income household might own a teapot and cups, buy sugar from a grocer, wear printed cotton, and read newspapers in a coffeehouse. Probate inventories, the lists of goods drawn up at a person's death, show these items appearing more frequently across the century, and they indicate that consumption was socially graded, since the poor consumed smaller quantities and cheaper versions. In rural eastern and southern Europe, consumption of imported goods was typically lower, and local grains, dairy products, and regional drinks remained central.

Women's work was often at the center of these changes. Women managed household budgets, prepared food, spun and sewed, and, in many regions, worked in shops, markets, and textile production, so the new goods affected their labor as well as their purchases. Meanwhile, workers who processed sugar in refineries, loaded ships in ports, or operated textile machines were drawn into global supply chains without necessarily knowing where the raw materials originated. The history of the household therefore connects directly to the plantation, the ship, and the factory, and it reminds students that global history includes everyday experience.

Knowledge, Technology, Science, and Intellectual Exchange across Borders

European science and learning developed within wider networks of knowledge. The medieval translation movements described above brought Greek and Arabic mathematics, medicine, astronomy, optics, and philosophy into Latin Europe, and the Scientific Revolution of Chapter 81 built on that inheritance. Optical theory, including the work of Ibn al-Haytham, informed later European inquiry. Astronomers of the Islamic world, including the circle associated with the observatory at Maragha, developed mathematical models whose resemblance to techniques in Copernicus's work has led some historians to propose a line of transmission, though the route and the extent of dependence remain debated. Chapter 81 should be read with this wider setting in mind, because Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, and Newton worked within a tradition that had been enriched by sources from many places.

Printing, the universities, and scientific societies gave European knowledge distinctive institutional forms. Johannes Gutenberg's movable type press of about 1450 emerged after woodblock printing and movable type had been used in East Asia, including in Korea, and it spread rapidly because of European commercial and urban conditions and alphabetic scripts. Universities, academies, and scientific societies such as the Royal Society and the Académie des Sciences organized inquiry, correspondence, and publication. These institutions mattered, but they relied on observations, specimens, and information collected well beyond Europe. Navigation depended on instruments and charts that combined Mediterranean, Arab, Portuguese, and later Dutch and English experience, and cartography integrated information from pilots and informants of many origins.

The expansion of European trade and empire created new channels for gathering knowledge about nature. Jesuit missionaries in China transmitted astronomical and mathematical knowledge in both directions and produced translations and descriptions that informed European debates. Naturalists collected plants and animals in the Americas, Africa, and Asia, and botanical gardens such as Kew and the Jardin du Roi in Paris acted as clearinghouses for specimens. Indigenous and local knowledge was often decisive. The use of cinchona bark from the Andes to treat fever, which became the source of quinine, reflected Indigenous medical practice. The practice of inoculation against smallpox was known in the Ottoman world and was described to British audiences by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu in 1717, in a letter from Constantinople, before inoculation received wider adoption in Europe. In many cases, European institutions renamed, abstracted, and published such knowledge in ways that obscured its origins and the people who held it.

The circulation of knowledge also raises questions of authorship and credit. A plant specimen gathered through an Indigenous guide might enter a European botanical collection under the name of a European collector, while a remedy used locally for generations could be described in a metropolitan journal as a discovery. Conversely, local specialists could adapt foreign instruments and ideas for purposes not anticipated by their European interlocutors. Historians working with travel accounts, correspondence, technical manuals, specimens, and translated texts therefore ask whose observations entered the record and whose contributions were obscured. The spread of knowledge was neither a simple one-way transfer to Europe nor an exchange among equally powerful partners. The institutional ability to publish, classify, and claim authority over knowledge was itself unevenly distributed.

Historians therefore separate genuine European innovations from the claim that Europe developed on its own. Europeans did create new instruments, mathematical methods, institutions, and, by the nineteenth century, industrial technologies that changed the world. They also drew continually on sources elsewhere, as did every other society that has produced science. The Scientific Revolution is best understood as a set of developments whose European setting mattered a great deal, whose participants depended on networks that were global in range, and whose results circulated back out, in the same way that Chinese, Indian, Ottoman, and Persian scholars read, adapted, and challenged European works.

Division III · European Power and the Changing Global Order Section 144.9

Enlightenment, Universal Rights, and the Contradictions of European Expansion

The Enlightenment, treated in Chapters 82 through 86, produced arguments about natural rights, popular sovereignty, religious toleration, constitutional government, and the criticism of arbitrary power, and those arguments traveled widely. They were also entangled with the contradictions of the age. Several of the most influential writers wrote about liberty while living in societies that sustained slavery and colonial rule, and some, including Immanuel Kant and, in a different manner, Johann Friedrich Blumenbach, contributed to the development of racial classifications that later served to justify inequality. Enlightenment thought was not a single doctrine, and it included both defenders of empire and sharp critics of it, among them Denis Diderot and the Abbé Raynal's collaborators, who denounced colonial abuses and the slave trade.

The Atlantic revolutions put these ideas to the test. The American Revolution of 1776 to 1783 proclaimed natural rights while leaving slavery intact in the new republic. The French Revolution, treated in Chapters 88 and 89, issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789, and its debates over whether the rights applied to free people of color, to the enslaved, to women, and to Jews showed how contested the meaning of universality was. Olympe de Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Female Citizen of 1791 challenged the exclusion of women. The Revolution abolished slavery in the French colonies in 1794 under pressure from events in Saint Domingue, and Napoleon moved to restore it in 1802.

The Haitian Revolution, which began in 1791 and ended with the proclamation of independence on January 1, 1804, was the largest and most successful revolt of enslaved people in the Atlantic world. Leaders such as Toussaint Louverture and Jean Jacques Dessalines mobilized and transformed the language of rights, and they defeated French, Spanish, and British forces. The creation of Haiti showed that the principles of liberty and equality could be claimed by those whom European authorities had excluded, and its example was feared by slaveholders and celebrated by abolitionists. Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of its empire through legislation of 1833, and abolitionism grew through a combination of religious conviction, political mobilization, resistance by the enslaved, and economic change, a mixture that historians weigh differently.

The Haitian Revolution provides a particularly revealing test of claims to universal rights. People held in slavery in the French colony of Saint-Domingue acted upon and transformed revolutionary debates about citizenship and emancipation, ultimately defeating efforts to preserve or reimpose colonial domination and establishing Haiti in 1804. Their actions exposed the inconsistency between declarations of human rights and the continued property claims of slaveholders. Abolitionist movements also drew on religious organizing, enslaved peoples' testimony, economic arguments, and political mobilization across the Atlantic world. The history of universalism is therefore not only the history of ideas proclaimed in European capitals; it is also a history of people outside or beneath those political orders insisting that announced principles apply to them. The gap between principle and practice generated conflict that reshaped the principles themselves.

Colonized and formerly colonized thinkers appropriated, challenged, and reformulated European universalist claims. Reformers in India, such as Ram Mohan Roy, engaged with liberal and Christian ideas while criticizing colonial practice, and activists in the Americas, Africa, and Asia used the language of rights and self determination in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries to demand independence and equality. Liberty and equality were not uniquely European intellectual achievements, since comparable ideas existed in other traditions, and European governments seldom applied them consistently. The long history of the idea of universal rights is therefore the history of a principle whose meaning was extended through struggle by people who were often excluded from it at the outset.

Division III · European Power and the Changing Global Order Section 144.10

Industrialization, the Great Divergence, and the Sources of European Power

Chapters 93 through 95 described the Industrial Revolution in Britain and its uneven spread across Belgium, France, the German lands, and other regions. The transformation involved coal and steam power, mechanized textile production, iron and steel, railways, new forms of finance, and a growing urban labor force, and it changed the relative economic capacities of states. By the middle of the nineteenth century, Britain's industrial output was unmatched, and by the early twentieth century Germany and the United States were industrial rivals. Industrialization remained geographically uneven within Europe. Regions of Belgium, northern France, the Ruhr, Saxony, Bohemia, and northern Italy industrialized much earlier than most of Iberia, southern Italy, the Balkans, and the Russian Empire, which had pockets of rapid industrial development within a largely agrarian economy.

This divergence in economic capacity became globally significant in comparison with the large Asian economies. Until at least the eighteenth century, the most developed regions of China, India, and the Ottoman and Persian worlds sustained high levels of production, commerce, and urban life, and historians debate when and why the economic performance of northwestern Europe began to pull ahead. The explanations include coal and the availability of energy, technological and scientific culture, institutions that protected property and encouraged investment, high wages that made labor saving machines profitable, the state's fiscal and military capacities, colonial markets and raw materials, and the effects of warfare and political competition. No one factor suffices.

Comparison requires choosing meaningful economic units and acknowledging the uncertainty of the data. The industrializing districts of England cannot simply be compared with the average conditions of the entire Qing Empire, any more than the prosperous Lower Yangzi region should stand for all of China. Historians compare household incomes, agricultural productivity, urban wages, market integration, consumption, and the relative cost of labor and fuel, but surviving records are uneven and often measure different populations. Some reconstructions suggest relatively high living standards in particular Asian regions before industrialization, while others argue for an earlier northwestern European advantage. Both possibilities are compatible with the undisputed fact that mechanized industrial growth subsequently widened the gap. The chronology of divergence and the causes of the acceleration should be treated as related but distinct questions.

Energy and production offer another way of testing explanations. In Britain, accessible coalfields near manufacturing districts and waterways supported uses of steam that would have been more expensive in places where fuel had to travel farther. Robert Allen's high-wage and cheap-energy explanation asks why replacing workers with machinery made economic sense under British factor prices, while Joel Mokyr emphasizes the accumulation and circulation of practical knowledge. Kenneth Pomeranz places greater weight on ecological constraints and resources drawn from the Americas. None of these accounts excludes political institutions or imperial markets, but they assign different causal importance to them. A rigorous comparison asks whether an explanation works for both the initial British case and the later industrialization of Belgium, Germany, the United States, and Japan, which did not possess identical conditions.

Colonial relationships further complicate the comparison. British textile factories increasingly used cotton produced by enslaved workers in the Americas, yet the industrial advantage involved more than raw cotton supplies: mechanical spinning, transport, investment, factory organization, marketing, and state policy also mattered. In India, British manufactured textiles competed with longstanding skilled production, and political power changed tariffs, purchasing, and commercial opportunities. The relationship between empire and industry was therefore dynamic. Industrial capacity could facilitate conquest, while conquest and privileged trade could in turn alter markets and resources available to manufacturers. Distinguishing these directions of causation prevents the chapter from replacing a myth of self-sufficient European achievement with an equally simplistic theory of external extraction as the sole cause.

Industrialization also depended on connections beyond Europe. Cotton mills, as noted above, drew raw material from slave plantations in the Americas, and their products were sold in markets from India to West Africa. Colonial territories supplied sugar, timber, grain, and capital opportunities, and shipping, insurance, and banking sustained trade. At the same time, the industrial transformation involved internal developments, including agricultural improvement, the availability of coal in Britain, new mechanical skills, and a legal and financial system favorable to enterprise. A responsible account therefore avoids two simplifications, one attributing industrialization to European cultural superiority and the other reducing it to the profits of empire and slavery.

Historical Debate

Why Did Industrialization and the Great Divergence Occur?

The question: Why did sustained industrial growth begin in parts of northwestern Europe, especially Britain, and why did the economic gap between those regions and the major Asian economies widen from the late eighteenth century? Historians use the term Great Divergence for this widening gap, and they disagree about when it began and what caused it.

Interpretation A, contingent circumstances and global connections: Kenneth Pomeranz, in The Great Divergence (2000), argued that the most advanced regions of Europe and Asia were broadly comparable until the late eighteenth century and that Britain's later advantage owed much to accessible coal and to the resources of the Americas, which relieved land and resource constraints. Other scholars, including Prasannan Parthasarathi, emphasize competition between British and Indian textile producers and the policies of states.
Interpretation B, institutions, knowledge, factor prices, and earlier divergence: Other scholars place more weight on internal European factors. Robert Allen argues that high wages and cheap energy made mechanization profitable in Britain. Joel Mokyr stresses a culture of useful knowledge and scientific exchange. Others point to institutions, state capacity, and, on the basis of quantitative estimates of income and population, argue that Europe's advantage appeared earlier than Pomeranz proposed.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on estimates of wages, prices, urbanization, agricultural productivity, energy use, and trade, together with studies of institutions and technological change. The data are difficult to compare across regions and periods, and many figures are scholarly estimates, so conclusions often rest on how the estimates are constructed and which comparisons are chosen.

Why it matters: The debate affects whether European industrialization is viewed as the expression of a distinctive European path or as a contingent outcome of global interaction, and it shapes the assessment of how far later European predominance was deserved, inevitable, or reversible. It also bears on the interpretation of the twenty first century growth of Asian economies, which some historians regard as a return toward earlier proportions.

Student task: Select one factor mentioned in either interpretation, for example coal, wages, institutions, or colonial resources, and explain what additional kind of evidence would help determine its importance. Then explain why a single cause cannot account for the divergence.

Division III · European Power and the Changing Global Order Section 144.11

European Imperial Predominance, Global Institutions, and Resistance

In the nineteenth century, a small group of European states, above all Britain and France and later Germany, together with Russia in its own sphere, exercised an extraordinary degree of influence over world affairs. The sources of that influence were industrial capacity, steam powered warships, improved rifles and artillery, quinine and other medical advances, telegraphs, railways, and the financial markets of London and Paris. Naval power allowed the British to project force across the oceans, and imperial rivalry shaped diplomacy, as Chapters 104 through 107 described. The predominance was concentrated, because it belonged to particular states, and it was temporary, because other powers, including the United States and Japan, were industrializing quickly.

Influence took several forms. Formal colonial conquest, which Chapter 104 treated in the case of Africa and the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885, was one. Another was informal empire, in which European states used trade treaties, extraterritorial legal privileges, naval demonstrations, and financial leverage to secure advantages without annexing territory. The Opium Wars in China, ending with the Treaty of Nanking in 1842 and renewed conflict in 1856 to 1860, opened Chinese ports and imposed concessions. The Ottoman Public Debt Administration, established in 1881 after the state's default, placed part of the empire's revenue under foreign creditor supervision. British occupation of Egypt in 1882 followed financial and strategic crises connected with the Suez Canal, completed in 1869. These cases show that the international hierarchy was enforced through finance and law as well as conquest.

European states also built the framework of international law and trade. Doctrines about sovereignty, the standard of civilization, and the right of intervention were developed to regulate relations among Europeans and between Europeans and others, and they were often applied unequally, treating non European states as lacking full sovereignty. The gold standard, the telegraph, and shipping conventions integrated global markets, and European settlers, soldiers, administrators, and missionaries moved around the world in large numbers. Territorial sovereignty and political hierarchy were thus linked, and the consequences included the redrawing of boundaries without regard to local political and social arrangements, a legacy examined in Chapters 104, 105, and 128.

European predominance should also be measured against evidence of limits and reversals. Britain could compel commercial concessions after the Opium Wars, yet even major imperial powers faced resistance that required costly garrisons, alliances, and administrative compromises. The Indian uprising of 1857 challenged East India Company rule and led to direct crown government in 1858, demonstrating that imperial governance could be radically reorganized by resistance. Japan's victory over Russia in 1904 to 1905 showed that industrial military power was not an exclusively European possession. African states and political movements developed their own strategies of warfare, negotiation, and reform, while industrial and constitutional experiments emerged beyond European rule. These comparisons situate Europe's nineteenth-century advantages as real but contingent, uneven, and challenged.

Resistance, adaptation, and state building outside Europe shaped the outcome. In Japan, the Meiji reforms after 1868 built a centralized state and industrial base, and Japan's victory over Russia in 1904 to 1905 shocked European observers. Ethiopia defeated an Italian army at Adwa in 1896. Ottoman and Egyptian reformers, Chinese self strengthening advocates, and Indian nationalists used European institutions and ideas for their own ends. The Indian Rebellion of 1857, the Maji Maji rebellion in German East Africa in 1905 to 1907, and numerous other revolts showed that colonial rule rested on contested ground. European predominance depended on the weakness, divisions, or accommodation of others, and where those conditions changed it could not be maintained.

Division III · European Power and the Changing Global Order Section 144.12

The World Wars and the Destruction of European Global Predominance

The First World War began in Europe in 1914 and expanded through empires, alliances, and economic connections. The Second World War had interconnected but distinct origins in Asia and Europe, including Japan's conquest of Manchuria in 1931, the full-scale Sino-Japanese War from July 1937, and Germany's invasion of Poland in September 1939. Both conflicts developed into global wars through imperial systems, alliances, and the actions of powers outside Europe. In the First World War, European colonial powers mobilized resources and people from their territories. More than a million Indian soldiers served overseas, and France recruited large numbers of soldiers and laborers from West Africa, North Africa, and Indochina. British and French authorities recruited approximately 140,000 Chinese laborers for service mainly in France and Belgium, including about 100,000 in the British Chinese Labour Corps and about 40,000 under French arrangements. The Ottoman Empire, Japan, and the United States also became combatants. The war destroyed four dynastic empires, the Romanov, Habsburg, Hohenzollern, and Ottoman, and the peace settlements of 1919 created new states in central and eastern Europe while extending European mandates over former Ottoman and German territories, as Chapter 113 described. Wilsonian and Bolshevik appeals to self determination inspired anticolonial movements, though the peace conference largely withheld their application to colonized peoples.

The interwar period delivered further blows, including the Great Depression, which reduced trade and strained colonial economies, and the rise of fascism and Nazism, which put Europe on the path to a second world war. The Second World War was more destructive still. It mobilized colonial populations again, with over two and a half million Indian volunteers in the British Indian Army and tens of thousands from Africa, the Caribbean, and elsewhere, and it ended in the occupation of Germany, the exhaustion of Britain and France, and the dominance of the United States and the Soviet Union. Occupation of European colonial possessions by Japan in 1941 to 1945 shattered the prestige of European rule in Southeast Asia, and wartime promises, economic dislocation, and the strengthening of nationalist movements accelerated the end of empire.

The decline of European global predominance must be specified carefully. It was relative, since the United States and the Soviet Union acquired greater military and economic weight, and Britain and France lost the ability to sustain their empires, as the Suez crisis of 1956 dramatically showed. It was not a disappearance of European influence. Western Europe's economies recovered, as Chapter 126 described, European states remained major trading partners, financial centers, and sources of technology, and European languages, legal systems, and cultural forms continued to operate worldwide. Eastern Europe, meanwhile, came under Soviet influence, so the experience of postwar Europe was not uniform.

The global chronology of the Second World War particularly resists an exclusively European frame. Japan's expansion in Manchuria and China preceded the outbreak of war in Europe in 1939, and Chinese resistance consumed enormous military and civilian resources before the Pacific War widened in December 1941. European powers simultaneously fought to defend imperial possessions in Asia, Africa, and the Middle East, and colonial troops and workers participated in fronts far from their homes. The surrender of Japan in 1945 created openings for independence movements in territories previously held by European states, but outcomes differed: Indonesia and Vietnam experienced prolonged struggles, while other transitions involved negotiation and political bargaining. The wars were global not merely because European conflicts spread outward, but because different regional conflicts, empires, and political struggles became connected.

The Holocaust, treated in Chapter 121, must remain central to any assessment of twentieth century European history. Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered about six million Jews, along with large numbers of Roma, Soviet prisoners of war, people with disabilities, Poles, and other victims, through shootings, ghettos, forced labor, and extermination camps. The genocide was carried out within Europe by European states and individuals, and it was enabled by antisemitism, racial ideology, bureaucratic organization, and the participation or acquiescence of many. It ended the long history of Jewish life in many regions of central and eastern Europe, and it became a foundational reference for postwar law and memory, including the development of the concept of genocide and the Nuremberg trials. A history of Europe's global position that omitted it would misrepresent both the continent's twentieth century and the moral questions that its history raises.

Division IV · Europe's Reorganization in a Post-Imperial World Section 144.13

Cold War Europe within a Divided International System

After 1945, Europe was divided between a zone in which the Soviet Union exercised decisive influence and a zone tied to the United States, as Chapters 124 through 126 explained. Germany was occupied in four zones, and the division hardened with the Berlin blockade of 1948 to 1949 and the creation of the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic in 1949. The Truman Doctrine of 1947, the Marshall Plan of 1948 to 1952, and the formation of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization in April 1949 tied western Europe to the United States economically and strategically. In the east, communist governments consolidated power with Soviet backing, the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance coordinated economic relations, and the Warsaw Pact, created in 1955, institutionalized military integration.

The reality was more varied than a simple division suggests. Yugoslavia broke with Stalin in 1948 and pursued an independent communist path that later helped to inspire the Non-Aligned Movement. Finland, Austria after the State Treaty of 1955, Sweden, Switzerland, and Ireland maintained forms of neutrality. Eastern European states differed in their economies, policies toward religion and agriculture, and relations with Moscow, as the uprisings in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 and the later Solidarity movement in Poland, treated in Chapters 129 and 132, showed. Within western Europe, security dependence on the United States coexisted with different national policies, including France's withdrawal from NATO's integrated command in 1966.

The division of Europe also generated disputes within each bloc about how much freedom governments could exercise. The Hungarian uprising of 1956 and the suppression of reform in Czechoslovakia in 1968 revealed the limits of sovereign choice under Soviet predominance. In the West, the United States was indispensable to defense planning, but European governments disagreed over nuclear weapons, colonial wars, and relations with communist states. West Germany's Ostpolitik sought agreements with eastern neighbors, and the Helsinki Final Act of 1975 combined recognition of existing frontiers with commitments relating to human rights and contacts across borders. These agreements did not end geopolitical rivalry, yet their provisions subsequently gave citizens and opposition movements a language for holding governments to their undertakings. Military division and diplomatic communication operated simultaneously.

The Cold War further reveals the continuing importance of European empires after 1945. The wars in Indochina and Algeria were shaped by decolonization and domestic political conflict as well as by competition for superpower support. Newly independent governments did not invariably follow the priorities of either Washington or Moscow, and nonalignment often meant bargaining with both rather than withdrawal from international politics. Western European economic recovery depended partly on American aid and security guarantees, but it also involved domestic reforms and inter-European trade. In the Soviet sphere, reconstruction and industrialization followed different political rules and resource arrangements. A comparison that treats the European Cold War solely as a stand-off between two armed camps overlooks the economic, colonial, and social relationships that tied it to wider global transformations.

The Cold War cannot be understood within Europe alone. The superpower rivalry extended to Korea, Vietnam, Cuba, the Middle East, and Africa, and anticolonial movements and newly independent states often sought to avoid alignment with either bloc. Europe's relative immobility in the decades after 1949, with its borders largely stable and its military confrontation deterred by nuclear weapons, contrasted with intense conflict elsewhere. Strategic decisions about nuclear deployment, alliance commitments, and economic assistance were made in Washington and Moscow as well as in European capitals, and European history became inseparable from the actions of powers whose influence extended far beyond the continent.

The industrial rise of the United States and Russia, the weakening of European empires by anticolonial movements and by the First World War, and the ideological conflict between liberal capitalist and communist systems since 1917.

The destruction of Germany and the exhaustion of Britain and France in 1945, disagreements among the Allies over Germany and eastern Europe, and the perceived need for American economic support in western Europe.

The occupation and division of Germany, the formation of rival economic and military blocs, and the incorporation of eastern Europe into a Soviet dominated system.

American aid and security guarantees in the west, Soviet control and communist rule in the east, and the loss of independent great power status for Britain and France.

Long-term consequences

Decades of division, the origins of western European integration, welfare state expansion, a postponed reckoning in the east with the experience of occupation, and a legacy of political difference after 1989.

Historians disagree about the relative responsibility of the superpowers, the role of ideology compared with security concerns, and European agency, because European leaders sought, as well as accepted, American involvement and shaped the institutions that resulted.

Historical complexity: The division of Europe was the result of the interaction of wartime circumstances, ideological conflict, and decisions by many actors. It was not a single cause with a predetermined result.

Three Postwar Visions: The UN Charter (1945), the Schuman Declaration (1950), and the Bandung Communiqué (1955)

Context: These three documents represent different answers to the question of how the postwar world should be organized. They were created by different authors, for different audiences, and under different circumstances. Students should read them in full in reliable published editions. The excerpts here are brief or are labeled paraphrases, and the paraphrases are not quotations.

Document 1, Charter of the United Nations · Date: signed at San Francisco, June 26, 1945, in force October 24, 1945 · Type: multilateral treaty and founding legal text, drafted by delegations of the victorious and associated states

Brief quotation (English text of the Preamble) The Preamble states the determination "to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war." Paraphrase of further content: the Charter establishes the principle of the sovereign equality of member states, restricts the use of force, creates a Security Council in which five powers hold permanent seats and vetoes, and includes provisions on the administration of non self governing territories.

Document 2, Schuman Declaration · Date: May 9, 1950, Paris · Type: a public statement issued by the French foreign minister, Robert Schuman, a proposal prepared with Jean Monnet's team

Paraphrase and brief quotation (translation) The declaration proposes placing French and German coal and steel production under a common High Authority, open to other European countries. It says Europe would be built through concrete achievements that create what the text calls "de facto solidarity," and presents the plan as a means of making war between France and Germany materially impossible. It also envisages European resources supporting the development of Africa, illustrating the colonial assumptions still present in postwar integration proposals.

Document 3, Final Communiqué of the Asian-African Conference · Date: Bandung, Indonesia, April 18 to 24, 1955 · Type: a joint statement issued by representatives of twenty nine Asian and African governments

Paraphrase only The communiqué, which includes a declaration on world peace and cooperation, condemns colonialism, racial discrimination, and the domination of peoples by foreign powers, affirms respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, supports cultural and economic cooperation among participants, and calls for peaceful settlement of disputes. It was issued without the participation of European colonial powers or the superpowers.

Analysis: The Charter expresses a global legal order built on state sovereignty and collective security, but its drafters were dominated by the great powers, and it left colonial empires largely in place while providing a framework that anticolonial movements later invoked. The Schuman Declaration offers a regional strategy of functional cooperation, aimed at resolving a specific European problem, Franco-German rivalry, through economic interdependence, and it expressly described the development of Africa as one of Europe's tasks. That language reflected the continuing colonial context of several prospective member states, a relationship that the declaration did not examine from the viewpoint of colonized populations. The Bandung communiqué articulates a vision of international relations from the perspective of states that had recently been, or still were, under colonial rule, and it shows how many participants hoped to stand outside the Cold War blocs. Taken together, the documents show that the postwar order was a matter of competing proposals. Each has limits as evidence. None shows how ordinary people experienced the arrangements it described, and each reflects the interests and rhetorical aims of its authors.

Division IV · Europe's Reorganization in a Post-Imperial World Section 144.14

Decolonization, Migration, and the Remaking of Europe's Global Relationships

Chapter 128 examined decolonization in detail. Its consequences for Europe's global relationships can be summarized here. The independence of India and Pakistan in 1947, Indonesia in 1949, and a succession of African and Asian states between the 1950s and the 1970s ended the formal empires of Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Portugal. The processes differed sharply. Britain negotiated most transfers of power, although with violent episodes in Kenya, Malaya, and Cyprus. France fought wars in Indochina, ending in 1954, and Algeria, ending with independence in 1962 after a brutal conflict, whereas most French sub-Saharan African territories became independent in 1960 through negotiation. Portugal, under an authoritarian regime, held its African colonies until the Carnation Revolution of 1974 led to independence in 1975. Each transition left different legacies of borders, institutions, and economic dependence.

European states adjusted to the loss of formal control while sustaining many relationships. The British Commonwealth, the French community of cooperation and its monetary arrangements, such as the CFA franc zone, trade ties, security agreements, development assistance, and cultural institutions all preserved forms of connection, and critics sometimes described these as neocolonial, because they continued unequal dependence. Newly independent states, for their part, pursued their own strategies, including nonalignment, regional organizations, and diversified partnerships. The central historical question is how Europe adapted to a world in which formerly colonized societies exercised independent political authority while economic and institutional inequalities often persisted.

Migration reshaped European societies. Colonial and postcolonial ties produced flows of people from the Caribbean, South Asia, and Africa to Britain, from North and West Africa and Indochina to France, from Indonesia and the Caribbean to the Netherlands, from Portuguese Africa to Portugal, and from many places to Belgium. The arrival of the Empire Windrush in 1948 and the British Nationality Act of the same year are often used as symbols of the first British postwar migration, which later governments restricted through legislation such as the Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962. Algeria's independence prompted the departure of several hundred thousand European settlers and of Algerians who had supported France to the metropole. West Germany, which did not have a large postwar colonial empire, recruited temporary labor through agreements such as the 1961 agreement with Turkey, and when recruitment stopped in 1973, many workers stayed and were joined by families, as Chapter 137 discussed.

The end of formal empire changed European foreign policy without erasing unequal relationships. Governments and businesses retained trade connections, ownership interests, transport networks, educational institutions, and sometimes military bases in territories that had become independent. Newly sovereign states negotiated the terms of these relationships and developed alternative alliances, regional organizations, and claims to control resources. At the same time, migration linked former colonies to metropolitan labor markets and welfare systems. The experiences of people arriving from the Caribbean, North Africa, South Asia, and other regions differed according to citizenship law, recruitment arrangements, language, and discrimination. Their children and grandchildren were not simply temporary outsiders in the societies where they lived; they became participants in the redefinition of European culture and politics. The history of Europe's post-imperial position consequently includes transformations within European cities as well as diplomatic change overseas.

The demographic consequences were wide. New communities brought languages, religions, cuisines, and political experiences, and they became part of European social and political life, while also encountering discrimination, housing and labor market inequalities, and contested claims about citizenship and belonging. These developments raised questions about national identity, race, and the memory of empire that remain unresolved. They also showed that European societies had been formed in relation to the wider world for centuries, and that postwar migration continued, in new forms, a long history of movement between Europe and other regions.

Division IV · Europe's Reorganization in a Post-Imperial World Section 144.15

European Integration, International Cooperation, and the Reinvention of Regional Power

Integration began as an effort to reorganize relations among European states after two devastating wars, as Chapter 133 explained. The Schuman Declaration of May 9, 1950 led to the Treaty of Paris of April 18, 1951, which created the European Coal and Steel Community among France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. The Treaties of Rome, signed on March 25, 1957, established the European Economic Community and Euratom. The founding states pursued several objectives, including economic recovery, the containment of German power through shared institutions, access to markets, and security in the Cold War context. Their motives were mixed, and integration was not the inevitable result of postwar conditions, since other proposals, such as a European Defence Community, failed in 1954.

The European Communities expanded in stages. The United Kingdom, Ireland, and Denmark joined in 1973, Greece in 1981, and Spain and Portugal in 1986, after the end of their authoritarian regimes, as Chapter 131 described. The Single European Act of 1986 aimed at a single market. The Maastricht Treaty, signed on February 7, 1992 and in force from November 1, 1993, created the European Union, introduced European citizenship, and set the framework for monetary union, and the euro was introduced for accounting in 1999 and as notes and coins in 2002, as Chapter 134 described. After 1989, enlargement brought in ten states in 2004, including eight formerly communist ones, followed by Bulgaria and Romania in 2007 and Croatia in 2013, and the Lisbon Treaty entered into force on December 1, 2009.

Integration is distinct from the formation of a single European nation or civilization. The Union consists of sovereign states that have transferred specific powers to common institutions, including the European Commission, the Council, the European Parliament, and the Court of Justice, and it coexists with national identities, languages, and political systems. It also differs from other European international bodies. The Council of Europe, founded in 1949, is a separate organization concerned with human rights, democracy, and the rule of law, with a much larger membership than the Union and the European Convention on Human Rights as a central instrument, and it is not a constituent institution of the European Union. NATO, created in 1949, is a military alliance that includes North American members. The Schengen arrangements, which abolished many border checks among participating states, and the eurozone cover overlapping but not identical groups of states.

The early Communities also emerged while several founders retained colonial territories. The Schuman Declaration explicitly referred to African development as an objective that greater European resources could serve, and the Treaty of Rome contained arrangements concerning overseas countries and territories linked to member states. These provisions remind students that European integration and decolonization were overlapping historical processes, not separate eras. Later external trade and development relationships involved independent states negotiating with European institutions, often against unequal economic backgrounds. Evaluating European integration thus requires two perspectives: the effort to reconcile former European adversaries and the changing position of a regional economic bloc within wider structures of trade and political authority.

Integration must be analyzed in relation to globalization and international order. The European Union is among the world's largest trading blocs and a significant regulatory power, and its common commercial policy shapes relations with other regions. Its enlargement has raised questions about the eastern limits of Europe, and its development has prompted debates about democratic legitimacy, the limits of shared sovereignty, and the balance between national and common authority, which Chapters 134, 138, and 139 examined. Historians treat these debates as continuing features of an institution that has been repeatedly modified, challenged, and renegotiated.

Historical Vocabulary
Supranational Authority, Integration, and International Cooperation

Supranational authority refers to institutions that can make decisions binding on member states within defined areas, in contrast with intergovernmental arrangements, in which states cooperate while retaining final decision making power. The European Union combines both elements. Integration denotes the process by which states link their economies, laws, and institutions, and it can proceed in some policy areas while stalling in others. International cooperation is a wider term that covers treaties, alliances, and organizations of all kinds, including the United Nations and NATO. Students should not use these terms interchangeably, and they should not assume that integration implied the creation of a European superstate, a goal some advocates favored and many opponents feared but which the treaties did not establish.

Europe after 1989: Globalization, Security, and International Interdependence

The opening of the Berlin Wall on November 9, 1989, German unification on October 3, 1990, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991 ended the division of Europe, as Chapter 132 described. Post communist states pursued market reforms and democratization, with varying results, as Chapter 136 explained, and many joined NATO and the European Union. The Yugoslav wars of 1991 to 2001, including the siege of Sarajevo and the killing of more than eight thousand Bosniak men and boys at Srebrenica in July 1995, showed that nationalism and violence could resurface within Europe, and they led to international intervention and a reconsideration of European security structures, treated in Chapter 135. These events complicated expectations that the end of the Cold War would produce a settled continental order.

Economic globalization linked European economies more closely to world markets, to Chinese manufacturing, to American technology and finance, and to global supply chains and energy markets. The global financial crisis of 2008 and the eurozone sovereign debt crisis that began in 2010, notably in Greece, exposed the vulnerability of the common currency and provoked austerity policies, political backlash, and debates about solidarity, as Chapter 138 examined. The United Kingdom held a referendum on June 23, 2016, voted to leave the European Union, and ceased to be a member on January 31, 2020, with a transition period ending on December 31, 2020 and a trade agreement applying from January 1, 2021. The movements of refugees in 2015 and 2016, debates over migration, and the growth of populist parties, discussed in Chapter 137, accompanied these crises.

Security reopened as a central question. Russia annexed Crimea in March 2014 and supported armed separatists in eastern Ukraine, and on February 24, 2022 it began a full scale invasion of Ukraine, which Chapter 139 narrated. The invasion prompted large scale sanctions, military assistance to Ukraine from many European states and the United States, the reduction of European dependence on Russian energy, the granting of European Union candidate status to Ukraine in June 2022, the formal opening of Ukraine's EU accession negotiations in June 2024, and the accession of Finland to NATO in April 2023 and Sweden in March 2024. The EU and Ukraine opened negotiations on the fundamentals cluster in June 2026, external relations in July, and the internal market and competitiveness clusters in October 2026; these were stages in a continuing process, not an accession or a guarantee of its outcome. Students should treat these as ongoing developments as of this chapter's cutoff of November 20, 2026. Their outcomes and settlement cannot yet be written as completed history, and this chapter does not narrate or predict them. They belong within a longer history of contested sovereignty and borders in eastern Europe, in which empires, states, and peoples have repeatedly disputed territories such as Ukraine.

The European Union's enlargement and security debates also made the contrast between geography and institutional membership especially visible. EU candidate status and accession negotiations are legal-political procedures governed by stated criteria and decisions of member governments, not automatic recognition of a territory as culturally European. Ukraine's negotiations in 2026 illustrated the distinction: the opening of several thematic negotiating clusters marked procedural progress, while remaining clusters, reforms, and the final terms of membership had not been resolved by the information cutoff. Other European or partly European states maintained different kinds of association with the Union. The institutional map therefore remained changeable, and it could not be used as a timeless definition of the continent.

Europe's relations with other regions have also changed. Its ties with the United States have been shaped by the NATO alliance, trade, technology, and periodic disagreements over security burdens and economic policy. Its trade and investment relationship with China has grown while becoming a source of strategic debate. Its relations with Mediterranean and African neighbors involve migration, energy, development, and security. European climate policy, which includes commitments under the Paris Agreement of 2015 and the European Green Deal announced in 2019, reflects shared environmental concerns and also the connection between energy security and foreign policy. The picture is one of changing patterns of dependence and cooperation in trade, technology, finance, energy, and security, and the section describes the historical circumstances of contemporary Europe without forecasting its future.

Division V · Reconsidering European History and European Identity Section 144.17

Europe's Boundaries and Internal Diversity across Historical Time

The preceding sections allow a comparison among the ways Europe has been defined. In the ancient Mediterranean, Europe was a geographic division of a world centered on an inland sea, and the people who lived around that sea had overlapping connections. In the medieval period, belonging was defined by religion and by local and dynastic ties, so that Latin Christians, Orthodox Christians, Muslims, and Jews lived within and across what later became Europe's boundaries. Early modern dynastic states defined themselves through monarchy, law, and confession, and Europe was a system of states and a community of diplomacy. In the nineteenth century, national identities came to the forefront, as Chapters 97 and 100 explained. In the twentieth and twenty first centuries, institutions such as the Council of Europe and the European Union have given Europe a new political form, which includes only some of the continent's states.

Internal diversity is a central historical characteristic of Europe and not a recent consequence of migration. The languages of Europe include Romance, Germanic, Slavic, Celtic, Baltic, Finno-Ugric, Greek, Albanian, Turkic, and Basque, among others, and Latin, Greek, Church Slavonic, Hebrew, Yiddish, Arabic, and Ottoman Turkish were all historically significant. Religions included multiple Christian traditions, Judaism, and Islam, and the experiences of Roma, Jews, Muslims, and other minorities, treated across many chapters, belong to the center of European history. Regional cultures, borderlands such as Alsace Lorraine, Galicia, Silesia, the Adriatic, and the Black Sea littoral, and multi ethnic empires like the Habsburg and Ottoman states produced societies in which people moved among several languages and loyalties. The modern linkage of one language, one nation, and one state is a product of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and was often imposed through forced assimilation or population exchange, as the history of the twentieth century showed.

Borderland communities also show how identification could be layered in everyday life. A person might use one language in a household, another in commerce, and a third in administration; religious affiliation might connect them to communities across a frontier while a dynasty claimed their political allegiance. State-building projects sometimes encouraged this plurality and at other times attempted to standardize schooling, language, names, and citizenship. The history of the Balkans, Habsburg lands, the Baltic, and Iberia shows that cultural identities did not automatically map onto the borders drawn by states. Likewise, modern migrant and minority communities may combine local, national, religious, and European affiliations without those identifications being mutually exclusive. Historical comparisons become misleading when one of these layers is treated as the only authentic form of belonging.

A distinction must also be drawn between historical belonging and contemporary institutional membership. A society that has participated in European history for a millennium may not be a member of the European Union, and membership in the Union does not by itself define who is European. Norway, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, Serbia, Ukraine, and Turkey, for example, have different relationships to the Union and different histories of connection with the continent. Treating membership as a test of belonging would flatten the evidence. European identity has also been constructed through comparisons with those described as outsiders, such as Muslims, Mongols, Ottomans, colonized peoples, and, in many periods, Jews and Roma, and the exclusion of such groups helped define what Europeans said they were. Recognizing this process is part of understanding how identities form.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Competing Definitions of Europe across Historical Periods

What Changed

  • The principal definitions shifted from a geographic division in antiquity to Christendom in the Middle Ages, a state system in the early modern period, a civilization and a set of nations in the nineteenth century, and an institutional project after 1950
  • The relationship between Europe and the wider world moved from exchange among comparable partners to unequal colonial and commercial relationships and then to postcolonial interdependence
  • The populations regarded as European expanded and contracted, and the definition of who counted as European was repeatedly contested

What Remained Continuous

  • The ambiguity of the eastern boundary and the uncertain place of Russia, the Caucasus, and the Ottoman and Turkish world
  • Linguistic, religious, and regional diversity and the presence of minority communities
  • Connection with Africa, Asia, and, from the sixteenth century, the Americas through trade, migration, and conflict

Who experienced change most directly? Populations in borderlands and colonial territories, minorities whose status depended on shifting definitions of belonging, and migrants experienced the changes most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural populations in regions with stable agricultural economies and local identities often experienced greater continuity in daily life, even as states and empires around them changed. Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that no single definition of Europe is valid for all periods.

Division V · Reconsidering European History and European Identity Section 144.18

European Influence, Relative Decline, and Global Interdependence

Assessing Europe's position requires specifying what kind of influence is under discussion. Military and territorial power, economic output, technological leadership, diplomatic weight, and cultural reach do not move together. In the late nineteenth century, a few European states held a combination of all of these. Since the world wars, the distribution has changed. The United States and the Soviet Union held military predominance during the Cold War, and the economies of East Asia, and China in particular, grew rapidly from the late twentieth century, so Europe's share of world output and population fell, although the figures depend on the measure used and on whether Europe is treated as one unit or many.

Relative decline should not be confused with universal economic decline or institutional disappearance. European societies remain among the wealthiest in the world, and they sustain major universities, research institutions, industries, and cultural centers. European states and the Union participate extensively in international organizations, trade agreements, development policy, scientific collaboration, education exchanges, and diplomacy, and European law and regulation affect firms and governments far beyond the continent. At the same time, Europeans face demographic aging, dependence on external energy and technology, and uncertainty about security, so the picture contains strengths and vulnerabilities.

Distinctions among actors matter. Britain, France, Germany, Poland, Italy, Sweden, and Turkey possess different capacities, interests, and alliances, and the European Union acts as a collective in trade and regulation but not in defense or, in most respects, in foreign policy, where unanimity among members is usually required. The wider continent includes states outside the Union with their own priorities. Generalizing about Europe's power without specifying which Europe is intended therefore produces confusion.

An evaluation of economic weight needs to specify its indicator. A region's share of world output, total trade, population, or industrial exports answers a different question from output per person or standards of living. Exchange-rate comparisons and purchasing-power comparisons can produce different assessments, and both are sensitive to the definition of the region being measured. The European Union is not identical to the whole continent, and its membership has changed over time. A falling share of world production may result partly from rapid growth elsewhere rather than a fall in European production. At the same time, some European regions and social groups have experienced industrial closures, unemployment, or financial shocks, so aggregate prosperity does not make every local trajectory favorable. Reliable analysis keeps relative global position, domestic living conditions, and distributional inequality distinct.

Different forms of influence also operate through different institutions. France and the United Kingdom retain permanent seats on the United Nations Security Council and possess nuclear forces, while other European states rely more heavily on alliances and collective institutions. The European Union negotiates common trade policy and develops rules that affect international producers seeking access to its market, but it is not a unitary military state and its common foreign policy has different decision procedures from its trade policy. European scientific and cultural institutions exert influence through research partnerships, higher education, publishing, languages, and heritage, though these networks depend on contributions and choices made outside Europe. Calling these relationships global power may be appropriate in specific settings, but the term should not imply that legal regulation, cultural reach, and military coercion are interchangeable.

The long comparison therefore runs from nineteenth-century imperial sovereignty, through twentieth-century military dependence and regional integration, to contemporary interdependence. Particular European states lost direct control of large colonial territories; European firms and institutions nevertheless continued to participate in international finance, trade, scientific production, and diplomacy. Outside powers also gained leverage over European choices through security commitments, energy markets, supply chains, and technologies. The question is not whether Europe simply rose or fell, but which actors could make binding decisions, by what means, over whom, and under what constraints. This historical test is more informative than a single ranking of civilizations or continents.

International influence depends on relationships with other regions, not only on domestic developments. Trade partners, security allies, sources of energy and raw materials, and the policies of other major powers shape what European states can do. This chapter's evidence suggests that explanations of European power should avoid two simplified narratives, one of European exceptionalism, in which Europe's achievements arose from unique qualities, and the other of inevitable decline, in which the loss of predominance was unavoidable and complete. A comparison of changing forms of power, based on evidence, shows a more complex pattern, in which forms of influence changed with the circumstances that produced them.

Division V · Reconsidering European History and European Identity Section 144.19

Rethinking European History: Memory, Perspective, and Historical Interpretation

European history has itself been written within particular contexts. Historians of the nineteenth century, working in an age of nation building, produced national histories that traced the origins of France, Germany, Italy, and other states in the remote past, an approach this textbook has avoided, as the series' rules on national master narratives required. Others wrote histories of progress, in which Europe advanced from darkness to enlightenment and then to modernity, or histories of civilization that treated Europe as the pinnacle of human development and justified imperial rule. Twentieth century historians reacted to the world wars by questioning these assumptions, and since the 1960s, social history, women's history, environmental history, and the history of everyday life have enlarged the range of historical actors and evidence.

Colonial and postcolonial scholarship, and global history more broadly, have changed the questions historians ask. They have shown how empire shaped European societies, how non European actors influenced European developments, and how categories such as civilization and race were produced in the encounter between Europeans and others. Critics of some of these approaches argue that they can overstate the role of empire in European development, neglect internal factors, or apply present day moral categories too readily, and these debates are part of the scholarly conversation. The best work tests claims against evidence, and the contributions of different approaches complement one another when they are used critically.

Public memory is a central arena for these questions. Museums and monuments make claims about the past, and recent controversies show how contested those claims can be. Protesters pulled down the statue of the slave trader Edward Colston in Bristol on June 7, 2020. Belgium has debated its memory of the Congo Free State under Leopold II, which lasted from 1885 to 1908. A report commissioned by the French president in 2018 by Felwine Sarr and Bénédicte Savoy examined the restitution of African cultural property held in French museums, and Germany returned a number of Benin Bronzes to Nigeria in 2022. Debates in Germany over how to remember the Nazi past, including the Historians' Dispute of the 1980s, and in eastern Europe over communist and wartime history, show that memory is also contested within societies.

Global and connected histories also invite students to compare archives created on different sides of an encounter. A European treaty may describe the incorporation of a territory as a legal acquisition, while a local petition or oral tradition records dispossession or resistance. A commercial ledger can establish quantities traded without revealing the experiences of people whose labor produced the goods. Comparing these sources does not mean treating every account as equally reliable in every respect; it means asking what each could know, which interests shaped its language, and what independent evidence can test its claims. The same method applies to the history of migration, religious conflict, scientific exchange, and twentieth-century war. It permits a history in which European actors remain central to many developments but no longer monopolize the explanatory perspective.

Students must distinguish among established historical facts, legitimate interpretive disagreements, and political efforts to construct selective memories. That the Holocaust occurred and was carried out by Nazi Germany and its collaborators is established by overwhelming evidence, and denial or minimization is a distortion, not an interpretation. The relative importance of different causes of the First World War, or the weight of colonial resources in industrialization, are matters of interpretation on which scholars can reasonably differ. Political actors, by contrast, may promote narratives that omit inconvenient evidence for present purposes. Europe is therefore not only the subject of historical investigation. It is a changing category through which historians have organized knowledge, and understanding that is part of historical literacy.

Division V · Reconsidering European History and European Identity Section 144.20

Europe and the Wider World: What Does European History Mean?

This final section returns to the problems with which the textbook began. Chapter 1 asked what Europe is, and it identified four difficulties: the definition of Europe, its geographical position, its internal diversity, and its interaction with surrounding societies. The evidence of the intervening chapters permits an answer to each. Europe's definition has changed, and no single definition fits every period. Its geographical position, described in Chapter 2, offered connection by sea, river, and plain more than isolation, and the Mediterranean and the steppe were as much part of European history as the Rhine and the Alps. Its internal diversity was always great, in languages, religions, political forms, and ways of life. Its interaction with other regions was continuous, and it shaped European societies at every stage.

Chapters 140 through 143 supplied four comparative findings that bear on this answer. First, religion, examined in Chapter 140, shaped but never exhausted European identity. Christianity organized institutions, law, and learning, yet Jews, Muslims, dissenters, and unbelievers were part of European societies, Orthodox and Latin Christianity developed differently, and secularization proceeded unevenly. Second, as Chapter 141 showed, warfare, taxation, law, and the formation of states produced a variety of political institutions, including kingdoms, republics, composite monarchies, empires, and federations, not one inevitable constitutional model. Third, Chapter 142 demonstrated that empire and migration connected European societies to populations and institutions throughout the world, from Roman provinces to Atlantic plantations to postcolonial cities. Fourth, Chapter 143 showed that property, labor, gender, family, and social hierarchy shaped ordinary experience and changed through unequal historical processes.

These dimensions intersect. The religious divisions of the sixteenth century affected the formation of states, and the states' competition drove overseas expansion, whose revenues and labor systems altered social hierarchies at home. Colonial commodities changed households and the gendered division of labor, while migration, voluntary and coerced, brought new populations into European towns and reshaped religious and cultural life. The ideas of rights and equality developed in the Enlightenment were applied, resisted, and extended within and beyond Europe, and the world wars, driven by state competition and ideology and fought with colonial resources, undermined the empires that had helped to sustain European power. European history is therefore neither simply the history of nations within a continent nor the history of Europe's expansion across the globe. It is the history of a set of societies whose internal development and external connections were bound together.

The interpretation that emerges rejects several familiar narratives. Europe was not a self contained civilization passing through predetermined stages from Greece to Rome to Christendom to modernity. Its achievements in law, science, art, political experiment, and economic organization were real and were the product of specific historical conditions, many of which involved exchange with other regions. Its coercive practices, including conquest, slavery, forced labor, colonial violence, persecution, and genocide, were also real, and they were enabled by the same institutions and capacities that supported its achievements. Neither celebration nor condemnation is an adequate historical method, and the book has tried to explain how the two coexisted.

Nor was the contemporary European order the culmination of the historical process. The integration project, the institutions of the Union, the Council of Europe, and the security arrangements of the present each resulted from choices made under particular conditions after 1945 and 1989, and they remain subject to revision. The relationship between historical belonging and institutional membership, the status of the eastern borderlands, the place of postcolonial and migrant communities, and the meaning of sovereignty are continuing questions, and a textbook can supply the historical background for them but cannot resolve them. Students should be wary of any claim, from any direction, that history has settled what Europe must be.

The textbook's final conclusion can therefore be stated in this way. Europe was not formed as a self contained civilization moving through a predetermined sequence of historical stages. Its societies emerged and changed through interactions among diverse populations, institutions, environments, and wider networks of exchange and conflict. The boundaries of Europe, the meanings attached to European identity, and the continent's position in the wider world were repeatedly contested and transformed. European history is therefore the history of both internal development and enduring global connection. Understanding Europe requires examining not only what Europeans did, but also how the wider world shaped the societies they became. The answer to the opening question is thus historical and open ended. Europe has meant different things to different people at different times, and the work of understanding it, like the history itself, continues.

Chapter Summary

This chapter reconsidered Europe as a changing geographical, political, cultural, and global historical category, returning to the question posed in Chapter 1 after the evidence of the intervening chapters. It began by tracing the history of the word Europe from Greek geographical usage, in which the boundaries were already conventional, through Carolingian, humanist, and early modern uses, to the nineteenth century conventions that placed the eastern limit at the Urals. The central analytical rule was that Europe is a historical category whose meanings changed, and that no single definition, whether geographic, religious, civilizational, or institutional, describes all periods and communities.

The chapter reexamined Europe's physical setting and found that its coasts, rivers, plains, and mountains facilitated connection with Africa, Asia, and the Atlantic, while remaining conditions and not causes. It then traced the participation of ancient and medieval societies in wider systems. Prehistoric Europe was shaped by migration and exchange, Greek and Roman societies were Mediterranean ones whose empires spanned three continents, and medieval Europes were plural, containing Latin, Byzantine, Orthodox, Muslim, Jewish, and steppe communities linked to Eurasian trade and learning.

Maritime expansion from the fifteenth century reoriented European connections without beginning global trade. European merchants entered Indian Ocean and Asian networks controlled by stronger powers, and they relied on American silver and on local intermediaries. Conquest, colonial rule, and Atlantic slavery transformed both European and colonized societies, with catastrophic consequences for Indigenous and African peoples, and the asymmetries of the period varied by region.

Global exchange changed ordinary European life. The Columbian Exchange, sugar, coffee, tea, tobacco, and cotton linked households to plantations and distant markets, though at different speeds in different regions. European knowledge, including the Scientific Revolution, developed within networks that drew on Islamic, Asian, Indigenous, and other sources, and distinguishing European innovation from external contribution requires attention to both.

The Enlightenment and the Atlantic revolutions proclaimed universal rights while coexisting with slavery and colonial rule, and the Haitian Revolution and the arguments of colonized thinkers extended the meaning of those rights. Industrialization, which began unevenly in northwestern Europe, produced a divergence from the major Asian economies, and the historical debate over its causes, including coal, institutions, wages, knowledge, and colonial resources, shows that no single factor suffices.

Nineteenth century European predominance was concentrated in a few states and exercised through conquest, informal empire, finance, and law, and it was resisted and adapted to outside Europe. The world wars, in which colonial peoples served in large numbers, destroyed empires and weakened European states, while the Holocaust remained central to the moral assessment of the century. The Cold War divided Europe within a global rivalry, and decolonization and postcolonial migration brought the history of empire into domestic European societies.

European integration, from the Schuman Declaration of 1950 through the Treaties of Paris and Rome, Maastricht, and enlargement, reorganized relations among states, and it must be distinguished from the Council of Europe, NATO, and the formation of a single nation. The period after 1989 brought post communist transformation, the Yugoslav wars, globalization, financial crisis, Brexit, and, from 2014 and especially February 24, 2022, renewed conflict over European security, which the chapter treats as ongoing and as not yet completed history.

The chapter's last division argued that internal diversity is a central characteristic of Europe, that relative decline in global predominance differs from universal decline, and that memory and historiography shape how Europe is understood. Museums, monuments, and public controversies show that the past remains contested, and students must distinguish established facts from legitimate interpretive disagreements and from selective political narratives.

The final interpretation combined these findings with those of Chapters 140 through 143. Europe was neither a self contained civilization nor a mere stage for expansion. Its societies developed through interactions among diverse populations, environments, institutions, and wider networks, and its boundaries, meanings, and global position were repeatedly contested. European history is the history of both internal development and enduring global connection.

Key Terms

Europe
A geographical expression for the western part of Eurasia, and also a historical, cultural, and political idea whose boundaries and content changed over time.
Eurasia
The single landmass comprising Europe and Asia, of which the division into continents is a historical convention.
Christendom
The community of Christians and the institutional world shaped by Christianity, divided between Latin and Orthodox traditions and never a single polity.
Civilization
A nineteenth century concept that ranked societies by assumed level of development, used to justify imperial rule and now treated cautiously.
Cultural exchange
The movement and reinterpretation of ideas, goods, and practices between societies, usually unequal in the power of the parties.
Al-Andalus
The Muslim ruled territories of the Iberian Peninsula from the eighth century to 1492, home to Muslim, Jewish, and Christian communities.
Mongol Empire
The thirteenth century Eurasian empire whose political unity across much of the continent facilitated commerce, travel, and the spread of disease.
Tordesillas
The 1494 agreement between Portugal and Castile dividing prospective overseas claims, binding on neither non European peoples nor other European states.
Columbian Exchange
The transfer of plants, animals, microbes, and people between the Americas and the Old World after 1492.
Chartered company
A joint stock enterprise, such as the Dutch East India Company, licensed by a state to trade and exercise governmental powers overseas.
Plantation system
An agricultural system producing cash crops for distant markets with coerced labor, notably enslaved Africans in the Americas.
Atlantic slave trade
The forced transportation of an estimated twelve million Africans to the Americas between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries.
Industrious revolution
A historians' term for increased household market labor in early modern Europe to afford new consumer goods.
Scientific Revolution
The transformation of ideas about nature between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, developed within networks of knowledge that extended beyond Europe.
Universal rights
The claim that rights belong to all human beings, proclaimed in Enlightenment and revolutionary documents and contested over who was included.
Haitian Revolution
The revolt of enslaved and free people of color in Saint Domingue, 1791 to 1804, that created an independent state.
Great Divergence
The widening economic gap between northwestern Europe and major Asian economies from the late eighteenth century, whose causes and timing are debated.
Informal empire
Influence over another state through trade treaties, finance, and coercion without formal annexation.
Berlin Conference
The 1884 to 1885 meeting of European powers and others that established rules for claiming African territory, without African participation.
Decolonization
The end of formal colonial rule, mostly between the 1940s and 1970s, through negotiation, revolt, and war.
Non-alignment
A policy of declining to join either Cold War bloc, articulated by states meeting at Bandung in 1955 and later organized as the Non-Aligned Movement.
Marshall Plan
The American program of economic assistance to western Europe from 1948 to 1952.
NATO
The North Atlantic Treaty Organization, a military alliance founded in 1949 that includes North American and European members.
Council of Europe
A separate organization founded in 1949 for human rights, democracy, and the rule of law, not an institution of the European Union.
European Union
The union of sovereign states established by the Maastricht Treaty, in force from 1993, with supranational and intergovernmental institutions.
Supranational
Describing institutions whose decisions bind member states in defined areas, as distinct from intergovernmental cooperation.
Schengen area
The group of states that have abolished many internal border checks, overlapping with but not identical to EU membership.
Postcolonial migration
The movement of people from former colonies to former colonial powers after decolonization.
Eurocentrism
An approach that treats Europe as the normal or central reference point in explaining world history.
Historiography
The history of how history has been written, and the study of differing interpretations.
Historical memory
The ways societies remember, commemorate, and contest the past, which may diverge from historical evidence.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 144 matters because it asks students to see the whole history of Europe as the history of a category that was repeatedly made and remade. The chapters before it narrated societies, states, wars, religions, and economies. This one asks what those narratives reveal about the idea that organizes them, and it shows that the idea itself has a history, in which geography, religion, empire, and institutions each supplied meanings that were contested.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that run through all six volumes: the continuous interaction of Europe with other regions, the interdependence of European achievements and coercion, the contingency of power, and the way in which memory and interpretation shape what is remembered. Many of its themes carried forward. The legacies of empire and migration, the unresolved status of the eastern borderlands, and the institutions created after 1945 remain part of the present, and they will continue to shape the questions that historians ask.

The chapter connects regions that the textbook treated separately, from Iberia and the Atlantic to the Baltic, the Balkans, the Black Sea, and the steppe, and it connects Europe to Africa, Asia, and the Americas. In the argument of Volume VI, it shows that the division of Europe, decolonization, integration, and the crises after 1989 were episodes in a longer history of redefinition, not a final settlement.

Students who finish this textbook should carry forward a habit of mind rather than a single answer. That habit consists of asking who is speaking, what is included and excluded, which connections are visible and which are hidden, and what evidence supports a claim. With that habit, the question with which the textbook began remains open, and students are equipped to continue examining it.
