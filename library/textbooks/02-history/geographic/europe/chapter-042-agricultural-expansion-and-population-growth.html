Agricultural Expansion and Population Growth

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 1085, as the surviving evidence suggests, King William I of England ordered a survey of an astonishing kind: commissioners were to ask, place by place, how much land there was, who held it, how many plow teams worked it, and what meadow, woodland, fisheries, and mills it contained. The questions themselves reveal a society measuring a countryside that was already undergoing a major period of demographic and agricultural transformation.

The survey that produced Domesday Book, completed in 1086, was an English royal undertaking, and it should not be mistaken for a portrait of all of Europe. Yet the categories it used, plow teams, meadow, woodland, mills, and the value of estates, point to features of rural life that appear in very different records across the continent in the following two centuries. Monastic cartularies in Burgundy and Catalonia, estate surveys in northern Italy, charters of settlement in the lands east of the Elbe, and royal inquests in England and France all record lords, peasants, and communities adding fields, building mills, founding villages, and arguing over who had the right to use land that had once been woodland, marsh, or rough pasture. Taken together, such records reveal that between roughly the tenth and the early fourteenth centuries much of Europe experienced sustained growth in population, cultivated land, and the number of settlements.

This chapter asks why that expansion occurred, how it worked, and what it changed. It opens Part VII by examining the material foundation on which the political, commercial, religious, and intellectual developments of the High Middle Ages rested. Cathedrals, universities, fairs, and crusading armies were all supported by the labor of a rural population that, in many regions, was growing and producing more than before. Chapter 34 has already examined lordship, dependency, and the problem of "feudalism." This chapter does not repeat that discussion. It asks instead a different question: why did the European countryside expand so dramatically after about 1000, how did people produce more food, how did settlement spread, and how did a growing population transform both landscapes and rural communities?

The answer developed here avoids two tempting simplifications. The first is the idea of a single "agricultural revolution" in which a few inventions, usually the heavy plow, the horse collar, and three-field rotation, suddenly transformed medieval farming. The evidence shows a more complicated pattern of regional, cumulative, and adaptive change, in which technology mattered but was never the only factor. The second simplification is the idea of a uniform European countryside. Expansion looked very different in the clay lowlands of northern France, the irrigated plains of Lombardy, the marshes of Holland, the Castilian frontier, the forests of Bohemia, and the high valleys of the Alps. Part of the work of this chapter is to show what these environments shared and how they differed.

The chapter also treats the countryside as something that people actively made rather than merely inhabited. Forests that had supplied timber, pasture, game, and fuel were cleared, and the clearing created conflict as well as new fields. Marshes were drained by dike builders whose works required new forms of cooperation. Villages were founded, reorganized, or relocated, so that the "medieval village" familiar to modern readers was itself a historical product of this period. Chapter 2 introduced the idea that Europe's landscape has been shaped by human activity for thousands of years, from the first clearances of the Neolithic examined in Chapter 4 onward. The High Middle Ages represent one of the most intensive phases of that long process.

The evidence is uneven, and the chapter pays attention to it throughout. No medieval authority counted the population of Europe, and the figures that modern historians use are reconstructions from tax lists, estate surveys, church foundations, archaeology, and fossil pollen. Many written sources were produced by monasteries and lords, who wrote about land, rights, and revenue rather than about the experience of the peasants who did most of the work. Archaeologists, palynologists, and historians of landscape now supplement these documents, and the combination of methods has substantially changed older assumptions about how and when the countryside grew.

Chapter 42 stands at a threshold. It looks back to the rural society of Chapter 34 and the post-Carolingian kingdoms of Chapter 33, which emerged after the pressures of Viking and Magyar raiding examined in Chapter 32. It looks forward to the towns and commerce of Chapter 43, which depended on agricultural surplus, and to the everyday social world of Chapter 49. It also anticipates, without narrating, the crisis of the fourteenth century in Chapter 50, for the same expansion that created prosperity also produced a larger population dependent on an intensively exploited agricultural system. Within Volume II, the chapter marks the shift from the formation of Latin Christendom and its neighbors to a period of confident growth and institutional elaboration.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the condition of the European countryside around 1000 and explain why historians reconstruct medieval population growth from indirect rather than census evidence.
  2. Evaluate the principal kinds of evidence, including estate surveys, Domesday Book, church foundations, archaeology, and pollen records, used to establish growth in population and cultivated land, and identify the limits of each.
  3. Explain why population grew between roughly 1000 and 1300 as the result of several interacting factors rather than a single cause.
  4. Assess the role of climate, including the Medieval Climate Anomaly, while avoiding simple climatic determinism.
  5. Analyze forest clearance, marsh drainage, and land reclamation as processes that created new farmland, altered ecosystems, and generated conflict over common resources.
  6. Distinguish among the major agricultural techniques of the period, including plows, harnessing, rotations, and manuring, and explain why they were adopted unevenly.
  7. Explain the importance of water and wind power and the social and environmental consequences of milling.
  8. Trace the foundation and reorganization of villages and field systems and the roles of lords, peasants, and communities in that process.
  9. Compare the roles of monasteries, secular lords, princes, and peasant settlers in organized colonization, and evaluate older myths about the Cistercians.
  10. Interpret eastern and northern settlement expansion, including the Ostsiedlung, without projecting later nationalist narratives onto it.
  11. Compare agricultural systems in northwestern, Mediterranean, central and eastern European, and mountain regions.
  12. Connect High Medieval agricultural expansion to the commercial growth of Chapter 43 and the pressures that become important in Chapter 50.

Historical Context

The rural Europe of around 1000 had been shaped by a long and uneven inheritance. In parts of the Mediterranean, the landscape still bore the marks of Roman estates, roads, and field divisions, although these had been transformed and in many districts abandoned or reorganized in the centuries examined in Part IV and Part V. In northern Gaul and the Rhineland, Carolingian estates had provided a framework for agricultural production on large holdings, as Chapter 31 and Chapter 34 explained. In regions outside the old Roman frontiers, including much of Scandinavia, the Baltic, and eastern central Europe, settlement patterns followed different logics, and rural communities were organized under political and legal arrangements that bore little resemblance to those of the former Roman provinces. The countryside of 1000 was therefore not a single inherited system but a patchwork of regional arrangements.

Two features of the preceding period shaped what followed. The first was insecurity. The Viking, Magyar, and Muslim raids examined in Chapter 32 had disrupted settlement and trade in many coastal and riverine districts in the ninth and tenth centuries, although their effects varied greatly and should not be exaggerated into a general collapse. By around 1000, many of these pressures had diminished or been absorbed through settlement, conversion, and the consolidation of kingdoms and principalities, a process traced in Chapter 33 and Chapter 35. The Magyars, for example, had settled in the Pannonian Basin and, under rulers such as Stephen, entered the Latin Christian political world. The second feature was the growth of local lordship and territorial authority, which provided, in many places, a more predictable framework for rural production. How far this greater security actually encouraged expansion is a question this chapter considers carefully in Section 42.3.

The physical setting is that described in Chapter 2. Much of temperate Europe remained covered with deciduous and mixed forest, interspersed with cultivated land that had been won from woodland over earlier millennia. Heavier clay soils, light loess soils, thin upland soils, Mediterranean hillsides, river floodplains, and coastal marshes offered very different possibilities. Rivers and seas, as that chapter showed, provided transport that made it possible to move bulky agricultural produce, and mountains shaped patterns of seasonal pastoral movement. These environmental differences remain essential for understanding why expansion took such varied forms.

Outside the Latin West, comparable processes occurred, though with different chronologies and institutions. In the Byzantine territories of the Balkans and Anatolia, historians have argued for rural growth and increasing settlement density between about the tenth and twelfth centuries, supported by a strong fiscal state, though the evidence is debated and regionally patchy. In Muslim al-Andalus, irrigated agriculture, a diverse range of crops, and urban markets formed a distinct agricultural system that would be inherited, adapted, and in some places disrupted by the Christian conquests discussed in Chapter 47. This chapter concentrates on the Latin West and its eastern and northern frontiers, but it does not treat them as the only European countrysides.

The chapter relies on a range of evidence quite different from narrative chronicles. Documentary sources include estate surveys, land charters, cartularies, tax and rent records, royal inquests, and the settlement agreements that rulers and lords made with colonists. Archaeologists excavate village sites, field boundaries, mills, and cemeteries, and survey deserted settlements. Palynologists, whose methods were introduced in Chapter 2, use fossil pollen to track the rise and fall of cereal cultivation and the retreat of tree cover. Dendrochronology provides precise dates for timbers in mills and buildings. Place names, which often record clearings, new settlements, and the names of founders, offer another strand of evidence. None of these sources is complete, and part of the historian's task is to compare them and to ask which kinds of people and places they leave out.

Section 42.1

Europe around 1000: Land, People, and Limits

Around the year 1000, Europe was overwhelmingly rural. The great majority of people, in every region examined in this textbook, lived in villages, hamlets, or isolated farmsteads and worked the land, either directly as cultivators and herders or as members of households that depended on agriculture. Towns existed and in the Mediterranean and Byzantine worlds some were large, but they were small in number and, in most of northern and central Europe, small in size. The economic base of society, and therefore of every kingdom, church, and aristocracy discussed in Parts V and VI, was the produce of fields, pastures, orchards, vineyards, and woodlands.

That countryside contained large areas that were cultivated only lightly or not at all. Extensive forests covered much of northern France, the German lands, Poland, Bohemia, Scandinavia, and the East European Plain. Marshes and fens occupied river mouths and coastal lowlands from Flanders to the Baltic. Heath, rough grazing, and uplands formed a large share of the land in the British Isles, Iberia, and the mountains. These zones were not empty. They provided timber, fuel, grazing for pigs, cattle, and sheep, game, honey, nuts, fish, and fowl, and they were embedded in local customs and rights. Describing them as "wilderness" would be misleading. They were a managed and used part of the rural economy, simply not one dominated by plowed fields.

Population density varied enormously. Some regions, such as parts of Lombardy, Flanders, and the Paris Basin, had relatively dense settlement, while others, such as large parts of Scandinavia, the Baltic, and the East European forest zone, were sparsely populated. In many regions, villages were small and widely spaced, with substantial areas between them. Many settlements were also unstable: communities moved, fields were abandoned and recultivated, and the pattern of cultivation changed over time. The idea of a fixed, ancient village landscape reaching back unchanged to antiquity does not survive close archaeological scrutiny, a point developed in Section 42.8.

It would be a mistake to describe this rural Europe as "backward" in comparison with the later period of growth. The farmers of the tenth century were skilled at managing soils, animals, and seasons with the tools and institutions available to them. The question this chapter pursues is not why earlier farmers failed, but why, after about 1000, cultivated land, settlement density, and population began to increase so strongly in many regions, and how those increases were achieved. To answer it, historians first have to establish that growth occurred and measure how much, a problem to which Section 42.2 turns.

Section 42.2

How Do We Know the Population Grew?

No medieval government conducted a census of Europe, and no kingdom of the period possessed the administrative machinery to count its people systematically. Population history for the High Middle Ages is therefore a work of reconstruction from indirect evidence. This does not mean that historians simply guess. It means that they combine many kinds of evidence, each with its own biases, and look for patterns that hold across different sources and regions.

Written records provide the most direct clues. Domesday Book, compiled in 1086, lists hundreds of manors and settlements together with plow teams, mills, and the number of recorded tenants, which can be used to estimate the population of England, though the survey recorded heads of households and certain categories of tenant rather than every inhabitant, and scholars debate the multipliers used to convert these into totals. Many estimates place England's population in the region of 1.5 to 2 million in the late eleventh century and perhaps 4 to 6 million by about 1300, but these are modern scholarly estimates with wide margins of uncertainty. Later records, such as the Hundred Rolls of 1279, the poll taxes of the late fourteenth century, and numerous estate surveys, allow historians to work backward and forward from better documented moments. In Italy, communal records and fiscal surveys allow similar reconstructions for some cities and their surrounding districts, and in France, hearth counts and church records provide partial evidence for the later thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries.

Other evidence is indirect but valuable. The number of parishes, parish churches, and monasteries in a region grew in many places, suggesting more communities and more people to support them. Archaeological surveys identify the foundation dates of villages and farmsteads and map the expansion of cultivated areas. Pollen cores from lakes and peat bogs, as Chapter 2 explained, show rising proportions of cereal and grass pollen and declining tree pollen in many regions, offering a long term picture of clearance. Cemetery excavations can reveal the age structure and health of populations. Place names ending in elements meaning "clearing," "new village," or "burnt land" often point to expansion in the period. Estate records show the subdivision of holdings, the opening of new arable, and rising rents and land values in many areas.

Historians therefore generally agree that population and settlement grew substantially, in most regions, between about 1000 and 1300. They disagree about the exact totals, about the pace and timing of growth in individual regions, and about whether growth continued everywhere until the early fourteenth century or began to slow earlier in some districts. Published reconstructions for the whole of Europe vary substantially with boundaries and methods, and some serious estimates place the starting figure higher. Some widely used estimates suggest that the population may have roughly doubled over these three centuries, from something in the order of 35 to 40 million to perhaps 70 to 80 million, but such figures are modern estimates built on large assumptions and should be treated as ranges, not as documented totals. A single smooth graph of European population can mislead students into thinking that medieval authorities supplied returns that they did not.

Historical Methods

Counting People without a Census

The problem: No continent wide record of medieval population exists. Historians must infer numbers from documents produced for other purposes, such as taxation, estate management, and legal disputes.

Direct documentary evidence: Surveys such as Domesday Book and the Hundred Rolls, tax lists, hearth counts, and estate records provide counts of households, tenants, or taxable units. These must be converted into estimates of total population, and the multipliers used are themselves debated. Such records also omit groups such as the poor, the landless, clergy, and sometimes women and children.

Indirect evidence: Numbers of parishes and churches, the foundation of villages and monasteries, the expansion of arable land, rising rents, and subdivision of holdings all suggest demographic growth. None of these measures people directly.

Scientific evidence: Pollen cores, tree rings, and archaeological survey add independent evidence for land clearance and settlement, but they do not translate straightforwardly into numbers of people.

The historian's approach: Confidence comes from agreement among independent kinds of evidence, not from any single figure.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a tax record undercount the population it describes?
  2. Why is the number of parish churches in a region an indirect rather than a direct measure of population?
  3. How can the agreement of several different kinds of evidence strengthen a historical conclusion even when none of them is precise?
Section 42.3

Why Population Expanded

The reasons for medieval population growth have been debated for generations, and the most persuasive answers combine several factors. No single cause is sufficient, and the factors reinforced one another in ways that differed by region.

One frequently cited factor is greater security. The decline of the major external raids discussed in Chapter 32, together with the consolidation of local lordship and the emergence of stronger kingdoms and principalities, meant that in many districts farmers could invest in fields, buildings, and equipment with more confidence that they would be able to harvest what they planted. This should not be overstated. Local warfare, aristocratic violence, and royal campaigns remained frequent in the High Middle Ages, and some regions, such as the Iberian frontier and the Baltic, were in fact zones of continuing military conflict. Security in this sense was relative and uneven.

A second factor was the expansion of cultivation itself. As land was cleared and drained and as existing fields were used more intensively, food supply rose, and larger numbers of children could survive to adulthood and form households of their own. A third factor was technological and organizational change, examined in Sections 42.6 and 42.7, which in some regions improved the productivity of land and labor. A fourth was the changing structure of rural society. Lords and rulers, as Section 42.9 explains, had strong incentives to attract settlers to new lands because settlers meant rents, tithes, and military and labor services. The terms offered to settlers, including hereditary tenure, reduced obligations, and legal privileges, in turn made migration and household formation more attractive to peasants.

Marriage and household patterns probably mattered too, though the evidence is more difficult to interpret. Where new land and new holdings were available, young couples could form independent households earlier than in a land-short society, which would have supported higher birth rates. Medieval demographic behavior varied by region and social group, and historians remain cautious about generalizing. The growth of markets, treated in Chapter 43, added another incentive: peasants and lords who could sell produce had reasons to bring more land into cultivation or specialize in particular crops, such as wine, wool, or grain. Finally, climatic conditions, discussed in Section 42.4, may have been favorable in some regions for part of the period.

What emerges is a feedback process. Population growth increased demand for food and created pressure and incentive to clear new land and intensify cultivation. Greater cultivation and settlement raised output and allowed more people to survive and form households. More households meant further growth. This interaction avoids the simplistic claim that a single invention, such as the heavy plow, suddenly caused a population explosion. It also explains why growth could continue for centuries and, eventually, why it could reach limits.

Cause and Effect

The Expansion Cycle: Population, Land, and Output

Long-term causes

Slow recovery from the disruptions of the post-Roman and Viking age, the consolidation of local lordship and larger political units, the spread of church organization, and cumulative improvements in tools and methods of cultivation created a framework for growth.

Short-term causes

Reduced external raiding in many regions, incentives offered by lords and rulers to settlers, access to cleared or drainable land, favorable climatic conditions in parts of Europe, and expanding local and regional markets encouraged more households to produce more.

The transformation

Population growth increased demand for food and land. That demand encouraged clearance, drainage, and more intensive use of existing fields. Expanded output supported further population growth, in a reinforcing cycle that lasted in many regions for about three centuries.

Immediate effects

More villages, more mills, larger cultivated area, more parishes, expanding markets for rural produce, and rising rents and land values in many areas.

Long-term consequences

A far larger rural population, more connected to markets and towns, and increasingly dependent on a heavily exploited agricultural base. By the early fourteenth century, diminishing returns and vulnerability to bad harvests became serious in many regions, as Chapter 50 examines.

Historical complexity

No single factor started or sustained the cycle. Security, technology, settlement incentives, markets, and climate interacted differently in each region, and growth sometimes ran ahead of what agricultural productivity could comfortably support.
Section 42.4

Climate and the Medieval Environment

Chapter 2 introduced the Medieval Climate Anomaly, the period of relatively mild conditions in parts of the North Atlantic region that scientists generally date to approximately 950 to 1250. This period coincides with much of the agricultural expansion examined in this chapter, and it is natural to ask whether warmer weather helped to drive it. The evidence supports a cautious answer. Tree rings, glacier records, lake sediments, and other proxy sources indicate that conditions in some regions were favorable for agriculture during portions of these centuries, with generally longer growing seasons and, in some places, the cultivation of grapes and cereals at higher latitudes or altitudes than later became possible. Records of vineyards in England and in parts of northern Europe, for example, have sometimes been cited as evidence, though vineyards also reflect markets, tastes, and monastic needs for wine, and not only climate.

The relationship should not be reduced to a simple chain of causation in which warmer weather produced an agricultural revolution, which produced a population boom. Climate varied across regions and across decades, and even in a generally favorable period there were poor harvests, floods, and droughts. The timing and strength of the climatic signal differ in different proxy records, and specialists continue to debate how uniform the anomaly was. Moreover, agricultural expansion occurred in very different environments, including Mediterranean lands and the cold northern reaches of Scandinavia, where the climatic pattern was not the same. Human institutions, technology, settlement policy, labor supply, and markets mattered at least as much. A favorable climate may have made expansion easier in some regions, but it did not make expansion happen.

This section also corrects an assumption that people of the High Middle Ages simply lived within environmental conditions given to them. They changed their environment on a large scale. Clearing forest altered local water cycles, soils, and wildlife. Drainage changed coastlines and river courses. Dams and mill ponds modified streams. The expansion of arable and pasture altered the balance of species and, in places, increased soil erosion. These transformations make the High Middle Ages a significant period in European environmental history, in which people were not merely responding to nature but remaking it.

Section 42.5

Clearing Forests, Draining Marshes, Making Fields

The most visible form of agricultural expansion was the extension of cultivated land. In much of western and central Europe, this meant the clearing of woodland. The process, known in Old French and English sources as assarting, ranged from the work of individual peasant households who cleared a few acres on the edge of a forest to large organized projects in which lords, monasteries, or rulers directed the settlement of entire districts. Clearance could be gradual, as farmers extended fields outward from existing villages, or it could be sudden and planned, as when new settlements were laid out in cleared forest according to a regular design.

Historical Vocabulary
Assart

An assart was a piece of woodland or waste that had been cleared and brought into cultivation. The word derives from medieval French and appears frequently in English, French, and related records. In England, assarts in royal forest were often legally problematic, since forest law protected game and royal rights, and clearing could result in fines. The term matters because assart records, including payments, disputes, and surveys, are among the most useful documents for tracing clearance. It should not be equated with permanent field enclosure or with a modern idea of deforestation as a single act. Assarting was typically piecemeal, repeated, and negotiated.

Marshes and low-lying coastal lands were transformed through drainage and embankment. The coasts of Flanders, Zeeland, and Holland offer the best known examples. From the eleventh and twelfth centuries, communities built dikes and drainage channels to protect and reclaim land, creating what in later centuries would be called polders. In Holland, reclamation of peat bogs proceeded through the digging of drainage ditches into which water could flow, a process that gradually caused the peat to subside and made continuing drainage and protection necessary. This created a lasting interdependence among neighbors, because a failure to maintain dikes or drains in one place threatened the land of others. Cooperative institutions arose to manage these shared risks, including the water boards, such as that of Rijnland, whose heemraden (dike and drainage officials) are documented in a surviving charter of 1255. That charter confirmed the authority of officials who already existed and did not found the institution, but it shows the kind of body that could levy contributions, regulate maintenance, and settle disputes. Comparable projects reshaped the English Fenland, the coastal marshes of Lincolnshire and Kent, the Elbe and Weser marshes, and wetlands in northern Italy, where monasteries and communes were active in drainage and in the construction of irrigation canals.

Clearance extended into other kinds of landscape as well. Vineyards expanded in Burgundy, Bordeaux, the Rhineland, and elsewhere, in some places on slopes that had previously been rough pasture or woodland. In upland regions, farmers pushed cultivation to higher altitudes in the warm centuries of the period, though many such fields would later be abandoned. Pasture and woodland were frequently converted to arable, creating a tension between different uses of land. Historians have also drawn attention to the fact that the movement was not simply outward: improvement and intensification occurred within long settled districts as well, through the subdivision of holdings, the reorganization of fields, and more careful management of manure and fallow.

Clearance was never a simple matter of converting useless wilderness into useful farmland. Forests were economically important. They supplied timber for building and fuel, charcoal for metalworking, bark for tanning, nuts and fruit, grazing and pannage for pigs, and game. Many were subject to common rights, under which villagers could collect wood, graze animals, or gather other resources. Rulers and lords also claimed forest as hunting preserves, and in England and parts of France this was protected by special legal regimes. Cleared land therefore replaced one productive landscape with another and could deprive some users of resources they depended on. Disputes over assarts, over the enclosure of commons, and over rights to wood and pasture appear regularly in the records. In this sense, cultivated land expanded at the expense of other productive landscapes, and the winners and losers were not always the same people.

Section 42.6

Agricultural Techniques: Plows, Fields, and Animals

Older textbooks often described a "medieval agricultural revolution" in which a package of innovations, the heavy plow, the horse collar, horseshoes, and three-field rotation, transformed farming in the early Middle Ages and prepared the ground for population growth. This view owes much to scholarship of the mid twentieth century, including the influential work of Lynn White Jr., who emphasized the social effects of technological change. Subsequent research has qualified the picture. Many of the techniques in question were known earlier than once supposed, were adopted slowly and unevenly, and mattered differently in different soils and climates. Technological change was real, but it was regional, cumulative, and adaptive.

The plow offers a good example. In much of the Mediterranean, farmers used the light plow, or ard, which scratched a shallow furrow in dry, thin soils and was well suited to the drought-prone conditions of the region. In the heavy clay soils of northwestern Europe, a wheeled plow with a coulter, a plowshare, and a mouldboard that turned the sod could cut deeper, turn the soil over, and improve drainage, though it required a larger team and more capital. Evidence for such plows appears in various regions from the early Middle Ages, including among Slavic-speaking populations, and they spread over many centuries. Even in the north, farmers used lighter implements on lighter soils. The heavy plow mattered greatly in some environments and much less in others, and it should not be treated as a universal marker of progress.

Animal power was similarly varied. Oxen had long been the principal draft animals of European farming and remained important throughout the period. Horses were faster and more versatile, but they were more expensive to keep, since they required oats or other fodder. The horse collar, a padded harness fitted around the shoulders and chest that was well suited to heavy traction, and nailed horseshoes, which protected hooves, became increasingly widespread from roughly the ninth through twelfth centuries in parts of western Europe. Older claims that earlier harnesses throttled horses have been substantially revised by experimental archaeology and reassessment of ancient evidence, so the collar should not be read as rescuing an inherently ineffective technology. Records, notably from England, show a growing use of horses for plowing and, especially, for hauling, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. But horses did not simply replace oxen. Many peasants could not afford them, many soils and regions favored oxen, and the choice depended on the price of fodder, the nature of the work, and local customs. Farmers often combined the two.

Historical Vocabulary
Open Fields, Fallow, and Crop Rotation

An open field system was an arrangement in which the arable land of a village was divided into large fields, each subdivided into strips held by different households, and in which community decisions governed cropping and grazing. Fallow land was left uncultivated for a season to recover and to provide grazing. In a two-field system, half of the arable lay fallow each year. In a three-field or three-course rotation, the arable was divided into three parts, typically with one sown in autumn with winter grain, one in spring with oats, barley, or legumes, and one fallow, so that a smaller proportion of land lay idle. These systems were not universal. They developed gradually, took many local forms, and coexisted with enclosed fields, individual holdings, and other arrangements. They matter because they show how communities coordinated production. They should not be treated as the standard medieval European system.

Crop rotation and the management of fertility also changed. The two-field system had long been common, especially in the Mediterranean and in many northern districts. In some regions, particularly in northern France, parts of England, and the Rhineland, a three-field rotation became more widespread between the twelfth and the thirteenth centuries, though references to three-year rotations occur earlier on some Carolingian estates. The advantages included a smaller fallow, a more varied harvest, and a spring crop of oats that could feed horses. Farmers also grew peas, beans, and vetches, which, though medieval cultivators did not know about nitrogen fixation, they recognized as beneficial to subsequent crops and useful for food and fodder. Manure from livestock was crucial to fertility, and the balance between arable and pasture was a constant concern, since more cultivated land meant less grazing and therefore less manure. Marling and other soil improvement are recorded in some regions.

The pattern that emerges is one of incremental improvement within regional constraints. Farmers adjusted their tools, crops, and rotations to soil and climate, and they did so with the resources and incentives available to them. Yields remained low by modern standards. Estimates for cereal yields are difficult to make and vary widely, but historians commonly describe returns of several grains harvested for each grain sown, often modest, with considerable variation between well-managed demesne lands and peasant holdings. These constraints meant that expansion of the cultivated area was at least as important to growth as improvement in yields.

Historical Debate

Was There a Medieval Agricultural Revolution?

The question: Did medieval farmers, between roughly the eighth and thirteenth centuries, experience a coherent technological transformation of agriculture that made population growth possible, or was the change more gradual and regionally varied?

Interpretation A: A technological transformation of farming. Some historians, building on earlier scholarship by writers such as Lynn White Jr. and on rural historians such as Georges Duby, have emphasized the combined effects of the heavy plow, improved harnessing, horse traction, rotation systems, and mills. In this view, the cumulative impact of these changes increased productivity, supported larger populations, and altered the organization of villages and estates.
Interpretation B: Gradual, regional, and demographically driven change. Other historians stress that many techniques were older, that adoption was slow and regionally uneven, that yields are difficult to measure and may have risen only modestly, and that the expansion of cultivated area, institutional change, and population pressure itself explain growth at least as well as technology. On this view, the term "revolution" implies a speed and uniformity that the evidence does not support, and technologies were adopted when local conditions made them worthwhile.

Evidence: The debate rests on archaeological finds of tools, documentary references to plows, horses, and rotations, estate accounts that sometimes record yields and costs, and comparisons across regions. The evidence is richest for England and parts of France and weakest for much of eastern and southern Europe, which makes generalization risky.

Why it matters: If technology was the primary driver, growth might be explained as a response to productivity gains. If expansion of land and population pressure were primary, the relationship is more complex and implies that growth could reach limits when new land was exhausted and technology offered no further breakthrough.

Student task: Using Sections 42.5 through 42.7, identify evidence that supports each interpretation. Then explain which kinds of additional evidence, such as measured yields from many regions, would help historians resolve the question.

Section 42.7

Mills, Water, and Mechanical Power

Agricultural growth involved more than planting additional acres. It also involved greater use of nonhuman energy. Watermills, known since antiquity, became far more numerous in much of Europe in the centuries after 1000. Domesday Book records several thousand mills in England in 1086, mostly watermills, a number that implies dense use of streams for milling even before the period of greatest expansion. By the thirteenth century, mills were a normal feature of rural life across much of western and central Europe, and archaeology continues to reveal the remains of mill races, dams, and wheel pits.

The principal function of the mill was the grinding of grain into flour, which saved enormous amounts of household labor compared with the hand querns that had long been used. Mills were costly to build and maintain, and their construction typically required a dam or weir, a mill race, and a wheel and gearing, along with a continuing supply of water. Lords, monasteries, and sometimes communities built them, and in many regions lords claimed the right to compel their tenants to use the lord's mill and to pay a share of the grain as a fee. Such banal or monopoly rights could be a major source of lordly income, and they generated frequent resentment, including disputes over household hand mills that peasants used to avoid the fee. Mills therefore belong to the history of lordship examined in Chapter 34 as well as to the history of agricultural technology.

Windmills expanded the possibilities of mechanical power. Written records of windmills in northwestern Europe begin in the later twelfth century, and they were particularly useful in flat regions, such as parts of England, Flanders, and the Low Countries, where there were few suitable streams or where streams froze or dried in some seasons. Early windmills were typically post mills, in which the whole structure could be rotated to face the wind. The later development of wind-driven drainage pumps in the Low Countries, which belongs largely to a later period, would transform land reclamation, but even in the High Middle Ages windmills and watermills extended mechanization to new districts. Other processes were also mechanized. Fulling mills, in which water-driven hammers pounded cloth, appear in the eleventh and twelfth centuries and became important to the textile trades discussed in Chapter 43. Mills were also used for tanning, forging, and, in some regions, sawing.

The spread of mills had environmental consequences. Dams and mill ponds altered stream flow, interfered with fish migration, flooded meadow, and created disputes among neighbors upstream and downstream. Court records and charters preserve many such quarrels, which show that medieval communities understood water as a shared and contested resource. Mills also illustrate a broader point: expansion depended on capital investment, organization, and cooperation, as well as on the labor of individual households, and the people who could afford to build and control such facilities gained economic and legal advantages over those who could not.

Section 42.8

New Villages and the Reorganization of Settlement

Agricultural expansion did not only change fields. It changed the places where people lived. Rural settlement in medieval Europe took a number of forms. In many parts of northern France, England's midlands, the Rhineland, and parts of central Europe, people lived in nucleated villages, clusters of houses and farm buildings around a church, a green, or a street, surrounded by arable fields. In other regions, including western France, much of Brittany, parts of the British Isles, Scandinavia, and mountain areas, people lived in dispersed hamlets and isolated farmsteads. Settlement forms depended on soils, terrain, water, inheritance customs, the history of lordship, and the way agriculture was organized.

The familiar image of an ancient, unchanging medieval village is, in many regions, an illusion. Archaeological and documentary research indicates that numerous villages were founded, reorganized, relocated, or given a regular plan in the period between the tenth and thirteenth centuries. In parts of England, scattered earlier settlements were consolidated into nucleated villages and associated with open field systems, a process some historians have linked to growing population, lordly planning, and the need to coordinate plowing and grazing. In other regions, entirely new villages were laid out on cleared land, often with regular plots along a street or a series of parallel holdings extending from the farmstead into the surrounding land. The fields might be arranged in long strips, in blocks, or in other patterns suited to local conditions. The medieval countryside was therefore, to a large extent, being built in these centuries.

Settlements were also bound together by institutions. The parish church, which became the center of religious life for rural communities and was frequently funded by tithes, appeared or was rebuilt in thousands of villages. Many communities managed their own affairs in an assembly, a village court, or informal gatherings of householders, and decided when to plow, sow, and harvest, who could graze animals on the stubble, and how to use the commons. Rights of common pasture and rights to wood and waste were crucial to the economy of many households, especially the poorer ones. The initiative for founding or reorganizing settlements came from several directions: lords who wished to attract tenants, rulers who wished to occupy territory, monasteries that organized colonization, and peasants themselves, who moved to new lands or reorganized their own communities. The balance among these forces differed from region to region and is often difficult to recover from surviving evidence.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Agricultural Year in a Northern Open-Field Village

The agricultural year gave structure to rural life, though its details varied widely with climate and soil. In many northern grain-growing districts, plowing for winter crops began in the autumn, followed by sowing of wheat or rye. Spring brought the sowing of oats, barley, peas, and beans, and the plowing of the fallow. Early summer was a time of weeding, of hay-making in meadows, and of sheep-shearing. Late summer was the grain harvest, which was labor intensive and brought together all available hands, including women and children, who bound sheaves and gleaned. After harvest, the stubble was opened to the village livestock. Autumn saw threshing, the slaughter of animals that could not be fed through the winter, and a new cycle of plowing. Winter work included maintenance of tools, hedges, and ditches, the care of livestock, spinning, and the carting of manure.

This pattern was coordinated by community custom, and it depended on shared use of draft animals, plows, and fields. It also depended on gendered divisions of labor that varied by place, with women prominent in dairying, poultry, brewing, gardening, and much harvest work. In Mediterranean regions, the cycle differed, with olive and grape harvests, irrigation, and summer drought shaping the calendar, and in mountain regions the movement of flocks to high pasture gave a different rhythm. The wider experience of household life, diet, health, and leisure is treated in Chapter 49. The point here is that expansion meant more fields to plow and harvest, more animals to tend, and more infrastructure to maintain, and it meant it for households whose labor was the principal resource.

Section 42.9

Monasteries, Lords, and Organized Colonization

Much clearance and settlement was directed or sponsored by institutions. Secular lords, bishops, abbots, and rulers all had reasons to attract settlers and organize new land. They wanted rents and services, tithes, a larger population for defense, and greater territorial control. Their tools included grants of land on favorable terms, exemptions from certain dues for a number of years, rights to use woodland and pasture, legal privileges, and the construction of mills and churches. Recruiting settlers often required these inducements, since peasants had choices, especially where land was abundant and lords competed for labor.

Monasteries were prominent in this process. Benedictine houses had long been major landholders, as Chapter 34 noted, and they continued to develop estates and sponsor clearance. In the twelfth century the Cistercian Order, founded at Cîteaux around 1098 and expanded dramatically after the foundation of Clairvaux in 1115, became especially associated with the creation of large, carefully managed estates. Cistercian houses were often established in relatively remote locations, held land in large consolidated blocks, and organized production through granges, outlying farms worked partly by lay brothers, members of the community who took vows but were not ordained monks, and partly by hired labor. Cistercian estates specialized in sheep, grain, and other products in different regions, and the order's rules limited certain kinds of income, which encouraged direct management of land.

Older accounts often portrayed the Cistercians as heroic monks who entered empty wilderness and by their industry and technical skill invented efficient agriculture. Modern scholarship has complicated that story. Many Cistercian sites were not empty but had been previously settled, farmed, or used by local people, and in some regions the creation of consolidated granges involved the displacement or reorganization of existing peasant communities. The monks also took over mills, vineyards, and fields from earlier donors. Modern scholarship goes further than correcting the myth of empty wilderness. Cistercian granges were largely assembled from land that was already settled and cultivated, and large scale pioneering clearance and drainage is no longer regarded as characteristic of Cistercian agriculture. The order's major contribution often lay in consolidating, reorganizing, and intensively managing existing holdings, and in developing specialized production such as wool in northern England and parts of Iberia. Some houses did take part in local clearance or reclamation, and their organizational skills, access to capital through donations, and networks across Europe were real advantages. But they were participants in a wider movement, not its originators, and peasants performed much of the actual labor of clearance and cultivation, often with strong incentives of their own.

The same applies to other religious and secular institutions. Cathedral chapters, military orders, bishops, and aristocratic families sponsored settlements, and rulers used settlement as a tool of territorial consolidation. The result was a landscape in which authority, economy, and religion were intertwined: a new village might include a parish church, a lord's mill, a tithe barn, and a legal charter, all of which reflected the interests of different parties. Agricultural expansion therefore did not simply increase output. It also gave institutions new sources of revenue and new means of exercising power, a point that connects this chapter to the church and state developments examined in Chapters 44 through 46.

Section 42.10

Settlement Frontiers: Northern, Central, and Eastern Europe

The expansion of settlement was not limited to western Europe. Some of the most dramatic changes occurred in northern, central, and eastern Europe, where settlement frontiers moved across forest, marsh, and thinly populated land. These movements involved migration and the creation of new communities, and they were often organized through agreements among rulers, lords, and settlers.

One of the best-known examples is the Ostsiedlung, the eastward settlement of German-speaking peasants, townspeople, clergy, and lords into the regions east of the Elbe and Saale rivers and further into Silesia, Pomerania, Bohemia, Moravia, Poland, and later parts of the Baltic coast and Prussia. Earlier settlement of Slavic-speaking and other populations had long existed in these regions, and the movement occurred over several centuries. An early documented example is the arrangement traditionally dated 1106, though some scholars propose a date nearer 1113, in which an archbishop of Hamburg-Bremen invited Dutch settlers from Holland to drain and cultivate marshland near Bremen, a scheme that offers a template for later undertakings (see the Primary Source Window below). In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Polish dukes, Bohemian kings, Silesian princes, and the Hungarian monarchs, among others, encouraged the settlement of new villages and towns, offering colonists favorable legal terms.

Historical Vocabulary
Locator and Settlement Law

A locator was an entrepreneur, often of knightly or burgher background, who contracted with a lord or ruler to organize the founding of a village or town. The locator recruited settlers, laid out plots, and often received a larger holding, hereditary office, and a share of certain revenues in return. Settlers frequently received land on favorable terms under a legal framework often called "German law" in Polish and Bohemian sources, which could include hereditary tenure, defined rents, exemption from some obligations, and more predictable courts. The term is historically important because it reveals that colonization was commonly a contractual business arrangement. Students should not assume that "German law" meant that all settlers were ethnically German or that all local inhabitants were displaced, since the framework was also applied to native populations and was adopted by local rulers for their own purposes.

The historiography of the Ostsiedlung illustrates how later political memory can distort the past. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, some German nationalist historians presented it as a civilizing "German conquest of the East," while some nationalist writers elsewhere portrayed it as an aggressive foreign invasion. Contemporary scholarship tends to reject both, emphasizing instead that the movement involved migration by many people in different roles, that rulers frequently invited settlers for their own reasons, that settlers and local populations interacted, intermarried, and borrowed from each other, and that the process produced linguistic and cultural mixing as well as conflict. Violence did occur, notably in the conquest and conversion of Baltic and Prussian peoples by military orders such as the Teutonic Order in the thirteenth century, whose wider crusading context is treated in Chapter 47, while Chapter 54 returns to the Teutonic state and the Baltic political world in the later Middle Ages, and local populations were sometimes marginalized. But the colonization of villages and the founding of towns under negotiated law was a broader phenomenon than conquest, and it left many areas of mixed populations. Local populations did not simply vanish, and older communities often persisted alongside newer ones.

Similar movements occurred elsewhere. In Hungary, kings welcomed settlers from the west from the twelfth century, including the groups later known as the Transylvanian Saxons, who received collective privileges, including those confirmed in the Diploma Andreanum of 1224, in return for service and defense of the frontier. In the Alps, the Walser, Alemannic-speaking migrants from the upper Valais, established high-altitude settlements in valleys across the Alps in the twelfth through fourteenth centuries, in some cases on terms of unusual freedom. In Scandinavia, the expansion of agricultural settlement into interior districts and the consolidation of farms accompanied the growth of kingdoms and the church, though the evidence is less abundant than for regions further south. On the Iberian frontier, the Christian advance southward was followed by resettlement through municipal charters, discussed in Section 42.11. Across these regions, the common features were the use of privilege and law to attract settlers, the importance of rulers and lords as sponsors, and the coexistence of newcomers with older populations.

Section 42.11

Regional Europes: Different Agricultural Systems

The preceding sections have repeatedly shown that expansion took different forms in different places. This section gathers those differences. The central corrective of the chapter is that agricultural expansion occurred across Europe, but not through one universal package of techniques. Treating northern France, with its open fields, heavy plows, and grain, as "medieval Europe" would erase the experience of most of the continent.

In northwestern Europe, including northern France, the Low Countries, England, and the Rhineland, heavier soils, a generally moist climate, and mixed farming of grain and livestock shaped agriculture. Open fields were common in many districts, especially in a broad belt running through the English midlands and northern France, though enclosed fields and dispersed settlement characterized other areas. Both oxen and horses were used, and the three-field rotation spread in some regions. Coastal districts contributed reclamation of marsh and peat, and, as Chapter 43 explains, regions such as Flanders developed strong links between agriculture, textile production, and towns.

In Mediterranean Europe, including Italy, southern France, Catalonia, Iberia, and the Byzantine Aegean and Balkans, different conditions prevailed. Hot dry summers favored wheat, vines, olives, and sheep and goat herding, and many farmers used the light plow suited to thin soils. Irrigation was important in some areas. In northern Italy, canals and drainage works supported intensive cultivation in Lombardy and the Po Valley, and the growth of communes and cities went hand in hand with rural change, since urban elites invested in land and cities needed food. In Muslim al-Andalus, irrigated gardens and a varied range of crops formed a sophisticated agricultural system, and historians continue to debate how far crops and techniques diffused from the Islamic world into the wider Mediterranean, an argument that has been important but also contested. As Christian kingdoms advanced southward, notably after the capture of Toledo in 1085, the conquest of the Ebro valley, and the thirteenth century conquests of Córdoba, Valencia, and Seville, resettlement followed. Rulers and towns issued charters granting land and privileges to settlers, and many Muslim farmers, known in later sources as mudéjars, continued to work the land under Christian rule, notably in the Valencian region, where they maintained irrigation systems. In Castile, large scale sheep herding and seasonal movement of flocks across the plateau led to the privileges of the Mesta, confirmed in 1273, and helped support an expanding wool trade that became increasingly important in the later Middle Ages.

In central and eastern Europe, as Section 42.10 showed, expansion was characterized by frontier settlement, forest clearance, new villages, and negotiated legal arrangements, with rulers playing a prominent role. In many districts, population density remained lower than in the west, and land was relatively abundant, which gave settlers bargaining power. Byzantine and Orthodox lands in the Balkans and Anatolia, where state fiscal systems and large estates interacted with village communities, followed their own patterns, and historians still debate the extent and timing of their rural growth. In mountain and pastoral regions, including the Alps, the Pyrenees, the Apennines, and the highlands of Scotland and Scandinavia, livestock, transhumance, and mixed economies were central. Seasonal movement of animals, dairying, and the sale of cheese, wool, and hides were as important as cereal growing, and high valley colonization could be quite independent of the lowland developments that dominate older accounts.

Regional Variation

One Expansion, Many Systems

Northwestern Europe: Heavier soils, mixed grain and livestock farming, open fields in many districts, growing use of horses alongside oxen, three-field rotation in some regions, and large scale drainage on the coasts.
Mediterranean Europe: Wheat, vines, olives, and sheep, lighter plows, irrigation and canal building in places such as Lombardy and al-Andalus, close ties between estates and cities, and frontier resettlement in Iberia.
Central and eastern Europe: Forest clearance, new villages under settlement charters, migration by settlers of different origins, ruler sponsorship, and abundant land relative to labor.
Mountain and pastoral regions: Transhumance, livestock products, high valley colonization such as the Walser migrations, and mixed economies where cereals were secondary or supplementary.
Scandinavia and the north: Expanding farm settlement into interior districts, a major role for fishing, livestock, and woodland products, and agricultural limits imposed by short growing seasons.

Why the comparison matters: The same period of growth produced different landscapes and social arrangements, and the techniques that mattered in one region could be irrelevant in another.

Growth Creates New Opportunities and New Limits

By the thirteenth century, population and cultivated area had expanded enormously in many regions. The consequences were substantial. Agricultural output rose in total, though not necessarily per acre or per person. Rural populations were larger and villages more numerous. Households and regions increasingly specialized, in wool, wine, grain, flax, livestock, or dairy products, and a larger share of produce entered markets. Rural households purchased more manufactured goods and metal tools, and lords converted some obligations into money payments. The countryside was more closely tied to towns, which depended on rural food supplies and raw materials and offered markets for agricultural produce. These developments provided the foundation for the urban growth, fairs, and commercial networks examined in Chapter 43, and they helped to fund the expansion of church institutions, royal government, and cultural life examined in Chapters 44 through 48.

Growth also had costs. As population rose, cultivation moved onto poorer soils and marginal land, which yielded less and was more vulnerable to bad weather. Commons shrank in many places as land was converted to arable, reducing the grazing and fuel on which poorer households relied. In some regions, holdings were divided among heirs into smaller and smaller units, and by the late thirteenth century a significant number of peasant households in parts of England, France, and elsewhere held plots too small to support a family without additional work or wages. Forests were reduced and, in some areas, timber and fuel became scarcer and dearer. Soil erosion and exhaustion affected some districts. The need to feed a growing population meant dependence on regular harvests, and a succession of poor years could have severe effects. Historians use the concept of diminishing returns to describe the way in which each additional unit of land or labor yielded progressively less, though the extent to which this applied in particular regions is debated, and not all regions reached the same limits at the same time.

This chapter does not narrate the crisis that followed. The famines, climatic stress, and demographic catastrophe of the fourteenth century, including the Great Famine of 1315 to 1317 and the Black Death from 1347, belong to Chapter 50, and it would be misleading to treat the expansion of the High Middle Ages as merely a prelude to disaster. Many regions continued to prosper into the early fourteenth century, and the achievements of this period, including new fields, mills, villages, and organizations, endured. What Chapter 42 can establish is that the success of the expansion had created a far larger population dependent on a heavily exploited agricultural system, and that growth based on bringing in new land and labor was likely to face limits once the available land was occupied.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Medieval Countryside, c. 1000 to c. 1300

What Changed

  • Cultivated area and the number of villages and farmsteads increased substantially in most regions
  • Forests, marshes, and rough grazing were reduced in many districts, and the use of commons changed
  • Mills, horses, new rotations, and improved plows were adopted in many regions, though unevenly
  • Many villages were founded or reorganized, and rural parishes multiplied
  • The countryside was increasingly connected to markets, money, and towns

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture remained the foundation of the economy, and most people remained rural
  • Household labor, including that of women and children, remained the main source of agricultural work
  • Yields remained low by modern standards, and harvests remained vulnerable to weather
  • Lordship, church tithes, and customary obligations continued to claim a share of produce
  • Local environment, soil, and climate continued to shape what was possible

Who experienced change most directly: Settlers on new land, villagers whose commons were reduced, households near mills and markets, and communities in frontier regions experienced the sharpest changes. Who experienced greater continuity: Farmers in older, settled districts with stable field systems and households in remote upland and pastoral areas often saw gradual change in daily routines.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents the picture of a static medieval countryside and the opposite error of imagining a sudden transformation of rural life.

Chapter Summary

Between roughly 1000 and 1300, much of Europe experienced sustained demographic and agricultural expansion. Europeans cleared forests, drained wetlands, founded settlements, intensified cultivation, adopted and adapted new techniques, and increasingly used animal and mechanical power. These changes were not the result of a single technological agricultural revolution, nor did they occur uniformly across Europe. Population growth and agricultural expansion reinforced one another, transforming landscapes and rural communities while creating both the surplus that supported High Medieval economic growth and the ecological and demographic pressures that would become important in the fourteenth century.

Around 1000, Europe was overwhelmingly rural, with extensive forests, marshes, and rough grazing, and with highly uneven population density. These landscapes were not empty wilderness but were used for timber, pasture, game, and other resources. Because no census existed, population growth has to be reconstructed from estate surveys, Domesday Book, tax records, church foundations, archaeological survey, and pollen evidence. Historians agree that population and settlement expanded substantially across these centuries while disagreeing about precise totals and regional timing, and estimates that put European population at roughly doubling should be treated as ranges.

The reasons for growth were several. Reduced external raiding and consolidating local lordship provided relative security, cultivation expanded, technology and organization improved, lords and rulers had incentives to attract settlers, and expanding markets rewarded production. Climate, including the Medieval Climate Anomaly, may have helped in some regions, but historians reject any simple chain from warmth to agricultural revolution. The central process was a feedback cycle in which population growth encouraged clearance and intensification, and greater output allowed more people to survive.

Expansion changed the physical landscape. Assarting and forest clearance, marsh drainage, dike building, vineyard expansion, and upland cultivation created new fields, but they also reduced woodland and commons that many people used and produced conflicts among lords, monasteries, villagers, and rulers. Cooperative institutions such as the water boards of Holland arose to manage reclaimed land. These transformations make the period an important chapter in European environmental history.

Agricultural techniques changed in regionally varied and cumulative ways. Heavy plows mattered in heavy northern soils but little in Mediterranean lands. Horses spread but did not simply replace oxen. Two-field and three-field rotations, legumes, and manuring improved the use of land in different places. Watermills and windmills extended nonhuman power, enriched lords through milling rights, and altered waterways. The historical debate over a medieval agricultural revolution shows that the evidence supports gradual, regionally varied change more strongly than a sudden transformation.

Settlement itself was remade. Many villages were founded, reorganized, or relocated, and field systems, parish churches, and common rights gave structure to rural communities. Lords, bishops, monasteries, and rulers sponsored colonization by offering favorable terms, and peasants themselves had strong incentives to move to new land. The Cistercians were important consolidators and managers of estates, often assembled from land already settled and cultivated, but they were not typically pioneers of large scale clearance and did not invent efficient agriculture in empty wilderness, and much of the labor and initiative came from peasants.

The expansion was European in scope. In central and eastern Europe, the Ostsiedlung and similar movements in Hungary and the Alps involved settlement under negotiated charters, with newcomers and local populations interacting in complex ways that later nationalist narratives distorted. On the Iberian frontier, Christian resettlement and large scale sheep herding developed alongside continuing Muslim farming communities. Mediterranean, northwestern, central and eastern, and mountain regions each followed distinct agricultural systems, and no single model, whether the French open field village or any other, represents the whole.

By the thirteenth century, growth had produced larger rural populations, more villages, greater specialization, and closer ties between countryside and towns, laying the foundations for the commercial revival of Chapter 43. It had also brought cultivation onto poorer land, reduced commons, fragmented holdings, pressured forests, and left a larger population dependent on harvests. The fourteenth century crisis belongs to Chapter 50, but Chapter 42 shows that the success of High Medieval expansion created a society that was larger, more connected, and more exposed.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Assart
A piece of woodland or waste cleared and brought into cultivation; records of assarts, including fines and disputes, are major evidence for clearance.
Demesne
The portion of an estate retained and managed directly by a lord, as distinct from land held by tenants.
Open field
A system in which a village's arable land lay in large fields divided into strips held by different households, with community regulation of cropping and grazing.
Fallow
Land left uncultivated for a season to recover fertility and provide grazing.
Three-field rotation
A system dividing arable land into three parts, with winter grain, spring crops, and fallow in turn, which reduced the proportion of idle land compared with a two-field system.
Two-field system
A rotation in which half the arable lay fallow each year, common in Mediterranean and many northern districts.
Mouldboard plow
A wheeled plow with a blade, coulter, and curved board that cut and turned the soil, especially valuable in heavy clay soils.
Ard
A light plow that scratched a shallow furrow, well suited to thin, dry soils such as those of much of the Mediterranean.
Horse collar
A padded harness fitted around the horse's shoulders and chest that became widespread in medieval Europe and was well suited to heavy traction; its importance does not mean that earlier horse harnessing was inherently ineffective.
Commons
Land or resources, such as pasture, woodland, and waste, to which villagers held customary rights of use.
Nucleated village
A compact cluster of houses and farm buildings surrounded by fields, as opposed to dispersed settlement.
Dispersed settlement
A pattern of isolated farmsteads and small hamlets spread across the landscape.
Grange
An outlying farm of a monastery, especially a Cistercian house, managed as a unit of production.
Lay brother
A member of a religious community, particularly among the Cistercians, who took vows but was not ordained and performed agricultural and practical labor.
Polder
Land reclaimed from water by dikes and drainage and kept dry through continuing maintenance.
Dike
An embankment of earth, clay, or similar material built to hold back the sea or a river and to protect or reclaim low-lying land; its maintenance was typically a collective responsibility enforced by local institutions.
Water board
A cooperative institution, such as that of Rijnland, responsible for maintaining dikes and drainage and authorized to levy contributions.
Ostsiedlung
The medieval settlement of German-speaking and other colonists in the lands east of the Elbe and Saale and beyond, involving migration, new villages and towns, and interaction with local populations.
Locator
An entrepreneur who contracted with a lord or ruler to recruit settlers and found a village or town.
Transhumance
The seasonal movement of livestock between lowland and highland pastures.
Mesta
The Castilian association of sheep owners whose privileges, confirmed in 1273, supported large scale migratory sheep herding.
Banal mill
A mill whose use was imposed on tenants by a lord, who claimed a fee or share of the grain.
Tithe
A customary payment to the church, theoretically a tenth of produce, an important source of income for parishes and religious institutions.
Palynology
The study of fossil pollen, used to reconstruct vegetation, clearance, and cultivation.
Medieval Climate Anomaly
A period of relatively favorable climate in parts of the North Atlantic region, roughly 950 to 1250, whose extent and effects remain debated.
Diminishing returns
The tendency for additional land or labor applied to a given system to yield progressively smaller additional output.
Mudéjar
A Muslim living under Christian rule in the Iberian kingdoms, many of whom continued farming and maintained irrigation systems.
Domesday Book
The record of the survey of England ordered by William I and completed in 1086, noting land, plow teams, mills, and woodland.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it reveals the material foundation on which the rest of Part VII stands. The towns, universities, cathedrals, crusading armies, and expanding governments of the eleventh through thirteenth centuries were supported by a rural population that was growing, bringing more land into cultivation, and producing a surplus that others could claim and spend. Understanding that foundation changes how a student reads the political and cultural achievements of the period, because it shows the countryside not as a static backdrop but as an arena of change.

The chapter also shows how historians approach a problem for which the evidence is indirect and incomplete. Population, yields, and settlement dates are reconstructed from tax lists, surveys, pollen, and excavation, and the confident conclusion that growth occurred sits alongside real uncertainty about its scale. It shows, too, how historians should resist single explanations. Technology, climate, security, lordship, markets, and the choices of ordinary households all mattered, and they interacted in ways that varied from the marshes of Holland to the forests of Bohemia and the pastures of Castile.

Several developments introduced here continue beyond the chapter. The village and field systems created in these centuries, the mills and dikes, and the parish and settlement patterns shaped rural Europe for hundreds of years. The legal and contractual forms of colonization influenced eastern and central Europe long afterward, and their memory was later claimed and contested by national historians. The pressures created by growth, including fragmented holdings, reduced commons, and dependence on harvests, set the stage for the crisis examined in Chapter 50, though that crisis was not predetermined.

Within Volume II, this chapter marks a transition from the formation of Latin Christendom and its neighbors to the confident growth and elaboration of the High Middle Ages. It connects regions that older narratives often separate, linking the Low Countries to the Elbe, the Po Valley to the Iberian frontier, and the Alps to the Baltic through shared processes of settlement and land use. Chapter 43 now turns to the towns, merchants, and markets that grew alongside and depended upon this expanding countryside.
