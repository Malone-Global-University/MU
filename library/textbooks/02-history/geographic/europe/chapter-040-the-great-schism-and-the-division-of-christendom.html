The Great Schism and the Division of Christendom

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 16 July 1054, in the great church of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, papal envoys laid a document on the altar and departed. The act has come to symbolize the division of Christendom, yet the people who performed it did not believe they were splitting the church in two, and most of their contemporaries did not notice that Christianity had permanently divided that summer.

According to the surviving accounts, the envoys were led by Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida, and the document was a sentence of excommunication directed at the patriarch of Constantinople, Michael Cerularius, and his supporters. Within days Cerularius and his synod answered with a condemnation of the document and its bearers. The pope who had sent the legates, Leo IX, had died in April, around the time they reached the city, and historians have disagreed over how far his death affected the canonical authority of the legation's later acts. Nothing in the aftermath resembled a formal declaration that the Greek and Latin churches were now separate bodies. Byzantine and Latin Christians continued to meet, trade, intermarry, fight alongside one another, and, in many places, worship in each other's churches for decades afterward.

Why, then, does the year 1054 stand at the center of so many textbook accounts of medieval Europe? The answer lies partly in what the episode revealed and partly in what later generations made of it. The quarrel of 1054 exposed several long developing fault lines at once: rival understandings of papal authority and the standing of the other ancient patriarchates, differing liturgical customs, a dispute over the wording of the Nicene Creed, the political ambitions of a reforming papacy, and the entanglement of both churches with the Normans of southern Italy and the Byzantine emperors. After the Fourth Crusade of 1204, examined in Chapter 41, the memory of 1054 acquired heavier symbolic weight, and later polemicists on both sides treated it as the moment of rupture.

This chapter therefore asks a more demanding question than "What happened in 1054?" It asks how a single, imperially protected Christian church of the late Roman world became, over roughly six centuries, two institutional and cultural communities, Latin and Greek, each convinced of its own orthodoxy and increasingly suspicious of the other. The chapter treats the separation as a process with political, linguistic, institutional, theological, and commercial dimensions rather than a single dramatic event. It also stresses that Christendom was never perfectly unified before the eleventh century. The churches that rejected the Council of Chalcedon in 451, such as the Armenian, Coptic, and Syriac communities, had already separated from both Rome and Constantinople, a division older than the Latin and Greek one.

The chapter's responsibility within Part VI is specific. Chapter 38 examined the missionary work and cultural transmission that carried Orthodox Christianity into the Slavic world, and Chapter 39 traced the political histories of Bulgaria, Serbia, and Kievan Rus. Chapter 40 now explains the growing institutional, theological, linguistic, and political separation between Latin and Greek Christianity, a separation that affected all of those regions because they lay between the two ecclesiastical spheres. Chapter 41 then follows the Fourth Crusade, Byzantine restoration, Balkan transformation, Ottoman expansion, and the fall of Constantinople in 1453. This chapter keeps its focus on the division itself and does not narrate the later political history belonging to those chapters.

Historians reconstruct this subject from an unusually varied body of evidence. Papal letters, conciliar canons, and theological treatises survive from both sides, as do the documents produced during the confrontation of 1054, polemical lists of the "errors" of the other church, chronicles, histories such as Anna Komnene's Alexiad, commercial privileges granted to Italian merchants, and monastic and liturgical manuscripts. Most of these sources were written by clergy, officials, or members of elite families, and many were produced precisely because someone wished to prove the other side wrong. Readers must therefore ask not only what these texts say but what they were trying to accomplish.

Finally, this chapter asks students to keep three levels of analysis in view. The first is the institutional level of popes, patriarchs, councils, and emperors. The second is the level of ideas, where theologians argued over the Holy Spirit, the sacrament of the Eucharist, and the nature of church authority. The third is the level of ordinary religious life, where merchants, soldiers, monks, pilgrims, and families encountered difference in daily practice. The division of Christendom emerged from the interaction of all three, and it was neither inevitable nor complete in 1054.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Christendom was already plural before 1054 and distinguish the post-Chalcedonian divisions from the Latin and Greek separation.
  2. Trace the political, linguistic, and institutional drift between Rome and Constantinople from the fifth through the ninth centuries.
  3. Analyze the pentarchy and the competing Roman and Constantinopolitan understandings of ecclesiastical authority.
  4. Distinguish doctrinal disputes, especially the filioque, from disputes over liturgy, discipline, and jurisdiction.
  5. Describe the Photian affair and explain why it mattered for Bulgaria and the wider Slavic world.
  6. Contextualize the events of 1054 within papal reform, Norman expansion in southern Italy, and Byzantine imperial politics.
  7. Evaluate the claim that 1054 was "the" schism by weighing evidence for process and for event.
  8. Assess how the Crusades, Italian commercial privileges, and episodes of violence deepened mutual hostility between 1054 and the late twelfth century.
  9. Compare how borderland societies, including Rus, Bulgaria, Serbia, Hungary, Dalmatia, and southern Italy, experienced the division differently.
  10. Interpret primary evidence from the controversy of 1054 with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitation.
  11. Distinguish historical reconstruction from later memory, including the ecumenical declarations of 1965.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. When did the separation between Latin and Greek Christianity begin, and why is it difficult to assign a single date?
  2. Why did a dispute over authority prove more difficult to resolve than disputes over customs such as fasting or bread?
  3. How did the end of unified Roman imperial rule in the Mediterranean shape the relationship between the two churches?
  4. Why did the reform movement in the western church, which sought purity and discipline, make compromise with Constantinople harder?
  5. In what ways did political and military interests, particularly in southern Italy, shape the quarrel of 1054?
  6. How do historians evaluate sources that were written to condemn the other side?
  7. How did societies on the frontier between Latin and Orthodox spheres, from Rus to Dalmatia, manage religious difference?
  8. How did later events and later memory transform 1054 into a symbol of division?

Historical Context

The church that Constantine and his successors favored, examined in Chapter 25, took shape within a Roman Empire that spoke principally Latin in the west and Greek in the east. Its leadership was organized around bishops, and by the fourth and fifth centuries a few bishops possessed unusual prestige. Rome claimed the authority of the apostle Peter and Paul and the dignity of the old imperial capital. Constantinople, refounded by Constantine, became the seat of emperors and acquired ecclesiastical standing as "New Rome." Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem, as ancient apostolic and scriptural centers, completed what later writers called the pentarchy. As Chapter 26 explained, the councils of the fourth through the seventh centuries defined doctrine, but they also contested jurisdiction, and the church that emerged from late antiquity already contained competing hierarchies and competing languages of authority.

The political framework then changed. In the west, the post-Roman kingdoms examined in Part V did not share a single government with the emperor in Constantinople, while the eastern empire survived and, as Chapters 36 and 37 showed, endured severe contraction under Persian, Arab, and Balkan pressure. The bishop of Rome lived within a landscape increasingly shaped by Lombard, Frankish, and local power, while the patriarch of Constantinople lived in the shadow of an emperor who claimed to govern the church in cooperation with its clergy. The two ecclesiastical centers still corresponded and attended councils, but they did so under different political conditions. The Arab conquests of the seventh century also removed Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem from Byzantine rule, leaving three of the five patriarchal sees under Muslim governance and concentrating Byzantine political power among the patriarchates more heavily in Constantinople, which weakened the practical balance of the pentarchy.

Language compounded these developments. In the late Roman Empire, many educated people moved between Latin and Greek. By the early medieval period, far fewer did so. Knowledge of Greek declined sharply in the Latin west, and knowledge of Latin declined in the Greek east, so that translations, summaries, and secondhand reports mediated theological communication. Misunderstandings that might once have been resolved by an informed reader became harder to untangle. The chapter will return to this problem repeatedly, since several of the disputes discussed below were intensified by imperfect translation of technical vocabulary and by unfamiliarity with the other side's customs.

By the early eleventh century, the Latin west was undergoing dramatic transformation, as Chapter 42 and the following chapters on the High Middle Ages will show. Population and agricultural output were rising, monasteries and cathedral schools were multiplying, and a movement of church reform was gathering strength, which Chapter 44 examines in connection with the investiture conflict. Meanwhile, the Byzantine empire under the Macedonian dynasty had recovered much territory, absorbed Bulgaria in 1018 as Chapter 39 explained, and stood at a height of prestige. Rome and Constantinople were therefore not a failing east and a rising west but two confident and expanding Christian polities whose leaders took their own claims seriously. That confidence is essential to understanding why neither side readily yielded when disputes arose.

Students should finally distinguish between the historical meaning of the words used by contemporaries and the labels applied by modern scholarship. The term "Great Schism" is also used for the Western Schism of 1378 to 1417, examined in Chapter 53, in which rival popes claimed authority within Latin Christianity. To avoid confusion, this chapter uses "the Schism" or "the East-West division" for the separation of Latin and Greek churches. Equally, "Orthodox" and "Catholic" were not yet exclusive denominational labels in the eleventh century. Both communities described themselves as orthodox, meaning rightly believing, and as catholic, meaning universal. Using later confessional labels uncritically would distort the history.

Autocephaly

Section 40.1

One Christendom or Several?

It is tempting to imagine a single, harmonious Christian church of the first millennium that suddenly broke apart in the eleventh century. The evidence points elsewhere. By the sixth century the churches that accepted the Council of Chalcedon's definition of Christ as one person in two natures were already separated from those that rejected it. The Armenian church formally repudiated Chalcedon in the early sixth century, and the Coptic church of Egypt, the Syriac Orthodox church, and the Ethiopian church followed their own paths. The Church of the East, centered in the Persian Empire and Mesopotamia, had separated earlier still. These communities lay mostly outside what modern geographers call Europe, but some, particularly Armenians, were present in Byzantine territory and in the Balkans, and their existence reminds students that "Christendom" never meant a single institution.

What changed in the eleventh century was the relative importance of the two remaining large communities that did accept Chalcedon. These were the Greek-speaking church centered on Constantinople and the Latin-speaking church centered on Rome. Both claimed continuity with the apostles, both regarded the ecumenical councils as authoritative, and both saw themselves as the guardians of right belief. The Slavic churches, Bulgarian, Serbian, and Rus, which Chapters 38 and 39 examined, looked chiefly to Constantinople. Hungary, Poland, Bohemia, and the Scandinavian kingdoms, examined in Chapter 35, looked to Rome. The line between these spheres was neither sharp nor stable, and the borderlands between them, discussed in Section 40.8, were zones of overlapping jurisdiction and mixed practice.

The concept of the pentarchy provides a useful framework, though students should use it with care. Under the late Roman emperors, and especially in the legal codification attributed to Justinian, the five great sees of Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem were regarded as together governing the church. This view fitted well with Byzantine imperial theory, in which the emperor, the patriarchs, and councils cooperated. It fitted the Roman view less comfortably, since Rome claimed a distinctive primacy derived from Peter rather than merely a first place among equals. After the Arab conquests, three of the five patriarchates lay under Muslim rule, and the Greek church's remaining weight rested on Constantinople. The pentarchy thus remained a theory that seemed to describe the church even as the practical balance tilted.

The central historical question, then, is how two churches that shared so much, scripture, creed, sacramental life, monasticism, councils, and the memory of the martyrs, came to regard each other with suspicion and, in some circles, outright hostility. Answering that question requires moving between the high politics of popes and emperors and the more diffuse changes in language, education, and local custom that shaped what ordinary clergy and laypeople expected from their church.

Section 40.2

Rome and Constantinople from Chalcedon to Iconoclasm

The relationship between the sees of Rome and Constantinople began with an inherent tension. The Council of Constantinople in 381 declared that the bishop of Constantinople should rank immediately after the bishop of Rome, because Constantinople was New Rome. The Council of Chalcedon in 451 repeated and strengthened this principle in its twenty-eighth canon, granting Constantinople privileges comparable to those of Rome and giving it jurisdiction over several eastern regions. Pope Leo I rejected the canon, arguing that rank in the church rested on apostolic foundation rather than on political importance. The disagreement was therefore one of principle. Constantinople's claim rested on imperial status, while Rome's rested on Peter, and each side could cite precedent without conceding that the other's reasoning had force.

The sees came into open conflict during the Acacian Schism of 484 to 519. In an attempt to reconcile Christians who rejected Chalcedon, the emperor Zeno and Patriarch Acacius issued a conciliatory formula, the Henotikon. Rome objected and broke communion with Constantinople, and the breach lasted thirty-five years until Emperor Justin I and Pope Hormisdas restored communion on terms favorable to Rome. The episode matters for two reasons. It shows that formal breaks between the two sees could be repaired, and it shows that Rome's view of its authority was often asserted most firmly when imperial policy seemed to threaten doctrine. Constantinople, for its part, learned that its patriarchs needed to maintain communion with Rome to claim universality.

In the sixth century the emperor Justinian reconquered Italy, restoring imperial authority over Rome, and the papacy for a time operated within Byzantine control. Popes of this period were often Greek-speaking, and several came from the eastern provinces. This arrangement, sometimes called the Byzantine papacy, ended gradually as Lombard invasions weakened the empire's hold on Italy and as the popes found themselves increasingly responsible for the defense and government of Rome. By the seventh century the papacy still sought imperial protection but no longer relied on it entirely, and the cultural gap between the Greek east and the Latin west was widening.

The Quinisext Council, held in Constantinople in 692 to complete earlier councils' work on discipline, illustrates the divergence in custom. Its canons addressed clerical marriage, fasting practices, and various observances, and several differed from Roman practice. Pope Sergius I refused to subscribe, and later popes accepted some canons while rejecting others. The council did not cause a rupture, but it displayed a recurring pattern in which each church regarded its own customs as normative and the other's as deviations requiring correction. Chapter 37 examined how the Byzantine church and state navigated the subsequent iconoclast controversy. For present purposes the significant result was that the controversy shifted papal allegiances. Pope Gregory III and his successors opposed the iconoclast emperors, and traditional accounts hold that Emperor Leo III retaliated by transferring southern Italy, Sicily, and Illyricum from papal to Constantinopolitan ecclesiastical jurisdiction. The date, scope, and even occurrence of that transfer are debated, but the territorial dispute it represents persisted and complicated later relations in southern Italy and the Balkans.

By the middle of the eighth century, Byzantine authority in central Italy had collapsed. The Lombards captured Ravenna in 751, and the papacy turned to the Frankish kings for protection. This is the moment at which the political geography of Latin Christendom began to separate institutionally from the Byzantine empire. The papacy had not decided to leave the empire. It had run out of alternatives, and the consequences, discussed next, were large.

Section 40.3

Franks, Emperors, and the Photian Affair

The alliance between the papacy and the Frankish rulers, traced in Chapter 31, gave the popes a powerful protector and gave the Carolingian kings access to the legitimacy that a papal blessing could confer. The coronation of Charlemagne as emperor on Christmas Day in 800 created an imperial title in the west that the Byzantine court could not accept as equal. At that time the throne in Constantinople was held by the empress Irene, and Byzantine writers tended to regard the coronation as an usurpation of the Roman imperial name. Eventually Constantinople grudgingly acknowledged Charlemagne's successors in a limited sense, but the existence of a western emperor installed by the pope introduced a political rivalry that no earlier period had known. For the first time, two rulers claimed the heritage of Rome.

Frankish theologians were also active critics of Byzantine practice. The Carolingian work known as the Libri Carolini, produced in response to a mistranslated version of the Second Council of Nicaea's decisions on icons, criticized the Greek position, though it was not endorsed in full by the papacy. Frankish councils promoted the insertion of the filioque into the Creed, as explained in Section 40.4. Here, the essential point is that Frankish learning, ambition, and confidence brought the Latin west into more direct and more critical contact with the Greek church than had occurred for generations, and that contact produced friction as well as exchange.

The first serious rupture of the ninth century came with the affair surrounding Photius. In 858 the emperor Michael III's government removed Patriarch Ignatius and installed Photius, a distinguished scholar and official who was a layman and who was rapidly ordained through the clerical ranks. Ignatius's supporters protested, and the matter went to Pope Nicholas I, who took the opportunity to assert Roman authority. A council in Constantinople in 861 confirmed Photius, and Nicholas, after contested negotiations and in the context of his dispute over Illyricum and southern Italy, annulled the decision in 863 and declared Photius deposed. Photius responded in turn. The dispute sharpened when Boris of Bulgaria, whose conversion Chapter 38 examined, sought to place his church under Constantinople's authority or to secure its autonomy, and Latin missionaries from Rome arrived in Bulgaria as competitors to Greek clergy.

In an encyclical of 867 Photius attacked Latin missionaries and criticized Latin practices, including the use of the filioque, fasting customs, and clerical celibacy, and he questioned the authority of Rome to judge the matter. The immediate context shows that jurisdiction over a newly converted Slavic kingdom drove the dispute at least as much as doctrine did. A change of ruler in Constantinople then reversed the situation. The emperor Basil I deposed Photius and restored Ignatius, and a council held in 869 and 870, counted in the Latin tradition as the Eighth Ecumenical Council, condemned Photius. Bulgaria's church eventually came under Constantinople. When Ignatius died, Photius returned to the patriarchate in 877, and a council in 879 and 880 recognized him with the participation of papal legates, though the later Latin tradition did not accept it as ecumenical. The affair thus ended in a patched peace rather than a lasting rupture.

The Photian affair is a prime example of why historians resist treating the division as inevitable. Communion was broken and restored. The principal protagonists, including Pope John VIII, were willing to compromise. Yet the affair left lasting residue. It established that disputes over the Creed, over Slavic jurisdiction, and over Roman authority could converge, and it fixed in Greek memory a list of Latin "errors" that polemicists would reuse. Historians who study Byzantine anti-Latin writing have shown that these lists tended to grow over time, as each generation of writers added new complaints to the inherited stock.

Section 40.4

Authority, Creed, and Practice

The subjects that separated Latin and Greek Christians fall into three overlapping groups, and distinguishing them is essential for understanding why some disagreements could be tolerated and others could not. The first concerned authority, the second concerned doctrine and the wording of the Creed, and the third concerned liturgy and discipline. Medieval participants themselves did not always keep the categories apart, and the polemical literature frequently treated trivial customs as evidence of grave theological error. Students should therefore read the sources critically while recognizing that for participants the issues were sincerely felt.

The question of authority was the most fundamental. By the eleventh century, the papacy increasingly understood itself to possess universal jurisdiction over the church, including the authority to judge, depose, and discipline bishops everywhere, and that authority was grounded in the succession from Peter. The Greek church acknowledged Rome's special honor and its historic role as a guardian of doctrine, and Byzantine writers often addressed popes respectfully. But Greek ecclesiology placed ultimate authority in the whole church acting through ecumenical councils, and it understood the patriarchs as collaborators in governance. A papal claim to rule the entire church by right of Peter struck many Greeks as an innovation. Reformers in Rome, such as Humbert, saw Greek resistance as disobedience. Neither side was inventing its position for the occasion, which is why the question proved so difficult to resolve through diplomacy.

The doctrinal dispute concerned the procession of the Holy Spirit. The Nicene Creed, as received from the councils of 325 and 381, states that the Spirit proceeds from the Father. The filioque, Latin for "and the Son," is traditionally associated with the Third Council of Toledo in 589 and with Visigothic anti-Arian theology, which affirmed the divinity of the Son, although the textual history of its insertion into the creed at Toledo is disputed. Its use became securely established in the Frankish world, where Charlemagne's theologians defended it and the Council of Aachen in 809 supported it. Pope Leo III accepted the theology but is reported to have declined to insert the phrase into the Creed as sung in Rome. Rome adopted the insertion in the early eleventh century, traditionally in 1014, as an outcome of influence from the north. Greek theologians objected on two grounds. They held that the doctrine of a double procession compromised the monarchy of the Father as the single source of divinity, and they argued that no church had the right to alter the text of a creed established by an ecumenical council without the consent of the whole church. Latin theologians countered that the phrase expressed a legitimate clarification. The dispute therefore combined a question of theology with a question of procedure and authority.

A third set of disagreements concerned liturgical and disciplinary customs. The Latin church used unleavened bread, azymes, for the Eucharist, while the Greek church used leavened bread. The Greeks regarded the Latin practice as evidence of a deficient understanding, arguing that it recalled Jewish observance, while the Latins defended it by appeal to the Last Supper tradition. Latin clergy were increasingly required to be celibate, and reform Rome insisted on this strictly, while the Greek church permitted married men to be ordained to the priesthood though not to the episcopate. Other points included the Latin custom of fasting on Saturdays, differences in the rules of Lent, the shaving of beards, the use of certain foods, and the manner of baptism. Many of these customs were also found elsewhere. The Armenian church, for example, used unleavened bread, a fact that Greek polemicists used against Armenians in similar terms. The significance of these disagreements lay less in their theological weight than in what they came to symbolize. When a church regards the other's habits as signs of heresy, even minor differences can become barriers to communion.

It is important to recognize that many contemporaries distinguished serious from tolerable differences. The patriarch of Antioch, Peter III, writing in 1054, reportedly treated several of the Latin practices as acceptable and regarded the filioque as the principal concern. Many Latin and Greek clergy in border regions, such as southern Italy, practiced side by side without constant conflict. These nuances should caution students against treating the disputes as a uniform wall of opposition.

Section 40.5

Reform Rome, Norman Italy, and Constantinople

The crisis of 1054 occurred at a particular juncture in the histories of both churches. In the Latin west, the papacy was being transformed. After a period in which local aristocratic families and, later, German emperors dominated papal elections, a movement of reform gained momentum from the 1040s. Its leaders attacked simony, the purchase of church offices, and clerical marriage, called Nicolaitism by its opponents, and insisted that the church should govern itself free from lay control. Leo IX, elected in 1049 with imperial backing, symbolized this program. He traveled widely, held councils, and surrounded himself with reformers from Lorraine and Italy, including Humbert, a monk from Burgundy who became cardinal bishop of Silva Candida. Chapter 44 examines the reform movement and the investiture conflict at length. The point here is that the reform papacy articulated its claims to universal authority with unusual vigor and with a conviction that discipline and doctrine required uniformity.

Southern Italy was the immediate field in which reform ambitions, Byzantine interests, and Norman power collided. For centuries, Byzantine authority had reached Apulia and Calabria, and Greek-rite monasteries and churches flourished there alongside Latin ones. The region also contained Lombard principalities and Muslim-ruled Sicily. From the early eleventh century, Norman adventurers arrived as mercenaries and then established their own lordships. Their expansion threatened Byzantine holdings and also the papal territories and the independence of Latin bishops. Leo IX, hoping to expel the Normans, formed a military alliance with the Byzantine commander in Italy and raised an army. At Civitate in June 1053 the Normans defeated the papal forces and took the pope prisoner. The defeat humiliated the pope, and it left both Rome and Constantinople with a continuing interest in finding a way to cooperate against the Normans, an interest that colored the diplomacy of the following year.

In Constantinople, Michael Cerularius had become patriarch in 1043, after a career that included political involvement and temporary exile. He was a formidable figure, capable of acting with considerable independence toward the emperor, and he saw the Latin presence in the empire, including in southern Italy, with growing concern. The emperor Constantine IX Monomachos governed a prosperous but politically fragile realm. He considered the Normans a pressing danger, and he wanted a good relationship with the papacy. That policy created a difference between emperor and patriarch which shaped the events of 1054 and which helps explain why the emperor was prepared to receive the papal legates warmly while the patriarch was not.

The immediate trigger was a campaign against Latin customs in the Greek church. In or around 1053, Latin sources report, Cerularius closed Latin churches in Constantinople and objected to the use of unleavened bread, treating Latin sacraments with suspicion. A letter on Latin practice, attributed in its composition to Leo of Ohrid, archbishop of Ohrid in the former Bulgarian lands, and addressed to a bishop in Apulia, attacked the azymes, Saturday fasting, and other customs. It was meant to circulate among Latin clergy in southern Italy, where Greek and Latin churches coexisted. Humbert translated the letter into Latin, and Leo IX replied in a lengthy letter that defended Rome's primacy in forceful terms and drew on the Donation of Constantine, a document later demonstrated to be a forgery, as part of its argument. The use of such a text shows how reform Rome sometimes relied on inherited material that later scholarship would discredit. The exchange also shows that, even before the legates arrived, the dispute had moved from custom to authority.

Section 40.6

The Events of 1054

In the spring of 1054, a papal legation traveled to Constantinople carrying letters to the emperor and the patriarch. Its principal members were Cardinal Humbert, Frederick of Lorraine, who was the pope's chancellor and later became Pope Stephen IX, and Peter, archbishop of Amalfi. The ostensible purpose was to seek an alliance with the emperor against the Normans and to resolve the dispute with the patriarch. The legates reached Constantinople around April 1054. Leo IX died on 19 April, shortly after or around the time of their arrival. His death before Humbert's action of July raised questions about the continuing canonical authority of the legation, and historians have disagreed over the validity of its subsequent acts. The papal throne remained vacant until the election of Victor II in 1055.

The legates received an enthusiastic reception from the emperor, who favored cooperation. Their relations with Cerularius were poor from the outset. Humbert wrote in forthright and sometimes insulting language, and the patriarch declined to meet the legation in a manner the Romans found acceptable. The emperor tried to mediate. A monk of the Studite monastery, Nicetas Stethatos, had written a treatise attacking Latin customs, and at the emperor's urging he withdrew it publicly, an indication that the court was willing to sacrifice patriarchal sympathizers for the sake of an understanding. Cerularius, however, did not yield, and the legates eventually lost patience.

On Saturday, 16 July 1054, the legates entered Hagia Sophia before the liturgy and placed on the altar a document that excommunicated Cerularius and his associates, then left the city. The date is given in the Julian calendar, then used by both churches, and Byzantine writers dated years from the creation of the world, so that the year of these events was 6562 in Byzantine reckoning. The text accused Cerularius and his supporters of simony and of several errors, including the removal of the filioque and the practice of clerical marriage, and it labeled them with a catalogue of ancient heresies. The bull directed its condemnation at the patriarch, at named individuals, and at their followers. It did not condemn the Greek church as a whole, and it praised the emperor and the imperial city.

Cerularius, once he learned of the bull, appealed to the emperor and the people of Constantinople, and the legates' departure was followed by public agitation. The emperor, unwilling to abandon his policy of cooperation, was compelled to take account of the patriarch's popular support. At a synod held in the following days, Cerularius and the bishops condemned the document and the legates who had left it, and they, in their turn, anathematized those responsible. The Greek synodal record directed its sentence against the legates and their document, not against the pope or the Latin church generally. Cerularius circulated a statement of the episode in letters to the other eastern patriarchs, presenting the Roman action as improper. No formal act declared the churches to be in permanent schism. Cerularius remained patriarch until 1058, when Emperor Isaac I Komnenos forced him from office, and he died in 1059.

For the reform party in Rome, Humbert's role in the affair advanced his standing, and he remained influential in the following decade as a theorist of papal authority. Yet the mission failed in its immediate purposes. It produced no alliance with Constantinople against the Normans, it did not bring Cerularius to submission, and it left a bitter record. What the event established, in short, was not a schism in the institutional sense but a dramatic and well documented breakdown of communication between two leaders of the churches. It provided a vivid episode to which later writers could return, and it did so, in part, because the dispute it represented was never resolved.

Section 40.7

After 1054: Crusade, Commerce, and Hardening Divisions

After 1054 the two churches continued to function without a formal declaration of separation. Latin merchants, soldiers, and clergy lived in the Byzantine empire, Greek monks and clerics lived in Latin lands, and sporadic efforts at negotiation continued. The political circumstances nevertheless changed. In 1071 the Normans captured Bari, the last Byzantine stronghold in Italy, and the Byzantine empire suffered a catastrophic defeat to the Seljuk Turks at Manzikert in Anatolia. The empire's recovery under Alexios I Komnenos, who took the throne in 1081, depended on Latin military help, mercenaries, and naval power. Alexios granted the Venetians extensive commercial privileges in a chrysobull of 1082 in return for naval support against the Normans. These privileges, later extended to Pisa and Genoa, created sizable Latin merchant quarters in Constantinople and other Byzantine cities and encouraged resentment among the Greek population.

The Crusades, examined fully in Chapter 47, added further complexity. Alexios sought military assistance from the west, and at the Council of Piacenza in 1095, according to Latin chroniclers, imperial envoys appealed for help. At Clermont in November 1095, Pope Urban II called for an expedition to the East, framing it as a pilgrimage and a war to aid eastern Christians and to recover Jerusalem. Urban hoped the expedition might also improve relations between the churches. In 1098, at a council at Bari, he heard debate on the filioque with Greek-rite clergy of southern Italy, and Anselm of Canterbury spoke in defense of the Latin doctrine. The crusader armies that reached Byzantine territory, however, brought tensions with the emperor over oaths, territories, and supply. When crusaders captured Antioch in 1098 and Jerusalem in 1099, they established Latin rulers and, soon, Latin patriarchs in cities that had long had Greek and Syriac Christian hierarchies. A Latin patriarch of Antioch, Bernard of Valence, was installed in 1100 after the Greek patriarch left, creating a pair of rival claimants to the same see.

Commerce, military cooperation, and everyday contact generated both coexistence and friction. In the twelfth century, the Komnenian emperors employed Latin knights, built alliances with western rulers through marriage, and engaged in negotiations with the papacy over possible reunion. The emperor Manuel I, for instance, pursued a policy of close contact with the west. Greek authors of the period, including Anna Komnene, wrote about the crusaders with a mixture of admiration, fear, and contempt, depicting them as brave but ill-mannered and untrustworthy. Meanwhile, a Greek canon-law tradition evolved that debated how Latins should be treated. Some commentators, such as Theodore Balsamon in the late twelfth century, regarded Latins as separated from the Greek church in a manner requiring certain conditions for communion, while others treated the matter more flexibly. The evidence indicates uneven and local practice rather than a uniform rule.

Violence accelerated the hardening of attitudes. In 1182, during the political upheaval that brought Andronikos I Komnenos to power, a mob in Constantinople attacked the Latin population, killing many and selling others into slavery. Latin writers recorded the event with outrage. In 1185, Norman forces from Sicily sacked Thessalonica, the second city of the empire, with great brutality, and Greek writers described the atrocities. Each act of violence supplied material for polemic and strengthened the conviction among many that the other side was inherently hostile. The memory of these events, together with commercial resentments and the crusaders' conduct, set the stage for the conquest of Constantinople in 1204, described in Chapter 41. By that point the issue was no longer merely whether bishops would recognize each other's authority but whether the faithful of each church could regard the others as fellow Christians. After 1204 the establishment of Latin bishops in Greek sees made the division institutional in a way it had not been in 1054.

Section 40.8

Borderlands of Division

The separation of the churches did not fall along a clean line on the map. Some of the most revealing evidence comes from regions where Latin and Greek traditions met. In southern Italy and Sicily, Greek-rite monasteries, bishops, and communities lived alongside Latin ones for centuries. The Greek-rite monastery of Grottaferrata, founded near Rome around 1004 by followers of Nilus of Rossano, remained in communion with the papacy while following the Greek liturgy. The Norman rulers, including Roger II of Sicily in the following century, governed a kingdom where Greek, Latin, and Arabic were all used in administration, and where churches of both rites received patronage. Such examples caution against equating liturgical difference with institutional separation.

In the Balkans, the line between spheres was fluid. Chapter 39 described how Bulgaria and Serbia developed in relation to Byzantium, and after Basil II's conquest of Bulgaria in 1018 the archbishopric of Ohrid, whose archbishop Leo wrote the letter that opened the controversy of 1053, functioned as a large Greek-oriented church with Slavic clergy and liturgy. On the Adriatic coast, the Dalmatian cities and much of Croatia belonged to the Latin church, which restricted Slavonic liturgy at councils held in Split in the tenth century, while the interior and the territories toward Byzantium tended toward the Greek sphere. Principalities such as Duklja, situated between the Adriatic and the interior, maintained contact with both Rome and Constantinople as political circumstances suggested. These rulers sometimes appealed to the pope and sometimes to the emperor, treating ecclesiastical affiliation as an instrument of diplomacy as well as of faith.

Rus presents a particularly instructive case. Kyiv received Christianity from Byzantium around 988, as Chapter 39 explained, and its metropolitan was appointed from Constantinople. Yet Rus princes maintained dynastic ties with Latin Europe. Olga of Kyiv had earlier sent a request to Otto I of Germany for missionaries, and Bruno of Querfurt traveled in the region around the beginning of the eleventh century. Yaroslav the Wise married his children into the royal houses of France, Norway, Hungary, and elsewhere, and his daughter Anne became queen of France. In 1075 a son of Prince Izyaslav sought papal confirmation of his claim to power, and Pope Gregory VII responded. When merchants from Bari carried the relics of Saint Nicholas from Myra to Bari in 1087, the cult spread among both Latin and Orthodox Christians, and the feast of the translation was observed in Rus. These facts do not show that Rus was Latin. They show that its rulers acted within a wide Christian world and that confessional lines were less fixed than later accounts suggest, although the Kievan church also produced anti-Latin writings in the following generations.

Hungary and Poland illustrate the Latin side of the frontier. King Stephen of Hungary was crowned around the year 1000 with papal recognition, and Mieszko of Poland was baptized in 966 through connections with Bohemia and the Latin church. But both lands maintained contacts with Byzantium and the Orthodox world, whether through marriage, trade, or military cooperation, and both contained Orthodox populations in some regions. The Lithuanian and Baltic frontier, which Chapters 35 and 54 treat in different periods, would later bring Latin and Orthodox rulers into contest. The common pattern is that frontier societies often negotiated religious difference more flexibly than central authorities, while political actors sometimes used religious allegiance to bargain with rival powers.

Jews and Muslims lived in several of these borderlands as well, and their presence shaped Christian attitudes in ways that the polemical literature reveals. Greek writers who accused the Latins of Jewish customs, such as the use of unleavened bread, drew on a long tradition of anti-Jewish rhetoric. The comparison reminds students that Christian disputes were conducted in a world where Christians defined themselves partly against other religious communities, and that this rhetoric imposed costs on those communities. The Muslim presence in Sicily until the Norman conquest in the later eleventh century, and the Muslim neighbors of Byzantium in Anatolia and the Levant, formed part of the setting in which both churches formulated their identities.

Section 40.9

Evidence, Memory, and the Meaning of the Schism

The sources for the division fall into several categories, each with its limits. Official documents, including papal letters, patriarchal letters, conciliar canons, and the texts of 1054, tell us what the institutions chose to assert but not necessarily what ordinary clergy or laypeople believed. Polemical treatises, such as lists of Latin errors written by Greek authors and responses by Latin authors, reveal the intellectual weapons available to each side but were composed for persuasion. Historical narratives, including Byzantine chronicles and histories and Latin annals, supply chronology and judgment, though they were written with their own biases. Legal and commercial documents, such as trade privileges and monastic charters, show how people in practice lived alongside members of the other church. A well rounded reconstruction therefore requires reading these categories against each other.

Because the sources were often produced in the aftermath of conflict, later memory shaped the record. After 1204, Greek writers could point to the conquest of Constantinople as the culmination of Latin hostility, and Latin writers could point to the failure of Greek submission. Later still, during the Reformation and the Counter-Reformation, Protestant and Catholic authors cited the medieval division in their own polemics. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, nationalist movements in the Balkans and in Russia sometimes treated Orthodoxy as a marker of national identity and the schism as a defining moment, an interpretation that projects later identities onto earlier periods. Historians now generally emphasize that "Orthodox" and "Catholic" as fixed confessional identities crystallized gradually, and that the eleventh century was one stage in a longer process.

The modern ecumenical movement has also engaged with the memory of 1054. On 7 December 1965, at the close of the Second Vatican Council, Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras I of Constantinople issued a joint declaration, read simultaneously in Rome and Constantinople, expressing regret for the events of 1054 and removing from memory and from the midst of the Church the sentences of excommunication associated with them. The declaration expressly did not restore full communion, and it was symbolic in what it removed, since, as this chapter has shown, the original sentences were directed at particular individuals rather than at the churches as wholes. It shows how historical research, especially the recognition that the 1054 anathemas were personal, could inform later efforts at reconciliation. It also shows that the meaning of the event continued to evolve long after it occurred.

Historical Vocabulary
Schism

A schism, from a Greek word meaning a split, is a breach of communion among Christians that need not involve disagreement over fundamental doctrine. It differs from heresy, which concerns erroneous belief, although medieval writers often blurred the two. The word matters to this chapter because the division between Latin and Greek Christians involved both: authority and ritual practice were disputed as well as doctrine. A schism could be partial, local, or temporary, and some breaks, such as the Acacian Schism, were repaired. Modern readers should resist assuming that "schism" always implied a clean and permanent separation. The Greek and Latin churches drifted into mutual non-recognition over generations, and the events of 1054 were one marker in that process.

Historical Vocabulary
Pentarchy

The pentarchy was the theory that the church was governed by five great patriarchal sees: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. Developed in late antique councils and imperial law, it fitted Byzantine ideals of cooperative governance. Rome accepted the special standing of these sees but claimed a primacy of a different kind, rooted in Peter. The theory mattered because it offered a framework within which Constantinople could claim equality with Rome. After the Arab conquests, three of the five sees lay in Muslim territory, so the pentarchy described a diminishing reality. Students should not assume that either church accepted the other's reading of it.

Historical Vocabulary
Filioque

Filioque, Latin for "and from the Son," names the phrase added in the west to the Nicene Creed's statement about the procession of the Holy Spirit. The addition is traditionally associated with Visigothic Iberia, though the textual history of its insertion is disputed, and it became securely established in Frankish lands before Rome adopted it in the early eleventh century. The dispute that followed had two parts that are easy to confuse: whether the doctrine was correct, and whether any church could add to the creed without the agreement of the whole. Some Greek and Latin theologians thought the difference could be reconciled, while others regarded it as fundamental. The term appears often in modern discussions of Orthodox and Catholic relations, which makes it important to understand its medieval origins.

Primary Source Window

The Documents of July 1054: Humbert's Bull and the Response of Cerularius's Synod

Creators: Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida, with fellow legates, and the patriarchal synod of Constantinople under Michael Cerularius · Date: 16 July and the following days of July 1054 · Type: An ecclesiastical sentence of excommunication and a synodal response, preserved in Latin and Greek manuscripts and in later copies

Context: The legates had arrived on a mission to the emperor and patriarch. After failing to obtain Cerularius's submission, they composed a written sentence and deposited it on the altar of Hagia Sophia. The patriarch and his synod issued a written reply. The bull is addressed principally against Cerularius and named associates. Its Latin text survives in medieval collections, and the Greek response is preserved in the synodal record. The reading offered here is a paraphrase of their contents and not a translation.

Paraphrase of the contents The legates' sentence declares that Cerularius and his followers have committed offenses against the Roman church and the faith. It charges them with simony, with permitting clerical marriage, with deleting the Latin wording of the Creed concerning the Spirit, and with other practices the legates regard as heretical, labeling the accused with the names of ancient heresies. It praises the emperor and the city's faithful citizens and condemns the patriarch alone with those who follow him. The Greek synod's reply treats the document as an unlawful and insulting act, rejects the legates' claimed authority and the authenticity of their commission, and pronounces a condemnation against the legates and those responsible for the text. This is a paraphrase and not a quotation from either document.

Analysis: The documents reveal how each party understood its authority and its opponent's faults. The legates addressed their sentence to a person, not to the Greek church, which indicates that they intended to discipline a recalcitrant bishop rather than to define a division. The synod's response likewise targeted the legates. Whether Leo IX's death in April undermined the legates' authority is a separate and later canonical question, on which historians disagree, and it should not be confused with the arguments the Greek synod itself made. Both texts are polemical in tone, and both draw on a vocabulary of heresy that exaggerates the actual differences. Their limitations are considerable. They represent the views of senior clerics and not of the wider Christian population, they were written in the heat of confrontation, and the manuscript tradition includes later copying, so students should be cautious about treating wording as precise. They also tell us little about the emperor's intentions, which are known only from other sources.

Historical Debate

Was 1054 the Schism?

The question: Should historians treat the events of 1054 as the moment at which the Latin and Greek churches separated, or as one episode in a longer process of estrangement that continued to the thirteenth century and beyond?

Interpretation A, 1054 as a decisive turning point: Some scholars emphasize that the confrontation brought long accumulating disputes to a head and produced a documentary record of mutual condemnation. In this view the episode inaugurated a period in which the churches acted increasingly as separate entities, and later events built on the foundation it provided. They point to the fact that the dispute combined authority, doctrine, and practice, and that neither side retracted its position.
Interpretation B, 1054 as a symbol of a gradual process: Many historians argue instead that 1054 was a limited quarrel between individuals that contemporaries did not regard as a permanent division. They stress that communion continued in many places, that further negotiation occurred, that the sentences concerned individuals, and that the decisive hardening came later, particularly with the Crusades and the events of 1204. In this reading, the significance of 1054 was largely created by later memory.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same sources of 1054, but they differ in weighting. The first emphasizes the content of the confrontation and its roots in earlier quarrels. The second emphasizes evidence of continued contact after 1054, the limited language of the sentences, the lack of any formal declaration of schism, and the later development of institutional and popular hostility. Most current scholarship leans toward the second view while acknowledging that the first captures why the event retained symbolic power.

Why it matters: How historians date the separation affects how they explain it. If the division was an event, one looks for a decisive cause. If it was a process, one must examine many interacting factors, including politics, commerce, violence, and language.

Student task: Identify at least three pieces of evidence from this chapter that support each interpretation and explain which combination you find most persuasive, noting what additional evidence would help resolve the question.

Multiple Perspectives

A Church Divided: Perspectives on the Quarrel with the Other Church

From the perspective of a reforming Roman cleric: The church must be purified of simony and lay control, and unity of discipline under Rome's authority is essential. Greek resistance to papal primacy and variation in the Creed look like disobedience that reform cannot tolerate.
From the perspective of a Byzantine patriarch or court cleric: The church is governed by councils and patriarchs in cooperation with a Christian emperor. Alterations to the Creed and claims of unilateral papal rule are innovations that threaten the common tradition of the whole church.
From the perspective of a Byzantine emperor: The empire faces Norman and other enemies and needs a working relationship with the papacy. Ecclesiastical disputes are best contained, since a breach with Rome could weaken diplomatic and military options.
From the perspective of an Italian merchant in Constantinople: Trade privileges and personal safety matter more than doctrine, though a Latin worshipper in a Greek city may face suspicion, and local hostility can turn violent in times of political crisis.
From the perspective of a Greek-rite monk in southern Italy: Daily liturgy and community are far more important than distant disputes. Norman lords and Latin bishops introduce pressure to conform, but Greek monasteries often continue to receive patronage.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Communion and Division from the Fifth Century to the Early Thirteenth

What Changed

  • The political framework: from a shared empire to rival western and eastern imperial claims and a papacy aligned with Frankish and German rulers
  • The linguistic situation: from widespread bilingualism among elites to limited Greek in the west and limited Latin in the east
  • The papacy's self-understanding: from a see with special honor to a claimant to universal jurisdiction under reform
  • The practical situation of the Latins in the east: from small numbers to substantial merchant quarters and crusader states

What Stayed the Same

  • Shared scripture, the councils' definitions, the sacramental system, monasticism, and the veneration of saints
  • The tension between Rome's claim of Petrine primacy and Constantinople's claim of imperial standing
  • Frequent contact among merchants, pilgrims, diplomats, and monks even in periods of tension
  • The ability of local communities, especially on frontiers, to maintain mixed practice

Who experienced change most directly? Clergy and rulers experienced the sharpest shifts, as did Latin and Greek populations in cities where commercial and military presence changed. Peasants and ordinary parishioners in many regions experienced greater continuity in daily worship. Why the pattern matters: It shows that institutional division could develop faster at the level of leadership than at the level of local practice.

Cause and Effect

The Division of Latin and Greek Christendom

Long-term causes

Divergent political frameworks after the Roman west fragmented, a declining shared knowledge of Greek and Latin, competing ecclesiologies of primacy and conciliarity, and the dispute over the Creed.

Short-term causes

The reform papacy's claims, the Norman threat in southern Italy, Cerularius's campaign against Latin custom, and the legates' confrontational mission in 1054.

Immediate effects

Mutual condemnations of individuals, a failed diplomatic mission, and a durable record of polemic, though communion continued in many practical contexts.

Long-term consequences

Hardening of attitudes through the Crusades, commercial rivalry, violence, and the Latin conquest of 1204, culminating in two distinct churches with their own institutions and identities.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the division. Theological difference mattered, yet political rivalry, institutional claims, language, and violence shaped how seriously the differences were taken. Historians also debate how far later events, rather than earlier disagreements, produced the lasting separation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with Religious Difference

For most people, the dispute between patriarch and pope had little immediate bearing on daily life. Parishioners attended local liturgies in the language and customs they knew, whether Greek, Latin, or Slavonic, and they relied on local clergy for baptisms, marriages, and burials. Differences became visible where populations mixed. In Constantinople, Italian merchants worshipped in Latin churches within their quarters, and Latin soldiers served in imperial armies. In southern Italy, Greek and Latin monasteries often stood within sight of each other. Married Greek priests lived in village households, while Latin reformers pressed for celibate clergy, a contrast that affected family structure and inheritance among the clergy.

Pilgrims traveled between Latin and Greek lands to Jerusalem, Rome, Bari, and other shrines, and they encountered unfamiliar customs. The chronicles and travel accounts that survive often record surprise or criticism, but also hospitality. Commercial and military cooperation produced intermarriage among elites, including dynastic marriages that joined Byzantine and western families. Yet episodes such as the massacre of 1182 show that hostility could erupt violently when political conditions turned, particularly when resentment of foreign privileges had accumulated. Understanding the division therefore requires attention to how people lived with difference as well as to what theologians wrote.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the gradual separation of the Latin and Greek churches and the symbolic episode of 1054 that later generations treated as its moment. The central argument is that the division of Christendom was a process rather than an event. It unfolded over several centuries as political frameworks, languages, institutions, and ideas diverged, and it became durable only after events in the later eleventh and twelfth centuries deepened mutual hostility.

The chapter began by establishing that Christendom was already plural. The churches that rejected the Council of Chalcedon had separated from both Rome and Constantinople, so the Latin and Greek division was one among several. The pentarchy provided a framework in which five sees shared governance, but Rome and Constantinople understood their relationship differently, the first appealing to Peter and the second to imperial status. Disagreements arose early, at Chalcedon in 451, and produced a real breach in the Acacian Schism, which was healed in 519.

Political change widened the gap. The disappearance of unified imperial rule in the Mediterranean, the Byzantine loss of central Italy, the papal alliance with the Franks, and the coronation of Charlemagne in 800 introduced a rival imperial authority in the west. The decline of mutual linguistic knowledge made communication more difficult. The Photian affair of the ninth century showed that jurisdiction over a newly converted Slavic realm could provoke open conflict, and it exposed the issues of creed, authority, and custom that polemicists would cite thereafter.

The disagreements themselves fell into three groups. The first concerned authority: whether the pope held universal jurisdiction by right of Peter or the church was governed by councils and patriarchs in cooperation. The second concerned doctrine and procedure, especially the filioque, which raised both a theological question and the question of whether a church could alter the Creed unilaterally. The third concerned liturgy and discipline, including unleavened bread, clerical celibacy, and fasting. Contemporaries did not always distinguish these groups, and some, like Peter of Antioch, regarded many differences as tolerable.

The events of 1054 occurred in a particular conjuncture. A reforming papacy under Leo IX and Humbert insisted on discipline and universal authority, the Normans threatened both Byzantine and papal interests in southern Italy, and the patriarch Michael Cerularius challenged Latin customs. The papal legation of that year, which reached Constantinople around the time of Leo's death, failed to secure either an alliance or the patriarch's submission. The bull of 16 July and the synod's response condemned individuals, not the churches, and neither side declared a permanent division.

After 1054, relations continued in a mixed pattern of cooperation and friction. Alexios I relied on Latin help and granted trading privileges to Venice. The First Crusade established Latin patriarchs in the Levant, and the presence of rival hierarchs in the same sees created institutional conflict. The massacre of Latins in Constantinople in 1182 and the Norman sack of Thessalonica in 1185 intensified popular hostility. The Latin conquest of Constantinople in 1204, examined in Chapter 41, made the division institutional in a way that earlier disputes had not.

Borderlands reveal that the separation was uneven. Southern Italy and Sicily contained Greek and Latin communities in close proximity. The Balkans and the Adriatic were zones of overlapping jurisdiction. Rus, though ecclesiastically tied to Constantinople, maintained extensive dynastic and cultural contacts with Latin Europe. Hungary and Poland were Latin kingdoms with Byzantine and Orthodox contacts. In these regions rulers frequently used ecclesiastical affiliation as a diplomatic resource, and local clergy and laypeople often managed difference more flexibly than central authorities did.

The evidence for these developments is largely clerical and polemical, and the chapter has stressed the need to read sources critically. The question of whether 1054 should be treated as the schism remains a matter of interpretation. Most current scholarship stresses process, the limited and personal nature of the 1054 sentences, and the decisive role of later events, while recognizing that the confrontation retained symbolic power. The ecumenical declarations of 1965 illustrate how later generations reinterpreted the episode.

Finally, the chapter has argued that the division was contingent. Nothing in the early disagreements guaranteed that Latin and Greek Christianity would become separate churches. Different decisions, a different political situation in southern Italy, or a more successful diplomatic mission might have produced other outcomes. The result was shaped by interacting causes, among them political rivalry, institutional claims, language, commerce, and violence, and it is more accurately explained by their combination than by a single dispute or personality.

These themes will recur. Chapter 41 follows the Fourth Crusade, the Byzantine restoration, and the Ottoman conquest, in which relations between Latin and Greek Christians affected the empire's survival. The relationship between Rome and the Orthodox churches also shaped the confessional landscape of eastern and central Europe in later centuries, including the settlements discussed in the chapters on the Reformation and the early modern state.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Schism
A breach of communion between Christian bodies, which may arise from disputes over authority or practice as well as doctrine.
Heresy
Teaching judged by church authorities to contradict orthodox belief; medieval polemicists often applied the term loosely to customs and practices.
Excommunication
A church sanction excluding a person from the sacraments and the community of the faithful, directed at individuals in 1054.
Anathema
A solemn ecclesiastical curse or condemnation, used in the sentences exchanged in July 1054.
Pentarchy
The theory that Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem jointly governed the church.
Patriarch
The title of the senior bishop of one of the great sees, especially in the eastern church.
Primacy
The special position of the bishop of Rome, understood in the west as universal jurisdiction rooted in Peter.
Conciliarity
The principle that ultimate authority in the church rests in councils representing the whole church.
Communion
Mutual recognition among churches or Christians, expressed in shared sacraments and the recognition of one another's bishops.
Filioque
The Latin phrase "and from the Son" added to the Creed's statement on the Spirit's procession.
Nicene Creed
The statement of faith associated with the councils of 325 and 381, recited in the liturgy of both churches.
Azymes
Unleavened bread used for the Eucharist in the Latin church and criticized by Greek polemicists.
Chalcedon
The council of 451 that defined Christ's two natures and, in canon 28, raised Constantinople's rank.
New Rome
Title of Constantinople as the second imperial capital, the basis of its ecclesiastical claims.
Acacian Schism
The breach between Rome and Constantinople from 484 to 519, over efforts to reconcile opponents of Chalcedon.
Photian affair
The ninth-century dispute over the patriarchate of Photius and jurisdiction over Bulgaria.
Legate
A representative sent with authority from the pope, such as Humbert in 1054.
Simony
The purchase or sale of church offices, a principal target of the reform movement.
Nicolaitism
A reformers' term for clerical marriage and concubinage.
Chrysobull
A Byzantine imperial charter sealed with gold, used to grant privileges such as those given to Venice in 1082.
Quinisext Council
The council of 692 in Constantinople whose disciplinary canons Rome did not fully accept.
Donation of Constantine
A forged document, later shown to be spurious, used to support papal claims of authority.
Polemic
Writing intended to attack another position, a major source type for this chapter.
Non-Chalcedonian churches
The Armenian, Coptic, Syriac, and Ethiopian churches that did not accept Chalcedon.
Autocephaly
Self-governing status of a church with its own head, relevant to Ohrid and the Slavic churches.
Civitate
The battle of June 1053 in which the Normans defeated and captured Pope Leo IX.
Latin quarters
Districts of Constantinople where Italian merchants lived under trade privileges.
Ecumenical council
A council of bishops from the whole church whose decisions were regarded as binding; the list of such councils differs between traditions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a division can be created slowly and remembered quickly. The altar scene of July 1054 is memorable, but the history it stands for took centuries to unfold and involved political events, institutional claims, commercial contacts, and episodes of violence as much as theology. Understanding that difference helps students avoid a common error of historical thinking, the habit of locating complex transformations in a single date and a single cause.

The chapter also reveals how the unity of Roman late antiquity, examined in Volume I, gave way to a plural medieval Europe. The Latin west, shaped by post-Roman kingdoms, Frankish and German rulers, and a reforming papacy, and the Greek east, shaped by an enduring empire and its patriarchate, followed related but increasingly distinct paths. The Slavic and Balkan societies discussed in the preceding chapters of Part VI lived between these paths, and their histories cannot be understood without recognizing that choice of church was often a choice of political alignment as well as of faith.

The consequences extended well beyond the medieval period. The conquest of 1204 and the later Ottoman advance, treated in Chapter 41, took place in a Christian world already divided, and the memory of that division shaped Orthodox and Catholic identities in eastern and central Europe for centuries. Even in the modern period, debates about the boundaries of Europe and about the relationship between religion and national identity have drawn on narratives of an eastern and western Christendom. Those narratives are historically interesting precisely because they are partial and constructed, as this chapter has shown.

For the argument of Volume II, the chapter contributes an essential qualification. Medieval Christendom was one of the central organizing ideas of the period, yet it was never a single institution. The same period that saw the Latin west expand and reform saw the Greek east defend and transform its empire, and both were part of the same European story. Taking the division seriously, without treating it as inevitable and without forgetting the many ways in which Latin and Greek Christians continued to interact, is part of the more balanced account of Europe that this textbook seeks to provide.
