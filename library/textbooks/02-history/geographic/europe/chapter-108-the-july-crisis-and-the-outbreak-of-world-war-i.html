The July Crisis and the Outbreak of World War I

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

A war did not begin on a Sarajevo street corner on June 28, 1914. It began over the following five weeks, through a sequence of decisions made in Vienna, Berlin, St. Petersburg, Paris, Belgrade, and London, each of which narrowed the options available to the governments that followed.

On the morning of Sunday, June 28, 1914, the motorcade of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir presumptive to the Austro-Hungarian throne, moved through Sarajevo, the capital of the province of Bosnia-Herzegovina. The visit followed army maneuvers in the province, which Austria-Hungary had occupied in 1878 and annexed in 1908. An initial attack with a bomb failed to harm the Archduke. Later that morning, after a change of plan and a wrong turn, his car stopped within a few meters of a nineteen-year-old assassin, Gavrilo Princip, who fired two shots that killed the Archduke and his wife, Sophie, Duchess of Hohenberg. Those facts are well established by court records, eyewitness testimony, and the statements of the conspirators themselves.

What followed is far less obvious than later memory suggests. Assassinations of royalty and political leaders were not unknown in the Europe of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and none had previously led to a continental war. In the summer of 1914, however, the killing was received by a leadership in Vienna that had long sought a way to confront Serbia, by a German government prepared to support that confrontation, by a Russian government that regarded Serbian independence as a matter of security and prestige, and by a French and British diplomatic system in which assurances to partners mattered as much as formal obligations. The assassination supplied an occasion. The war arose from the decisions governments made about that occasion.

This chapter reconstructs those decisions. It follows the July Crisis from the assassination through the German assurance of support in early July, the Austro-Hungarian ultimatum of July 23, Serbia's reply of July 25, the Austrian declaration of war on July 28, the failure of last mediation efforts, the Russian general mobilization of July 30 to 31, the German ultimatums and declarations of war on Russia and France, the invasion of Luxembourg and Belgium, and Britain's entry into the war on August 4. It ends in the first week of August, when a Balkan confrontation had become a continental war and the groundwork of a wider conflict had been laid. The campaigns that followed belong to Chapter 109.

The chapter also asks how historians know what they claim to know. The documentary record of July 1914 is unusually abundant, but it is far from neutral. Governments published selective collections of diplomatic papers, the so-called colour books, in August 1914 to justify their conduct. After 1918, revolutionary and republican governments in Russia, Germany, and Austria released further records, often for political purposes, in debates over responsibility for the war. Diaries, memoirs, trial transcripts, newspapers, telegrams, military timetables, and parliamentary records add detail, but many memoirs were written after the catastrophe by participants with every reason to defend themselves. Students should treat each source as evidence created by someone for a purpose.

Three distinctions govern the narrative. First, the assassination did not automatically cause a European war: ultimatums, mobilizations, declarations of war, and invasions were decisions. Second, alliances did not operate as an automatic chain reaction: their legal terms, military arrangements, political expectations, and the actual choices made under pressure must be examined separately. Third, responsibility must be judged from evidence. Recognizing that several governments contributed to escalation does not require assigning them equal responsibility.

Chapter 107 examined the conditions that made Europe vulnerable to war: alliance blocs, arms competition, imperial rivalry, Balkan nationalism, and the weakening of diplomatic crisis management. Chapter 108 turns from conditions to choices. As the first chapter of Volume V, it also marks a larger transition. The nineteenth-century European state system, whose mechanisms the chapters of Volume IV traced, failed in a way that opened the age of world wars, mass death, and revolution examined across Part XVII and Part XVIII. That failure was not inevitable, and understanding why it occurred requires close attention to who decided what, when, and on what information.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Reconstruct the events of June 28, 1914 in Sarajevo and distinguish the actions of the conspirators from the question of Serbian state responsibility.
  2. Explain the German assurances of July 5 to 6 and the Austro-Hungarian decision to seek a confrontation with Serbia.
  3. Analyze the demands of the July 23 ultimatum and the content of Serbia's July 25 reply, identifying which demands were accepted, qualified, or refused.
  4. Trace the sequence from Austria-Hungary's declaration of war on Serbia to the Russian, German, French, and British decisions of July 28 to August 4.
  5. Distinguish partial mobilization, general mobilization, ultimatum, and declaration of war, and explain why governments treated them differently.
  6. Assess how the actual terms of the alliance commitments, and the choices of governments under pressure, shaped the widening of the crisis.
  7. Evaluate the relationship between German operational planning and political decision making in the first days of August.
  8. Explain why Belgian neutrality, cabinet divisions, and strategic interest together produced Britain's decision for war, and why Italy and other states remained neutral.
  9. Compare the responses of soldiers, families, and civilians to mobilization across different European societies.
  10. Interpret competing historical arguments about responsibility for the war and its preventability, using evidence rather than assertion.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did an assassination in Bosnia lead to a crisis between sovereign governments, when earlier political murders had not?
  2. What evidence allows historians to judge the extent of Serbian official involvement, and what remains uncertain?
  3. Why did Germany's leaders offer Austria-Hungary support, and what risks did they accept in doing so?
  4. Why did diplomatic mediation succeed in earlier twentieth-century crises but fail in July 1914?
  5. To what degree did military plans and mobilization timetables constrain political decision makers, and where did governments retain freedom of action?
  6. How did alliance commitments actually operate, and how did they differ from the way later generations remembered them?
  7. Why did Britain enter the war when it had no treaty obligation to France or Russia, and what role did Belgian neutrality play?
  8. How should historians weigh Austrian, German, Russian, Serbian, French, and British decisions without either blaming a single government or treating all as equally responsible?

Historical Context

Chapter 107 traced the long development of the diplomatic and military situation that Europe's governments inherited in the summer of 1914. A few elements require reminder. Two alliance groupings dominated the continent. Germany and Austria-Hungary were bound by the Dual Alliance of 1879, a defensive treaty directed principally at Russia, and Italy was linked to both through the Triple Alliance, renewed in 1912, which was also defensive in character. France and Russia were joined by a military convention negotiated in the early 1890s, under which each pledged to assist the other if attacked by Germany, and which linked the mobilization of the Triple Alliance powers to common action by the two partners. Britain, by contrast, was tied to France by the Entente Cordiale of 1904 and to Russia by the convention of 1907, but neither was a treaty of alliance requiring military assistance. Britain's freedom of action remained, formally, intact.

The Balkans were the region of greatest instability. Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina in 1908 had humiliated Serbia and strained relations with Russia. The Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 expelled most Ottoman authority from southeastern Europe, roughly doubled Serbia's territory, and led to the creation of an independent Albania under pressure from Vienna. For many in the Habsburg leadership, a stronger Serbia appeared to threaten the stability of a multinational monarchy with a large South Slav population. For many in Serbia and among South Slav nationalists elsewhere, the Balkan victories raised hopes of further national unification. Russia, still recovering from its defeat by Japan in 1905 and the revolutionary upheaval that followed, viewed Serbia as a client and the Straits that controlled access to the Black Sea as a vital economic and strategic concern.

Decision making differed sharply among the powers. In Germany the Kaiser, Wilhelm II, held constitutional supremacy over the army and the appointment of the Chancellor, Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg, while the Chief of the General Staff, Helmuth von Moltke the Younger, commanded military planning with little civilian oversight. In Russia, Nicholas II made final decisions on mobilization after advice from the Foreign Minister, Sergei Sazonov, the War Minister, and the General Staff. In France the President, Raymond Poincaré, had substantial influence over foreign policy, though the government depended on a parliamentary majority. In Britain the Cabinet decided policy, and the Foreign Secretary, Sir Edward Grey, had to maintain a divided Liberal government, a skeptical party, and a Parliament that had never approved a binding commitment to fight on the continent. In Austria-Hungary, decisions depended on the Emperor, Franz Joseph, the Common Foreign Minister, Leopold Berchtold, the military leadership under Franz Conrad von Hötzendorf, and the separate governments of Austria and Hungary, whose Prime Minister, István Tisza, held an effective veto over some decisions.

Each government also faced domestic distractions in July 1914. Britain was preoccupied with the Irish Home Rule crisis, France with the sensational trial of Henriette Caillaux for murdering a newspaper editor, and Russia with a wave of labor unrest in St. Petersburg. Austria-Hungary's parliament in Vienna had been suspended since March 1914. These circumstances did not cause the war, but they help explain why several governments entered the crisis with limited attention, incomplete information, or political vulnerabilities. Finally, all the Great Powers had constructed military systems whose mobilization plans were designed to deploy massive conscript armies by rail. How far those plans constrained decision makers is a question the chapter will examine directly.

Division 1 — Sarajevo and the Decisions That Initiated the Crisis

Division I: Sarajevo and the Decisions That Initiated the Crisis · Section 108.1

Europe in June 1914: Security, Sovereignty, and the Possibility of War

In June 1914 few Europeans expected an imminent continental war, though many expected that one might come eventually. Diplomats had survived a series of crises since 1905, including two over Morocco, the Bosnian annexation, and the Balkan Wars, and had come to believe that war could be avoided at the last moment. Yet the same experiences taught different lessons to different governments. In Berlin and Vienna, some leaders concluded that firmness had succeeded and that their rivals would back down again. In Russia and France, many concluded that concessions to Germany or Austria-Hungary had gone far enough. Each side expected the other to blink. The result was a crisis in which several governments believed they were acting defensively, and in which the cost of retreat looked greater than the risk of escalation.

The alliance structure shaped expectations, but its operation must be described precisely. The Dual Alliance obliged Germany to assist Austria-Hungary if Russia attacked it, but did not oblige Germany to support Austrian aggression against Serbia. The Franco-Russian arrangement obliged France to act if Germany attacked Russia, but did not bind France to every Russian initiative in the Balkans. The Triple Alliance included Italy, but its terms were defensive and required consultation, and Italy's relations with Austria-Hungary were distrustful. Britain was bound by no military treaty. Alliances therefore functioned less as legal triggers than as expectations of support, whose reliability each partner feared to test. Governments worried that an ally who was abandoned might seek terms elsewhere. That fear, rather than a treaty clause, often pushed governments toward firmer language than they might otherwise have used.

Sovereign decision making is the second foundation of the chapter. No treaty, plan, or timetable declared war. Individuals and cabinets did, drawing on advice from officials who often had incomplete information, divergent priorities, and limited ability to communicate quickly. Recognizing this distinction does not deny that structural pressures mattered: arms competition, military plans, and mutual suspicion narrowed what leaders believed they could do. It simply keeps the focus on the decisions that governments made when confronted by those pressures, and on the fact that different decisions were available.

A useful comparison begins with the range of options actually available in late June. Vienna could investigate the attack and pursue a judicial or diplomatic demand; it could threaten limited military retaliation; or it could attempt to eliminate Serbia as an independent strategic challenger. Berlin could warn Vienna against an operation likely to bring Russia in, instead of endorsing a forceful course. St. Petersburg could support Serbian independence without promising to accept every Serbian decision. Paris could reaffirm its Russian alliance while pressing for specific restraint. London could offer mediation without promising its armed forces. These alternatives had different political costs, and none was guaranteed to succeed, but they existed. The crucial analytical task is to identify which actors narrowed the options and when, instead of reading every later decision back into June 28.

The positions of lesser states likewise mattered. Belgium had a recognized neutral status and a government able to refuse a foreign army's demand for transit; Luxembourg occupied a different position as a small neutral state with limited military capacity. Serbia had just emerged from two exhausting Balkan Wars with territorial gains and fierce civil-military competition. Italy, despite its signature on the Triple Alliance, disputed territory with Austria-Hungary and might refuse to regard a Habsburg offensive as a defensive casus foederis. Romania's ties to the Central Powers were not equivalent to a popular mandate for war. Those differences explain why a map of treaty affiliations in June 1914 could not reliably predict the map of belligerents in August.

Historical Vocabulary
July Crisis, Casus Belli, Alliance Obligation, Diplomatic Guarantee

July Crisis means the diplomatic confrontation that widened from the Sarajevo assassinations through the European war declarations in summer 1914. A casus belli is the event or claim a government offers as a ground for war; the claim does not by itself establish legal or moral sufficiency. An alliance obligation requires reading the conditions of a specific treaty, including the difference between defense against attack and backing a partner's own offensive. A diplomatic guarantee is a promise concerning a state or legal status, whose practical execution may require a separate government decision.

Section 108.2

Sarajevo, June 28, 1914: The Assassination of Franz Ferdinand

Franz Ferdinand's visit to Bosnia was official. As Inspector General of the armed forces, he observed army maneuvers in the hills near Sarajevo, and the visit to the provincial capital on June 28 was added to the program. Bosnia-Herzegovina was governed jointly by the two halves of the monarchy through a common finance ministry, with a military governor, Oskar Potiorek, in Sarajevo. The date was sensitive: June 28 is the feast of St. Vitus, associated in Serbian national memory with the Battle of Kosovo of 1389. Many contemporaries and later historians have questioned the decision to schedule the visit on that day, and security arrangements were uneven, with few troops on the streets and no effective cordon.

The plot was carried out by a small group of young men, most of them Bosnian Serbs and teenagers or in their early twenties, and one a Bosnian Muslim. They belonged to the diffuse revolutionary milieu known as Young Bosnia, which drew on South Slav nationalism, anti-Habsburg feeling, and an admiration for earlier assassins and revolutionaries. The organizer within Sarajevo was Danilo Ilić. Princip, Nedeljko Čabrinović, and Trifko Grabež had traveled from Belgrade, where they obtained pistols and bombs, and had crossed into Bosnia with help from border networks. On June 28 seven conspirators positioned themselves along the route. The first to act, Čabrinović, threw a bomb at the Archduke's car shortly after ten o'clock. It bounced off the vehicle and exploded beneath the car behind, wounding several people. Čabrinović swallowed a cyanide capsule that failed and jumped into the shallow Miljacka river, where he was captured.

After a reception at the city hall, Franz Ferdinand decided to visit the wounded in hospital. The drivers had not been informed of a change of route, and the lead car turned onto a side street. When Potiorek ordered the driver to reverse, the car stopped within a few meters of Princip, who had positioned himself near a delicatessen on the corner. He fired two shots. Sophie was struck in the abdomen and the Archduke in the neck. Both died shortly afterward. Princip was seized and, because he was under twenty at the time of the crime, escaped the death penalty and was sentenced to twenty years in prison. He died of tuberculosis in the fortress of Theresienstadt in April 1918. Several co-conspirators, including Ilić, were later executed.

Within Bosnia, the immediate response included anti-Serb demonstrations in Sarajevo and other towns, with the destruction of property, as crowds, some stirred by local officials, turned on Serb businesses and homes. Martial law and arrests followed. The funeral arrangements for the Archduke and his wife, which were notably modest because of court protocol regarding the morganatic marriage, reflected the Habsburg court's mixed attitude toward the couple. Historians generally reject claims that the Austrian court as a whole mourned deeply or that public opinion in the monarchy demanded immediate war. The killing shocked many, but it was a political pretext more than a popular catalyst. The evidence for the event itself includes the trial transcript, police investigations, eyewitness statements, press reports, and the later memoirs of survivors, which must be used cautiously because they were shaped by the very different politics of Yugoslavia in the 1920s and afterward.

Because Sarajevo lay in an annexed province, the assassination was simultaneously an attack on a Habsburg dynastic figure and an act within a contested imperial political space. Young Bosnia was not a centralized party with a single constitution or chain of command: its members were inspired by varied combinations of South Slav unity, Serbian nationalism, anti-imperial revolution, and romanticized political violence. Their identities and aims cannot simply be equated with those of the Serbian cabinet. In Sarajevo the conspirators also depended on local travel arrangements, weapons and improvised opportunities. The sequence of a failed bomb followed by a chance encounter makes it especially inappropriate to describe the killing as an inevitable success of an elaborate state operation.

Subsequent judicial documents require a careful chronology of their own. The Sarajevo trial took place months after the murders, when the diplomatic catastrophe had already changed the stakes of testimony. Prisoners were questioned under intense pressure, and later writers sometimes interpreted the evidence through the politics of a newly created Yugoslavia. Even the number of conspirators depends on whether the historian counts only people stationed along the route or a wider support network. A textbook should distinguish details attested close to June 28 from reminiscences recorded many years later. The violence directed against local Serbs immediately afterward was also part of the event's history: it illustrates that state officials and crowds could make politically consequential choices before any European mobilization had been ordered.

Section 108.3

Serbia, Austria-Hungary, and the Question of Responsibility

The central question for Vienna in the days after the assassination was whether the Serbian state, as distinct from individual conspirators, bore responsibility. The question is more complicated than either contemporaries or later nationalists acknowledged. The conspirators were Habsburg subjects, not Serbian citizens, and they acted in the name of South Slav liberation, not of the Serbian kingdom as such. But they had obtained weapons from Serbian sources. Milan Ciganović, a Bosnian Serb employed by the Serbian state railways, and Major Vojislav Tankosić, an army officer associated with the Black Hand, had supplied the pistols and bombs and helped arrange the passage of the conspirators across the frontier. Border officials in the Serbian army or customs service assisted. Serbian military intelligence was led by Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, who headed the secret officers' society Union or Death. Many historians believe that Apis and associates facilitated the plot, though the precise extent of his role and intentions remains uncertain, and his later trial and execution by the Serbian government in 1917 was itself politically charged.

The Serbian civilian government under Nikola Pašić was a different matter. Pašić's government was in conflict with the officer faction over the precedence of civil and military officials in the new territories won in the Balkan Wars, a dispute so severe that in June 1914 King Peter transferred his powers to his son, Crown Prince Alexander, as regent. Serbia also faced a parliamentary election in August. There is evidence that some Serbian officials had heard of a plot against the Archduke and that a vague warning was passed to Vienna, but the warning was too indirect to be understood. The extent to which the Serbian cabinet knew the details is still unproven. Austria-Hungary's own investigating official, Friedrich von Wiesner, reported to Berchtold in mid-July that the investigation had found no evidence of Serbian government complicity, though he believed that Serbian authorities had at least tolerated nationalist agitation. His report was inconvenient, and it was not made central to the Austrian case.

Vienna's grievances against Serbia predated June 28. Many Austrian leaders, including Conrad, had urged war against Serbia repeatedly since 1908, and viewed Serbian nationalism as a mortal danger to a state in which Slavic populations were large and which could not easily be reconciled with South Slav national claims. Serbia, for its part, regarded the Habsburg monarchy as an obstacle to the unification of South Slavs and sponsored propaganda societies such as Narodna Odbrana, which Austria alleged operated across the frontier. Historians therefore distinguish between three levels of responsibility: that of the individual assassins, that of a network that included Serbian officers and officials who supplied and enabled them, and that of the Serbian state as an institution, for which evidence remains incomplete. Vienna conflated these levels, and its ultimatum treated the Serbian state as responsible without having established that case. That does not mean that Serbia was blameless. It means that the assassination did not by itself justify the extent of the Austrian demands.

The distinction between knowledge, assistance, authorization, and control is fundamental. A military officer might supply weapons without instructions from the civilian cabinet; a border official might facilitate passage for reasons of sympathy; a prime minister might receive vague intelligence without identifying the participants or being able to prevent the operation. Each claim requires its own documents. Vienna's diplomats could reasonably demand cooperation against clandestine border trafficking, but the finding that Serbian agents had assisted conspirators would not by itself establish a Serbian government order to kill the heir. Conversely, the absence of evidence against the cabinet did not exonerate every state employee or officer. Simplified accounts tend to choose one extreme because the institutional differences are harder to narrate.

Austro-Hungarian policy cannot be reduced to a response to a police report. Berchtold and Conrad viewed Serbia through years of military and nationalities disputes. Successive Serbian victories had increased its territorial reach, its confidence, and the perceived appeal of South Slav unification among Habsburg subjects. Tisza worried about the consequences of annexing further Slavs and about exposing the monarchy to Russian intervention; his objections were strategic rather than a categorical rejection of force. The Serbian government itself was divided between elected civilian authorities and officers whose political influence had been strengthened by Balkan warfare. What took place after Sarajevo was therefore a contest between different institutional actors, not merely an exchange between two unitary national wills.

Section 108.4

The German Blank Cheque and Austria-Hungary's Decision for Confrontation

The assassination reopened an internal debate in Vienna. Conrad advocated immediate military action. Berchtold favored decisive action but needed German backing and the consent of Tisza, who feared that war with Serbia could draw in Russia and weaken Hungary's position. Franz Joseph, aged eighty-three, wrote a letter to Wilhelm II, accompanied by a memorandum emphasizing the need to isolate and diminish Serbia. On July 5 Count Alexander Hoyos, Berchtold's chief of staff, carried these documents to Berlin. At Potsdam that day Wilhelm II met the Austro-Hungarian ambassador, László Szögyény, and said, according to Szögyény's report, that Austria-Hungary could count on Germany's support even if the action led to serious complications with Russia. The next day Bethmann Hollweg confirmed the assurance. Wilhelm left on July 6 for his annual cruise to Norwegian waters, a departure that gave the impression of normalcy.

These assurances, later called the blank cheque, are among the most controversial decisions of the crisis. The German leadership was not simply responding to a request for help. By July 1914 Germany's military and political leaders had come to regard Russia's rapid rearmament and railway construction as a growing danger, expecting that Russian strength would reach a threshold in 1916 or 1917 that would leave Germany unable to defend itself. Some, including Moltke and Jagow, argued that a confrontation would be better sooner than later. Others, including Bethmann Hollweg, appear to have hoped that Austria-Hungary could act quickly against Serbia and that Russia would not intervene, because the Tsar would not defend regicides and because Russia was not ready for war. They recognized that a wider war was possible and accepted the risk. Historians debate how far this was a calculated gamble to split the Entente, how far a preventive-war strategy, and how far a miscalculation in which Berlin expected a localized conflict. The documentary record includes marginal notes by Wilhelm, reports from ambassadors, and a diary attributed to Bethmann's aide Kurt Riezler, whose authenticity and dating have themselves been challenged.

Within Austria-Hungary, the Council of Ministers met on July 7. Most participants favored a military response, but Tisza insisted on first presenting Serbia with demands, and on assurances that the monarchy would not annex Serbian territory. By July 14 he had accepted the idea of an ultimatum, on the understanding that its terms would be hard enough that Serbia would probably refuse. The final decision, taken on July 19, was to present an ultimatum after the French president left Russia, and to prepare for war if it was not accepted. At this stage Austro-Hungarian leaders were seeking a war against Serbia, not a war against Russia. They believed, or hoped, that German support would deter Russian intervention. If it did not, Austria-Hungary would need both its Balkan forces and its northern armies. That ambiguity, between a limited Balkan operation and a larger contest, haunted Austro-Hungarian military planning throughout the crisis.

The documentary record separates the German assurance from the later Austrian ultimatum. Szögyény reported what Wilhelm II said after the July 5 conversation, and Bethmann Hollweg's confirmation strengthened Vienna's belief that the German ally would not abandon it if Russia intervened. The assurance was not an automatic application of the 1879 treaty, whose specified defensive triggers differed from a Habsburg initiative against Serbia. Nor did it mean that Berlin drafted each Austrian demand. Rather, Berlin offered political and potentially military backing at the moment Vienna was choosing whether and how to use force. That distinction helps assign responsibility to the governments making distinct decisions while showing how their choices reinforced each other.

The German calculation also contained competing expectations. One possibility was that Russia would remain passive and Austria could punish Serbia; another was that Russia would intervene, causing the crisis to expand. Leaders could consider the second outcome a tolerable danger without consistently seeking a general war as their first preference. Some military figures used preventive-war reasoning because they expected Russian armaments to grow stronger, but it does not follow that an agreed state plan for general war existed on July 5. Meanwhile Vienna's demand that Serbia be politically reduced or humiliated grew more precise during the following days. The decisive departure from previous crises was not merely the existence of an alliance, but a series of choices to pursue coercion despite known risks.

Historical Vocabulary
Blank cheque, localization, deterrence, escalation

The phrase blank cheque (in German, Blankoscheck) refers to the German assurances of July 5 to 6, 1914, that Austria-Hungary could rely on German support in a confrontation with Serbia, without Germany specifying limits or conditions. The expression is a retrospective metaphor, not a term used in the original documents. It suggests unconditional authorization, which overstates the extent to which Germany controlled the outcome, since Berlin neither dictated the ultimatum's contents nor learned its text before July 22. At the same time, the term captures something real: Germany did not request that Vienna moderate its demands, and encouraged action quickly. Historians therefore use the phrase carefully, as shorthand for an assurance of support whose consequences German leaders accepted.

Localization was the policy of attempting to confine an armed dispute to Austria-Hungary and Serbia. Deterrence depended on convincing Russia that intervention would have intolerable consequences; escalation meant a conflict widening in scope, intensity or participants. The German assurance strengthened Vienna even while Berlin publicly advocated localization. Students should evaluate the contradiction between rhetoric and the risk governments knowingly accepted.

Division 2 — The Ultimatum and the Failure to Contain the Austro-Serbian Conflict

Division II: The Ultimatum and the Failure to Contain the Austro-Serbian Conflict · Section 108.5

The July Delay: Preparations, Secrecy, and European Diplomacy

Almost three weeks separated the assassination from the ultimatum. During that interval Austria-Hungary conducted its investigation, tried to persuade Tisza, awaited the end of the harvest leave for many troops, and sought to deliver its demands when the French president and prime minister were at sea after their visit to St. Petersburg. The delay had consequences. It dissipated the wave of sympathy that the assassination had aroused in other capitals, it allowed Russia and France to coordinate, and it gave Vienna's critics time to argue that Austria had been less concerned about the crime than about using it.

Secrecy was a deliberate policy. Austria-Hungary concealed its plans from other governments, and Germany encouraged an impression of normalcy: the Kaiser cruised in Norwegian waters, Moltke and other senior officials took leave, and Berlin publicly maintained that the matter concerned Austria-Hungary and Serbia alone. In reality the German government was informed in general terms of Vienna's plan and, by about July 22, had received the text of the ultimatum. It did not seek to soften it. Other governments received hints but not documents. Poincaré and Viviani, traveling by sea and with limited communication, were unable to react in detail. Britain, whose diplomats had been warned of Austrian anger, did not appreciate the severity of the intended demands.

The Franco-Russian meetings of July 20 to 23 have attracted considerable attention because they coincided with the ultimatum's preparation. Poincaré and Viviani affirmed the alliance, discussed the Balkan situation, and, as the diplomatic record suggests, assured the Russian leadership of French support. Historians disagree on whether this amounted to encouragement of Russian firmness or an ordinary reaffirmation of alliance. The French documentary record is incomplete, since some papers were later edited or lost, and the contemporary Russian accounts reflect their own concerns. What is clear is that when the ultimatum arrived, the French and Russian leaders were already in contact and that Paris did not urge restraint upon St. Petersburg with the vigor that some later critics believed necessary.

Delays also altered the evidentiary position. Foreign embassies heard rumors that the monarchy would make serious demands, but they did not know the final wording, the precise deadline, or the fact that Vienna's senior leadership regarded rejection as likely. British officials, accustomed to crises in which states demanded satisfaction and then negotiated, initially underestimated the degree to which a punitive decision had already been taken. Austrian officials worried that a longer interval would reduce the political shock of the assassination, yet they also required time to reconcile the two halves of the monarchy and make military preparations. Secrecy therefore protected the proposed operation while denying other governments an opportunity to negotiate its substance before the ultimatum was delivered.

In assessing the Russian and French meetings, historians must distinguish minutes, ambassadorial reconstructions, public speeches, and memoirs written afterward. The precise language exchanged between Poincaré, Viviani, Nicholas II, and Sazonov is not fully recoverable from one authoritative transcript. France's alliance with Russia made support against German aggression a major expectation, but it did not settle what France would do if an Austro-Serbian confrontation started under legally contested circumstances. Similarly, a warning that Russia could not accept the destruction of Serbia was not identical to an order for Russian general mobilization. The July delay created opportunities for diplomacy, but the uneven distribution of information made those opportunities difficult to use.

Section 108.6

The Austro-Hungarian Ultimatum of July 23, 1914

At about six in the evening on July 23, the Austro-Hungarian minister in Belgrade, Baron Wladimir Giesl von Gieslingen, delivered a note to the Serbian government. Pašić himself was away from the capital, and the note was received by the acting finance minister. The note, a lengthy document, accused Serbia of tolerating a subversive movement directed against the monarchy and demanded a public declaration to be published in the official gazette. It included ten specific demands, to be accepted within forty-eight hours. Giesl was instructed to break off relations and leave Belgrade if the response was not a complete acceptance.

The demands fell into several categories. Some asked Serbia to suppress newspapers and publications hostile to the monarchy, to dissolve the nationalist society Narodna Odbrana and similar organizations, and to eliminate anti-Austrian propaganda from education. Others demanded the removal of officers and officials whom Austria-Hungary would name, the arrest of Major Tankosić and Milan Ciganović, and measures against arms smuggling, including punishment of border officials. Two demands, numbered five and six, were the most contentious. They required Serbia to accept the collaboration of Austro-Hungarian officials in suppressing subversion on Serbian territory, and the participation of Austro-Hungarian delegates in a judicial inquiry into the plot. Serbian leaders regarded these as incompatible with national sovereignty. The note also required Serbia to explain hostile statements by its officials and to report the execution of each measure.

Historians generally agree that the ultimatum was designed to be difficult to accept in full. The leading Austrian ministers expected a refusal or an evasive reply and had already decided on war in that event. This does not mean that every demand was unreasonable. Several, such as the suppression of nationalist propaganda and the investigation of arms trafficking, were not unusual in principle. But the deadline, the demand for foreign participation in an inquiry on Serbian soil, and the instruction to break off relations if the demands were not unconditionally accepted ensured that the note functioned as an instrument of confrontation rather than negotiation. The reactions of foreign diplomats reflected that judgment. The British Foreign Secretary, Grey, reportedly told the Austrian ambassador that he had never seen one state address to another a document of such a formidable character. The Russian Foreign Minister, Sazonov, reportedly said on reading it that it meant a European war.

The note's design reveals how substantive demands and procedure interacted. Its public declaration called upon Serbia to condemn the agitation alleged to have encouraged the crime; other numbered points targeted newspapers, organizations, educators, officers, and frontier personnel. Point five asked for Austro-Hungarian collaboration in suppressing the anti-Habsburg movement inside Serbia. Point six concerned participation by Austro-Hungarian delegates in the judicial investigation relating to conspirators. Both raised questions of external jurisdiction, but they were not identical: police cooperation might be arranged by treaty or agreement, whereas participation in a sovereign state's judicial inquiry could mean very different things depending on powers granted to foreign agents. The ultimatum left insufficient space to negotiate those distinctions.

Its unusually short deadline was not the only obstacle. The Austrian representative had instructions to leave unless an unconditional response arrived, and Vienna's leaders had already committed themselves politically to a military outcome if the note were not accepted in full. A government interested principally in securing arrests and suppressing illegal networks could have sought reciprocal evidence gathering, a mixed commission, or an extension for detailed legal language. Those avenues were debated outside Vienna but were not adopted by its decision makers. The historical question is therefore not whether every demand was intrinsically impossible, but why a program of intrusive demands was coupled to a deadline and decision procedure that effectively foreclosed ordinary bargaining.

Section 108.7

Serbia's Reply of July 25 and the Breaking of Diplomatic Relations

Serbia had forty-eight hours in which to decide. Its government sought advice from Russia, France, and Britain, and discussed the ultimatum at length. Russia counseled both restraint and diplomatic support. Britain urged conciliation. On July 25, shortly before the deadline of six in the evening, Pašić personally delivered the reply to Giesl. By then the Serbian government had already ordered mobilization and begun transferring its administrative functions toward the interior city of Niš. The reply was carefully drafted and conciliatory in tone. It accepted most of the demands, qualified several, and rejected one in principle. Giesl read it and left Belgrade by train that evening, breaking diplomatic relations. Austria-Hungary ordered partial mobilization, effective from July 28, against Serbia.

The reply's contents are essential to the evaluation of the crisis, and the Primary Source Window below sets the demands beside Serbia's answers. In summary, Serbia accepted most demands relating to publications, societies, education, border control, and official explanations, often with the condition that Austria-Hungary supply evidence. It stated that it had arrested Tankosić, and that Ciganović had not been located. On demand five, it said it did not clearly understand what the collaboration meant, and that it would accept cooperation consistent with international law and criminal procedure. On demand six, it declined the participation of Austro-Hungarian delegates as contrary to its constitution and laws, while offering to give information to the Austrian authorities in appropriate cases. It added that if Austria-Hungary was not satisfied, Serbia was willing to refer the dispute to the International Court at The Hague or to the Great Powers.

Serbia's answer should be read clause by clause rather than summarized as acceptance of nine points or rejection of one. The government agreed to publish a condemnation of anti-Austrian agitation and to take many promised measures against publications, schools, and nationalist associations. But it qualified the removal of particular officials by asking for evidence, and its offers concerning foreign collaboration were framed around constitutional and international-law limits. On the judicial inquiry it rejected direct foreign participation in Serbia's own investigation, while offering to supply information, cooperate within legal bounds, and submit unresolved issues to international adjudication. Those legal qualifications mattered even where the overall language was unusually conciliatory.

For contemporary readers, the difference between a reply that was diplomatically substantial and one that met every prescribed condition had immediate consequences. Vienna judged that Serbian qualifications were inadequate, and Giesl followed his instructions to break relations. Other governments regarded the answer as a possible starting point for negotiations; Wilhelm II's later marginal notes indicated that he saw concessions significant enough to alter the case for an immediate war. Neither reaction can be understood without the actual text of both documents. Students should ask whether Vienna objected because certain points had been rejected, because the qualifications threatened to limit Austrian power, or because its policy had already moved beyond the goal of obtaining formal Serbian compliance.

Section 108.8

From Diplomatic Confrontation to War, July 25–28

The three days after Giesl's departure were decisive. In St. Petersburg a Russian council of ministers had met on July 24, and on July 25 the Tsar approved a set of preparatory military measures, known as the Period Preparatory to War, together with the principle of partial mobilization against Austria-Hungary should the latter attack Serbia. Sazonov declared that Russia could not remain indifferent to the fate of Serbia. In Vienna, ministers regarded the Serbian reply as inadequate. In Berlin, Jagow publicly maintained that the quarrel was a local affair, and rejected on July 27 Grey's proposal, made the previous day, that the ambassadors of Britain, France, Germany, and Italy meet in London to find a settlement. Germany argued that such a conference would amount to a court of arbitration over Austria-Hungary, which was not acceptable.

On July 28 Franz Joseph, at Bad Ischl, signed a declaration of war against Serbia, which Berchtold sent by open telegram that day. Berchtold's motives included the desire to forestall mediation, as well as a belief that a state of war would make it harder for Russia to restore the situation. Conrad's military timetable indicated that operations could not begin until mid-August, so the declaration preceded the ability to act. Austrian artillery on the Danube shelled Belgrade the next day. Serbia, already mobilized, appealed to Russia, and its army prepared to defend the northern frontier.

Austria-Hungary's declaration of war was the first formal state of war of the crisis and a decisive step. Before it, a negotiated outcome, perhaps with Serbian concessions, had been possible in principle. After it, mediators had to ask Vienna to halt operations that had already been publicly begun. Russia concluded that Austria-Hungary had no intention of negotiating seriously, and on July 28 Sazonov persuaded Nicholas II to approve partial mobilization in four military districts facing Austria-Hungary: Kiev, Odessa, Moscow, and Kazan. The order took effect on July 29. Russian leaders presented the step as a deterrent and as a lever for diplomacy rather than as the beginning of war with Germany, though the practical difficulty of partial mobilization, discussed in Section 108.10, soon undermined that distinction.

The shift to armed measures was neither instant nor uniform. Serbia began military precautions and moved parts of its government away from exposed Belgrade; Austria-Hungary prepared forces against Serbia while still managing the implications of its Russian frontier. The two armies operated with different mobilization schedules and strategic assumptions, and some diplomatic messages passed through capitals only after their governments had made initial readiness decisions. A formal declaration on July 28 established a legal state of war, but it did not settle the question whether the conflict would remain localized. Indeed, the effect of the declaration was to make other governments evaluate whether their warnings and alliance expectations required a further response.

Vienna's leaders still hoped that Berlin would deter Russia or that limited success against Serbia would be achieved before a broader intervention. Russian leaders believed that standing aside would leave Serbia isolated and destroy Russia's credibility in southeastern Europe, yet they also feared the military inferiority and economic disruption of general war. Britain was concerned about preventing a continental conflict without agreeing to compel its partners to accept a particular settlement. This asymmetry helps explain diplomatic confusion: states sent proposals designed to preserve their own freedom and prestige, while recipients interpreted the same proposals as evidence that the other side would not yield. The formal outbreak of an Austro-Serbian war was a critical step, not the end of the causal chain.

Division 3 — Mobilization, Military Plans, and Continental Escalation

Last Mediation Efforts and the Failure of Localization, July 28–30

Between July 28 and 30 several mediation efforts were attempted, and each failed. Grey proposed a conference in London and, as it was rejected, suggested other forms of mediation. The Kaiser, reading the Serbian reply on July 28, wrote that it removed almost every reason for war, and proposed that Austria-Hungary occupy Belgrade as a pledge for the fulfillment of Serbia's promises and then negotiate. This proposal, called the Halt in Belgrade, was passed to Vienna by Bethmann Hollweg, but only after delay and in a form that Vienna could easily set aside. On the night of July 29 and the following day Bethmann sent increasingly urgent telegrams to Vienna, warning that Germany could not allow itself to be drawn frivolously into a world conflagration by Austria-Hungary's policy.

At the same time, the Russian and Austrian governments conducted direct discussions. Sazonov, in a proposal known as the Sazonov formula, offered on July 30 to suspend Russian military preparations if Austria-Hungary removed from its demands those that infringed Serbian sovereignty. Berchtold, under pressure from Berlin, briefly agreed to resume talks with Russia, but without pausing military operations. The Tsar and the Kaiser also exchanged a series of personal telegrams, written in English and addressed by their family nicknames, in which each professed a desire for peace while blaming the other side's measures. These exchanges have often been cited as evidence of the leaders' good intentions. They also reveal how limited the monarchs' control over events had become.

Why did mediation fail? Three factors stand out. First, the principal parties had incompatible aims. Vienna wished to reduce Serbia as a political and military force, while Russia would not accept the subordination of Serbia. Second, the speed of events outpaced diplomacy. Austria's declaration, Russia's partial mobilization, and Germany's reaction to Russia's measures occurred within a few days, leaving little time for negotiators. Third, mixed signals from Berlin undercut efforts at restraint. While Bethmann urged Vienna to negotiate, Moltke, on July 30, telegraphed Conrad urging Austria-Hungary to mobilize against Russia at once and promising that Germany would do likewise. The two messages reflected a division in German leadership between civilian and military views. Their effect was to leave Vienna uncertain about what Berlin truly wanted, and to give the more determined Austrian leaders a justification for continuing.

Grey's suggested ambassadors' conference was one variant of a broader great-power concert. He did not claim that the powers could simply impose a peace on Serbia and Austria-Hungary; he hoped that Germany, France, Italy, and Britain could identify an acceptable formula while Vienna and St. Petersburg paused military action. German officials resisted some conference proposals as interference in a dispute they insisted belonged only to Austria-Hungary and Serbia. Direct Austro-Russian talks were considered but moved slowly. Wilhelm II's idea that the Austrians might occupy Belgrade as a pledge or bargaining position offered a different route, but any partial occupation would still have involved coercion, security risks, and agreement by the governments whose forces were already moving.

Timing is essential to understanding why the proposals failed. A telegram written before one cabinet meeting might arrive after another government had authorized mobilization; an emperor's marginal comment was not necessarily transmitted in time to change a general staff's instructions. German officials did not always pass on diplomatic suggestions to Vienna with urgency, and Vienna had little incentive to suspend operations after declaring war. At the same time, Russian military advisers argued that further delay would damage their readiness against a potential German attack. The record shows multiple plausible openings for negotiation, but no single plan whose success is established by the fact that it was suggested. Explaining failure requires identifying the governments that rejected, delayed, or subordinated mediation to military and political objectives.

Section 108.10

Russia's Military Preparations and the Decision for General Mobilization

The decision for Russian general mobilization is among the most analyzed in the crisis, because it produced the German response that carried Germany into war with Russia and France. Its sequence requires care. On July 25 Russia began preparatory measures. On July 28 Sazonov and the Tsar decided on partial mobilization against Austria-Hungary, and the order was issued on July 29. That same day, the Russian General Staff, under Nikolai Yanushkevich, and the War Minister, Vladimir Sukhomlinov, informed Sazonov that partial mobilization was technically hard to carry out without disrupting the system for a later general mobilization. Sazonov, who feared that Germany would soon intervene, urged general mobilization. Late on July 29 Nicholas II agreed, but after a telegram from Wilhelm II he cancelled the order and returned to partial mobilization.

On July 30 Sazonov met the Tsar again and argued that war was inevitable and that Russia should not be caught unprepared. Nicholas gave his approval, and the order for general mobilization was issued that afternoon, taking effect on July 31. The German ambassador, Friedrich Pourtalès, had warned Sazonov on July 29 that further Russian mobilization could force Germany to mobilize, and the German government later cited the Russian decision as the immediate cause of its own. Russian leaders did not intend a war with Germany at this stage, but they believed that the German reluctance to restrain Austria-Hungary left them little alternative, and they feared that delay would leave their army at a disadvantage given the slower pace of Russian mobilization.

Historians weigh this decision differently. Some, including Sean McMeekin, have emphasized the Russian role, arguing that Russian leaders pursued ambitions in the Balkans and the Straits and took decisions that made war more likely. Others point out that Russia's general mobilization followed Austria's declaration of war on Serbia, the bombardment of Belgrade, Germany's refusal of mediation, and indications that Germany would back Austria-Hungary to the end. A more cautious view, held by many scholars, holds that the Russian decision made war much more likely, but that Russian leaders acted in a context shaped by decisions made earlier in Vienna and Berlin. A mobilization was not a declaration of war. It did, however, start a process that German military planning treated as a threat, and it removed the possibility that a war could remain limited.

Russian mobilization decisions became controversial partly because administrative labels concealed practical differences. Preparatory measures could include recalling personnel, protecting railways, concentrating supplies, and restricting leave before a formal mobilization order. The Russian military expected a war with Austria-Hungary to risk a German intervention, but its plans were not easily divided into a southern partial mobilization and a separate contingency against Germany. Civilian leaders sought a way to signal determination without inviting immediate German countermeasures; generals argued that an incomplete plan might leave their army unable to respond if the war widened. This conflict among political warning, technical feasibility, and fear of falling behind shaped the decisions of July 29 and 30.

Nicholas II's authorization of general mobilization on July 30 was therefore momentous, not merely another precaution. It mobilized forces on a scale that German leaders understood as a threat even though Russia had not declared war on Germany. Russian policy was in turn influenced by reports of Austrian fighting, by memories of diplomatic retreat in 1908–1909, and by French support for the alliance. The same facts can sustain two judgments: that Russian mobilization widened the danger and that it was taken in a crisis already driven toward coercion by Austrian and German decisions. A responsible narrative holds these propositions together instead of making mobilization either morally neutral or a self-evident act of aggression.

Historical Vocabulary
Preparatory Measures, Partial and General Mobilization, Ultimatum

Preparatory measures could ready supplies, communications, leave and transport before the formal call-up. Partial mobilization activated selected districts or formations; whether it was operationally feasible varied by state. General mobilization activated the armed forces on a much wider basis, with railway and supply consequences. An ultimatum was a demand tied to a deadline and threatened response, not a synonym for a mobilization order. These categories must be placed on separate tracks when analyzing Russian and German decisions in late July.

Section 108.11

German Ultimatums and the Declaration of War on Russia

On July 31 Germany took a decisive step. The government proclaimed Kriegsgefahrzustand, a state of imminent danger of war, which was a preliminary stage to mobilization. It then sent ultimatums to Russia and France. Russia was given twelve hours to cease its military preparations against Germany and Austria-Hungary, and France eighteen hours to declare whether it would remain neutral in a war between Germany and Russia. The same day, Austria-Hungary ordered general mobilization, effective August 4. In Paris, the socialist leader Jean Jaurès, who had worked to prevent war, was assassinated by a nationalist, Raoul Villain.

Russia did not reply to the German ultimatum, and on August 1 Germany declared war on Russia. At the same time it ordered mobilization, signed by Wilhelm II in the late afternoon. The relationship between political and military authority in Germany was crucial here. The Prussian-German system gave the Kaiser supreme command, and Moltke and the War Ministry under Erich von Falkenhayn had significant influence over timing. Bethmann Hollweg wished to ensure that Russia appeared the aggressor, which was important to secure the support of the Social Democratic Party in the Reichstag and to ensure British neutrality. The German leadership considered this essential, and the declaration of war on Russia was presented as a response to Russian mobilization.

On the evening of August 1 an episode occurred that illustrates both the pressure of planning and the possibility of political choice. The German ambassador in London, Prince Lichnowsky, reported what he understood to be a British suggestion that if Germany refrained from attacking France, Britain would remain neutral and guarantee French neutrality. The Kaiser was delighted and declared that the army could simply be sent east. Moltke objected that the deployment could not be improvised, because the plan was prepared only for a campaign beginning in the west. The apparent offer was subsequently clarified as a misunderstanding: Grey had not made the proposal as Lichnowsky understood it, and the King’s reply to Wilhelm confirmed that no such binding neutrality guarantee had been offered. The episode shows that German military planning was inflexible, but also that the Kaiser, for a moment, considered an alternative, and that Moltke resisted the change, while subsequent British clarification also removed the apparent diplomatic alternative.

The sequence of German warnings matters more than a single declaration. German officials warned St. Petersburg that continuing mobilization would provoke German countermeasures. On July 31 Germany declared a state of imminent danger of war and demanded cessation of Russian mobilization within a short deadline; it simultaneously asked France whether it would remain neutral in a German-Russian war. These communications did not reveal a completed diplomatic bargain. Germany's military planning was premised upon rapid western deployment if a large eastern conflict began, while its political leadership sought to attribute the moment of escalation to Russia's general mobilization. Whether Germany could safely delay deployment was a question about risks and choices, not simply railway arithmetic.

Germany declared war on Russia on August 1. That legal act did not follow automatically from the Dual Alliance: the treaty's defensive provisions and the political decisions made in July remained distinct. Wilhelm II, Bethmann Hollweg, Jagow, and Moltke did not always agree on the order or feasibility of measures. Some of the emperor's last-minute inquiries reflected a desire to avoid a western war if possible, but they also came after diplomacy and preparation had placed serious limits on German choices. The government then moved toward conflict with France even though a request for French neutrality had not produced a viable disengagement of the two alliance systems.

Section 108.12

France, Military Planning, and Germany's Turn toward the West

France's position was determined by its alliance with Russia and by its own military planning. The French government, led by Poincaré and Viviani, had returned from Russia on July 29, and on July 30 the army was ordered to withdraw ten kilometers from the German frontier, in an attempt to show that France would not provoke a clash. Joffre, the Chief of the General Staff, pressed for mobilization as the German measures developed. On the afternoon of August 1 the Council of Ministers approved general mobilization, effective August 2. The French and German orders were issued within hours of one another, the French shortly before the German. Neither government wished to be the second to mobilize.

The German response to French mobilization followed from a plan that had been developed over a decade. The Schlieffen Plan, named after Alfred von Schlieffen, who prepared a memorandum in 1905 while Chief of the General Staff, envisaged defeating France quickly through a wide sweep through Belgium, before Russia could complete its mobilization. Moltke modified it, strengthening the left wing, abandoning the violation of Dutch territory, and depending on the rapid seizure of the Belgian fortress of Liège. Historians continue to debate how far Schlieffen's memorandum was a detailed operational plan and how far it was a theoretical study, but there is no doubt that by 1914 the German army had prepared an offensive in the west, and had abandoned an eastern deployment plan in 1913. French planning was also offensive. Plan XVII, adopted in 1913, committed French armies to attack toward Lorraine and the Ardennes, and, if necessary, into Belgium.

On August 3 Germany declared war on France, citing false claims of French violations of the frontier and bombing of German territory. The decision flowed from military necessity as Moltke understood it: Germany needed to attack France before Russia was ready. It was nonetheless a political decision, taken by a government that could have chosen to remain on the defensive in the west while fighting Russia in the east. That option, which Moltke rejected as impossible, was never seriously tested. The case illustrates the central point about military plans in 1914. They constrained choices, and they did so because political leaders accepted the plans as limits. The responsibility for accepting them lay with those leaders.

French decision makers confronted a double obligation as they understood it: preserving the alliance with Russia and reducing the possibility that France itself would be portrayed as the aggressor. They took military precautions before general mobilization and, at points, ordered troops to maintain distance from the frontier to avoid incidents that could be used in German propaganda. France's general-mobilization order on August 1 came shortly before Germany's corresponding order, but the clock times alone cannot prove who began preparing for conflict. Preparations, warnings, legal decrees, and war declarations were separate categories, and every state had acted in more than one category before August began.

German western planning was not an immutable script drafted once by Schlieffen. The operational concept evolved under Moltke, assigning extensive forces to a sweep through Belgium and Luxembourg against France while seeking to contain Russia initially in the east. Strategic planners believed speed critical, but political leaders still had to authorize hostile acts on neutral territory. The German declaration of war on France on August 3 was accompanied by claims concerning frontier attacks, some of which proved unreliable; it did not constitute proof that France had launched a German-scale invasion. Mapping the border, railways, fortress systems and intended routes helps students see why an army plan affected diplomacy, without allowing the plan to substitute for a government's legal responsibility for the invasion.

Division IV: Neutrality, British Entry, and European Societies in Early August · Section 108.13

Luxembourg, Belgium, and the Violation of Neutrality

Luxembourg, a neutral grand duchy under the guarantee of the Treaty of London of 1867, was occupied by German troops on August 2, partly to secure the railways that ran through it. The Grand Duchess, Marie-Adélaïde, protested, and the government remained in place under German occupation. Belgium was a more serious matter. Its neutrality had been guaranteed since 1839 by Britain, France, Prussia, Austria, and Russia, and the German plan required passage through its territory. On the evening of August 2 the German minister in Brussels delivered an ultimatum giving the Belgian government twelve hours to allow passage, alleging, without evidence, that France intended to invade through Belgium.

The Belgian cabinet, under King Albert I, rejected the ultimatum early on August 3. The King issued an appeal to Britain, and the government decided to resist. German troops crossed the frontier on the morning of August 4, and attacked the forts of Liège the next day. In the Reichstag on August 4 Bethmann Hollweg acknowledged that the invasion contravened international law, and said Germany would make amends, invoking necessity. That admission, together with the later reports of atrocities against Belgian civilians examined in Chapter 109, made Belgium central to Allied propaganda. Belgium was also a colonial power, ruling the Congo, which meant that the war reached Central Africa from its outset.

The violation of Belgian neutrality was thus both a military and a political event. Militarily, it was the key to the German offensive. Politically, it gave Britain a reason for intervention that was easier to explain to a skeptical public than the defense of France or Russia. Germany's leaders had expected that Britain might intervene but hoped that the British would not do so over what they considered a technical matter, and some of them, including Bethmann Hollweg, were reported to dismiss the treaty obligation as a scrap of paper. The gamble that British intervention would come too late, or not at all, was one of the major misjudgments of the German government.

Luxembourg's position illustrates the gradations between diplomatic demand and territorial action. German forces entered the Grand Duchy on August 2 and occupied its railway network while asserting military necessity. Belgium, by contrast, received a German note demanding free passage and threatening treatment as an enemy if it refused. The Belgian government rejected the proposal, reasoning that accepting a foreign army's passage would itself sacrifice the neutrality it had promised to maintain. The German army crossed the Belgian frontier on August 4. These are distinct steps: occupation, ultimatum, refusal, and invasion must not be collapsed into a single event dated August 3.

Belgian neutrality was rooted in nineteenth-century international agreements and in Belgian political agency, not simply in a British pretext. Britain's 1839 treaty relationship was among the legal considerations discussed by ministers, but precisely what action the treaty legally obliged Britain to take in every conceivable case was debated. German leaders viewed their planned transit as an operational necessity to defeat France quickly; Britain viewed control of the Channel coast by a hostile power as a strategic concern independent of formal legal commitments. For Belgium the central issue was that neither military urgency nor promises of eventual restitution gave Germany the right to pass without consent. Its refusal mattered even though Belgian forces could not be expected to match Germany's military strength alone.

Section 108.14

Britain Decides: Cabinet Divisions, Belgian Neutrality, and August 4

Britain's decision was the last of the great decisions of the crisis and the one that most depended on a divided cabinet. Throughout July, Grey had worked to prevent war, proposing conferences and mediation, while refusing to promise either France or Russia that Britain would fight, and refusing to warn Germany that it would. This ambiguity reflected both the divisions of his Liberal party and his own assessment. The Anglo-French staff talks, authorized since 1906, and the naval arrangements of 1912, in which the French fleet was concentrated in the Mediterranean while the British protected the Channel, had created a moral expectation of support, though Grey had written in 1912 that these did not commit Britain to fight. On July 29 he warned the German ambassador that Britain might not remain aside, and rejected a German proposal that Britain remain neutral in return for a promise not to take French territory in Europe.

The Cabinet met repeatedly between August 1 and August 4. A substantial group of ministers favored neutrality, and two, John Burns and Viscount Morley, resigned. Others considered resigning but remained. On August 2 the Cabinet authorized Grey to assure France that the Royal Navy would protect the French Channel coast against German naval attack, and the Conservative opposition leaders, Bonar Law and Lord Lansdowne, wrote to Asquith offering support for intervention. The German ultimatum to Belgium changed the debate. On August 3 Grey addressed the House of Commons and presented the case for intervention on the grounds of obligations to France, Belgian neutrality, and British interest in preventing a single power from dominating the continent and the Channel coast. The Irish Nationalist leader, John Redmond, offered the support of the Irish Volunteers for the defense of Ireland, an important gesture given the domestic Home Rule crisis.

On August 4, after the German invasion of Belgium, Britain sent an ultimatum demanding assurance that Belgian neutrality would be respected by midnight in Berlin. No answer came, and Britain was at war from eleven o'clock that evening, London time. The declaration applied to the whole British Empire, and the governments of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and India soon pledged support, although the dominions determined the scale of their own participation. Historians generally agree that Belgium was necessary to unite the Cabinet and Parliament, but also that strategic reasoning, including fear of German domination, underlay Britain's decision. The 1904 entente did not compel Britain to fight, and the 1839 guarantee did not clearly require it either. The decision was one of judgment about interest and honor, made by a government that had been unwilling to commit itself in advance.

Britain's choice emerged from cabinet meetings, parliamentary argument, and rapidly changing reports from Brussels and Paris. Ministers differed over whether a continental war threatened British interests, whether earlier naval conversations with France had created a moral commitment, and whether neutral Belgium gave the cabinet a legally and politically intelligible ground for intervention. Grey's August 3 statement to the House of Commons explained both Britain's freedom from an automatic French alliance obligation and the potential consequences if Germany dominated western Europe. A parliamentary speech was a public justification, not a private confession of every minister's motive. Internal notes and resignation threats demonstrate a more divided political process than later accounts of national unanimity imply.

The German demand to Belgium materially changed the debate because it connected treaty commitments, public concern for a small neutral state, and British strategic calculation. Yet Belgian neutrality should neither be treated as an invented excuse nor as the sole sufficient cause of intervention. A Germany victorious over France might threaten the balance Britain had long sought to protect; a government that stayed neutral despite invasion of Belgium could also face a legitimacy crisis at home. Britain issued an ultimatum demanding respect for Belgian neutrality and entered war on August 4 after Germany failed to comply. The decision was British, made by a divided cabinet under acute pressure, and not a mechanical execution of the 1904 entente.

Section 108.15

Neutrality, Conditional Alliances, and the Choices of Other Powers

Not every state with an alliance entered the war. Italy, a partner of Germany and Austria-Hungary in the Triple Alliance, declared neutrality on August 2. The Italian government under Antonio Salandra argued that the alliance was defensive, that Austria-Hungary had acted aggressively against Serbia without consulting Rome, as the treaty required, and that Italy therefore had no obligation to fight. Behind that legal argument lay strategic interest: Italy claimed territories, including Trentino and Trieste, held by Austria-Hungary, and its leaders understood that Britain's navy could threaten its coasts. Italy would enter the war on the Entente side in May 1915, after bargaining with both camps, a development examined in Chapter 109.

Romania, bound since 1883 by a secret treaty to Austria-Hungary, also declared neutrality. A Crown Council at Sinaia on August 3 concluded that the casus foederis had not arisen, because Austria-Hungary had not been attacked, and public opinion, concerned about the Romanian population of Transylvania under Hungarian rule, did not favor war on Vienna's side. Bulgaria, Greece, and other Balkan states likewise waited, treating neutrality as a bargaining position and watching for promises of territory. The Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain, and the Scandinavian kingdoms remained neutral, their governments aware that their economies and territorial security depended on staying out.

The Ottoman Empire presents a particular case. On August 2 a small group of Ottoman leaders, including the War Minister Enver Pasha and the Grand Vizier Said Halim Pasha, signed a secret alliance with Germany, aimed at Russia. The treaty did not commit the Empire to immediate active war. The government ordered mobilization and declared armed neutrality on August 3, while debating, in a divided cabinet, when and whether to fight. The German warships Goeben and Breslau reached Constantinople on August 10, and the Empire entered the war in late October after a naval attack on Russian Black Sea ports. The Ottoman case shows that an alliance signed under crisis conditions could be a bargain with conditions, and that the Ottoman government acted from its own calculations about security, the Straits, and the threat from Russia. Japan, allied to Britain since 1902, would declare war on Germany on August 23, extending the conflict toward East Asia and the Pacific.

Italy's neutrality offers an especially clear demonstration that treaty labels did not determine behavior. Rome maintained that the Triple Alliance was defensive and that Austria-Hungary's initiative against Serbia had not met its conditions. Italian leaders also had unresolved territorial disagreements with Vienna, particularly over Italian-speaking Habsburg lands, and their military and financial readiness offered reasons for caution. Italy's declaration of neutrality was therefore both a legal argument and a political calculation. In Romania, the crown's earlier links to the Central Powers did not compel ministers and political forces to follow Germany and Austria-Hungary into war. The decisions taken in Rome and Bucharest exposed the difference between an agreement among dynasties and a state decision backed by institutions and public opinion.

The Ottoman case was different again. A secret treaty was signed between Germany and the Ottoman Empire on August 2, but this did not mean Ottoman armed forces immediately entered combat against the Entente. Ottoman ministers were divided, strategic negotiations continued, and subsequent actions brought the empire into the wider conflict later in 1914. Similar distinctions applied to neutral states outside Europe whose trade, shipping, and diplomatic contacts were affected immediately by the European crisis. The opening war was multinational and imperial in its consequences from the start, but the participation of each belligerent must be dated on its own terms. Such distinctions also guard against a map that misleadingly assigns the later wartime coalition structure to the first days of August.

Section 108.16

Mobilization and Everyday Life: Soldiers, Families, and Civilian Reactions

For millions of Europeans the crisis became real only when mobilization notices appeared. Reservists left their workplaces and villages and reported to depots, railways were reserved for troop movements, horses were requisitioned, and food and fuel supplies were reorganized. The timing was significant in rural societies, because mobilization came during the harvest, removing men and horses from the fields. In France and Russia in particular, women, children, and the elderly were left to bring in crops. Families faced uncertainty about wages, rents, and credit, and states established allowances and relief for dependents, with varying generosity. The experience differed among social groups: urban professionals, peasants, industrial workers, and aristocratic officers saw the call-up in different ways, and the poor were hit hardest by economic disruption.

Public reactions varied. Large crowds gathered in Berlin, Vienna, Paris, London, and St. Petersburg to hear the news, and some demonstrations were patriotic and enthusiastic. Older accounts spoke of a Europe united by war enthusiasm, the so-called spirit of 1914. Scholars such as Jeffrey Verhey for Germany and Jean-Jacques Becker for France have shown that this picture was incomplete. Enthusiasm was strongest among urban, educated, and middle-class groups, while many peasants and workers reacted with anxiety, resignation, or a sense of duty. In Germany, Social Democrats organized large antiwar demonstrations in late July, and then, on August 4, the party's Reichstag delegation voted for war credits, accepting the political truce known as the Burgfrieden. In France, Poincaré appealed for a union sacrée, and the authorities refrained from the mass arrests of suspected dissidents that had been planned. After Jaurès's assassination, socialists and trade unionists rallied to the national defense.

Financial panic accompanied military preparation. In the last week of July the stock exchanges of Vienna, Berlin, Paris, and London closed or were disrupted, banks faced demands for gold, and the Bank of England raised its rate from four to ten percent between July 30 and August 1. The British government extended the August bank holiday and declared a moratorium on certain debts. Tourists scrambled to return home, consumers hoarded food, and governments imposed controls. Hostility toward foreigners appeared rapidly, with spy scares and attacks on property belonging to those regarded as enemy nationals, including the German embassy in St. Petersburg, which was sacked on August 4. Multiethnic empires faced special problems. In Austria-Hungary, Czech, Serb, Polish, Ukrainian, Romanian, and Jewish conscripts joined the army, some with enthusiasm and some with reluctance, and authorities watched for disloyalty. In Russia, reports described both patriotic demonstrations and disorder among reservists, and the government prohibited the sale of vodka during mobilization.

Mobilization disrupted more than military rosters. Men left farms, mines, workshops and offices; administrators had to register horses, vehicles, wagons and food; railways prioritized military movements over civilian travel. Schools and municipal buildings became points for registration and collection. In some places crowds sang, waved flags, or accompanied departing units, but enthusiasm could coexist with fear, and photographs of well-dressed urban crowds cannot measure the feelings of every rural family or industrial worker. The difference between a volunteer crowd and a conscript household is crucial. People who cheered a troop train might not know when an enlisted relative would return or how wages and rents would be paid in his absence.

Financial anxiety intensified the disruption. European exchanges faced closures and payment difficulties as the value of bills, securities and foreign balances became uncertain. In Britain, the Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 3 percent immediately before July 30 to 4 percent that day, 8 percent on July 31, and 10 percent on August 1; emergency monetary arrangements followed. These figures document a liquidity crisis rather than a precise measure of ordinary public opinion. Households experienced events unevenly: a metropolitan bank clerk, a Serbian refugee, a Belgian shopkeeper fearing invasion, and a Russian peasant reservist confronted very different dangers. The chapter's household reconstruction should therefore be read as a teaching device grounded in administrative and personal records, not an eyewitness biography of an invented family.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Call-Up: Households, Harvests, and Railways

Mobilization turned personal timetables into military ones. A French or German reservist received a call-up booklet or poster, reported to a depot within a prescribed number of days, and traveled by a train whose schedule had been designed years earlier. The departure of a husband, son, or hired laborer could end the household's income and removed the labor on which the family farm depended. Local authorities, churches, and charities organized collections and committees to assist the families left behind.

Historians reconstruct these experiences from diaries, letters, newspapers, police reports, prefects' dispatches in France, and later memoirs, each of which has limits. Letters and diaries come disproportionately from the literate, newspaper accounts reflect censorship and editorial choice, and later memoirs were shaped by what four years of war had made of the memory of August. The richest evidence therefore describes the urban middle class, and the experiences of agricultural laborers, domestic servants, and minority communities, including Jews, who served in every army, must be reconstructed with particular care.

Division 5 — The Outbreak, Competing Interpretations, and the Transition to Total War

Division V: The Outbreak, Competing Interpretations, and the Transition to Total War · Section 108.17

Initial War Aims, Defensive Claims, and Uncertain Expectations

What did governments want when they entered the war? In August 1914 no power had a fully developed set of war aims, and the aims that emerged later, as the war grew more costly, differed from the motives of July. Still, the initial objectives reveal much. Austria-Hungary sought to reduce Serbia as a political and military force, ending its capacity to threaten the monarchy, without necessarily annexing it, a limit imposed by Tisza. Its leaders also hoped to restore the prestige of a state many believed was in decline. Germany's aims were security against Russia, support of its only dependable ally, and, for some leaders, an opportunity to break what they called encirclement. The expansive aims of the September Program of 1914, which Fritz Fischer later made central to his thesis, belong to the war's first weeks and are examined with the wartime German state in Chapter 110.

Russia aimed to protect Serbia and its own prestige in the Balkans, to prevent Austrian and German dominance there, and to secure its economic and strategic interests in the Straits, through which a large portion of its grain exports passed. France, formally on the defensive, honored its alliance and sought to preserve its great-power position, while the recovery of Alsace-Lorraine, lost in 1871, remained a widely held national hope though not an official war aim of July. Britain's stated reasons were the defense of Belgian neutrality, its obligations of honor to France, and the preservation of the balance of power. Serbia sought survival, and Belgium independence.

Each government also insisted that it was acting defensively. Austria-Hungary invoked self-defense against terrorism, Germany the defense of its ally and itself against Russian mobilization, Russia the defense of Serbia, France the defense of its territory, and Britain the defense of a small neutral state. These claims were published in the colour books, the German White Book, the British Blue Book, the French Yellow Book, the Russian Orange Book, the Austrian Red Book, and the Serbian Blue Book, each selective and in some cases altered. Historians treat them as evidence of how governments wished to present their cases to domestic and international opinion, not as neutral accounts. Most governments also expected a short war, and few anticipated its length or scale, a misjudgment of enormous consequence examined in Chapters 109 and 110.

Austria-Hungary initially presented its case as a response to terrorism and subversion from Serbia. Its leadership's private discussions, however, went beyond the punishment of specific accomplices toward an attempt to reduce Serbia's political independence or influence. Tisza's resistance to annexation reflected the monarchy's demographic fears, not an abandonment of coercive ambitions. Germany's early rhetoric stressed loyalty to its ally and the localization of a quarrel; once general war appeared likely, officials also invoked Russia's mobilization and the necessity of acting before opponents became stronger. Public explanations and private objectives sometimes overlapped, but they should not be treated as identical forms of evidence.

Russia argued that the balance in the Balkans and its credibility would be damaged if Serbia were destroyed. France publicly emphasized defense of the homeland and alliance obligations; its actual readiness decisions were shaped by fear of a western German attack. Britain referred to Belgian neutrality, European equilibrium, and the risks of standing aside. No single comprehensive set of peace terms had been jointly agreed by all belligerents at the start of August. German annexationist thinking developed during the conflict, but later programs must not be inserted into late June as though they had already been adopted. The continuity-and-change exercise should contrast each state's late-June position, its late-July bargaining language, and its early-August justification, noting where evidence is incomplete.

Section 108.18

An Escalation That Was Not Automatic: Parallel Decision Timeline

The preceding sections have followed the crisis capital by capital. The following study assembles the principal decisions in parallel, to allow students to see how the choices of one government created the conditions for those of another. It also shows how little time separated the key decisions. What the sequence demonstrates is that each stage was a decision, that alternatives existed at each stage, and that many decisions were made in response to the actions of others, often on the basis of incomplete or mistaken information.

A parallel chronology changes the causal picture. Coercive diplomacy began with Vienna's choice to pursue an ultimatum and Berlin's assurance of support; mediation efforts appeared repeatedly, but governments declined or delayed particular formulas. Preparatory measures and formal mobilization proceeded alongside this diplomatic exchange rather than after it. Declarations of war and invasions occurred only at the end of the sequence. These tracks intersected but were not the same activity. By placing them side by side, students can see that a peace proposal could arrive while a state was preparing its rail network, or that a government could remain technically at peace while already pressing a neighbor with an ultimatum. The chronology therefore identifies decision points, not a mechanical countdown.

The most consequential branching points included Berlin's assurance to Vienna on July 5–6, Vienna's decision to draft demands unlikely to be accepted without qualification, the rejection of Serbia's July 25 answer, the choice to declare war on July 28 rather than negotiate, Russian authorization of general mobilization on July 30, German ultimatums on July 31, and the decision to send troops through neutral territory. None guarantees that an alternative would have preserved peace indefinitely. A restrained German assurance might still have left Vienna determined to punish Serbia; a modified ultimatum might not have dissolved Habsburg fears; a different Russian order might have provoked another confrontation later. But if the outcome depended on multiple political branches, then neither the assassination nor the railway schedules were a sufficient cause on their own.

Historians also distinguish information available at the time from later knowledge. Vienna did not know Britain's final decision while drafting its ultimatum; British ministers did not possess full knowledge of German deployment routes early in July; military advisers could not perfectly predict how quickly opponents would mobilize. Later records make uncertainty look like negligence or deception in ways contemporary officials could not always recognize. That observation does not excuse reckless choices. It demands that a historian evaluate what a government knew, what it reasonably could have learned, and how it responded to warnings when the consequences were still in the future.

Section 108.19

Historical Debate: Responsibility and the Preventability of War

Responsibility for the outbreak of the war has been debated since August 1914. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles in 1919 framed Germany and its allies’ responsibility for loss and damage from the war, not an original July 1914 confession of sole intent to start it; the clause is examined in Chapter 113, and German governments responded with a sustained campaign to refute it. In the 1920s and 1930s American historians such as Sidney Fay argued that responsibility was widely shared. After 1945 the debate shifted again, and since then it has produced a rich scholarship that cannot be reduced to two camps. The Historical Debate below presents the major lines of interpretation.

The debate over responsibility has changed as archives opened and political contexts shifted. Fritz Fischer's influential work emphasized German imperial ambitions and deliberate acceptance of a major-war risk, challenging earlier German interpretations that treated the war as a tragic accident. Samuel Williamson assigned particular attention to Austro-Hungarian agency and the Habsburg leadership's desire to confront Serbia. Christopher Clark reconstructed the many crises, rival national projects and failures of judgment that preceded the war, warning against a simple narrative of one state's guilt. Annika Mombauer has stressed the continuing importance of the German and Austrian decisions while also weighing the wider diplomatic system. These positions are not interchangeable; authors disagree about intentionality, how long-term objectives relate to July choices, and how responsibility should be distributed.

The word responsibility itself contains more than one proposition. Legal guilt for violating a treaty is not identical to political responsibility for widening a crisis, and neither is identical to moral culpability for launching a war. A historian might find a strong documentary case that Vienna planned to use the assassination to force a confrontation, that Berlin encouraged this course while knowing the Russian risk, and that St. Petersburg later widened the military danger through general mobilization. Those findings are not contradictory. Their relative weight depends on which decisions are considered necessary to the escalation and on the available alternatives. Equating all choices for the sake of symmetry is no more rigorous than ignoring non-German decisions altogether.

Treaty wording, dated diplomatic correspondence, military orders, marginal notes and ministerial minutes permit closer argument than retrospective slogans. Yet documentary abundance does not eliminate selection bias. The governments' wartime colour books were intended to persuade neutral audiences, and memoirs written after defeat or revolution often defend their authors. The debate must therefore identify precisely what a source proves, and what remains inference. Article 231 of the later Versailles treaty framed responsibility for loss and damage, and its reception as a war-guilt clause belongs partly to the postwar controversy; it should not be quoted as an original July 1914 confession. A careful conclusion can assign asymmetric responsibility without pretending that every motive has been reconstructed beyond dispute.

Historical Debate

Attribution, Intention and Preventability in the July Crisis

Question: Which decisions mattered most, and how should responsibility be weighed without claiming that war was predetermined?

Fritz Fischer and the German policy debate.

Fischer’s scholarship placed German ambitions and readiness to risk a major war at the center, drawing attention to political and strategic objectives. The inference from later war aims to early July intentions remains a point requiring documentary caution.

Samuel R. Williamson and Austro-Hungarian agency.

Williamson emphasizes the Habsburg decision to punish and politically reduce Serbia, resisting accounts in which Vienna appears simply to follow Berlin. His work asks what officials in the Dual Monarchy intended and could still have chosen.

Christopher Clark and a multi-centered crisis.

Clark foregrounds the Balkan crisis, competing nationalist programs, and dangerous choices across capitals, without treating diplomacy as an automatic mechanism. A multi-actor narrative does not by itself require assigning equal shares of responsibility.

Annika Mombauer and documentary asymmetry.

Mombauer examines the evidence for German and Austro-Hungarian initiative alongside Russian, French and British decisions and the persistent war-guilt controversy. The key task is to separate documented choices from retrospective public justifications.

Research checks: Compare Williamson’s scholarly chapter, the Open University chronology, and Mombauer’s historiographical analysis. Distinguish original diplomatic messages from later historians’ arguments. Consider two real alternatives—moderating the Austrian ultimatum and halting military escalation during mediation—and explain why neither outcome can be proved in advance.

Section 108.20

From a Balkan Crisis to a Continental War: A Final Causal Synthesis

By August 6, when Austria-Hungary declared war on Russia, the structure of the conflict was set. Two groups of powers faced each other across a continent, with Britain and, soon, the empires and dominions beyond Europe on one side, and with the Ottoman Empire on the other by the end of October. The Balkan quarrel that began the crisis had become subordinate to a larger confrontation between Germany and Russia, and between Germany and France and Britain. The war's global character, which lay in the empires, trade networks, and naval strength of its participants, was already apparent from the first week, with colonial fighting in Africa beginning in August and Japanese entry on August 23.

The synthesis in the Cause and Effect box distinguishes causes from consequences. The long-term conditions examined in Chapter 107 made the crisis dangerous, but they did not determine the decisions of July. Austria-Hungary's resolution to confront Serbia, Germany's support, Russia's mobilization, Germany's strategic plan and the decision to execute it, and Britain's calculation of interest all depended on choices. The chapter therefore leaves students with a double task: to appreciate the pressures that narrowed the options, and to recognize that those pressures never removed the possibility of a different decision.

The chapter closes at the point at which armies began to move. Chapter 109 examines the campaigns on the western, eastern, Balkan, and Mediterranean fronts, beginning with the German offensive through Belgium, the French offensives in Lorraine, the Russian advance into East Prussia, and the Austro-Hungarian operations in Serbia and Galicia. The expectations of a short war that prevailed in early August would be overturned. Chapter 110 examines how the war transformed home fronts, and Chapter 111 the revolutions that followed. The decisions of July and early August 1914 thus opened a sequence of consequences that this Volume will follow to the end of the Second World War.

Consider a counterfactual test at each link. Without the assassination, the particular July confrontation might not have occurred, but the continental rivalry would have continued. Without Germany's early assurance, Vienna might still have sought satisfaction, but its willingness to risk Russian intervention would have been altered. Without the ultimatum's intrusive terms and short deadline, Serbian concessions might have enabled mediation; without Vienna's rejection, the declaration of war on July 28 might have been avoided. Without Russian general mobilization or Germany's decision to treat it as a casus for war, escalation could have taken another form. Without Germany's planned passage through Belgium, Britain's immediate decision might have been more difficult. These are analytical alternatives, not proofs that any single policy would have permanently prevented war.

The human and institutional costs of those choices became visible within days. Diplomatic missions closed, railways turned toward mobilization, governments secured emergency economic measures, and Belgian civilians faced invasion. All participating rulers claimed defensive necessity in some form, but a claim is not the same as an objective description of who crossed borders or rejected proposals. Military commanders then became increasingly influential over the conduct of the conflict, while the political purposes for which war was supposedly undertaken were not yet coherent or shared. Chapter 109 begins with those campaigns; Chapter 110 examines the prolonged transformation of economy and society. Chapter 108 ends at the threshold, with the explanation that decisions made under uncertainty produced a war larger than most of the leaders had publicly promised or privately controlled.

Cause and Effect

From Sarajevo to Continental War

Long-term causes

Alliance blocs, arms competition, imperial rivalry, Balkan nationalism, Austro-Hungarian anxiety about South Slav nationalism, Russian concern over the Balkans and the Straits, and German fear of Russian growth, as examined in Chapter 107.

Short-term causes

The assassination, the German assurances of July 5 to 6, the ultimatum of July 23, the Austrian declaration of war of July 28, Russian mobilization, and the refusal of key governments to accept mediation.

Event or transformation

Between July 28 and August 4 a quarrel between Austria-Hungary and Serbia became a war involving Germany, Russia, France, and Britain.

Immediate effects

Mobilization of millions of men, invasion of Belgium and Luxembourg, the opening of fronts in the west and east, the extension of war to colonies, and financial and political disruption in every belligerent society.

Long-term consequences

Four years of industrialized warfare, the collapse of empires, revolution in Russia, the settlements of 1919, and the instability that shaped the interwar period, examined in Chapters 109 to 116.

Historical complexity

No single cause is sufficient. The assassination, the alliances, the plans, and the decisions each mattered, and they mattered in combination. Students should resist both the claim that the war was inevitable and the claim that it was the work of one actor.

Why the pattern matters: The outbreak shows how deterrence and alliance can fail when governments mistrust each other's intentions, when military plans reduce time for diplomacy, and when leaders accept risks they believe others will not.

Decision alternatives grounded in the chronology: (1) Vienna could have treated Serbia’s qualified July 25 answer as a basis for internationally supervised negotiation rather than severing relations and declaring war. (2) Berlin could have made its early-July assurance explicitly conditional on mediation or later responded differently to the Russian mobilization decision. (3) Britain might have clarified its position earlier, though neither British intervention nor successful deterrence can be assumed. Students should distinguish feasible contemporary options from unsupported claims that an alternative was guaranteed to prevent war.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand on June 28, 1914 became a continental war within five weeks. Its central argument is that the war arose from decisions, not from the assassination alone and not from alliances operating as an automatic mechanism. The killing supplied an occasion, and governments determined what would be made of it.

The assassination was carried out by Young Bosnia conspirators, most of them Habsburg subjects, who had obtained weapons through Serbian networks that included military officers. The extent of Serbian state responsibility remains unproven, and the Austro-Hungarian investigation itself found no clear evidence of government complicity. Vienna nonetheless treated the Serbian state as responsible.

Germany's assurances of July 5 to 6 encouraged Austria-Hungary to act firmly. German leaders accepted the risk of a wider war, motivated by fear of growing Russian strength and by a hope that the conflict could be localized. Austria-Hungary delayed its ultimatum until July 23, shaped it to be hard to accept, and had decided on war if it was refused.

Serbia's reply of July 25 accepted or qualified most demands and rejected the participation of Austro-Hungarian officials in its inquiry. Vienna rejected the reply, broke relations, and declared war on July 28. The primary documents show that Serbia's response was more conciliatory than Vienna claimed, though its acceptances were often conditional.

Mediation failed because of incompatible aims, speed, and mixed signals from Berlin. Russia moved from partial to general mobilization on July 30, and Germany responded with ultimatums and declarations of war on Russia and France. Military plans constrained choices, but political leaders retained the power to alter them, as the episode of August 1 shows.

Germany's invasion of neutral Belgium widened the war. Britain, bound by no treaty of alliance, entered on August 4 after a divided Cabinet concluded that Belgian neutrality, honor toward France, and the balance of power required it. Italy and Romania remained neutral, and the Ottoman Empire signed a secret alliance with Germany but did not yet fight.

Societies experienced mobilization differently. Enthusiasm was real but not universal, and anxiety, resignation, and duty were also widespread. Harvests, families, finance, and minorities were affected in ways that varied by region and class. The belief that the war would be short was widely held and badly mistaken.

Historians continue to debate responsibility. Interpretations emphasizing German and Austrian initiative and those emphasizing a complex multipolar system both draw on the same evidence. Most scholars agree that Vienna and Berlin made decisive choices while recognizing that Russia, Serbia, France, and Britain also contributed.

The chapter thus connects the structural conditions of Chapter 107 to the military campaigns of Chapter 109. It shows how a regional confrontation became continental war, and it begins the Volume's examination of European catastrophe without implying that catastrophe was predetermined.

State policy and international legal status of nonparticipation in an armed conflict, with attendant duties and rights.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a continental order failed within weeks. The states that entered the war were not driven by a single force. They were governments making choices under pressure, with imperfect information, inherited plans, and domestic constraints. Understanding those choices helps explain why the war began, and why it began when and as it did.

The chapter also reveals continuities across Volume IV and Volume V. The nationalism, imperial rivalry, and alliance politics traced earlier met in a Balkan borderland where Habsburg, Serbian, Russian, and Ottoman interests overlapped. The same borderland would shape the Yugoslav wars examined in Chapter 135, though those later events were not predetermined by the events of 1914.

Finally, the chapter prepares students for the catastrophe of the following decade. The war that began in August 1914 would destroy four empires, transform European societies, and open a period of revolution and extremes. The decisions of July suggest that these outcomes were not inevitable. They were the consequences of choices whose full implications were not seen by those who made them, and that recognition, rather than hindsight certainty, is the proper starting point for Volume V.
