The End of Western Roman Imperial Rule

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In the autumn of the year 476, a Roman general of barbarian descent named Odoacer removed a teenage emperor named Romulus Augustulus from the imperial palace at Ravenna, sent the imperial regalia of the western court to Constantinople, and did not appoint a successor. No source written at the moment describes it as the day an empire ended. Within a generation, however, the chronicler Marcellinus Comes would retrospectively frame 476 as precisely such an ending, and much later historians made the year into a conventional boundary between ancient and medieval history.

Picture the city of Ravenna in the early 470s, not Rome itself, since the western imperial court had relocated there generations earlier for its marshes and its access to the Adriatic, both of which made the city easier to defend than the old capital. Picture a palace bureaucracy still issuing laws in the name of an Augustus, still dating documents by consular years, still receiving embassies, and still, in form, governing a Roman empire in the west. Picture, at the same time, a treasury that could no longer reliably collect taxes from Gaul, Spain, or Britain, an army increasingly composed of federate soldiers loyal to their own commanders rather than to a fiscal system that could barely pay them, and a court whose emperors rose and fell within months at the direction of whichever general controlled the household troops that year. This was the western Roman government in its final two decades, an institution that still looked, on parchment, like the empire of Augustus or Constantine, but that had lost, piece by piece, the material foundations that had once made imperial rule effective across an entire subcontinent.

Chapter 27 examined how migration, warfare, negotiated settlement, and the rise of increasingly autonomous regional and barbarian powers had already transformed much of the western empire by the middle of the fifth century. By 455, the year that closed that chapter, the general Aetius was dead, murdered by his own emperor Valentinian III, who was in turn murdered within months by Aetius's own retainers. Rome had been sacked a second time, this time by the Vandal king Geiseric, and North Africa, one of the empire's richest tax paying provinces, had already been lost to Vandal control for over a decade. This chapter picks up precisely where that account leaves off, and it asks a narrower and more specific question than Chapter 27's broad survey of migration and settlement: why, over the following twenty one years, did a functioning, independent western imperial government cease to exist at all, and what, exactly, ended when it did?

The answer developed across this chapter is neither a story of sudden catastrophe nor a story of foreign conquest overwhelming a healthy state. It is, instead, a story of interlocking decline: territorial loss reduced tax revenue, reduced revenue weakened the army, a weakened army left emperors dependent on powerful generals and federate forces whose loyalty had to be purchased or negotiated rather than commanded, and the resulting political instability consumed resources and attention that might otherwise have gone toward defense or recovery. Each stage reinforced the others. By the time Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, the office of western emperor had already been drained, over decades, of most of the territorial reach, fiscal capacity, and military independence that had once defined Roman imperial authority.

This chapter also insists, from its opening pages, on a caution that later sections will develop at length. The year 476 has become, in popular memory, a shorthand for "the fall of Rome," as though a single dramatic event ended a civilization overnight. Careful attention to the sources complicates this shorthand considerably. A rival western emperor, Julius Nepos, continued to be recognized in Dalmatia and by the eastern government for four more years after Romulus Augustulus was deposed. Roman senators continued to sit in Rome, Roman law continued to govern property and inheritance, Roman taxation, however diminished, continued to be collected in Odoacer's Italy, and Roman bishops continued to write letters, hold councils, and administer their sees under the new political conditions. Perhaps most importantly of all, the Roman Empire itself did not end in 476. An emperor still ruled from Constantinople, commanding a fiscally healthier and militarily more capable state that would endure, in continuous succession from Augustus, for nearly a thousand years more.

Understanding what actually happened in the west between 455 and 480, then, requires holding two ideas together that can seem, at first, to be in tension. A distinctively western Roman imperial government, headquartered in Italy and ruling a separate court from Constantinople's, did come to an end in this period, and its disappearance mattered enormously for the political history of the regions that would become medieval Europe. At the very same time, an enormous amount of Roman institutional, legal, religious, social, and cultural life continued, sometimes for generations, under the new rulers who inherited the west's fragmented territories, and the Roman state itself continued without interruption in the eastern Mediterranean. This chapter's task is to explain the political and fiscal collapse of western imperial government with precision, while refusing the tempting shorthand that treats 476 as the day Rome, in any complete sense, fell.

Chapter 29 will take up the question of what specifically replaced western imperial government in Italy, Gaul, Iberia, and Britain, examining the Ostrogothic, Visigothic, Frankish, and Anglo-Saxon kingdoms that emerged from this transformation. That chapter belongs to the successor kingdoms themselves. This chapter's responsibility is narrower and more immediate: to trace, with as much precision as the fragmentary fifth century evidence allows, how and why the machinery of a separate western Roman government stopped functioning, and to establish clearly what, in the process, actually ended and what did not.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political and military condition of the western Roman Empire immediately after the deaths of Aetius and Valentinian III in 454 and 455.
  2. Trace the rapid succession of western emperors between 455 and 476 and identify the political forces that shaped and limited their authority.
  3. Analyze the career of Ricimer and explain why a powerful military patrician could make and remove emperors without claiming the imperial title himself.
  4. Connect the loss of North African and other provincial tax revenue to the western government's declining military and administrative capacity.
  5. Evaluate Majorian's reign as a test case for whether western imperial recovery remained possible after 457.
  6. Describe how effective western imperial authority contracted geographically across Gaul, Iberia, Africa, and Britain during this period.
  7. Explain the relationship between the western court and the eastern imperial government at Constantinople, including the significance of Anthemius's appointment.
  8. Assess the causes and consequences of the failed Roman expedition against Vandal Africa in 468.
  9. Distinguish the deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476 from a complete end of Roman government, identifying what continued afterward and what did not.
  10. Explain why the Roman Empire itself did not end in 476, and how the eastern Roman government's continuation should reshape how students understand this period.
  11. Distinguish disputes about evidence, chronology, and interpretation surrounding the traditional date of 476 from questions that are genuinely well established.
  12. Compare the historical experience of political collapse for emperors, generals, senators, taxpayers, soldiers, and provincial populations.

Historical Context

Chapter 27 traced the arrival, settlement, and gradual political consolidation of Gothic, Vandal, Burgundian, Frankish, and other peoples within and along the frontiers of the western Roman Empire across the late fourth and early fifth centuries. It explained how the imperial government, unable to reverse these settlements by force alone, increasingly relied on negotiated arrangements that granted federate status, land, and a measure of self government to groups whose military cooperation it needed and whose expulsion it could not accomplish. It also traced the extraordinary career of the general Flavius Aetius, who dominated western imperial politics for roughly two decades, balanced rival barbarian powers against one another, and, in 451, helped assemble the coalition that checked Attila's Hunnic invasion of Gaul at the Catalaunian Plains.

That chapter closed with the destruction of the very stability Aetius had maintained. In September 454, the emperor Valentinian III, resentful of Aetius's power and suspicious of his ambitions, personally killed the general in the imperial palace. Six months later, in March 455, two of Aetius's retainers assassinated Valentinian in turn. Valentinian left no adult son, and his death ended the western branch of the Theodosian dynasty, whose family connections had supplied or underpinned the recognized western emperorship for much of the period since Honorius's accession in 395, despite interruptions and contested successions along the way, including the brief reign of Constantius III and the usurpation of Joannes in the 420s. The senator Petronius Maximus seized the throne within days, married Valentinian's widow against her wishes, and, within roughly ten weeks, was dead as well, killed by a mob as the Vandal king Geiseric's fleet approached Rome. Geiseric sacked the city in June 455, carrying off treasure, the empress and her daughters, and, according to later tradition, considerable further wealth, though the precise scale of the sack, like much else in this period, is known chiefly through sources written well after the events they describe.

By the summer of 455, then, the western empire had lost, within a single year, its dominant general, its last Theodosian emperor, its brief successor, and had suffered a second sack of its ancient capital. It had also already lost, over the preceding two decades, effective control of most of Roman North Africa to Geiseric's Vandal kingdom, a loss whose fiscal consequences this chapter will examine closely, since Africa had supplied a very substantial share of the western government's tax revenue and grain supply. Large portions of Gaul and Spain were, in practice if not always in formal law, governed by Visigothic, Suevic, and Burgundian kings who acknowledged nominal imperial authority in varying degrees while exercising real power on their own account. Roman Britain had been effectively outside imperial administration since the early fifth century.

This chapter's evidence base is considerably thinner and more fragmentary than the evidence available for earlier periods of Roman history. No continuous narrative history of the western empire survives from these decades comparable to the works of earlier historians describing the high empire. Historians rely instead on a patchwork of brief chronicles, most notably the Gallic Chronicles compiled in southern Gaul, on the fragmentary Greek language history of Priscus and the later fragments of Malchus of Philadelphia, both writing chiefly about eastern affairs but preserving valuable notices about the west, on the letters and poems of the Gallo-Roman aristocrat and bishop Sidonius Apollinaris, on scattered legal texts preserved in later compilations, on inscriptions and coinage, and on later, sometimes unreliable, historical traditions such as the sixth century account of Jordanes. Each of these sources has significant limitations. Chronicles are often terse to the point of ambiguity, aristocratic letters reflect the perspective and self interest of a narrow senatorial class, and later historians writing decades or centuries afterward sometimes imposed hindsight, moral judgment, or literary convention on events they had not witnessed. Historians must therefore reconstruct the sequence, causation, and significance of these decades with considerable care, distinguishing what the contemporary or near contemporary evidence actually supports from later narrative traditions that simplified, dramatized, or moralized a far messier reality.

Section 28.1

The Western Empire after 455

By the middle of 455, the western Roman Empire still existed as a formal political entity, still possessed an imperial court, a senate, a bureaucracy, provincial governors, and an army, and still claimed sovereignty over Italy, Gaul, Spain, and, in theory, Africa and Britain. What it no longer possessed was a stable framework of succession, a dominant general capable of balancing rival interests as Aetius had, or reliable access to the revenue and manpower of some of its wealthiest provinces. The murders of Aetius and Valentinian III within six months of one another had removed, simultaneously, the two figures around whom western politics had organized itself for over a decade, and no comparably durable arrangement replaced them for the rest of the chapter's chronology.

The sack of Rome by Geiseric's Vandal forces in June 455, following swiftly on Petronius Maximus's brief and violent reign, was significant less for its material damage, which later tradition may well have exaggerated, than for what it revealed about the western government's diminished capacity to defend its own symbolic capital. Rome by this period was not the seat of imperial government, which had long since moved to Ravenna and, at times, Milan, but it remained the city of the senate, of Saint Peter's basilica, and of enormous symbolic weight. That Geiseric could sail from Carthage, land near Rome, and depart with treasure, hostages, and, according to later sources, the empress and her daughters, with no effective Roman naval or military response, illustrated starkly how far the balance of power between Ravenna and Carthage had shifted since Geiseric had seized Africa in the 430s.

The western government's structural problem in 455, and throughout the following two decades, was not merely a shortage of capable individuals. It was that the office of emperor increasingly required backing that the office itself could no longer generate independently. An emperor needed loyal troops, and loyal troops increasingly meant federate contingents commanded by their own leaders or a professional field army whose generals held the real decision making power. An emperor needed revenue, and revenue increasingly depended on provinces that were partly or wholly outside effective imperial control. An emperor needed legitimacy, and legitimacy, in the absence of continuous dynastic succession, increasingly depended on recognition from powerful generals, from provincial aristocracies, or from the eastern imperial court at Constantinople. Every emperor examined in this chapter had to secure some combination of these three forms of backing, military, fiscal, and political, and none after 455 managed to secure all three simultaneously for very long.

Section 28.2

Emperors, Generals, and the Problem of Political Authority

The nine men who held or claimed the western imperial title between 455 and 480 should not be memorized chiefly as a list of names and dates. Their rapid succession is significant because it demonstrates, case by case, how narrowly each depended on a specific sponsor and how quickly that dependence could turn fatal once the sponsor's interests changed or a rival sponsor intervened.

Avitus, a Gallo-Roman senator and former praetorian prefect of Gaul, was proclaimed emperor in 455 with the backing of the Visigothic king Theoderic II, whose kingdom centered on Toulouse had become one of the most powerful political forces in Gaul. Avitus's dependence on Visigothic support, rather than on the traditional Italian based military establishment, alarmed the generals Ricimer and Majorian, who defeated his forces in 456 and forced his abdication. His fall illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout this chapter, in which an emperor's regional or barbarian backing, while sufficient to secure the throne, proved insufficient to hold it against rivals rooted in Italy's own military establishment.

Majorian, examined at greater length in Section 28.5, succeeded Avitus in 457 with Ricimer's backing and pursued the most ambitious program of military and administrative recovery attempted in this period, before his failure and deposition in 461 demonstrated the limits even a determined and militarily capable emperor faced. Libius Severus, who followed, reigned largely as Ricimer's instrument, commanding little independent authority and receiving no formal recognition from the eastern court, a gap that itself reveals how far the informal balance of power between west and east had shifted. Following Severus's death in 465, the western throne remained vacant for roughly a year and a half, an interregnum during which Ricimer effectively governed Italy directly, until the eastern emperor Leo I nominated Anthemius, examined in Section 28.7, to fill the western office with eastern backing in 467.

Anthemius's reign ended in violent conflict with Ricimer in 472, examined in Section 28.9, and the brief reigns of Olybrius, Glycerius, and Julius Nepos that followed continued the pattern of emperors whose authority depended almost entirely on whichever general, faction, or foreign court currently supported them. Romulus Augustulus, proclaimed in 475 by his father Orestes, a former secretary to Attila the Hun who had risen within the western military hierarchy, was never recognized by Constantinople and held the throne for less than a year before Odoacer's coup in 476. Taken together, this sequence shows an office that had become, in practical terms, a prize contested among military factions rather than a stable institution transmitting authority through dynastic succession or broad political consensus.

Section 28.3

Ricimer and Government without Stable Imperial Power

No individual dominates the political history of the western empire between 456 and 472 more than Ricimer, and his career illustrates with particular clarity the widening gap between holding military power and holding the imperial title itself. Ricimer was the son of a Suevic father and a Visigothic royal mother, a lineage that, whatever his personal military talent, made his own accession to the purple politically difficult in a state whose senatorial and popular culture, whatever its practical accommodations with barbarian generals, still expected an emperor to be, at minimum, plausibly Roman in a way contemporaries would accept.

Appointed patrician and magister militum, supreme commander of the western field army, Ricimer used this military office to depose Avitus in 456, to elevate and later depose Majorian in 457 and 461, to install and effectively control Libius Severus from 461 to 465, to govern directly during the subsequent interregnum, to accept Anthemius's appointment from Constantinople in 467, and finally to make war on that same Anthemius in 472. For roughly sixteen years, Ricimer was arguably the single most powerful figure in the western empire, and yet he never claimed the imperial title for himself.

Historians have offered several, not mutually exclusive, explanations for this restraint. Ricimer's barbarian royal ancestry likely made his elevation to emperor difficult to secure broad senatorial or popular acceptance, given the continuing, if increasingly fictional, expectation that an emperor embody Roman civic and religious legitimacy in ways a man of his background might struggle to claim convincingly. The office of patrician and supreme military commander, moreover, already granted him effective control over appointments, military strategy, and, through his ability to make and remove emperors, ultimate political authority, without exposing him to the specific vulnerabilities, dynastic expectations, and ceremonial burdens that came with the imperial title itself. In this sense, Ricimer's career demonstrates that by the 460s, real power in the western empire had become detachable from the imperial office, a separation with profound implications for how contemporaries, and later historians, should understand what "being emperor" actually meant by this period.

Section 28.4

The Fiscal Foundations of Imperial Collapse

A succession of weak or short lived emperors and an overmighty general cannot, by themselves, explain why the western Roman state as a whole ceased to function. Political instability was as much a symptom as a cause. Underlying it lay a fiscal crisis whose roots reached back decades before 455 and whose consequences shaped every political development examined in this chapter.

The Roman imperial state, in the west as in the east, financed its armies, its bureaucracy, its court, its public works, and its diplomatic subsidies chiefly through land and poll taxes collected from its provinces, supplemented by customs duties and other levies. The wealth of a province, and therefore its value to the central government, depended on its agricultural productivity, its population, and the effectiveness of its administrative and tax collecting apparatus. North Africa, encompassing the exceptionally fertile territories of modern Tunisia and eastern Algeria, had long been one of the western empire's most valuable provinces, supplying grain for the city of Rome's public distributions and substantial tax revenue for the central treasury.

Geiseric's Vandals had seized Carthage and much of Roman Africa by 439, and despite a formal treaty in 442 that nominally restored some African territory to imperial administration, effective Vandal control over the richest African provinces persisted and, in practice, expanded over the following decades. The loss of Africa's revenue struck the western fiscal system at a moment when it could least absorb the shock, since Gaul and Spain, though not entirely lost to imperial administration, were increasingly governed in practice by Visigothic, Suevic, and Burgundian kings whose payments to the central government, where they occurred at all, fell well short of full provincial taxation.

The consequences of this contracting revenue base cascaded through every other dimension of imperial governance examined in this chapter. A government that could not reliably pay its soldiers could not maintain a large, loyal, professional field army independent of federate contingents whose leaders expected land, subsidies, or a share of local revenue in exchange for their service. A government that could not pay federate leaders adequately risked their defection, rebellion, or simple refusal to campaign on its behalf. A government whose military strength depended on negotiated arrangements with semi independent commanders and kings found its emperors correspondingly dependent on those same commanders for their own political survival, precisely the dynamic that produced Ricimer's dominance. In this light, the rapid turnover of emperors examined in Section 28.2 should be understood not as an isolated political pathology but as the visible symptom of a deeper fiscal and military weakness whose most consequential root cause was the loss of African, and the erosion of Gallic and Spanish, tax revenue.

Section 28.5

Majorian and the Last Serious Western Restoration

If the western empire's collapse after 455 sometimes reads, in hindsight, as an inevitable and irreversible process, the reign of Majorian, emperor from 457 to 461, should complicate that impression considerably. Majorian was an experienced military officer who had served under Aetius, and upon becoming emperor with Ricimer's initial backing, he pursued a genuinely ambitious program of recovery rather than simply presiding passively over further decline.

Majorian campaigned personally in Gaul, restoring a degree of imperial authority over territory that had drifted toward Visigothic control, and he issued a series of legislative reforms addressing taxation, the condition of curial councils, the treatment of the poor, and abuses in provincial administration, suggesting an emperor genuinely engaged with the practical business of governing rather than merely surviving factional politics in Ravenna. His most ambitious undertaking was a planned invasion of Vandal Africa, assembling a substantial fleet, reportedly numbering several hundred vessels, on the southeastern coast of Hispania, in the province of Carthaginiensis, probably near the port of Ilici, in 460, with the explicit aim of recovering the province whose loss, as Section 28.4 explained, lay near the root of the western government's fiscal weakness.

The expedition never sailed. According to the near contemporary chronicler Hydatius, Vandal forces, aided by traitors within the Roman camp, destroyed or captured a substantial portion of Majorian's assembled fleet along the Hispanic coast before it could cross to Africa. The loss ended any realistic prospect of recovering Africa during Majorian's reign, and it appears to have fatally undermined his standing with Ricimer, who had backed his accession but whose interests were increasingly threatened by an emperor governing with genuine independent authority and ambition. In 461, Ricimer's forces intercepted Majorian, forced his abdication, and executed him within days.

Majorian's reign matters historically for reasons beyond its ultimately tragic outcome. It demonstrates that contemporaries in the late 450s did not regard western imperial recovery as impossible, that a capable and energetic emperor could still, for a time, reassert meaningful authority over Gaul and mobilize substantial military resources, and that the failure of the African expedition, rather than any inherent or predetermined weakness, closed off Majorian's own best opportunity, and one of the western empire's last major opportunities, to restore its fiscal and territorial base, a prospect the still larger combined expedition of 468, examined in Section 28.8, would attempt once more before that possibility closed for good. His fall at Ricimer's hands, moreover, established a precedent, repeated with subsequent emperors, that an emperor who threatened to govern too independently of the dominant general risked deposition regardless of competence or achievement, a pattern that further discouraged future emperors from pursuing similarly ambitious programs.

Section 28.6

Provincial Authority Fragments

To speak of the "fragmentation" of western imperial authority in this period requires precision about what fragmented, where, and to what degree, since the process was neither uniform nor complete across every region simultaneously. This section surveys that geographic unevenness briefly, reserving detailed treatment of the successor kingdoms themselves for Chapter 29.

Italy remained the region most directly and continuously connected to the imperial court throughout this period, since Ravenna itself lay within it and successive emperors, whatever their weakness elsewhere, generally retained some functioning administrative and military presence in the Italian peninsula until Odoacer's coup in 476. Gaul presented a more complex picture. The Visigothic kingdom centered on Toulouse expanded its effective territorial control considerably during these decades, particularly under Theoderic II and his successor Euric, who by the 470s governed most of southern and southwestern Gaul with little more than nominal deference to Ravenna. Roman military authority in northern Gaul persisted longer in a more attenuated form, associated with commanders such as Aegidius and later his son Syagrius, who maintained an independent Roman military presence in the region conventionally associated with Soissons, operating with little effective direction from Italy, though the precise extent and political organization of this territory rest on very sparse surviving evidence and remain debated among modern historians. Chapter 29 will examine further how this Roman military presence was eventually absorbed by the Frankish kingdoms.

Iberia saw imperial authority contract still further, with Suevic kingdoms established in the northwest and growing Visigothic influence extending from Gaul into much of the peninsula's interior by the century's end. Roman Africa, as Sections 28.4 and 28.8 have already emphasized, remained under Vandal control throughout this entire period, its recovery attempted and decisively failed in 460 and again, on a far larger scale, in 468. Britain had already drifted outside effective Roman administration since the early fifth century, a process Chapter 27 examined, and this chapter's narrative of the imperial court's final decades has essentially no bearing on developments there, since Britain had ceased to be governed from Ravenna long before 455.

This uneven geography matters for how students should understand "the end of the western empire." What ended in 476 was not Roman government across a uniformly administered territory stretching from Britain to Africa, since most of that territory had already passed, in practical administrative terms, beyond Ravenna's effective control well before Romulus Augustulus's deposition. What ended, more precisely, was a separate imperial court and office governing, chiefly, Italy itself, the last region where a distinctively western Roman central government still functioned in anything like its traditional form.

Section 28.7

Constantinople and the Western Throne

Throughout the period this chapter examines, the western imperial office existed alongside, and increasingly in a position of dependence upon, the eastern imperial court at Constantinople. This relationship reflected an important and easily misunderstood feature of the late Roman constitutional order: there remained, throughout this period, a single Roman Empire governed through a collegial emperorship, even though the empire had been administered by separate eastern and western courts, each headed by its own Augustus, since the late fourth century. Contemporaries did not think of "eastern" and "western" empires as two separate states in the way modern readers might assume, but as two administrative halves of one Roman imperial system, in which more than one Augustus could simultaneously hold legitimate imperial authority.

This shared constitutional framework meant that eastern recognition of a western emperor's legitimacy carried real political weight, and its absence could seriously undermine a claimant's standing. Libius Severus, as Section 28.2 noted, never received recognition from the eastern emperor Leo I, a diplomatic isolation that limited his effective authority throughout his reign. Following Severus's death and the resulting interregnum, Leo I took the more direct step of nominating Anthemius, an eastern aristocrat already married to Marcia Euphemia, daughter of the earlier eastern emperor Marcian, to the western throne in 467, sending him west with eastern military and naval support. Anthemius then worked to bind himself to the existing western military establishment, arranging in that same year for his own daughter, Alypia, to marry Ricimer, a union intended to secure cooperation between the new emperor and the general who had, for over a decade, made and unmade his predecessors.

Anthemius's appointment illustrates both the continuing constitutional unity of the Roman imperial idea and the practical reality that the western office had become, by this point, substantially dependent on eastern initiative to fill vacancies with candidates possessing sufficient legitimacy and resources. Such cross-imperial appointment was not unprecedented in Roman collegial government, since emperors had long designated colleagues and subordinate rulers across the empire's territories, as when Constantius II elevated Julian to the rank of Caesar in Gaul a century earlier. What made the arrangement of 467 significant was not the mere fact of collegial appointment but the degree to which the western court had, by this date, become unusually dependent on eastern military resources and eastern political initiative simply to fill its own imperial vacancy, a dependence with no real parallel in the fourth century. The arrangement did not, however, resolve the western court's underlying weaknesses, since Anthemius still depended on Ricimer's cooperation to govern effectively in Italy, and the conflict between the two men that ultimately destroyed Anthemius's reign in 472, examined in Section 28.9, demonstrated that eastern backing alone could not substitute for a stable domestic power base within the western military establishment.

Section 28.8

The Vandal Problem and the Failed Expedition of 468

If Majorian's failed African expedition of 460 represented the western empire's own best attempt at fiscal and territorial recovery, the far larger combined eastern and western expedition of 468 represented the Roman imperial system's last serious attempt, drawing on the resources of both courts, to reverse the loss of Africa before it became permanent. Its failure proved even more consequential than Majorian's.

By the mid 460s, Geiseric's Vandal kingdom controlled not only Carthage and the rich African provinces but also a substantial fleet capable of raiding Sicily, Sardinia, and the Italian coastline itself, a naval threat that alarmed both western and eastern courts. The eastern emperor Leo I, whose government commanded considerably greater fiscal resources than Ravenna's by this period, agreed to fund and organize a massive combined expedition against the Vandal kingdom, placing the fleet under the command of Basiliscus, a relative of the empress. Ancient sources report expenditures and fleet sizes so large, in some cases well over a thousand ships and enormous sums of gold, that modern historians treat the specific figures with considerable caution, while agreeing that the scale of the undertaking was exceptional and reflected the perceived stakes involved.

The expedition ended in disaster. According to the surviving accounts, Basiliscus's fleet anchored near Cape Bon, and Geiseric, requesting a brief truce ostensibly to negotiate, used the delay to prepare fire ships, which he then launched against the anchored Roman vessels, taking advantage of a favorable wind. The resulting destruction of much of the Roman fleet forced Basiliscus to withdraw, and the campaign, on which both courts had staked enormous resources and prestige, collapsed without achieving its objective.

The consequences reverberated through both halves of the empire. For the eastern court, the failure represented an enormous financial loss without a comparable existential threat to its own core territories, and Constantinople, though embarrassed, retained the fiscal capacity to absorb the setback and continue functioning as a strong state. For the western court, by contrast, the failure of 468 closed off, decisively and finally, any realistic prospect that African revenue might be recovered to underwrite a broader restoration of western fiscal and military strength. Combined with Majorian's earlier failure in 460, the disaster of 468 confirmed that the Vandal kingdom in Africa was, for all practical purposes, a permanent loss, and that the western government would have to continue functioning, for whatever time remained to it, without the province that had once been among its most valuable.

Section 28.9

Ricimer's Final Wars and the Breakdown of Central Government

The years following the failed African expedition of 468 witnessed the final and most destructive breakdown of political order within Italy itself, as tension between Anthemius and Ricimer, simmering for several years over questions of authority, patronage, and strategy, erupted into open civil war. The details are recorded unevenly across the fragmentary sources, but the broad sequence is reasonably clear. Ricimer, based in Milan, mobilized forces against Anthemius in Rome, and after a period of maneuvering and attempted mediation, including an intervention by Epiphanius, bishop of Pavia, who traveled between the two camps seeking reconciliation, Ricimer's forces besieged and, in 472, captured Rome itself, sacking parts of the city and killing Anthemius.

Ricimer installed the senator Olybrius as emperor in Anthemius's place, but Ricimer himself died within weeks of the city's fall, and Olybrius died of illness only months later, leaving the western throne vacant yet again. That the western empire's historic and symbolic capital, Rome, could be besieged and sacked not by an external barbarian enemy but by Roman imperial forces contesting internal political control illustrates, more starkly than any other single episode in this chapter, how thoroughly the resources, energy, and legitimacy of the western government had come to be consumed by internal conflict rather than directed toward external defense or provincial administration.

The years immediately following Ricimer's death saw further instability, as Gundobad, Ricimer's nephew and successor as the dominant military figure in Italy, installed Glycerius as emperor in 473 without securing eastern recognition, prompting Constantinople to dispatch its own candidate, Julius Nepos, who successfully displaced Glycerius in 474. This brief reassertion of eastern oversight, examined further in the next section, proved short lived, but it demonstrates that even at this late stage, the constitutional link between the two halves of the empire, and Constantinople's capacity to intervene in western succession disputes, had not entirely disappeared.

Section 28.10

Julius Nepos, Romulus Augustulus, and 476

Julius Nepos, installed with eastern backing in 474, did not long survive as an effective ruler of Italy. In 475, the general Orestes, another veteran of complex fifth century military politics who had once served as a secretary in Attila's court, rebelled against Nepos, drove him from Ravenna to Dalmatia, and proclaimed his own young son, Romulus, emperor in his place. Contemporaries and later sources sometimes referred to this final claimant with the diminutive "Augustulus," meaning "little Augustus," a label that reflected both his youth and, in retrospect, the diminished condition of the office he briefly held. Crucially, Constantinople never recognized Romulus Augustulus's claim, continuing instead to regard Julius Nepos, ruling from Dalmatia, as the legitimate western emperor.

Orestes's regime lasted less than a year. Much of the western field army in Italy by this period consisted of federate troops, many of Germanic background, who had long served the Roman state in exchange for pay, land, or supplies. In 476, these troops, led by a commander named Odoacer, demanded that Orestes grant them a substantial allotment of land in Italy itself, a demand consistent with patterns of federate settlement examined in Chapter 27 but one that Orestes refused. The troops rebelled, killed Orestes, and Odoacer, now their acknowledged leader, entered Ravenna and deposed the young Romulus Augustulus on or around 4 September 476.

What Odoacer did next has attracted more historical attention than almost any other single decision in this chapter. Rather than proclaiming himself, or anyone else, as a new western emperor, Odoacer arranged for a senatorial embassy to carry the imperial regalia, the diadem and purple robes traditionally associated with the office, to Constantinople. According to the fragmentary account of the Greek historian Malchus, this embassy told the eastern emperor Zeno that Italy no longer required a separate emperor of its own, that Zeno's authority alone would suffice for both halves of the empire, and that Zeno should grant Odoacer recognition and standing in Italy through the rank of patrician. Zeno's actual response remained deliberately ambiguous, since he continued at the same time to insist on the legitimacy of Julius Nepos, still alive and still recognized by Constantinople as western emperor from his base in Dalmatia, and he reportedly urged Odoacer to accept Nepos's authority, an instruction Odoacer did not, in practice, follow. The precise constitutional standing this left Odoacer with in Italy, whether a fully regularized patrician office or a more improvised and locally asserted authority never fully ratified by Constantinople, remains a genuine question among modern historians rather than a settled matter.

Students should resist treating this sequence of events as a single clean historical break. No surviving contemporary source describes 476 as the day the Roman Empire ended, and Julius Nepos continued to be recognized by Constantinople as the legitimate western emperor from his base in Dalmatia until his assassination in 480. After Nepos's death, no further western emperor was appointed, leaving the western imperial office vacant in a more complete sense. Even then, as the next section explains, an enormous amount of what had constituted "Roman government" in Italy continued to function under Odoacer's rule.

Section 28.11

What Continued after the Western Emperor Disappeared

The Authoritative Chapter Outline's binding instruction that this chapter emphasize institutional continuity reflects a substantial body of modern scholarship that has, over recent decades, pushed back against older narratives treating 476 as the abrupt end of Roman civilization in the west. That pushback rests on considerable evidence, and it deserves careful attention here.

The Roman Senate continued to meet in Rome under Odoacer and, subsequently, under the Ostrogothic king Theoderic, who established his own rule over Italy in the 490s and whom Chapter 29 examines in detail. Senators continued to hold traditional offices, including the consulship, whose holders continued to be recorded and used for dating documents according to established Roman convention, well into the sixth century. Roman law, codified and elaborated across centuries, continued to govern property, inheritance, contracts, and legal procedure in Italy under Odoacer's rule, a continuity that itself illustrates adaptation within a changed political framework rather than a wholesale replacement of Roman institutions, even as the precise legal arrangements governing Odoacer's own troops and their settlement on Italian land remain, in their finer details, a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion.

Municipal government, taxation, and administrative record keeping likewise persisted in recognizably Roman forms. Odoacer did not dismantle the praetorian prefecture or the tax collecting apparatus of Italy; he governed through it, adapting Roman fiscal administration to support his own regime and his troops' land settlements rather than sweeping it away. Bishops continued to exercise the religious and, increasingly, civic authority they had accumulated over the preceding century, corresponding with one another, holding councils, and administering church property largely undisturbed by the change in secular rulership. The city of Rome itself, though no longer an imperial residence, remained a major population center whose aristocracy, churches, and civic institutions persisted for generations.

None of this continuity should obscure the genuine and significant political change this chapter has traced. Italy in 480 was no longer governed by an independent western Roman emperor answerable, in constitutional theory, to no earthly superior, but by Odoacer, a military ruler whose authority was expressed through Roman institutions and whose relationship to the patrician rank and to Zeno remained constitutionally ambiguous, a rule that would itself be replaced within less than two decades by Theoderic's Ostrogothic kingdom. The office of western Augustus, distinct from its eastern counterpart, effectively disappeared. But the society over which that office had once presided did not, in a single stroke, cease to be Roman. Recognizing both facts simultaneously, real political rupture alongside substantial institutional and cultural continuity, is essential to understanding this period accurately.

Section 28.12

Rome Survives in the East

No account of the end of western imperial rule is complete, or historically honest, without emphasizing what this chapter's narrative has implied throughout but must now state directly: the Roman Empire did not end in 476. An emperor, Zeno, continued to reign in Constantinople, commanding a state whose administrative, military, and fiscal institutions remained considerably stronger and more resilient than those of the west had been for decades. The eastern government's tax base, drawing on the wealthy provinces of Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor, and the Balkans, had not suffered comparable territorial losses to those that had crippled western revenues, and Constantinople itself, protected by formidable land walls and a defensible position, had not experienced anything resembling the repeated sacks and sieges that western Rome had endured.

This eastern continuation was not merely the survival of a separate or successor state; contemporaries, and the eastern government itself, understood it as the continuation of the same Roman Empire that had once encompassed the entire Mediterranean world, now administered from a single surviving court rather than two. The inhabitants of this state continued to call themselves Romans, in Greek "Rhomaioi," a self identification that would persist for another thousand years, until the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453, examined in Part VI of this textbook. Modern historians commonly use the term "Byzantine Empire" to describe this state's medieval history, a scholarly convention developed centuries later, but this chapter's readers should understand clearly that this labeling reflects modern historiographical convenience rather than how the empire's own subjects understood their political identity.

Placing the eastern empire's continuation alongside the western collapse this chapter has traced yields an important corrective to popular shorthand. "The fall of Rome" describes, at most, the disappearance of a separate western imperial office governing Italy and whatever remained of its western territories, a genuinely significant political transformation whose causes and consequences this chapter has examined in detail. It does not describe the end of the Roman Empire as a political institution, the end of Roman law, the end of Roman administrative practice, or the end of a Roman imperial identity, all of which continued, in modified but recognizable form, for centuries afterward in the east and, in more attenuated ways, within the successor kingdoms of the former west that Chapter 29 now takes up.

Historical Vocabulary
Patrician and Magister Militum

By the mid fifth century, patricius, or patrician, no longer referred, as it had in the early republic, to membership in an ancient aristocratic class, but had become an elite honorific rank, generally granted by the emperor, frequently held by exceptionally powerful political or military figures. Magister militum, or master of the soldiers, was one of the highest late Roman military commands, though its jurisdiction and practical authority varied considerably by holder and period. Aetius, and later Ricimer, held both the patrician rank and the office of magister militum together, a combination that, in their particular cases, gave them effective control over military appointments, strategy, and, in practice, over which candidates could successfully claim or retain the imperial throne. Understanding this office helps explain how a general could exercise something close to sovereign power without holding the imperial title itself.

Historical Vocabulary
Imperial Legitimacy and Recognition

In the late Roman constitutional system, an emperor's authority rested on a combination of factors, including dynastic descent, military acclamation, senatorial acknowledgment, and, by this period, recognition from the reigning emperor of the other imperial court. Recognition refers specifically to this last element, the formal acknowledgment by one court that the ruler of the other court held legitimate imperial authority. As Section 28.7 explains, several western claimants in this chapter, including Libius Severus and, later, Romulus Augustulus, never received eastern recognition, a gap that weakened their standing even when they controlled Italy militarily, and that helps explain why Constantinople continued to regard Julius Nepos, rather than Romulus Augustulus, as the legitimate western emperor after 476.

Primary Source Window

Marcellinus Comes and Malchus of Philadelphia on the Events of 476

Creators: Marcellinus Comes, a chancery official writing a Latin chronicle in Constantinople in the early sixth century, and Malchus of Philadelphia, a Greek speaking historian writing in the eastern empire in the late fifth century, whose work survives only in fragments preserved by later compilers · Type: A retrospective eastern chronicle entry and a fragmentary near contemporary historical narrative

Context: Neither source was written at the exact moment of Romulus Augustulus's deposition. Marcellinus compiled his chronicle roughly half a century later, working in Constantinople and writing with the considerable benefit, and the interpretive hindsight, of knowing how the following decades had unfolded. Malchus, writing closer to the events themselves, drew on reports and official communications reaching the eastern court.

Paraphrase of Marcellinus Comes's entry Marcellinus records that Odoacer, whom he labels rex Gothorum, "king of the Goths," an ethnic-political label reflecting the chronicler's own retrospective framing rather than a precise description of Odoacer's mixed federate following, seized control of Rome, and he states directly that the western empire of the Roman people, which Octavian Augustus had begun to rule in the seven hundred and ninth year from the city's founding, perished with this Romulus, sometimes called Augustulus, so that Roman kings thereafter ruled Italy.
Paraphrase of Malchus's account Malchus reports that a senatorial embassy, acting under the new political settlement in Italy, traveled to the eastern emperor Zeno to state that a separate emperor was no longer necessary and to request that Zeno grant Odoacer the rank and authority of patrician, while Zeno's reply remained deliberately unresolved, since he continued at the same time to insist on the legitimacy of Julius Nepos, still recognized as western emperor in Dalmatia.

Analysis: These two near contemporary and early sixth century sources illustrate strikingly different registers. Malchus presents the episode largely as an unresolved diplomatic negotiation over titles and recognition, consistent with the ambiguity this chapter has traced throughout the 470s. Marcellinus, writing a generation or more later from Constantinople, constructs 476 explicitly as the moment the western empire "perished," a formulation that would prove enormously influential for later historical tradition even though it reflects retrospective judgment rather than how the event was necessarily understood at the time it occurred. Students should also note that Marcellinus's label for Odoacer, "king of the Goths," reflects the chronicler's own simplifying classification rather than a precise ethnic description, since Odoacer in fact led a mixed following of federate soldiers of varied background; historians must read such labels as evidence of how a source categorized events, not as settled fact about the people being described.

  1. How does Marcellinus's later, more definitive framing of 476 differ from Malchus's more ambiguous, closer to contemporary account?
  2. Why might Malchus, writing from an eastern perspective closer to the events, have framed the episode as an unresolved question of Zeno's authority rather than as the end of an empire?
  3. What are the limitations of relying on a chronicle compiled a generation or more after the events it describes, or on fragments of a history preserved only through later excerpting?
  4. How does comparing an early, ambiguous account with a later, more decisive one help historians distinguish contemporary perception from retrospective interpretation?
Historical Debate

Did the Roman Empire Fall in 476?

The question: Should 476, the year Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, be treated as the meaningful end point of the western Roman Empire, or does careful attention to the evidence require a more qualified answer?

Interpretation A — 476 remains a useful, if imperfect, marker: Many historians continue to treat 476 as a convenient and defensible conventional date, since it marks the last occasion on which a separate western imperial office was filled by a claimant based in Italy, even an unrecognized one, and no subsequent attempt was made to revive that specific office in the west. As a chronological boundary separating ancient from medieval history in survey treatments, it offers a clear, teachable marker grounded in a real institutional change.
Interpretation B — 476 obscures more than it reveals: Other historians argue that fixing on 476 distorts the actual historical process this chapter has traced. Julius Nepos, not Romulus Augustulus, was the eastern court's recognized western emperor until 480, making that year, if any single year must be chosen, arguably more defensible. More fundamentally, this interpretation holds that western imperial authority had already been effectively hollowed out for decades before 476 through the fiscal and military decline examined in Sections 28.4 through 28.9, so that Odoacer's coup registered, at the time, as a more ambiguous diplomatic negotiation than a decisive rupture, a judgment supported by Malchus's near contemporary account examined in the Primary Source Window above, even though the later chronicler Marcellinus Comes would go on to frame the same year in far more definitive terms.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of fragmentary evidence, including Malchus's near contemporary account and Marcellinus Comes's retrospective early sixth century chronicle, but assign different weight to the institutional change of 476 relative to the longer process that preceded it.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes whether "the fall of Rome" is understood as a single dramatic event or as the visible endpoint of a much longer, more gradual, and more uneven transformation, a distinction with implications for how students interpret every subsequent chapter's account of continuity between the ancient and medieval worlds.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter's chronology and primary sources, explain which interpretation you find more persuasive, and identify what kind of additional evidence, if it existed, might help resolve the disagreement more decisively.

Continuity and Change Over Time

What Ended and What Endured, 455 to 480

What Changed

  • A continuous dynastic succession of western emperors, already interrupted in 455, gave way to a series of short lived rulers dependent on military and, at times, eastern backing
  • North African tax revenue and grain supply, lost to Vandal control, was never recovered despite two major military attempts, in 460 and 468
  • Effective imperial administration in Gaul, Spain, and Britain contracted substantially as Visigothic, Suevic, and other regional powers assumed practical governance
  • After 476, no new separate western Augustus based in Italy was appointed, ending, for practical purposes, the distinct western imperial office

What Stayed the Same

  • The Roman Senate continued to meet in Rome, and the consulship continued to be held and used for dating documents, well into the sixth century
  • Roman law continued to govern the Roman population of Italy under Odoacer's rule
  • Municipal administration, taxation, and provincial administrative structures continued to function, adapted rather than abolished by the new rulership
  • The Roman Empire itself continued, without interruption, as a functioning state governed from Constantinople

Why the pattern matters: Holding both columns in view at once prevents two opposite errors, treating 476 as a sudden civilizational collapse, or dismissing it as historically insignificant simply because Roman institutions persisted afterward in modified form.

Cause and Effect

From Lost Revenue to Lost Government

Long-term causes

The gradual settlement of Gothic, Vandal, and other peoples within imperial territory, and the loss of effective fiscal control over Africa, Gaul, and Spain, examined in Chapter 27, steadily reduced the revenue base available to the western government.

Short-term causes

The deaths of Aetius and Valentinian III in 454 and 455 removed the political balance that had held the western court together, while the failed African expeditions of 460 and 468 destroyed remaining prospects for fiscal recovery.

Effect

Emperors became dependent on powerful generals such as Ricimer, on regional aristocracies, and on eastern recognition, producing a rapid, unstable succession that culminated in Odoacer's deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476.

This chapter's caution

No single cause, whether a battle, an invasion, or one emperor's failure, explains the outcome; fiscal, military, and political weaknesses reinforced one another over more than two decades, and substantial Roman institutions survived the political rupture of 476 itself.

Historical complexity: The eastern empire faced comparable external pressures during this same period, including its own costly failure at Cape Bon in 468, yet did not experience a comparable collapse of central government, a contrast that underscores how much the west's particular fiscal and territorial losses, rather than external pressure alone, explain its distinct outcome.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxpayers, Soldiers, and Town Councils in a Fragmenting State

The political drama surrounding emperors and generals examined throughout this chapter should not obscure how these developments were experienced by people who never appeared in an imperial court. Curial councils, the local aristocratic bodies responsible in many towns for assessing and collecting taxes, faced growing pressure across the fifth century as the central government's fiscal demands remained heavy even as its ability to provide military protection and administrative support diminished in many regions. Landowners in provinces increasingly governed by Visigothic, Suevic, or Vandal authorities had to negotiate new relationships with rulers who were, in some cases, more immediately present and more capable of enforcing order locally than a distant and financially strained imperial government had been.

Soldiers, particularly the federate troops who came to make up an increasing share of the western field army, experienced these decades chiefly through questions of pay, supply, and, as the crisis of 476 itself demonstrated, land settlement, since Orestes's refusal to grant Odoacer's troops the allotments they demanded directly precipitated the coup that ended Romulus Augustulus's brief reign. For ordinary town dwelling populations across Italy, Gaul, and Spain, the disappearance of a distant emperor in Ravenna in 476 may often have mattered less in immediate daily terms, especially in regions already outside effective Ravenna control, than more immediate and local questions: who collected taxes in a given district, who commanded the nearest garrison, which bishop administered charitable relief, and which regional power, Roman, Visigothic, Vandal, or Suevic, could actually guarantee order and provide protection from raiding or banditry. Recognizing this gap between the perspective of the imperial court and the perspective of ordinary provincial populations helps explain why the transition this chapter traces, while a major turning point in the history of political institutions, did not necessarily register, for most people living through it, as an abrupt or total rupture in daily life.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the final two decades of a separate western Roman imperial government, beginning with the deaths of Aetius and Valentinian III in 454 and 455, which destroyed the political stability that had, however imperfectly, held the western court together, and ending with the death of the last recognized western emperor, Julius Nepos, in 480. Between these dates, nine men held or claimed the western imperial title, most for only a few years or months, in a pattern of instability that reflected deeper structural weaknesses rather than mere personal failures of leadership.

Chief among those structural weaknesses was fiscal decline. The loss of North African tax revenue and grain supply to Geiseric's Vandal kingdom, compounded by the erosion of effective imperial control over much of Gaul and Spain, steadily reduced the resources available to sustain a professional army, a functioning bureaucracy, and an independent imperial court. Two major military efforts to reverse this loss, Majorian's expedition of 460 and the massive combined eastern and western expedition of 468, both ended in catastrophic failure, closing off any realistic prospect of fiscal recovery.

This fiscal weakness in turn produced political dependence. Emperors after 455 relied on powerful generals, most notably Ricimer, whose command of the field army allowed him to make and depose emperors for sixteen years without claiming the imperial title himself, a separation of real power from formal office that illustrates how thoroughly the traditional structure of imperial authority had been transformed. Emperors also increasingly depended on recognition and, at times, direct appointment from the eastern imperial court at Constantinople, a dependence that culminated in Anthemius's installation in 467 and that continued to shape events through Julius Nepos's appointment in 474 and his continuing recognition even after his expulsion from Italy in 475.

The chapter examined the geographic unevenness of this fragmentation, noting that effective imperial authority had already contracted substantially in Gaul, Spain, and Britain well before 476, so that what ended in that year was, more precisely, a separate imperial office governing chiefly Italy rather than a uniformly administered empire stretching across the west. It examined in detail the events of 475 and 476, in which Orestes's proclamation of his son Romulus Augustulus, followed by Odoacer's rebellion and deposition of that young emperor, brought the office of western Augustus to what would prove to be its final occupant, while emphasizing that Julius Nepos's continuing recognized claim from Dalmatia complicates any simple reading of 476 as a clean historical break.

At the same time, this chapter has insisted throughout on institutional continuity alongside political rupture. The Roman Senate, Roman law, municipal administration, taxation, and ecclesiastical institutions continued to function in Italy under Odoacer's rule, adapted to a new political framework rather than swept away by it. Most importantly, the Roman Empire itself did not end in 476, since the eastern imperial government at Constantinople continued, uninterrupted, as a powerful and fiscally healthy state that would endure for nearly a thousand years more, a survival that fundamentally qualifies any claim that "Rome fell" in any complete or total sense during this period.

The Historical Debate feature examined directly whether 476 should be treated as a meaningful turning point or as a somewhat arbitrary marker imposed on a much longer and more gradual process, weighing the date's usefulness as a conventional teaching boundary against the evidence that the western government had already been substantially hollowed out for decades and that the near contemporary account of Malchus treated the year as an ambiguous diplomatic negotiation rather than with the decisive significance the later chronicler Marcellinus Comes would assign it.

These themes, fiscal decline driving political instability, the separation of real military power from formal imperial office, the uneven geography of provincial fragmentation, the complicated and contested significance of 476 itself, and the essential distinction between the end of a separate western imperial court and the survival of the Roman Empire in the east, will recur throughout this textbook's subsequent treatment of the post-Roman kingdoms, Byzantine history, and the long process by which medieval Europe emerged from the Roman world. Chapter 29 now turns to the successor kingdoms themselves, examining how the Ostrogoths, Visigoths, Anglo-Saxons, and Franks actually governed the territories whose Roman imperial administration this chapter has traced to its final, uneven end.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Patrician (late Roman)
An elite late Roman honorific rank frequently granted to exceptionally powerful political or military figures; Aetius and Ricimer both held the rank while exercising enormous influence over western government.
Magister militum
One of the highest late Roman military commands, literally "master of the soldiers"; the jurisdiction and authority attached to the office varied by holder and period, and some holders, including Aetius and Ricimer, accumulated extraordinary political influence beyond their formal military remit.
Federate
A people or military unit bound to Roman service by treaty, receiving pay, land, or supplies in exchange for military cooperation, a status held by many troops in Italy under Orestes and Odoacer.
Comitatensis
A term describing troops of the late Roman field army, the mobile forces attached to the imperial court and its generals rather than stationed permanently on the frontier, the core military establishment that generals such as Aetius, Ricimer, and Orestes commanded in this period.
Annona
The Roman system of grain and supply requisition and distribution, historically dependent heavily on North African production before the Vandal conquest disrupted it.
Fiscus
The imperial treasury and its associated system of revenue collection, whose shrinking capacity across this chapter's period underlay the western government's growing political instability.
Theodosian dynasty
The imperial family descending from Theodosius I, whose western line ended with the assassination of Valentinian III in 455.
Legitimacy
The quality of an emperor's authority being widely accepted as rightful, resting in this period on dynastic descent, military support, senatorial acknowledgment, and recognition from the other imperial court.
Recognition
The formal acknowledgment by one imperial court that the ruler of the other court held legitimate authority, absent for several western claimants examined in this chapter.
Consular dating
The Roman practice of naming years after the reigning consuls, a practice that continued in Italy well after 476, illustrating institutional continuity.
Praetorian prefecture
A major late Roman administrative division responsible for civil governance and taxation across a large territorial unit, whose apparatus in Italy continued functioning, adapted, under Odoacer.
Senatorial aristocracy
The wealthy landowning elite of Rome and Italy, who continued to hold office, property, and social standing before and after 476.
Curial class
Local town councillors responsible for assessing and collecting taxes, whose burdens grew heavier across the fifth century as central fiscal demands persisted amid administrative strain.
Vandal kingdom
The kingdom established by Geiseric in North Africa from the 430s onward, whose control of Carthage and the surrounding provinces deprived the western Roman government of essential tax revenue and grain.
Suevic kingdom
A kingdom established by the Suevi in northwestern Iberia, one of several regional powers that reduced effective imperial control over Spain in this period.
Visigothic kingdom
The kingdom centered on Toulouse, expanding under Theoderic II and Euric to control most of southern Gaul and increasing territory in Spain by the century's end.
Dalmatia
The Adriatic coastal region where Julius Nepos continued to rule and be recognized as legitimate western emperor after his expulsion from Italy in 475, until his death in 480.
Regalia
The symbolic imperial insignia, including the diadem and purple robes, which Odoacer sent to Constantinople in 476 as part of his message that Italy no longer required a separate western emperor.
Interregnum
A period without a reigning emperor, such as the eighteen months between the death of Libius Severus in 465 and Anthemius's installation in 467.
Deposition
The forced removal of a ruler from office, a fate suffered by several emperors examined in this chapter, most at Ricimer's or Odoacer's hands.
Institutional continuity
The persistence of established practices, laws, or organizations across a period of political change, illustrated in this chapter by the survival of the Roman Senate, law, and administration after 476.
Periodization
The scholarly practice of dividing history into named eras or turning points, examined critically in this chapter's treatment of 476 as a conventional but imperfect boundary.
Historiography
The study of how historians have interpreted and written about the past, relevant here to how later traditions dramatized 476 far more than near contemporary sources did.
Marcellinus Comes
A chancery official who compiled a Latin chronicle in Constantinople in the early sixth century, whose retrospective statement that the western empire "perished" with Romulus Augustulus in 476 shaped much later historical tradition.
Late antiquity
A modern scholarly period label describing the transitional centuries between the classical Roman world and the early medieval period, encompassing the developments examined in this chapter.

Identification & Significance

Aetius and Valentinian III
The dominant general and the last Theodosian emperor, whose mutual killing in 454 and 455 opened the unstable period this chapter examines.
Ricimer
The patrician and magister militum who made and deposed several western emperors between 456 and 472, illustrating the separation of real power from the imperial title.

Emperor from 457 to 461 whose serious attempt at recovery, including the failed African expedition of 460, demonstrates that western collapse was not inevitable at that stage.
Geiseric and the Vandal kingdom
The king and kingdom whose control of North Africa deprived the western government of essential revenue and whose naval power defeated two major Roman expeditions, in 460 and 468.
Anthemius
Emperor installed with eastern backing in 467, whose reign ended in civil war with Ricimer in 472, illustrating the western court's dependence on Constantinople.
Orestes and Romulus Augustulus
The general and his young son whose brief, unrecognized regime ended with Odoacer's coup on or around 4 September 476.
Odoacer
The federate military leader who deposed Romulus Augustulus, sent the imperial regalia to Constantinople, and governed Italy afterward through a regime that sought Roman patrician legitimacy, its precise constitutional status remaining disputed among historians.
Julius Nepos
The eastern court's recognized legitimate western emperor from 474 until his death in 480, ruling from Dalmatia after his expulsion from Italy, whose continuing claim complicates the significance of 476.
Zeno and Constantinople
The eastern emperor and imperial capital whose continuing, fiscally healthy government demonstrates that the Roman Empire itself survived the political rupture examined in this chapter.
Sidonius Apollinaris
A Gallo-Roman aristocrat and bishop whose surviving letters provide valuable evidence for elite social and political life in this period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has closed Part IV's examination of late antique transformation by tracing, with as much precision as the fragmentary evidence allows, why a functioning, independent western Roman imperial government ceased to exist by the final decades of the fifth century. The answer developed here resists both of the simplest available narratives, that a sudden barbarian conquest destroyed a healthy empire, and that nothing significant really changed at all. Instead, this chapter has shown a state weakened gradually by the interaction of lost revenue, military dependence, and political instability, each reinforcing the others across more than two decades, until the office of a separate western Augustus simply ceased to be filled.

At the same time, this chapter has insisted that the disappearance of that office did not mean the disappearance of Roman society, Roman law, Roman administration, or Roman identity in Italy, nor did it mean the end of the Roman Empire itself, which continued to govern a substantial and fiscally healthy state from Constantinople for centuries afterward. Holding these two truths together, genuine political rupture in the west alongside substantial institutional continuity and the empire's continuing survival in the east, is essential preparation for the chapters that follow.

This chapter also matters for the larger architecture of this Volume and this textbook. It closes Part IV's account of Late Antiquity's transformation of the Roman world, a transformation that began with the religious changes of Chapter 23 and the political crises of Chapter 24, continued through Constantine's reforms in Chapter 25 and the doctrinal and social developments of Chapter 26, and reached its migratory and military climax in Chapter 27. Chapter 28 has now shown how that long transformation culminated, in the specific case of the western imperial office, in institutional dissolution rather than institutional survival, a distinct outcome from the eastern empire's experience of the same broad pressures.

Chapter 29 now turns to the successor kingdoms that inherited the political landscape this chapter has described, examining how Ostrogothic Italy, Visigothic Iberia, Anglo-Saxon Britain, and Frankish Gaul actually governed the territories whose Roman imperial administration has now, in this chapter's narrative, reached its final and uneven end. Understanding both what collapsed and what endured in the period this chapter has examined is the essential foundation for understanding how those new kingdoms built, adapted, and departed from the Roman inheritance they received.
