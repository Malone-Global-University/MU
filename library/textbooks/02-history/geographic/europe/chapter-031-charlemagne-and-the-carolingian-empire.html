Charlemagne and the Carolingian Empire

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On Christmas Day in the year 800, in the old basilica of Saint Peter in Rome, a Frankish king who had spent most of his adult life leading armies through Saxon forests, Lombard hill towns, and Pyrenean passes knelt at prayer and rose, according to one influential later account, to find a crown placed upon his head and a Roman crowd acclaiming him emperor. Whatever actually happened in that church on that morning, and the sources disagree about it in instructive ways, the event compressed into a single ceremony a project that had been building for more than three decades: the transformation of a Frankish kingdom into something its own participants struggled to name precisely, a renewed western empire, Christian in purpose, Roman in aspiration, and Frankish in its actual machinery of government.

Chapter 30 traced the growing concentration of political and military power in Pippinid hands within a still Merovingian political order, culminating in Pepin III's assumption of kingship in 751 and the securely documented papal anointing of 754. That chapter closed with the new Carolingian dynasty established, bound to papal favor, and drawn into Italian affairs on the papacy's behalf, well before Pepin's death in 768 and the succession of his two sons, Charlemagne and Carloman. This chapter picks up that story and asks a different kind of question. It is not primarily a biography of Charlemagne, although his long reign, from 768 until his death in 814, necessarily supplies its chronological spine. It is instead an inquiry into how a Frankish royal house converted an already considerable inheritance into something new: an expansive Christian empire stretching from the Pyrenees to the Elbe and from the North Sea to central Italy, governed not through a modern bureaucracy but through a dense, improvised, and only partially successful combination of conquest, personal loyalty, written instruction, ecclesiastical reform, and renewed imperial title.

Consider three well documented circumstances that together suggest the scale of what this chapter must explain. Frankish sources record that in 772 Frankish forces destroyed a Saxon sacred site called the Irminsul, an act of calculated religious violence that opened decades of campaigning intended to compel conversion to Christianity through force as much as persuasion. Surviving manuscripts from Frankish monastic scriptoria, meanwhile, document the copying and correction of texts of Roman law, scripture, and patristic theology into a newly standardized and remarkably legible script that scholars today call Carolingian minuscule, part of an equally deliberate program to correct and preserve the textual foundations of Christian learning. And Carolingian capitularies and annals record that royal assemblies, gathering counts, bishops, and abbots at Aachen and other royal centers, regularly heard written royal instructions read aloud and renewed oaths of fidelity to a king who could not possibly supervise every corner of his enormous and diverse territories in person. Conquest, religious coercion, textual reform, and personal government by written instruction were not separate stories. They were different faces of a single historical project, and this chapter's task is to explain how they fit together.

This chapter also insists, from its opening pages, on a caution that will recur throughout: the Carolingian achievement was real, but it was neither inevitable nor complete. Charlemagne did not create a nation, did not invent Europe, and did not restore the Roman Empire in any simple sense, since a Roman emperor already reigned in Constantinople throughout Charlemagne's entire life, as Chapter 36 will examine further. What Charlemagne and his advisers did accomplish was the construction of an enormous, religiously charged, and genuinely ambitious political order built substantially on Frankish aristocratic cooperation, ecclesiastical partnership, and the personal authority of a single ruler, an order whose internal tensions and practical limits would become sharply visible within a generation of his death, a subject reserved for Chapter 32.

Understanding this project requires attention to several different kinds of historical evidence, each with its own strengths and limitations. Royal biography, most famously Einhard's Life of Charlemagne, written by a courtier who knew the emperor personally, offers vivid detail but also imitates classical Roman biographical conventions and was composed to praise its subject, sometimes years after the events it describes. Annals kept year by year in Frankish monasteries record campaigns, deaths, and important events with more chronological reliability but far less narrative interpretation. Capitularies, the written instructions Charlemagne issued to his officials, reveal the ambitions and anxieties of Carolingian government even when they cannot tell us how consistently those instructions were obeyed. Charters, letters, poetry, and manuscripts produced at Carolingian monasteries and at the royal court supply further evidence for administration, learning, and religious reform. Archaeology, including excavations at Aachen and other Carolingian sites, supplements and sometimes corrects the written record. This chapter draws on all of these kinds of evidence while remaining attentive to what each can and cannot establish.

Finally, this chapter situates the Carolingian project within the wider narrative of Part V, the formation of Latin Christendom. Chapter 29 examined the post Roman kingdoms that adapted Roman institutions to new political circumstances, and Chapter 30 traced how the Frankish kingdom in particular developed the aristocratic networks and church relationships that made the Carolingian dynasty possible. This chapter shows how the Carolingians took that Frankish inheritance and built upon it a much larger and more ideologically ambitious structure, one that fused Frankish kingship, Roman imperial memory, and a program of Christian reform into a single political vision. Chapter 32 will then examine why that structure could not be sustained intact, as division among Charlemagne's descendants, renewed Scandinavian raiding and, later in the ninth century, Magyar incursions, and the practical limits of Carolingian government combined to fragment the empire within decades of its founder's death.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Charlemagne's kingship built upon, rather than replaced, the Frankish political inheritance described in Chapter 30.
  2. Trace the major military campaigns of Charlemagne's reign, including the conquest of the Lombard kingdom, the Saxon wars, the Bavarian annexation, the Avar campaigns, and the Iberian frontier.
  3. Analyze the coercive dimensions of Carolingian expansion, particularly the forced Christianization of the Saxons, alongside its religious and cultural achievements.
  4. Describe the instruments of Carolingian government, including counts, capitularies, royal assemblies, and the missi dominici, and assess their effectiveness.
  5. Explain the Carolingian program of religious and moral reform, generally known by the Latin term correctio, and its relationship to royal authority.
  6. Evaluate the achievements and limits of the movement historians call the Carolingian Renaissance, distinguishing elite ecclesiastical reform from mass educational change.
  7. Analyze the circumstances and meaning of the imperial coronation of 800, including the disagreements among contemporary sources.
  8. Assess what a renewed western imperial title meant in relation to the continuing Roman Empire centered on Constantinople.
  9. Describe the court and palace culture of Aachen and its role within a still largely itinerant system of Carolingian kingship.
  10. Distinguish the ideological claims of Carolingian empire from the practical, uneven, and often personally mediated authority it actually exercised across its territories.
  11. Identify the kinds of evidence historians use to reconstruct Carolingian history and evaluate their respective strengths and limitations.
  12. Connect the achievements and unresolved tensions of Charlemagne's reign to the fragmentation examined in Chapter 32.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Charlemagne convert the Frankish kingdom he inherited in 768 into a much larger and more ambitious political order by 814?
  2. Why did conquest, forced conversion, religious reform, and educational renewal all belong to a single Carolingian political project rather than separate and unconnected undertakings?
  3. What tools did Carolingian government actually possess for overseeing a realm of such enormous size, and how effective were they in practice?
  4. Why does the imperial coronation of 800 remain a subject of historical debate rather than a simply narrated event?
  5. What did contemporaries mean by a renewed western empire, and how did that meaning relate to the continuing existence of the Roman Empire in Constantinople?
  6. In what ways did the Carolingian Renaissance represent a genuine cultural and religious achievement, and in what ways should its scope be understood as limited?
  7. How did different populations, Frankish aristocrats, conquered Saxons, Italian bishops, and monastic scholars among them, experience the Carolingian project differently?
  8. Why is it historically inaccurate to describe Charlemagne as having created Europe or as having simply restored the Roman Empire?

Historical Context

Chapter 30 explained how, within a Frankish political order that remained formally Merovingian, the Pippinid mayors of the palace steadily concentrated military command, landed wealth, and aristocratic and ecclesiastical alliances in their own hands, above all under Charles Martel, grandfather of Charlemagne, who built Pippinid dominance through repeated campaigns against rival Frankish nobles and neighboring peoples. His son Pepin III converted that accumulated power into royal title in 751, when Frankish nobles and churchmen set aside the last reigning Merovingian king, Childeric III, and elevated Pepin to the throne in his place. Whether an anointing accompanied that 751 accession remains a debated point among historians, since the tradition connecting it to the missionary bishop Boniface rests on later and uncertain evidence. What is securely documented is that in 754, Pope Stephen II traveled north across the Alps and personally anointed Pepin along with his two sons, Charlemagne and Carloman, an act that bound the new Carolingian dynasty to papal favor and, in the years that followed, drew Frankish military power into Italian affairs on the pope's behalf against the Lombard kingdom.

By the time Pepin III died in 768, the Frankish kingdom he passed to his sons already possessed several features essential to what would follow. It commanded substantial military manpower drawn from a landed aristocracy bound to the Carolingian house through grants of land, church benefices, and personal loyalty. It maintained a close, if not always comfortable, relationship with the papacy, one that had already produced Frankish military intervention against the Lombards in Italy during the 750s. It possessed an itinerant style of kingship, in which the king and his household moved seasonally among royal estates, monasteries, and fortified centers rather than governing from a single fixed capital. And it depended heavily on counts, local aristocrats invested with royal authority over defined territories, to exercise justice, raise troops, and collect dues in the king's name, a system inherited in modified form from late Roman and earlier Frankish practice.

Following Frankish custom, Pepin divided his kingdom between his two surviving sons rather than passing it undivided to a single heir. Charlemagne received the outer, largely northern and western portions of the realm, while Carloman received a central block of territory including much of Burgundy, Alsace, and Provence. The two brothers governed uneasily side by side for three years, their relationship strained by disputes over policy toward the Lombards and by their mother Bertrada's efforts to mediate between them. Carloman's sudden death in December 771 resolved the tension abruptly. Frankish nobles in Carloman's territories transferred their allegiance to Charlemagne rather than to Carloman's young sons, who disappear from the historical record, likely taking refuge with their mother at the Lombard court, a detail that would soon matter considerably for Charlemagne's dealings with the Lombard kingdom. From late 771 onward, Charlemagne ruled the entire Frankish realm as sole king, the platform from which the expansion and reorganization examined in this chapter proceeded.

It is essential to understand what kind of political structure Charlemagne inherited and what kind he did not. He did not inherit anything resembling a modern centralized state with standing bureaucratic institutions, a professional civil service, or uniform law codes enforced from a single capital. He inherited instead a network of personal relationships, an aristocracy whose cooperation had to be continually renewed through patronage, marriage alliance, and shared military success, a church whose bishops and abbots controlled substantial landed wealth and increasingly served as royal administrators as well as spiritual leaders, and a royal household capable of extraordinary military mobilization but limited in its capacity for sustained day to day oversight of distant territories. Everything examined in this chapter, conquest, governance, religious reform, and imperial ideology, must be understood against this background of real but bounded royal capacity.

Section 31.1

From Pepin III to Charlemagne

The events that placed the Carolingian dynasty on the Frankish throne belong to Chapter 30 and are not retold here in detail. It is enough to recall that in 751, with the support of Frankish nobles and, according to later Frankish sources, with some degree of papal sanction as well, Pepin III deposed Childeric III, the last reigning Merovingian king, and assumed the Frankish throne in his place. Whether an anointing accompanied that 751 accession remains debated among historians, since the traditional account linking it to the missionary bishop Boniface rests on later and uncertain testimony. Three years later, in 754, Pope Stephen II crossed the Alps in person, an extraordinary journey for an eighth century pope, and securely anointed Pepin along with his young sons Charlemagne and Carloman at the monastery of Saint Denis. That anointing bound the new dynasty's legitimacy to ecclesiastical sanction in a way the Merovingians had never required, and it committed Pepin, in exchange, to military support for the papacy against the Lombard kingdom then pressing on papal territories in central Italy.

Pepin honored that commitment with campaigns into Italy in 755 and 756, forcing the Lombard king Aistulf to cede territory that Pepin then granted to the papacy, a transfer of land later known as the Donation of Pepin and foundational to the later development of what historians call the Papal States. These campaigns established a pattern that Charlemagne would repeat on a much larger scale two decades later: Frankish military intervention in Italy on the papacy's behalf, followed by territorial settlement that reshaped the political map of the peninsula. When Pepin died in 768, he left his sons not only a kingdom but a set of ongoing commitments, a functioning alliance with Rome, and continuing tension with the Lombard kingdom to their south.

Frankish succession custom, unlike the primogeniture that would later characterize many medieval European monarchies, generally divided a king's territories among his surviving sons rather than concentrating the inheritance in a single heir. Pepin's kingdom was accordingly divided between Charlemagne and Carloman in 768, with Charlemagne receiving an outer arc of territory including Austrasia, Neustria, and parts of Aquitaine, while Carloman received a central block encompassing Burgundy, Provence, Alsace, and Alemannia. The division was not tidy, and the two brothers' territories interlocked in ways that made cooperation, or at least mutual tolerance, essential to effective government. That cooperation proved elusive.

Section 31.2

Charlemagne and the Consolidation of Carolingian Kingship

The three years of joint rule between 768 and 771 were marked by friction rather than partnership. Charlemagne and Carloman disagreed sharply over policy toward the Lombard kingdom, and their mother Bertrada attempted to broker a marriage alliance between one of her sons and a daughter of the Lombard king Desiderius, an alliance intended to stabilize relations between the Franks and the Lombards but one that alarmed the papacy, which viewed any Frankish Lombard rapprochement with suspicion given the papacy's ongoing territorial disputes with Lombard rulers. Einhard's later biography attributes this marriage to Charlemagne, and his repudiation of it within roughly a year to Charlemagne as well, though Einhard is the earliest surviving source to assign the marriage this way, and some modern historians have raised the possibility that Carloman, rather than Charlemagne, was the brother actually married to the Lombard princess. The episode, however it is finally resolved, further strained relations between the two brothers in the years before Carloman's death.

Carloman's sudden death in December 771 resolved the standoff decisively and, from Charlemagne's perspective, conveniently. Rather than accepting Carloman's young sons as rulers of their father's portion of the kingdom, the Frankish nobles and bishops of Carloman's territories transferred their allegiance to Charlemagne. Carloman's widow, Gerberga, fled with her sons to the Lombard court of Desiderius, seeking protection and, implicitly, Lombard support for her sons' claims, a decision that would shortly give Charlemagne a further pretext for war against the Lombards, examined in the next section. From late 771 onward, Charlemagne ruled the entire Frankish kingdom without a co ruler, commanding the aristocratic networks, ecclesiastical relationships, and military resources of both halves of his father's former realm.

This consolidation of sole rule should not be mistaken for the creation of a centralized administrative apparatus. What changed in 771 was the removal of a rival center of royal authority within the Frankish world, not the invention of new institutions of government. Charlemagne continued to rely, as his father and grandfather had, on the loyalty of counts and bishops distributed across his territories, on the itinerant movement of the royal household among estates and monasteries capable of supplying and hosting it, and on periodic assemblies at which the king's leading men gathered to hear his decisions, offer counsel, and renew their oaths of fidelity. What did change, gradually and cumulatively over the following four decades, was the scale of territory these institutions had to serve and the ambition of the political and religious program Charlemagne and his advisers sought to impose upon it.

Section 31.3

The Conquest of the Lombard Kingdom

Relations between the Franks and the Lombard kingdom deteriorated sharply after Charlemagne's repudiation of his Lombard wife and his sole assumption of the Frankish throne. Pope Hadrian I, elected in 772, faced renewed Lombard pressure on papal territories in central Italy under King Desiderius, who was also sheltering Carloman's widow and sons, a presence that implicitly challenged Charlemagne's legitimacy as sole Frankish king. When Desiderius pressed his own territorial claims against papal lands and refused Hadrian's demands to withdraw, the pope appealed to Charlemagne for the kind of military intervention his father Pepin had twice provided in the 750s.

Charlemagne responded with a full scale invasion of the Lombard kingdom in 773, leading Frankish forces across the Alps in a campaign that culminated in a prolonged siege of the Lombard capital at Pavia through the winter of 773 into 774. During the siege, Charlemagne traveled to Rome itself for Easter, a visit that reinforced his personal relationship with Hadrian and allowed the pope to confirm and, according to later and somewhat disputed tradition, expand the territorial grants his predecessors had received from Pepin. Pavia fell in June 774, Desiderius was deposed and sent into monastic confinement in Frankish territory, and Charlemagne assumed the title king of the Lombards in addition to his existing title king of the Franks, a dual kingship that placed the crowns of two formerly separate peoples on a single head.

The conquest of 774 did not simply extend Frankish territory southward. It fundamentally altered Charlemagne's relationship to Italian and papal politics, drawing him into a peninsula whose political structures, urban traditions, and relationship to Byzantine authority in the south differed considerably from anything in the Frankish heartlands. Charlemagne generally preserved existing Lombard administrative structures and law where practical, appointing Frankish counts alongside surviving Lombard officials rather than imposing uniform Frankish institutions overnight, a pattern of layered rather than wholesale administrative replacement that recurred, as later sections will show, in other conquered territories as well. The Lombard conquest also gave Charlemagne direct control over extensive Italian resources and, critically for later developments, direct proximity to Rome itself, a proximity that would matter enormously when the imperial question arose in 800.

Section 31.4

The Saxon Wars and Forced Christianization

No single Carolingian campaign occupied more of Charlemagne's reign, consumed more resources, or reveals more about the coercive dimension of Carolingian expansion than the long conflict with the Saxon peoples east of the lower Rhine. The Saxons in the later eighth century were organized not as a unified kingdom but as a loose confederation of regional groups, without a single ruler capable of negotiating a lasting settlement on behalf of the whole people, a fact that helps explain why the conflict dragged on intermittently for more than three decades rather than concluding in a single decisive campaign.

Frankish kings had clashed with Saxon groups intermittently for generations before Charlemagne, but the campaigns he launched beginning in 772 pursued conquest and religious conversion together in a way earlier Frankish Saxon warfare generally had not. In that first campaign, Frankish forces destroyed a Saxon sacred site known to Frankish sources as the Irminsul, apparently a pillar or tree of considerable religious significance to Saxon communities, an act of calculated symbolic violence against Saxon religious identity rather than merely a military maneuver. Over the following years, Frankish armies campaigned repeatedly into Saxon territory, securing oaths of submission and baptism that Saxon communities then frequently repudiated once Frankish forces withdrew, a pattern of conquest, apparent submission, and renewed revolt that recurred across the 770s and 780s.

The conflict escalated sharply in the 780s under the leadership of a Saxon figure the Frankish sources call Widukind, who organized sustained resistance and inflicted a serious defeat on a Frankish force at the Süntel hills in 782. Charlemagne's response, recorded in Frankish annals, was the mass execution of several thousand Saxon captives at Verden later that same year, an episode historians debate in terms of its exact scale and circumstances but which nonetheless illustrates the extreme severity Charlemagne was prepared to employ against continued Saxon resistance. In the years that followed, Charlemagne issued a harsh legal code for Saxony, the Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae, which prescribed the death penalty for a range of offenses against Christian practice, including refusing baptism, cremating the dead according to traditional custom, and conspiring against Frankish rule, provisions that made adherence to Christianity and submission to Frankish authority effectively inseparable requirements under Saxon law.

Widukind himself eventually submitted and accepted baptism around 785, with Charlemagne serving, according to Frankish sources, as his godfather, a gesture intended to bind the former Saxon leader into the Frankish Christian order through ritual kinship as well as political submission. Resistance nonetheless continued intermittently for another two decades, accompanied by further Frankish campaigns, forced population transfers that deported Saxon communities into Frankish territory, and continued efforts to establish churches, bishoprics, and monasteries as instruments of both religious and administrative control. The wars are generally reckoned to have ended around 804, when the last significant Saxon resistance was suppressed and further deportations of Saxon populations were carried out. In that same year, Charlemagne ceded an important part of the territory beyond the Elbe to the allied Obodrites, a Slavic people who had supported Frankish campaigns against the Saxons, a grant that served partly as a buffer against the Danes to the north, while other confiscated Saxon lands elsewhere could be redistributed within the wider Carolingian political and ecclesiastical order.

Historians today generally read the Saxon wars as a central and troubling episode in the Carolingian project rather than a peripheral one. The wars reveal that the Carolingian program of Christian renewal, examined further in Section 31.7, was not confined to persuasion, preaching, and institution building but could extend to mass violence, forced deportation, and the criminalization of traditional religious practice when applied to a resistant frontier population. Later Carolingian legislation somewhat moderated the harshest provisions of the Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae, suggesting that even within the Carolingian court some concern existed about the severity of the initial approach, but the broader pattern of coercive Christianization in Saxony should not be minimized or treated as incidental to an otherwise purely constructive program of Christian reform.

Section 31.5

Bavaria, the Avars, Iberia, and the Expanding Frontiers

While the Saxon wars occupied the northeastern frontier for decades, Charlemagne's reign also brought major campaigns and territorial changes on several other fronts, each revealing a different aspect of how Carolingian expansion actually worked in practice.

Bavaria had long been governed by its own ducal house with considerable internal autonomy, acknowledging Frankish overlordship in a generally loose and intermittent fashion. Duke Tassilo III, who had married a Lombard princess and maintained diplomatic ties independent of Frankish oversight, grew increasingly estranged from Charlemagne over the 770s and 780s. In 787 and 788, Charlemagne moved decisively against Tassilo, summoning him to a royal assembly, accusing him of conspiring with the Avars against Frankish interests, and securing his deposition and confinement to a monastery along with several of his family members. Bavaria was thereafter absorbed directly into the Frankish realm, governed by Frankish appointed counts rather than a semi independent ducal house, a pattern of annexation that removed a long standing source of frontier autonomy and brought Frankish authority directly against the Avar khaganate's western border.

The Avars, a steppe derived people whose khaganate controlled the Pannonian Basin, the fertile lowland encircled by the Carpathians discussed in Chapter 2, had accumulated substantial wealth over more than two centuries of raiding, tribute extraction, and control of trade routes linking central Europe to the wider Eurasian steppe corridor. Frankish campaigns against the Avars in the early 790s, intensified after the Bavarian annexation removed a buffer between the two powers, culminated in the destruction of the Avar royal center or encampment that Frankish sources called the Ring, where a major treasury was seized, an enormous hoard of accumulated wealth, distributed by Charlemagne among his followers and to churches and monasteries across the Frankish realm. The Avar campaigns illustrate how frontier warfare could generate resources that reinforced Carolingian patronage networks at the very core of the empire, linking distant military success to the aristocratic and ecclesiastical loyalty examined in Section 31.6.

Charlemagne's ventures south of the Pyrenees proved considerably less successful. In 778, responding to an invitation from Muslim governors in northeastern Iberia who were themselves in conflict with the Umayyad emirate centered at Córdoba, Charlemagne led an expedition into Iberia that failed to take the city of Zaragoza and was forced to withdraw. As the Frankish army retreated back across the Pyrenees, its rearguard, moving through a narrow mountain pass, was ambushed and destroyed by Basque forces at Roncesvalles, an episode of real military significance in its own time but one that later medieval literary tradition transformed, centuries afterward, into the heavily embellished and largely legendary Song of Roland, a text that should not be read as reliable historical evidence for the events of 778 themselves. Despite this setback, Frankish influence south of the Pyrenees expanded gradually and incrementally over subsequent decades, rather than through the immediate creation of a single frontier province, as Girona entered Carolingian rule in 785 and Barcelona was conquered in 801, among other frontier counties established across this period. Modern historians conventionally group these frontier counties under the label the Spanish March, a geographical description of a developing frontier rather than the name of one formally constituted province, intended to secure Frankish territory against both Muslim Iberia and the independent minded Basque populations of the western Pyrenees.

Taken together, these frontier campaigns demonstrate that Carolingian expansion did not proceed uniformly or with equal success on every front. Lombard Italy was conquered relatively quickly and absorbed with substantial continuity of existing institutions. Bavaria was annexed through political pressure and a single decisive confrontation rather than prolonged warfare. The Avars were defeated in a comparatively short but devastating campaign that yielded enormous plunder. Saxony required decades of brutal, repeatedly renewed warfare and mass coercion. And the Iberian frontier revealed the outer limits of what Frankish military power could reliably achieve. Students should resist any narrative that presents Carolingian expansion as a single uniform process of unification, since the empire that resulted from these varied campaigns governed its different regions in correspondingly varied ways.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitulary and Missi Dominici

A capitulary was a written royal instruction, typically organized into numbered chapters, or capitula, through which Carolingian kings issued legislation, administrative directives, and moral or religious instruction to their officials. Capitularies covered an enormous range of subjects, from criminal law to monastic discipline to instructions for royal estate managers, and their survival in considerable numbers gives historians unusually direct access to Carolingian governmental ambitions, even though the capitularies themselves cannot tell us how consistently their provisions were actually enforced across a realm of such size. The missi dominici, meaning roughly the lord's envoys, were royal agents, often dispatched in teams combining senior lay and ecclesiastical figures such as aristocrats, bishops, or abbots, sent from the court to designated regions to inspect local justice, hear complaints against local officials, and report back to the king. The missi represented Charlemagne's most direct attempt to extend royal oversight beyond what personal royal travel and static aristocratic loyalty alone could achieve, though their effectiveness depended heavily on the cooperation of the same local elites they were meant to supervise.

Historical Vocabulary
Renovatio and Correctio

Renovatio, a Latin term meaning renewal or restoration, appears in Carolingian court poetry and other writing associated with Charlemagne's circle to describe his rule as a renewal of Christian Roman order in the West, drawing on Roman imperial memory and Christian political thought without amounting to a single, fully worked out political theory. Correctio, meaning correction or setting right, describes the related but distinct Carolingian program of religious and moral reform examined in Section 31.7, resting on the conviction that a Christian king bore personal responsibility, before God, for the religious condition of his people. The two terms overlap in spirit but should not be treated as synonyms: renovatio describes an ideological claim about the character of Carolingian rule and its relationship to Roman and Christian precedent, while correctio describes the concrete program of clerical training, textual accuracy, liturgical uniformity, and pastoral reform through which that rule was meant to be exercised. Section 31.10 examines how renovatio helped Charlemagne's court articulate the meaning of the imperial title received in 800.

Section 31.6

Governing an Empire without a Modern State

Having surveyed how Charlemagne's territories expanded, this chapter now turns to a harder and, in many ways, more historically significant question: how did the Carolingians actually attempt to govern the enormous and varied territory their campaigns had assembled? The honest answer requires students to set aside modern assumptions about centralized bureaucratic government from the outset, since nothing resembling a permanent civil service, a standing tax administration, or a uniform code of law enforced identically across the realm existed under Charlemagne or, indeed, under any early medieval ruler.

The basic unit of local government remained the county, headed by a count appointed by the king from among the regional aristocracy, or occasionally a trusted outsider, and responsible within his county for administering justice, maintaining public order, raising and leading military forces when summoned, and collecting royal dues and hospitality obligations owed to the king and his traveling household. Counts were not salaried officials in any modern sense. They were rewarded through grants of land and office, through the prestige and local power the office conferred, and through their integration into networks of royal patronage that connected them to the court and to other aristocratic families across the realm. This meant that effective royal government depended fundamentally on maintaining the loyalty and cooperation of a landed aristocracy whose local power was, in practice, difficult for the king to fully control or remove.

Charlemagne and his advisers developed several mechanisms intended to strengthen royal oversight of this fundamentally decentralized system. Capitularies, discussed in the feature box above, allowed the king to issue detailed written instructions on an enormous range of subjects, from criminal procedure to the management of royal estates to instructions for how bishops should conduct visitations of their dioceses. The missi dominici, royal envoys often dispatched in teams combining senior lay and ecclesiastical figures, provided a mechanism for periodic inspection of local administration, though their visits were occasional rather than continuous and their success depended on the same local cooperation they were meant to supervise. Royal assemblies, held at least annually and often more frequently, brought together the leading men of the realm, counts, bishops, and abbots, to hear royal decisions, offer counsel, renew oaths of fidelity, and, in some cases, participate in military campaign planning, functioning as a crucial mechanism for reinforcing personal bonds between the king and his aristocracy across a territory too large for the king to visit every region in person on a regular basis.

Oaths of fidelity, sworn personally to the king by counts, bishops, and other important office holders, and in some cases required more broadly of free men across the realm, represented another crucial instrument of Carolingian government, binding officials to the king through a personal and, in Carolingian ideology, sacred obligation rather than through impersonal bureaucratic accountability alone. Royal estates, scattered across the kingdom and organized to supply the traveling royal household with food, lodging, and resources, provided the material foundation that allowed itinerant kingship to function, since the king's household could not remain permanently in one place without exhausting local resources and needed to move seasonally among estates capable of supporting it.

Even with all these mechanisms, Carolingian government remained heavily dependent on personal relationships, aristocratic cooperation, and the physical presence, or at least the credible threat of eventual royal attention, rather than on impersonal institutional routine. Distance, difficult communication, and the sheer scale of the empire meant that royal instructions issued from the court could take considerable time to reach distant regions and even longer to be verifiably implemented, if they were implemented consistently at all. Students should therefore understand Carolingian government as a serious and genuinely innovative attempt to strengthen royal oversight of an inherited aristocratic and ecclesiastical system, not as the creation of a modern centralized state capable of uniform administration across its entire territory.

Section 31.7

Kingship, Church, and the Program of Correctio

Alongside conquest and administrative reform, Charlemagne and his advisers pursued an ambitious program of religious and moral renewal that historians generally refer to using the Latin term correctio, meaning correction or setting right. This program rested on a distinctive Carolingian understanding of royal responsibility, one that held the Christian king personally accountable, before God, for the moral and religious condition of the entire Christian people under his rule, not merely for their military defense or civil order. This idea drew on biblical models of Israelite kingship, on earlier Christian political thought, and on the close working relationship between the Carolingian court and leading churchmen, several of whom, including Alcuin of York discussed in the next section, articulated and helped implement this vision of royal religious responsibility.

In practical terms, correctio touched nearly every dimension of ecclesiastical and religious life. Church councils, convened with royal encouragement and sometimes royal participation, addressed questions of clerical discipline, requiring priests to be properly trained, morally upright, and capable of instructing their congregations in basic Christian belief and practice. Bishops were expected to conduct regular visitations of the parishes within their dioceses, correcting abuses and ensuring adequate pastoral care reached rural populations who might otherwise have little sustained contact with organized Christian instruction. Monasteries were encouraged, and in some cases compelled, to adopt more uniform and rigorous standards of monastic discipline, a process that drew increasingly on the Rule of Saint Benedict as a common standard, a development that would be carried further in the following generation under Charlemagne's son Louis the Pious.

Correctio also addressed liturgical practice, since considerable regional variation existed across the Frankish realm in how the Christian liturgy was celebrated, what texts were used, and how consistently they had been accurately copied and transmitted. Carolingian reformers sought greater liturgical uniformity, drawing in part on Roman liturgical models obtained through direct contact with the papacy, part of a broader Carolingian effort to align Frankish religious practice more closely with what was understood to be authentic Roman Christian tradition, itself a reflection of the close Frankish papal relationship examined earlier in this chapter. Tithing, the obligation to contribute a portion of agricultural produce to support the church, was more systematically enforced during this period as well, providing churches and monasteries with a more secure economic foundation for their expanding pastoral and educational responsibilities.

It is essential for students to recognize that correctio was not confined to abstract theological or liturgical matters but extended, as Section 31.4 has already shown in the case of Saxony, to the outer edges of coercion when applied to resistant or newly conquered populations. Within the Frankish heartlands, correctio operated primarily through councils, capitularies, episcopal oversight, and educational reform, examined further in the following section. On the Saxon frontier, closely related religious goals were pursued through military conquest, legal compulsion, and, when resistance persisted, mass violence. This chapter's earlier caution against treating Carolingian Christian renewal as a purely constructive or purely coercive phenomenon applies with particular force here: the same underlying conviction, that a Christian king bore personal responsibility for the religious condition of his people, produced both the patient, institution building reforms examined in this section and the harsh compulsion examined in Section 31.4.

Section 31.8

Education, Books, and the Carolingian Renaissance

Closely connected to the program of religious correction examined in the previous section was an ambitious effort to improve the quality of Latin learning, textual accuracy, and clerical education across the Frankish realm, a movement historians conventionally label the Carolingian Renaissance. The term requires careful handling. It does not describe anything resembling a mass educational transformation reaching the general population, nor a rediscovery of classical learning comparable to the much later Italian Renaissance examined in Part IX of this textbook. It describes instead a concentrated, primarily ecclesiastical and court centered program of textual correction, manuscript production, and clerical education, whose effects, while genuinely significant for the later transmission of ancient and early Christian texts, were felt overwhelmingly among a relatively small educated elite of clergy, monks, and royal officials.

Charlemagne actively recruited scholars from across Latin Christendom to his court, drawing figures from Italy, Iberia, and, most famously, Anglo Saxon England, where a strong tradition of Latin learning had developed in monastic centers such as York. Alcuin of York, recruited to the Frankish court in the 780s, became the leading intellectual figure of this circle, overseeing efforts to correct and standardize biblical and liturgical texts, composing educational treatises, and eventually heading the monastery of Saint Martin at Tours, where scriptorium production of corrected texts continued after Alcuin's own direct involvement with the royal court diminished. Other significant figures associated with this court centered scholarly circle included Paul the Deacon, a Lombard historian, and later Frankish and continental scholars who continued the program of textual and educational reform into the following generation.

The most visible and enduring legacy of this movement is the development of Carolingian minuscule, a clear, rounded, and highly legible script that emerged at several Frankish monastic centers, including Corbie, in the decades before and during Charlemagne's reign, and that spread further across monastic scriptoria throughout the Frankish realm as Tours, under Alcuin's later direction, became a major center of its standardization and dissemination rather than the place of its original invention. Manuscripts copied in this script preserved an enormous body of classical Latin literature, patristic theology, and legal and administrative texts, many of which survive today only because Carolingian scribes copied them from older, more fragile, and less legible exemplars, some of which have since been lost entirely. Modern classical scholarship depends, to a degree not always appreciated outside specialist circles, on Carolingian era manuscript copies for its knowledge of numerous ancient Latin texts, a fact that gives the Carolingian textual program lasting significance far beyond its own immediate religious and administrative purposes.

Cathedral and monastic schools, encouraged and in some cases mandated by royal capitulary, provided the institutional setting for training clergy in Latin grammar, scripture, and the skills necessary for accurate liturgical and administrative practice. These schools should not be imagined as anything resembling universal or even widespread popular education. They served primarily to train clergy and, to a lesser extent, sons of the aristocracy destined for ecclesiastical or administrative careers, and their reach, while expanding under Carolingian patronage, remained heavily concentrated in monastic and cathedral centers rather than extending into rural parish level instruction for the general population. Historians therefore generally caution against describing the Carolingian Renaissance as anything like a broad cultural awakening reaching ordinary Frankish subjects, while still recognizing its genuine and historically consequential achievements in textual preservation, clerical training, and the standardization of Latin learning that would shape European intellectual life for centuries afterward.

Section 31.9

Christmas Day 800: The Imperial Coronation

By the final years of the eighth century, Charlemagne governed a territory of unprecedented scale for a Frankish king, encompassing the Frankish heartlands, the former Lombard kingdom, Bavaria, most of Saxony, and substantial frontier zones extending toward Iberia and the former Avar lands. The circumstances that brought him to Rome in the year 800, and to the imperial coronation that followed, arose from a specific political crisis in the city rather than from a long premeditated Frankish plan to seize an imperial title.

Pope Leo III, elected in 795, faced serious opposition within Rome from a faction connected to his predecessor's family, and in April 799 he was physically attacked by conspirators who reportedly attempted to blind him and cut out his tongue, injuries from which he apparently recovered, before fleeing to Frankish protection. Charlemagne, whose relationship with the papacy had already made him the effective protector of Roman ecclesiastical authority, arranged for Leo's safe return to Rome and eventually traveled there himself in late 800 to investigate the charges brought against the pope by his Roman opponents. In a carefully staged proceeding, Leo cleared himself of the accusations against him through a solemn oath, a resolution that restored his authority but also left him deeply indebted to Frankish protection.

It was in this charged atmosphere that, according to the most widely cited account, found in the Royal Frankish Annals and further elaborated by later sources, Leo III crowned Charlemagne emperor during Christmas Mass at Saint Peter's Basilica on December 25, 800, while the assembled Roman crowd acclaimed him with imperial titles. Einhard's later biography, however, records that Charlemagne himself claimed he would not have entered the church that day had he known what the pope intended, a statement scholars have interpreted in sharply differing ways, some taking it as a genuine expression of surprise or reluctance, others as a calculated later attempt, possibly reflecting the concerns of Charlemagne's own court, to suggest that the initiative for the coronation came from the pope rather than from Charlemagne himself, thereby protecting Frankish claims to imperial legitimacy from any appearance of having been granted, and therefore potentially revocable, by papal authority.

Historians cannot resolve with certainty how much advance planning preceded the coronation, whether Charlemagne genuinely did not anticipate the ceremony's precise form, or how contemporaries at the Frankish court and in Rome understood its significance in the moment. What can be established is that the ceremony combined several distinct elements, papal initiative in performing the coronation, Roman popular acclamation in the ancient manner associated with the elevation of Roman emperors, and Frankish military and political power that made Charlemagne the only plausible candidate for such an elevation in the West. The following section examines what this new title actually meant, and did not mean, within the wider context of continuing Roman imperial authority centered on Constantinople.

Historical Debate

What Did the Imperial Coronation of 800 Mean?

The question: Was the imperial coronation of Charlemagne primarily an initiative of Pope Leo III, seeking a powerful protector and asserting papal authority to confer imperial legitimacy, or was it primarily the culmination of Charlemagne's own long developing imperial ambitions, with the pope serving essentially as the instrument of a Frankish political program already well underway?

Interpretation A — Papal initiative: Some historians emphasize that Leo III faced a severe crisis of authority in Rome in 799 and 800, and argue that crowning Charlemagne served Leo's immediate need for a powerful protector while also asserting, through the act of coronation itself, a papal claim to authority over the making of emperors, a claim with significant implications for later medieval relations between popes and emperors.
Interpretation B — Frankish imperial ambition: Other historians point to the scale of Charlemagne's territorial control by 800, his patronage of scholars and artists working self consciously in a revived Roman idiom, and his household's evident interest in Roman imperial precedent, arguing that the coronation formalized ambitions already present at the Frankish court, with Einhard's later account of Charlemagne's surprise reflecting a subsequent effort to protect Frankish imperial claims from any implication that they depended on papal grant.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same limited and not fully consistent set of contemporary and near contemporary sources, principally the Royal Frankish Annals and Einhard's biography, neither of which offers an unambiguous, disinterested account of the ceremony's planning or meaning.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes broader assessments of the relative power and initiative of the papacy versus Frankish royal authority around 800, a question with long consequences for later medieval disputes between popes and emperors examined in Part VII.

Student task: Identify what specific evidence would need to survive, but does not, for historians to resolve this question with confidence, and explain why the absence of that evidence requires careful, qualified language rather than a single confident narrative.

Section 31.10

What Did a Renewed Western Empire Mean?

Whatever the precise circumstances of the coronation itself, its consequences required Charlemagne and his court to work out, in the years that followed, exactly what an imperial title conferred in Rome actually meant, a question complicated enormously by the fact that a Roman emperor already reigned, without interruption, in Constantinople, as this textbook's later treatment of Byzantium in Part VI will examine in full. Students must set aside any assumption that Charlemagne's coronation restored an empire that had ceased to exist. The Roman Empire in the East had never fallen, and its rulers, whom modern historians conventionally call Byzantine emperors while recognizing that they understood themselves simply as Roman emperors, continued to regard themselves as the sole legitimate heirs of Roman imperial authority.

The court reigning in Constantinople in the year 800 was, moreover, unusual in being headed by a woman, the Empress Irene, who had deposed and blinded her own son to secure sole rule, a situation some later medieval and even some near contemporary Frankish sources suggested may have provided additional pretext for arguing that the imperial throne in the East was, in Frankish eyes, effectively vacant or illegitimately held, a convenient argument for Frankish purposes though one Byzantine authorities naturally rejected. Byzantine reaction to Charlemagne's coronation was, in fact, initially hostile, since accepting a rival western imperial title implicitly challenged the claim of Constantinople's rulers to sole and universal Roman imperial authority.

Diplomatic negotiations between the Frankish and Byzantine courts continued over the following decade, addressing not only the imperial title question but also territorial disputes in the Adriatic region and broader questions of diplomatic recognition. In 812, a Byzantine embassy formally acknowledged Charlemagne's imperial title, though in terms that Byzantine sources framed carefully to avoid conceding that Charlemagne held the full, universal Roman imperial dignity claimed by the emperor in Constantinople. This settlement effectively established, without either side fully articulating it in these terms, a situation in which two distinct imperial titles coexisted, a western one centered on Frankish Christian kingship and Roman memory, and an eastern one continuing the unbroken line of Roman emperors, a duality with lasting consequences for medieval European political thought that this textbook will trace across subsequent chapters and volumes.

What, then, did Charlemagne's imperial title actually signify within the Frankish world itself? Court sources and poetry from Charlemagne's circle sometimes employed the language of renovatio, meaning renewal or restoration, presenting Charlemagne's rule as a renewal of Christian Roman order in the West rather than a simple continuation of ordinary Frankish kingship. This ideology drew on Roman imperial memory, Old Testament models of righteous kingship, and Augustinian ideas about the Christian ordering of temporal power, without amounting to a coherent, fully worked out political theory of empire in the sense later medieval or modern thinkers would develop. In practical administrative terms, the imperial title changed comparatively little about how Charlemagne actually governed his territories, since the counts, capitularies, assemblies, and personal loyalty networks examined in Section 31.6 continued to function largely as before. Its significance lay primarily in ideology and diplomatic status rather than in any immediate transformation of governmental machinery, a distinction students should keep clearly in view when assessing the coronation's historical importance.

Section 31.11

Court, Aachen, Culture, and Imperial Society

Although Carolingian kingship remained fundamentally itinerant throughout Charlemagne's reign, in that the royal household continued to move seasonally among estates, monasteries, and fortified centers capable of supplying it, the later decades of his reign saw Aachen, in the northern part of his territories, emerge as an increasingly favored residence, particularly after roughly 794. Charlemagne commissioned a substantial palace complex there, including a great hall for royal assemblies and a palace chapel, an octagonal domed structure influenced by Byzantine and Ravennate ecclesiastical architecture, that survives today as one of the most important physical remnants of Carolingian building and served as the site of Charlemagne's own burial after his death in 814.

Aachen should not be understood as a capital city in the modern sense, with permanent government offices, a resident bureaucracy, or a large urban population dependent on administrative functions. It was, rather, the most elaborately developed of several royal residences, a place where Charlemagne increasingly chose to spend the winter months in his later years, where major assemblies and important religious ceremonies were held, and where the scholarly circle associated with Alcuin and other court intellectuals, examined in Section 31.8, gathered and produced much of its most significant work. The palace at Aachen brought together, in a single physical setting, the political, religious, and cultural dimensions of Carolingian rule examined throughout this chapter: the great hall for assemblies of counts and bishops, the chapel for the liturgical and religious life at the center of correctio, and the scholarly circle producing corrected texts and educational treatises.

Court society at Aachen and at Charlemagne's other residences brought together Frankish aristocrats, churchmen, foreign scholars such as Alcuin, and, on occasion, embassies from distant powers, including, famously, gifts sent from the Abbasid caliph Harun al Rashid, evidence that Carolingian diplomacy extended well beyond Latin Christendom into the wider Mediterranean and Eurasian political world. This diplomatic and cultural richness, however, should not obscure the more ordinary realities of life for the great majority of the population within Charlemagne's territories, whose experience of imperial rule was mediated far more directly through local counts, bishops, and the obligations of military service, royal and local dues, hospitality obligations, and religious observance examined elsewhere in this chapter than through any direct contact with the court itself.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Merovingian and Carolingian Government Compared

What Stayed the Same

  • Government continued to depend fundamentally on counts drawn from the regional aristocracy, rewarded with land and office rather than salary
  • Kingship remained itinerant, with the royal household moving seasonally among estates and religious centers rather than governing from a single fixed capital
  • The church, and particularly bishops and abbots, continued to serve simultaneously as spiritual authorities and practical instruments of royal government
  • Royal power ultimately rested on the cooperation of a landed aristocracy whose local authority the king could influence but not eliminate

What Changed

  • The scale of territory under a single ruler expanded enormously, from the Frankish kingdom of the 760s to an empire stretching from the Pyrenees to the Elbe and into central Italy
  • Written capitularies became a far more systematic and wide ranging instrument of royal instruction than anything the Merovingian kings had employed
  • The missi dominici introduced a more deliberate, if still imperfect, mechanism for periodic royal inspection of local administration
  • Royal religious responsibility expanded into a comprehensive program of correctio touching clerical training, liturgy, monastic discipline, and education
  • An imperial title, absent under the Merovingians and largely absent under Pepin III as well, introduced a new and consequential, if administratively limited, ideological claim

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents two opposite errors, treating Charlemagne as having invented an entirely new form of government from nothing, or dismissing his reign as merely a larger version of unchanged Merovingian practice.

Cause and Effect

Explaining Carolingian Expansion

Long-term causes

Decades of Pippinid military consolidation under Charles Martel and Pepin III had already built a formidable aristocratic military network, landed wealth, and a close relationship with the papacy before Charlemagne's reign began.

Short-term causes

Specific circumstances, papal appeals against the Lombards, Tassilo's alleged conspiracy with the Avars, and Saxon raiding across the Frankish frontier, provided immediate pretexts and opportunities for the individual campaigns of Charlemagne's reign.

Effect

By 800, Charlemagne controlled a territory of unprecedented scale for a Frankish ruler, commanded enormous plunder and tribute from conquered peoples, and possessed sufficient prestige and Italian proximity to receive an imperial title in Rome.

This chapter's caution

Military success alone does not explain Carolingian expansion; sustained aristocratic cooperation, ecclesiastical partnership, and the redistribution of conquered wealth through patronage were equally necessary, and expansion proceeded with very different methods and different degrees of success on different frontiers.

Historical complexity: The same campaigns that generated the wealth and prestige underlying the imperial coronation of 800 also generated the mass violence documented in Saxony, a reminder that a single causal chain can produce outcomes historians must evaluate very differently.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under Carolingian Rule

For the great majority of people living within Charlemagne's territories, imperial coronation, court scholarship, and Roman imperial ideology remained distant matters, encountered, if at all, through the more immediate obligations of local government. Military service obligations fell heavily on landholders, who were expected to appear for campaigns with equipment appropriate to their wealth, a burden that shaped agricultural cycles and household economies across the realm, since prolonged absence for distant campaigns, such as those against the Avars or in Saxony, could disrupt planting and harvest for the families and dependents left behind.

Tithing, more systematically enforced under the program of correctio examined in Section 31.7, represented a further and often burdensome obligation, requiring agricultural producers to set aside a portion of their harvest for the support of local churches, a demand layered atop existing obligations to landlords and to royal officials collecting hospitality dues for the traveling court. Rural parish life, where it existed, increasingly brought ordinary Frankish subjects into more regular contact with priests expected, under Carolingian reform, to instruct them in basic Christian belief, a modest but genuine expansion of religious instruction reaching beyond the monastic and clerical elite most directly touched by the Carolingian Renaissance discussed in Section 31.8. By 813, a Frankish church council held at Tours would go further still, instructing preachers to render their sermons into the everyday spoken Romance or Germanic vernacular so that ordinary congregations could actually understand them, a later and more explicit step beyond the more general clerical and educational instructions of the 789 Admonitio Generalis discussed in the Primary Source Window. For conquered Saxon populations, by contrast, everyday life under early Carolingian rule could mean forced resettlement, the criminalization of long familiar religious and funerary practices, and the constant presence of Frankish garrisons and newly established churches functioning as instruments of both religious and political control.

Section 31.12

The Carolingian Empire in 814: Achievement and Limits

Charlemagne's earlier plans for the succession had, in fact, envisioned dividing his territories among several sons in a manner broadly consistent with Frankish custom, but the deaths of his sons Pepin of Italy in 810 and Charles the Younger in 811 left Louis the Pious, ruler of Aquitaine since childhood, as Charlemagne's only surviving legitimate adult son by the final years of the reign. In 813, with his own health failing, Charlemagne summoned Louis to Aachen and had him assume the imperial crown as co emperor before an assembly of Frankish magnates, an elevation performed without papal participation and evidently intended to settle the imperial succession clearly and in Charlemagne's own lifetime rather than leave it to be contested or negotiated after his death. This arrangement helps explain why the transfer of power in 814 proceeded relatively smoothly compared to the divided succession that had followed Pepin III's death in 768, a contingent outcome of recent deaths within the Carolingian family rather than any inherent solution to the problem of dividing power among royal heirs.

Charlemagne died at Aachen on January 28, 814, after more than four decades of rule, and was buried in the palace chapel he had built there. Louis the Pious, already installed as co emperor since 813, succeeded him as sole emperor without a contested transition, though this comparatively smooth succession should be understood as the product of the particular family circumstances just described rather than as evidence that the underlying structural problem of dividing Carolingian power among royal heirs had been resolved. That problem, familiar already from the division of 768, would resurface with considerably more destructive consequences among Louis the Pious's own sons a generation later, a subject Chapter 32 will examine in detail.

Assessing the empire Charlemagne left behind requires holding two things in mind at once, its genuine and considerable achievements, and its real and, in retrospect, serious structural limits. On the side of achievement, Charlemagne had assembled, through conquest, annexation, and alliance, a territory of a scale unmatched by any western ruler since the western Roman Empire itself, encompassing the Frankish heartlands, Lombard Italy, Bavaria, Saxony, and substantial frontier zones. He had developed, through capitularies, the missi dominici, and regular assemblies, meaningful if imperfect instruments for extending royal oversight across that vast territory. He had sponsored a program of religious and educational reform whose textual and institutional legacy, examined in Sections 31.7 and 31.8, would shape Latin Christian learning for centuries. And he had secured, through the imperial coronation of 800 and its eventual, if qualified, Byzantine recognition in 812, a renewed western imperial title that introduced enduring and consequential ideas about Christian empire into western European political thought.

On the side of structural limitation, the empire's government remained, as Section 31.6 has emphasized throughout, fundamentally dependent on the personal loyalty of a regional aristocracy whose local power the crown could influence but never fully control, on a system of communication and oversight that operated slowly and unevenly across enormous distances, and on the continued cooperation of a church whose own institutional interests did not always align perfectly with royal ambitions. The empire's different regions, Frankish, Lombard, Bavarian, Saxon, and frontier territories alike, retained distinctive local institutions, customs, and, in several cases, considerable memory of independent or semi independent political traditions predating Frankish conquest. Nothing in the structure Charlemagne left behind guaranteed that his successors would command the same military prestige, the same accumulated wealth from conquest, or the same personal authority that had held this vast and varied territory together during his own long reign.

This chapter closes, deliberately, at this point of achievement and unresolved tension rather than narrating what followed. Louis the Pious inherited an empire of extraordinary scale and ambition in 814, along with the practical challenges of governing it, sustaining its religious reform program, managing succession among his own sons, and responding to renewed external pressures along multiple frontiers. How those challenges played out, including the internal divisions among Louis's sons, the resumption and intensification of external raiding by Scandinavian forces and, later in the ninth century, by Magyar forces as well, and the eventual fragmentation of Carolingian political unity, is the subject to which Chapter 32 now turns.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Charlemagne, who inherited a portion of the Frankish kingdom already transformed by his father Pepin III into a dynasty with papal sanction and considerable aristocratic and military strength, built upon that inheritance to create a Christian empire of unprecedented scale in the early medieval West. The chapter's central argument has been that conquest, governance, religious reform, education, and imperial ideology were not separate stories but interlocking dimensions of a single, evolving political project, one whose methods and results varied considerably across the different frontiers and populations it encompassed.

Following the death of his brother Carloman in 771, Charlemagne became sole king of the Franks and began a series of military campaigns that transformed the territorial scope of Frankish kingship. The conquest of the Lombard kingdom in 774 brought northern and central Italy, along with a much closer relationship to the papacy, under Frankish control, generally preserving existing Lombard institutions rather than replacing them wholesale. The prolonged and repeatedly renewed Saxon wars, extending from 772 to 804, combined military conquest with forced Christianization, mass deportation, and, at moments such as the executions at Verden, severe violence, revealing the coercive dimension that could accompany the Carolingian program of Christian renewal when applied to resistant frontier populations. The annexation of Bavaria in 788 removed a semi autonomous frontier duchy through political pressure rather than prolonged warfare, while the campaigns against the Avar khaganate in the 790s yielded enormous plunder that reinforced Carolingian patronage networks at the core of the empire. The failed Iberian campaign of 778, and the disaster at Roncesvalles that accompanied its withdrawal, illustrated the real limits of Frankish military reach even as Carolingian frontier counties, later grouped under the conventional label the Spanish March, developed incrementally south of the Pyrenees over subsequent decades.

Governing this vastly expanded territory required instruments considerably more developed than those the Merovingians had employed, though nothing resembling a modern centralized bureaucratic state. Counts continued to administer local justice and military mobilization in exchange for land and office rather than salary, while capitularies, the missi dominici, and regular royal assemblies provided mechanisms, imperfect but genuine, for extending royal oversight and reinforcing personal bonds of loyalty across an empire too large for continuous direct royal supervision. Oaths of fidelity and the itinerant movement of the royal household among royal estates remained central to how this system actually functioned in practice.

Alongside conquest and administration, Charlemagne and his advisers pursued an ambitious program of religious and moral renewal, correctio, resting on the conviction that a Christian king bore personal responsibility for the religious condition of his people. This program touched clerical training, episcopal oversight, monastic discipline, liturgical uniformity, and tithing, and it connected closely to the educational and textual reforms historians call the Carolingian Renaissance, a movement centered on figures such as Alcuin of York and expressed most enduringly in the development of Carolingian minuscule and the large scale copying and correction of classical, patristic, and liturgical texts, a movement whose reach, while genuinely significant, remained concentrated among a relatively narrow ecclesiastical and court centered elite rather than extending broadly across the general population.

The imperial coronation of Christmas Day 800, performed by Pope Leo III amid a crisis of papal authority in Rome, created a renewed western imperial title whose precise origins and meaning remain subjects of genuine historical debate. This chapter has emphasized that the coronation did not restore a fallen Roman Empire, since a Roman emperor continued to reign, without interruption, in Constantinople, and that the resulting western imperial title required years of diplomatic negotiation, culminating in qualified Byzantine recognition in 812, to establish its place alongside continuing eastern Roman imperial authority. Within the Frankish world itself, the imperial title carried considerable ideological significance, expressed through the language of renovatio and through the cultural and religious life increasingly centered on the palace complex at Aachen, even as it changed comparatively little about the practical machinery of Carolingian government.

The chapter closed by assessing the empire Charlemagne left to his son Louis the Pious in 814 as a structure of genuine and considerable achievement resting nonetheless on real and, in time, consequential limitations, an assessment that sets up Chapter 32's examination of how those limitations, combined with renewed external pressures and internal succession disputes, contributed to the fragmentation of Carolingian political unity in the decades following Charlemagne's death.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Capitulary
A written royal instruction, organized into numbered chapters, through which Carolingian kings issued legislation and administrative directives across the realm.
Missi dominici
Royal envoys, often dispatched in teams combining senior lay and ecclesiastical figures such as aristocrats, bishops, or abbots, sent to inspect local administration and justice on the king's behalf.
Count
A regional official, generally drawn from the local aristocracy, responsible for justice, military mobilization, and royal dues within an assigned county.
Duchy
A territory governed by a duke with considerable regional autonomy, such as Bavaria before its annexation in 788.
Itinerant kingship
A style of royal government in which the king and his household moved seasonally among estates and religious centers rather than governing from a single fixed capital.
Correctio
The Carolingian program of religious, moral, and educational reform, resting on the idea that a Christian king was responsible for the religious condition of his people.
Renovatio
A Latin term meaning renewal, used in Carolingian court circles to describe the renewal of Christian Roman order under Charlemagne's rule.
Carolingian minuscule
A clear, standardized script developed and spread during the Carolingian period, through which a large body of ancient and early Christian texts was preserved.
Scriptorium
A room or workshop, typically within a monastery, where manuscripts were copied and corrected.
Benefice
A grant of land or income, often in exchange for military or administrative service, central to how Carolingian aristocrats were rewarded and bound to royal service.
Fidelity oath
A personal oath of loyalty sworn to the king by counts, bishops, and other officials, binding them to royal service through a sworn and, in Carolingian ideology, sacred obligation.
Khaganate
A steppe derived political structure headed by a khagan, such as the Avar khaganate of the Pannonian Basin defeated by Frankish campaigns in the 790s.
Coronation
A ceremony conferring royal or imperial title, in this chapter referring principally to Charlemagne's imperial coronation performed by Pope Leo III on Christmas Day 800.
Translatio imperii
A later medieval concept describing the transfer of imperial authority from one people or era to another, relevant to later interpretations of the 800 coronation though not a term contemporaries themselves used in this exact form.
Royal Frankish Annals
A year by year record kept at the Frankish court and in related centers, one of the principal contemporary sources for Charlemagne's reign.
Pannonian Basin
The fertile central European lowland, introduced in Chapter 2, controlled by the Avar khaganate and the target of major Frankish campaigns in the 790s.
Spanish March
A modern conventional label for the Carolingian frontier counties that developed south of the Pyrenees in the late eighth and early ninth centuries, especially after Girona entered Carolingian rule in 785 and Barcelona was conquered in 801, rather than one formally constituted province created immediately after 778.
Tithe
An obligation to contribute a portion of agricultural produce to support the church, more systematically enforced under Carolingian religious reform.
Pastoral reform
Efforts to improve the religious instruction and care local clergy provided to lay Christians, central to the program of correctio.
Palace chapel
The octagonal domed chapel built at Aachen under Charlemagne's patronage, drawing on Byzantine and Ravennate architectural models and later serving as his burial site.
Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae
A harsh legal code issued for conquered Saxony prescribing severe penalties, including death, for resistance to Frankish rule and Christian practice.
Estate management
The administration of royal lands and their agricultural production, essential to supplying the itinerant royal household and its traveling court.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined how a Frankish kingdom, already reshaped by the Pippinid rise to power traced in Chapter 30, became under Charlemagne a Christian empire of unprecedented scale for the early medieval West. The chapter's recurring argument has been that this transformation cannot be reduced to military conquest alone, nor to religious reform alone, nor to a single ceremony in Rome, but must be understood as the product of conquest, governance, religious correction, educational renewal, and imperial ideology working together, sometimes constructively and sometimes coercively, across an enormous and varied territory.

This chapter matters for the larger history of Latin Christendom because it establishes, in concrete institutional and ideological terms, several patterns that would recur across the following chapters of Part V and beyond: the fusion of royal and religious authority in the concept of correctio, the practical dependence of even the most ambitious early medieval government on aristocratic cooperation and personal loyalty rather than bureaucratic routine, and the introduction of a renewed western imperial title whose relationship to the continuing Roman Empire in the East would remain a live and consequential question for centuries of subsequent medieval political thought, examined further as this textbook proceeds through Part VI and beyond.

At the same time, this chapter has resisted any narrative that would present Charlemagne's achievement as either an inevitable triumph or an unblemished one. The forced Christianization of Saxony stands as a serious and necessary corrective to any account of Carolingian Christian renewal that emphasizes only its patient, institution building dimensions. The qualified and genuinely uncertain circumstances surrounding the coronation of 800 should caution students against treating dramatic historical moments as more clearly planned or more clearly understood by contemporaries than the surviving evidence actually allows. And the structural limits of Carolingian government, its dependence on personal relationships, its uneven reach across different regions, and its ultimate reliance on the continued military success and personal authority of a single aging ruler, prepare students directly for Chapter 32's examination of how quickly, and how seriously, that structure came under strain after 814.

Chapter 32 now turns to the fragmentation of the Carolingian political order, examining how division among Louis the Pious's sons, renewed Scandinavian raiding and, later in the ninth century, Magyar incursions, and the practical limits of Carolingian government already visible in this chapter combined to produce a very different political landscape across ninth and early tenth century western Europe, a landscape from which the durable kingdoms of England, France, and the German lands, examined in Chapter 33, would eventually emerge.
