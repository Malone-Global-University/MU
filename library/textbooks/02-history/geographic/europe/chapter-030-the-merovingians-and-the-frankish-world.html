The Merovingians and the Frankish World

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

When Clovis died in 511, he left behind not a single Frankish kingdom but a family, a treasury, a following of armed men, and a set of claims to territory that his four surviving sons would divide among themselves. For the next two and a half centuries, the Frankish world would be governed, again and again, through division and negotiation among members of a single royal house, rather than through the steady, centralized rule of one uninterrupted line of kings. Understanding that world means setting aside the assumption that a kingdom, once formed, should behave like a modern state.

Picture a royal estate somewhere in the Frankish countryside, perhaps near Soissons or Metz or Paris, in the middle years of the sixth century. The king and his household are not settled permanently in a single capital, since Merovingian rulers moved seasonally among royal villas, hunting lodges, and fortified residences, consuming the produce of their own estates as they traveled and holding court wherever they happened to be. Aristocratic retainers gather around the king's table, bishops arrive with petitions concerning church property or disputed elections, and messengers bring word of a rival kinsman's movements in a neighboring kingdom. A queen, perhaps ruling as regent for a young son, receives envoys from a Lombard duke or a Visigothic court, negotiating a marriage alliance that will bind two royal houses together, at least for a time. None of this resembles the bureaucratic apparatus of the later Roman Empire examined in Part III and Part IV, nor does it resemble the centralized monarchies that would eventually develop in later medieval western Europe, examined in Part VII. It is, instead, a distinctive form of early medieval government, built on kinship, loyalty, land, treasure, and the sacred prestige attached to a single royal dynasty.

Chapter 29 introduced the Franks as one of several post-Roman successor peoples who built new kingdoms from the wreckage of western Roman imperial authority, alongside the Ostrogoths in Italy, the Visigoths in Iberia, and the Anglo-Saxons in Britain. That chapter carried the Frankish story only as far as the reign and conversion of Clovis, whose baptism, discussed there, marked an important moment in the alignment of Frankish kingship with Catholic Christianity. This chapter now asks a more demanding question: once Clovis had died and his kingdom had passed, according to Frankish custom, to his sons rather than to a single heir, how did the Frankish political and social world actually function across the following two centuries, until the Merovingian dynasty itself was finally set aside in 751?

The answer this chapter develops rejects a once common but seriously misleading narrative, in which a vigorous founding king, Clovis, gave way to a succession of increasingly feeble descendants, sometimes called, in later and hostile sources, the "do-nothing kings," until a more capable aristocratic family, the ancestors of the Carolingians, stepped in to rescue a failing kingdom. That narrative, examined critically later in this chapter, derives substantially from sources written under Carolingian patronage, after the fact, to justify a change of dynasty. A more careful reading of the evidence shows something different and, in many respects, more historically interesting: a kingdom that repeatedly divided among royal heirs without dissolving, that depended at every level on cooperation and conflict between kings and powerful aristocratic families, that Christianized gradually and unevenly through the labor of bishops and monastic founders rather than through a single decisive conversion, and that maintained active, often violent, relationships with neighboring peoples across a very large part of what is now France, western Germany, the Low Countries, and Switzerland.

This chapter examines Merovingian kingship itself, the practical mechanics of a itinerant, personal, treasure based monarchy. It traces the emergence of distinct regional Frankish kingdoms, Austrasia in the northeast, Neustria in the northwest, and Burgundy in the southeast, alongside a more loosely governed Aquitaine in the south, regions that should not be mistaken for the later medieval or modern states that would eventually occupy roughly similar territory. It examines the aristocratic families, counts, and dukes whose landed wealth and military followings made them indispensable partners, and frequent rivals, of Merovingian kings. It gives serious attention to Frankish queens, including Brunhild and Fredegund, whose political influence was real and consequential even though our principal narrative source, the bishop and historian Gregory of Tours, describes them through a heavily moralized and partisan lens. It examines the slow, uneven Christianization of Frankish society through bishops, church councils, and an expanding network of monasteries, several of them founded under the influence of the Irish monk Columbanus. It considers ordinary social and economic life within the Frankish kingdoms, and it examines Frankish relations with neighboring Visigoths, Lombards, Bretons, Saxons, Frisians, Alamans, Bavarians, and the distant eastern Roman world. Finally, it traces the gradual concentration of political power in the office of the mayor of the palace, a development that set the stage for the Carolingian family's eventual assumption of royal power in 751, a transition that Chapter 31 will pick up as it turns to Charlemagne and the Carolingian Empire.

Two cautions should guide students throughout this chapter. First, the Frankish kingdoms examined here were not an early version of modern France, nor was Austrasia an early Germany waiting to separate from a Frankish whole. The regional divisions discussed in this chapter reflected the political geography, aristocratic networks, and dynastic arrangements of their own time, and they shifted repeatedly according to inheritance, conquest, and negotiation, not according to any later national logic. Second, political fragmentation, in the Merovingian case, did not mean political collapse. A kingdom divided among several royal kinsmen could remain, in important respects, a single connected political world, held together by overlapping legal traditions, shared aristocratic families whose landholdings crossed kingdom boundaries, a shared royal dynasty regarded as uniquely legitimate, and a shared, if unevenly developing, Christian church. Learning to see this kind of connected fragmentation, rather than assuming that division automatically meant weakness, is one of the central historical skills this chapter aims to develop.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Merovingian kingship actually functioned, including royal households, itinerant rulership, treasure, and gift giving.
  2. Trace the practice of dynastic partition among Clovis's heirs and evaluate why it did not automatically dissolve the Frankish kingdom.
  3. Distinguish Austrasia, Neustria, Burgundy, and Aquitaine as historically specific, changing regional political communities, rather than as embryonic nation-states.
  4. Analyze the expansion of Frankish power into Burgundy, Thuringia, Provence, and Alamannic and other neighboring territories.
  5. Assess the relationship between Merovingian kings and aristocratic families, counts, and dukes as one of mutual dependence as well as competition.
  6. Evaluate the political roles of Merovingian queens, including Brunhild and Fredegund, while accounting for the biases of Gregory of Tours as a source.
  7. Describe the gradual, uneven Christianization of Frankish society through bishops, church councils, and royal patronage.
  8. Explain the role of monasteries, including Columbanian foundations such as Luxeuil, as religious communities, landholders, and political networks.
  9. Describe social and economic life within the Frankish kingdoms, including estates, dependent labor, towns, and the transformation of Roman social patterns.
  10. Contextualize Frankish relations with the Visigoths, Lombards, Bretons, Saxons, Frisians, Alamans, Bavarians, and the eastern Roman world.
  11. Trace the growth of the mayoralty of the palace and evaluate why regional aristocratic offices gained increasing political weight.
  12. Critically evaluate the traditional "do-nothing kings" narrative and explain how it reflects later Carolingian political interests.

Historical Context

Chapter 29 traced the emergence of several post-Roman successor kingdoms across western Europe, including Ostrogothic Italy, Visigothic Iberia, Anglo-Saxon Britain, and Frankish Gaul, and examined how their rulers adapted, selectively, Roman administrative practices, law, and, in several cases, Roman Christianity, to govern populations that mixed newly arrived warrior elites with much larger numbers of provincial inhabitants whose families had lived under Roman rule for centuries. Clovis, ruler of the Salian Franks from the 480s, expanded Frankish power across much of Gaul through a combination of conquest, alliance, and the elimination of rival Frankish and Roman warlords, and, in a decision of lasting importance discussed in Chapter 29, accepted Catholic baptism rather than the Arian Christianity practiced by several neighboring Gothic peoples, a choice that aligned Frankish kingship with the Gallo-Roman episcopate and, over time, with the papacy.

When Clovis died in 511, Frankish custom, so far as it can be reconstructed from later evidence, treated the kingdom as a form of family property to be divided among surviving male heirs, rather than as an indivisible territorial state to be inherited whole by a single successor. This principle of partition, sometimes described by the Latin term used in later sources, distinguished Frankish practice from Roman imperial ideas of a single, indivisible imperial office, even though Merovingian kings continued to draw selectively on Roman administrative vocabulary, Roman law for their non-Frankish subjects, and Roman civic institutions where these remained useful. Clovis's four sons, Theuderic, Chlodomer, Childebert, and Chlothar, therefore divided the kingdom among themselves, each ruling a share of Frankish territory from his own principal residence, while continuing, at least in some periods, to cooperate in joint military campaigns and to regard themselves as members of a single royal family with a shared claim to rule all Franks.

This chapter's task is to explain what happened across the two centuries that followed this initial partition, a period during which the kingdom repeatedly divided, briefly reunited, divided again, and gradually developed regional political identities centered on Austrasia, Neustria, and Burgundy, while Aquitaine in the south remained more loosely and unevenly integrated into Frankish rule. Throughout this period, the Merovingian dynasty itself, however its members might fight one another, retained a distinctive and, so far as the evidence suggests, widely accepted claim to exclusive royal legitimacy among the Franks. That claim was tested at least once before 751, in the mid-seventh century episode involving the mayor Grimoald examined in Section 30.11, but the attempt failed and Merovingian kingship was restored, a striking demonstration of the enduring, quasi-sacred prestige attached to Merovingian blood, even as real political power increasingly passed into the hands of aristocratic officeholders, examined later in this chapter.

The evidence available for this period is considerably richer, in certain respects, than the evidence available for the earlier post-Roman kingdoms discussed in Chapter 29, chiefly because of the survival of Gregory of Tours's ten book Histories, commonly known by the traditional but somewhat misleading title History of the Franks, composed in the later sixth century by a bishop who was himself deeply involved in the political and ecclesiastical affairs he described. Gregory's work, discussed further in the Primary Source Window below, provides an extraordinarily detailed, though far from neutral, narrative of Frankish political life through the 590s. For the seventh and early eighth centuries, historians rely more heavily on continuations of Gregory's work, saints' lives, charters, church council records, and other administrative documents, sources that, taken together, allow a reasonably detailed reconstruction of Frankish political and religious life even as they present their own interpretive challenges.

Section 30.1

The Frankish World after Clovis, 511

Clovis died in 511 having brought under his authority most of Gaul north of the Loire, along with substantial territory to the south won at the expense of the Visigoths, who retreated after their defeat at Vouillé in 507, discussed in Chapter 29, into a much reduced Iberian kingdom. Frankish custom, as it can be reconstructed from later legal and narrative evidence, did not require that a king's territory pass undivided to a single heir. Instead, the kingdom was treated, in important respects, as family property, to be shared among a ruler's surviving sons. This did not mean that Frankish kingship was regarded as a merely private inheritance in the way a farm or a house might be divided; the royal title itself, and the sacred prestige historians associate with the Merovingian line, could not simply be parceled out. What was divided was territory, revenue, and the practical exercise of royal authority over particular regions and populations, while each of Clovis's sons who received a share was recognized as a legitimate Frankish king in his own right.

Clovis's four surviving sons, Theuderic, Chlodomer, Childebert, and Chlothar, therefore each inherited a portion of the kingdom, generally organized around a principal royal residence: Theuderic at Reims, Chlodomer at Orléans, Childebert at Paris, and Chlothar at Soissons. It is important for students to resist the temptation to read this division as the founding moment of later medieval or modern territorial states. The boundaries between these initial shares were neither fixed nor permanent, and the brothers continued, in various combinations, to cooperate in joint military campaigns, including further expansion into Burgundian and Thuringian territory examined in Section 30.4, even as they also, at times, turned against one another and against the surviving heirs of their own brothers when death or ambition offered the opportunity to expand their own share of the kingdom.

This pattern, division followed by opportunistic reconsolidation through inheritance, conquest, or the elimination of rival kinsmen, recurred throughout Merovingian history and should be understood as a structural feature of Frankish kingship rather than as evidence of dysfunction. A kingdom that could be divided could also, given the right combination of deaths, victories, and alliances, be reassembled, and Frankish history across the sixth and seventh centuries repeatedly moved between these two states, fragmentation and reunification, without the underlying political system itself collapsing. Understanding this rhythm is essential preparation for the material examined throughout the remainder of this chapter.

Section 30.2

Merovingian Kingship and the Royal Household

Merovingian kingship rested on a combination of dynastic legitimacy, military leadership, personal wealth, and reciprocal relationships with a following of aristocratic supporters, rather than on the bureaucratic, territorially defined sovereignty associated with later states. A Merovingian king's authority derived, in the first instance, from his membership in the Merovingian family, a lineage whose members were regarded, in ways not fully explained by surviving sources, as uniquely entitled to rule the Franks. This dynastic legitimacy was reinforced, though not created, by long hair, a distinctive marker of Merovingian royal status; cutting a Merovingian prince's hair could function as a way of disqualifying him from future kingship without necessarily killing him, a practice that illustrates how deeply embedded ideas of royal legitimacy were in visible bodily symbols as well as in blood descent. A later origin legend, recorded by the seventh century chronicler known as Fredegar, traced the Merovingian line to a supernatural union between a queen and a sea creature. Historians today treat this story as a later literary and political construction, reflecting how subsequent generations sought to explain and dramatize Merovingian distinctiveness, rather than as reliable evidence of what sixth century Franks actually believed about their kings.

Merovingian kings ruled without a fixed capital, moving seasonally among a network of royal estates, called villas, which supplied the royal household with food, drink, and other necessities as the king and his retinue traveled through his territory. This itinerant style of kingship served several practical purposes: it allowed a king to consume the produce of his own estates without the logistical burden of transporting that produce to a single fixed residence, it enabled the king to display his authority and dispense justice directly to different regional populations, and it allowed him to maintain personal contact with local aristocrats, bishops, and other notable figures whose cooperation his government required.

Royal wealth rested substantially on the royal fisc, the accumulated landed estates that had passed into Merovingian hands through conquest, confiscation, the absorption of former Roman imperial and municipal property, and inheritance. Kings used this landed wealth, along with treasure in the form of gold, silver, and precious objects, to reward loyal followers, a practice central to the reciprocal bond between a Merovingian king and his aristocratic supporters. A king who could not generate sufficient land and treasure to reward his followers risked losing their loyalty to a more generous rival, a dynamic that historians have identified as a significant driver of Merovingian civil conflict, since aristocratic support could shift toward whichever royal claimant appeared most capable of providing land, office, and plunder.

The royal household itself included a variety of officers whose titles echoed, though did not precisely reproduce, late Roman administrative terminology: a seneschal responsible for household provisioning, a constable responsible for horses and, eventually, military organization, a chamberlain responsible for the royal treasury, and, of increasing importance across the period examined in this chapter, a mayor of the palace, whose office is examined at length in Section 30.11. Kings also convened periodic assemblies of leading aristocrats and, at times, bishops, at which major decisions, including matters of war, law, and succession, might be discussed and legitimized, even though final decision making authority rested with the king rather than with any formal representative institution comparable to the later assemblies examined in Part VII.

Section 30.3

Partition, Reunion, and the Regional Kingdoms

Across the sixth and seventh centuries, the Frankish realm gradually developed three principal, recurring regional kingdoms, Austrasia, Neustria, and Burgundy, alongside a more loosely integrated Aquitaine in the south. Students should approach these names with considerable caution. They did not correspond to fixed, permanently bounded territories, nor did they possess the kind of institutional continuity associated with later medieval or modern states. Rather, they named political communities centered on particular royal courts, aristocratic networks, and regional interests, communities whose precise territorial extent shifted with each new partition, war, or dynastic settlement.

Austrasia, generally centered on Reims, Metz, and the Rhine and Meuse river valleys, represented the northeastern portion of Frankish territory, a region whose aristocracy, examined further in Section 30.5, would eventually produce the Pippinid family that founded the Carolingian dynasty. Neustria, generally centered on Paris, Soissons, and the Seine basin, represented the northwestern portion of Frankish territory and, at various points, served as the most consistently powerful of the regional kingdoms, particularly under Chlothar II and Dagobert I. Burgundy, incorporating territory won from the former Burgundian kingdom discussed in Chapter 29 and centered on the Rhône and Saône valleys, represented a southeastern Frankish sphere with its own distinct aristocratic and legal traditions, since Burgundian law remained an important legal reference point within the region alongside Frankish and Roman-derived law rather than being replaced outright.

Aquitaine, the extensive, predominantly Latin speaking region of southwestern Gaul won from the Visigoths, occupied a distinctive position within the Frankish world. Frankish kings claimed authority over Aquitaine and periodically campaigned there to enforce that claim, but the region's Gallo-Roman aristocracy retained considerable local autonomy, its own cultural traditions, and, at various points, effectively independent political leadership, a pattern that would continue to shape Aquitanian relations with Frankish, and later Carolingian, royal authority well into the eighth century, examined further in connection with Charles Martel's campaigns in Section 30.12.

Students should resist any temptation to read Austrasia as an early Germany and Neustria as an early France, a projection of much later national categories onto a political landscape that operated according to entirely different logics of dynasty, aristocratic network, and regional interest. Individuals and families frequently held land and office across the boundaries between these regional kingdoms, and the kingdoms themselves were periodically reunited under a single Merovingian ruler, as occurred under Chlothar I in 558, under Chlothar II after 613, and under Dagobert I in the 620s and 630s, before dividing again upon a king's death or through renewed civil conflict among his heirs. This recurring cycle of division and reunification is best understood not as evidence of a failing state but as the normal operating rhythm of Merovingian dynastic government.

Section 30.4

Conquest and the Expansion of Frankish Power

Clovis's sons, cooperating in some campaigns and acting independently in others, substantially extended Frankish authority beyond the territory their father had controlled at his death. In the 520s and 530s, Frankish forces conquered the Burgundian kingdom, discussed in Chapter 29, bringing its territory and, over time, much of its aristocracy into the Frankish orbit, with Burgundian law remaining an important legal tradition within the region alongside Frankish and Roman-derived law. Frankish forces also campaigned against the Thuringians, a people settled in central Germany, incorporating much of their territory into the Frankish sphere by the 530s, and against the Alamans, whose territory along the upper Rhine had already been partially subordinated under Clovis and was more fully absorbed under his successors.

Frankish kings also extended their influence southward into Provence, territory that had remained under Ostrogothic control following the earlier campaigns discussed in Chapter 29, acquiring the region through a combination of military pressure and negotiated agreement with the Ostrogothic monarchy in the 530s. This extension gave the Franks direct access to Mediterranean trade routes through the port of Marseille, a connection of some economic and cultural significance, since it allowed Mediterranean goods, including papyrus, wine, oil, and, until supply networks were disrupted by later Mediterranean political change, letters and manuscripts, to reach the Frankish interior more readily than would otherwise have been possible.

Frankish authority also extended, with varying degrees of firmness, over Bavarian territory to the southeast, where local dukes generally acknowledged a loose Frankish overlordship while retaining substantial local autonomy, a pattern that recurs throughout this chapter's account of Frankish relations with peripheral peoples. This expansion, taken as a whole, meant that by the middle of the sixth century, Frankish kings claimed authority, in varying degrees of practical firmness, over a very large portion of what is now France, western Germany, the Low Countries, and Switzerland, an extent of territory considerably larger than any other contemporary western European successor kingdom. This geographic reach, however, should not be mistaken for uniform administrative control; Frankish authority in more distant regions such as Bavaria, Thuringia, and Aquitaine remained considerably looser than in the Frankish heartlands of northern Gaul, a distinction of real importance for understanding the later history of Frankish, and eventually Carolingian, frontier relations.

Section 30.5

Aristocrats, Counts, Dukes, and Regional Power

No Merovingian king, however extensive his nominal territory, could govern effectively without the active cooperation of powerful landholding aristocratic families whose wealth, military followings, and local influence made them indispensable partners in Frankish government. This relationship between kings and aristocrats should be understood as one of genuine mutual dependence, not simply as a story of royal authority gradually eroded by selfish nobles, a framing this chapter deliberately avoids.

Frankish aristocrats derived their power from several overlapping sources: inherited landed estates, often extensive and scattered across several regions, which generated agricultural surplus and supported bands of armed retainers, a military following sometimes described using the Latin term comitatus; and, increasingly across the period examined in this chapter, royal office, particularly the offices of count and duke. Historians once traced the comitatus directly to an ancient Germanic warband tradition described in earlier Roman ethnographic writing, but this lineage is itself a historiographical claim rather than settled fact, and students should treat it as one interpretive tradition among others rather than as an established continuity stretching unbroken from pre-Roman Germanic society into the Frankish kingdoms.

Royal government at the local level rested substantially on the city, or civitas, inherited from the late Roman administrative map discussed in Part IV, together with its surrounding territory. A count, typically appointed by the king and often drawn from local landholding families, exercised judicial, military, and fiscal authority within a city and its territory on the king's behalf, collecting royal revenue, enforcing royal law, and organizing local military levies, generally working alongside the city's bishop, who frequently came from the same regional aristocratic milieu. A duke exercised broader, more variable authority, often but not exclusively military, over larger frontier or border regions; ducal power should not be understood as a simply higher administrative tier composed of several counties in any fixed or uniform arrangement, since the scope and character of ducal authority varied considerably from one region and period to another, particularly in exposed regions such as the Alamannic, Thuringian, and Aquitanian frontiers.

The relationship between royal government and regional elites received explicit attention in Chlothar II's Edict of Paris, issued in 614 following the reunification of the Frankish kingdoms discussed in Section 30.3. The edict addressed a range of concerns raised by aristocrats and bishops, including the conduct of royal judges, the handling of legal cases involving powerful local figures, and a preference, though not an absolute rule, for appointing counts from within the region they were to govern. Older historical writing sometimes described the edict as a Frankish counterpart to a document such as Magna Carta, imposing binding constitutional limits on royal power. Most historians today reject that framing and instead read the edict as part of Chlothar II's ordinary business of governing through negotiation with regional elites, addressing specific grievances about justice and office holding rather than establishing a fundamentally new relationship between crown and aristocracy.

These offices provided kings with a mechanism for extending their authority into regions they could not personally govern, since a king's own household and itinerant court could reach only a limited number of places at any given time. At the same time, office holding, land, and local prestige tended to concentrate within particular aristocratic families across generations, since a father's local influence, landholding, and following of armed retainers made his sons natural candidates for the same offices their father had held, even though Frankish kingship formally retained the right to appoint counts and dukes as it saw fit. Over time, this tendency toward informal hereditary succession to office strengthened the position of leading aristocratic families relative to any individual king, a development of considerable importance for understanding the eventual rise of the Pippinid mayors of the palace examined in Section 30.11.

Kings, for their part, retained significant tools for managing aristocratic power: the ability to grant or withhold land and office, the ability to summon aristocratic military support for royal campaigns, the ability to arbitrate disputes between rival aristocratic families, and, in more extreme cases, the ability to exile, imprison, or execute aristocrats who directly challenged royal authority. Aristocratic families, correspondingly, needed royal favor to secure office, to defend their landholdings against rival claimants, and to participate in the prestige and material rewards, including plunder from successful military campaigns, that royal service could provide. This reciprocal, often tense relationship between kings and aristocrats runs throughout Merovingian political history and should be understood as a defining structural feature of the Frankish world, rather than as a simple story of royal decline.

Section 30.6

Queens, Dynasties, and Merovingian Political Conflict

Merovingian royal women exercised genuine and, at times, decisive political power within a dynastic system that measured legitimacy through royal blood, marriage alliance, and, for a woman ruling on behalf of a young son, regency. The best documented and most historically significant example is the decades long rivalry between Brunhild, a Visigothic princess who married the Austrasian king Sigebert I, and Fredegund, a woman of far humbler origins who became the wife of the Neustrian king Chilperic I. Their conflict, driven by a combination of personal animosity, dynastic rivalry between the Austrasian and Neustrian courts, and competition over territory and succession, dominates the later books of Gregory of Tours's Histories and continued, through their sons and grandsons, for several decades after both women had themselves died or been killed.

Brunhild's career illustrates the scope of political authority available to a Merovingian queen under favorable circumstances. Following her husband's assassination, she governed as regent for her son and, later, for her grandsons, exercising authority over Austrasian, and at times Burgundian, government for several decades, negotiating with bishops, aristocrats, and foreign courts, and playing a central role in Frankish diplomatic relations with the eastern Roman world and with the Lombard kingdom in Italy. Her long tenure, however, also generated substantial aristocratic resentment, particularly among Austrasian nobles who resented her efforts to concentrate authority in her own hands and to advance her own family's interests, resentment that ultimately contributed to her downfall and execution, ordered by Chlothar II of Neustria, in 613.

Fredegund, for her part, is presented by Gregory of Tours in relentlessly hostile terms, associated in his narrative with murder, conspiracy, and ruthless ambition. Historians today generally treat Gregory's portrait with considerable caution, recognizing that Gregory, as a bishop closely tied to figures within the Austrasian and Burgundian political orbit, had strong reasons to present Fredegund unfavorably, and that surviving evidence for her actual conduct of government, as opposed to Gregory's moral characterization of her personality, remains limited. What can be said with more confidence is that Fredegund, like Brunhild, exercised substantial and sustained political authority as regent for her son, Chlothar II, whose eventual triumph over the Austrasian line and reunification of the Frankish kingdoms in 613 owed a great deal to the political foundations she had built during her own regency.

These examples illustrate a broader pattern worth emphasizing: Merovingian political conflict was not confined to relations among kings, but regularly involved queens, dowager queens, and royal mothers acting as central political actors in their own right, whether managing regencies, negotiating marriage alliances, directing court factions, or, in some documented instances, arranging the deaths of political rivals. This political agency operated within, rather than outside, the patriarchal structures of Frankish dynastic kingship, since a queen's authority depended fundamentally on her relationship to a legitimate Merovingian king, whether as wife, mother, or grandmother, rather than on any independent claim to rule in her own right. Even so, within those structures, royal women exercised real and, at times, decisive power, a fact this chapter treats as central to understanding Merovingian government rather than as a peripheral curiosity.

Section 30.7

Bishops, Christianity, and Frankish Government

Clovis's baptism, discussed in Chapter 29, aligned Frankish kingship with Catholic Christianity and with the Gallo-Roman episcopate, but it did not instantly transform Frankish society into a uniformly Christian one, nor did it settle, once and for all, the relationship between royal authority and church institutions. Christianization across the sixth and seventh centuries proceeded gradually, unevenly across different regions, and through the sustained labor of bishops, church councils, missionary monks, and, increasingly, monastic communities, rather than through any single decisive event.

Bishops occupied a position of considerable importance in Frankish government, particularly because many bishops, including Gregory of Tours himself, were drawn from the same landed Gallo-Roman aristocratic families whose wealth and local influence made them natural partners, and at times rivals, of Frankish kings. A bishop typically governed a diocese centered on a city, exercised authority over church property, clergy, and, in many cases, local charitable institutions, and served as an important intermediary between royal government and urban populations. Frankish kings, recognizing this influence, took an active interest in episcopal appointments, at times intervening directly to secure the election of a preferred candidate, a practice that would generate recurring tension between royal authority and claims to ecclesiastical independence throughout the medieval period, examined further in Part VII in connection with the Investiture Conflict.

Church councils, gatherings of bishops convened, in many cases, with royal encouragement or participation, addressed matters of doctrine, clerical discipline, church property, and, increasingly, the regulation of relations between Christian and Jewish communities within Frankish territory. These councils produced a substantial body of canon law that, together with royal legislation, shaped the legal and institutional framework within which Frankish Christianity developed. At the same time, popular religious practice among the broader Frankish population, including both Gallo-Roman provincials and Frankish settlers, continued to incorporate a wide range of customs, some drawn from pre-Christian tradition, that church councils repeatedly, and not always successfully, sought to regulate or suppress.

Royal patronage of the church took many forms beyond episcopal appointment, including grants of land to churches and monasteries, the endowment of shrines associated with popular saints, and public displays of royal piety intended to reinforce the sacred prestige of Frankish kingship. Saints' cults, in particular, played an important role in Frankish religious and political life, since a shrine associated with a locally venerated saint could become a significant center of pilgrimage, royal patronage, and, at times, political negotiation, as rival parties might seek to resolve disputes or seal agreements at a saint's tomb. This intertwining of royal authority, aristocratic patronage, and popular religious devotion illustrates why Frankish Christianization should be understood as a gradual, socially embedded process rather than a single event completed with Clovis's baptism.

Section 30.8

Monasteries and the Expansion of Religious Networks

Monasteries occupied an increasingly central place in Frankish religious, social, and political life across the period examined in this chapter, functioning simultaneously as communities of religious devotion, substantial landholders, centers of literacy and learning, and nodes within aristocratic and royal patronage networks. Early Frankish monastic foundations drew on a variety of traditions, including earlier Gallo-Roman monastic practice associated with figures such as Martin of Tours, discussed in earlier chapters of this textbook, and increasingly, from the 590s onward, the distinctive tradition introduced by the Irish monk Columbanus.

Columbanus arrived in Frankish territory around 590 and, with the support of the Burgundian court, founded the monastery of Luxeuil, along with several related foundations, introducing a monastic rule notable for its rigorous asceticism and its emphasis on frequent, repeatable penance and confession as an ongoing discipline of religious life, practices that differed in important respects from the somewhat more moderate Benedictine tradition that would eventually become dominant across western monasticism, discussed further in Part VII. Columbanus's position in Burgundy depended substantially on the personal favor of King Theuderic II, and his standing collapsed once he openly criticized the king's household and sexual conduct, a conflict that also entangled him with Brunhild, who by most accounts exercised considerable influence over Theuderic. At the same time, Columbanus's Irish practices, his monastery's unusual degree of independence from local episcopal oversight, and the resentment of Frankish bishops and nobles whose customary authority his foundations bypassed, all contributed to mounting opposition. The combination of royal displeasure and local ecclesiastical hostility led to his expulsion from Burgundian territory in 610, though his monastic foundations continued to flourish and to spawn further daughter houses across the Frankish world and beyond.

Monasteries served functions well beyond purely religious devotion. As substantial landholders, often endowed by royal or aristocratic patrons seeking spiritual benefit, prestige, or a secure institutional home for a family member, monasteries managed agricultural estates, organized dependent labor, and generated significant economic resources. As centers of literacy in a society where reading and writing remained restricted to a relatively narrow elite, monasteries preserved, copied, and, in some cases, produced texts, including saints' lives, liturgical materials, and historical narratives, a role of considerable importance for the preservation of both classical and early medieval textual culture examined further in Part VII. As institutions closely tied to aristocratic and royal patronage, monasteries also functioned as political instruments, providing aristocratic families with a means of consolidating landed wealth within a single institutional framework, a secure retirement or exile destination for royal or aristocratic figures, including deposed rulers or unwanted royal wives, and a network of religious and political influence that could extend across considerable distances through connections between a mother house and its daughter foundations.

The proliferation of Columbanian and other monastic foundations across Frankish territory during the late sixth and seventh centuries therefore represented far more than a purely religious development. It reflected, and reinforced, the close entanglement of aristocratic landed wealth, royal patronage, and Christian institutional life that characterized the Frankish world throughout the period examined in this chapter, an entanglement that would continue to shape Frankish, and later Carolingian, religious and political history well beyond the chronological boundaries of this chapter.

Section 30.9

Society within the Frankish World

Beneath the level of kings, queens, aristocrats, and bishops examined in the preceding sections, the great majority of the Frankish kingdom's population lived as agricultural producers, working land that belonged, in varying arrangements, to themselves, to aristocratic landowners, to the church, or to the crown itself. Rural society combined free peasant landholders, dependent tenants obligated to render labor, produce, or payment to a lord, and enslaved individuals. Rather than a simple linear decline from the scale of Roman era slavery discussed in Part III toward later medieval serfdom, modern scholarship emphasizes that unfreedom within Frankish society took varied and changing forms across this period, including outright slavery, several gradations of dependent tenancy, and status arrangements that are not always easy to translate into modern categories. Students should be careful not to project the more fully developed manorial and seigneurial arrangements examined in Chapter 34 back onto the Merovingian period, since Frankish rural social relations, though they display some continuity with later medieval lordship, had not yet crystallized into the more formalized structures of dependency that would develop across the following centuries.

Roman social and legal categories persisted in modified form within Frankish territory, particularly in regions with substantial Gallo-Roman populations. Historians traditionally describe Frankish law as operating on a principle of personal, rather than strictly territorial, law, with Salic law applying to Franks, Burgundian law within Burgundian territory, and Roman law for Gallo-Roman provincials. This model remains a useful starting point, but students should understand it as a simplified interpretive framework rather than a precise description of daily legal practice. Surviving evidence suggests considerable variation and even apparent contradiction in practice, with royal legislation, Roman-derived legal texts, regional custom, negotiated settlement, local courts, and sworn oaths all functioning as overlapping points of reference, rather than a single, consistently applied system sorting individuals neatly by ethnic or ancestral identity. This complexity required considerable practical accommodation in a society where Franks, Gallo-Romans, Burgundians, and other groups increasingly intermarried and lived alongside one another.

Wergild, a system of compensation payments intended to resolve disputes arising from injury or killing without escalating into prolonged blood feud, formed an important part of Frankish law, with payment amounts varying according to the victim's social status, sex, and legal category, a system that reveals a great deal about the hierarchical assumptions embedded within Frankish law even as it also provided a practical mechanism for containing violence within Frankish society. Towns, though generally smaller and less economically vibrant than their late Roman predecessors, continued to function as episcopal seats, markets, and, in some cases, royal residences, while long distance trade, though reduced compared to the height of Roman Mediterranean commerce, continued to bring goods including Mediterranean wine, oil, and luxury items into Frankish territory, particularly through the port of Marseille and along the Rhône corridor discussed in Section 30.4.

Family and household structures within Frankish society combined elements of Roman and Germanic legal tradition, with inheritance practices, marriage arrangements, and the legal standing of women varying according to social status, legal tradition, and regional custom. Aristocratic women, as Section 30.6 has shown, could exercise substantial political influence, particularly through marriage alliance and regency, while women of more modest social standing faced a considerably narrower range of legal and economic options, though surviving evidence, drawn overwhelmingly from sources produced by and for elite audiences, leaves the experience of ordinary Frankish women, and of the broader rural population generally, only partially visible to modern historians.

Section 30.10

The Franks and Their Neighbors

The Frankish kingdoms did not develop in isolation, but maintained constant, often turbulent relationships with a wide range of neighboring peoples and polities, relationships that combined warfare, diplomacy, marriage alliance, tribute, missionary activity, and commercial exchange in varying combinations depending on the neighbor and the period in question.

Relations with the Visigothic kingdom in Iberia, introduced in Chapter 29, remained significant throughout the Merovingian period, shaped by the memory of Clovis's earlier victory at Vouillé, ongoing Frankish interest in Visigothic held territory in southern Gaul, and marriage alliances, including Brunhild's own Visigothic origins, that periodically linked the two royal houses, sometimes with violent consequences when marriage diplomacy failed to secure lasting peace. Relations with the Lombard kingdom in Italy, also introduced in Chapter 29, similarly combined periods of military conflict, particularly over control of Alpine passes and northern Italian territory, with periods of diplomatic cooperation and marriage alliance, as Frankish and Lombard royal courts each sought advantage in a shifting Italian political landscape that also involved the eastern Roman Empire, examined in Part VI.

To the north and east, Frankish kings maintained a complex, often violent relationship with the Saxons, a loosely organized confederation of peoples settled in northern Germany who resisted Frankish overlordship and Christian missionary activity for centuries, a conflict that would culminate in Charlemagne's much more extensive and violent Saxon wars examined in Chapter 31. Relations with the Frisians, settled along the North Sea coast and important for their control of significant trading networks, similarly combined periods of Frankish military pressure with commercial exchange, since Frisian traders played a significant role in North Sea and Rhine commerce throughout the period examined in this chapter. The Bretons, occupying the Armorican peninsula in the far northwest, maintained a distinctive Brittonic identity and language, resisting full incorporation into Frankish territory while remaining subject to periodic Frankish military campaigns intended to enforce at least nominal overlordship.

To the southeast, Frankish authority over Bavarian and Alamannic territory, discussed in Section 30.4, remained looser than in the Frankish heartland, with local dukes exercising substantial autonomy while acknowledging, at least formally, Frankish overlordship. Frankish kings also maintained diplomatic relations with the distant eastern Roman Empire, examined in Part VI, exchanging embassies, gifts, and, at various points, seeking eastern Roman support against the Lombards or other common rivals, relationships that illustrate how Frankish political horizons extended well beyond the borders of Gaul itself. Taken together, these relationships demonstrate that the Frankish kingdom, however internally divided, functioned as an active participant within a much broader early medieval European and Mediterranean political system, rather than as an isolated successor state concerned only with its own internal affairs.

Section 30.11

Regional Power and the Rise of the Mayors of the Palace

The office of mayor of the palace, originally a relatively modest household position responsible for managing the king's estates and household affairs, grew across the seventh century into the single most powerful office within each Frankish regional kingdom, eventually overshadowing the Merovingian kings themselves in practical political and military authority. This development should not be understood, as older historical accounts sometimes suggested, as a simple story of naturally capable aristocratic mayors stepping in to fill a vacuum left by naturally incompetent kings. Rather, it reflected the structural dynamics already examined in Section 30.5: the tendency of Frankish royal offices to concentrate, informally, within particular aristocratic families across generations, combined with specific political circumstances that allowed certain families, particularly the Pippinid, or Arnulfing, family of Austrasia, to convert their control of the mayoralty into lasting dominance.

Each regional Frankish kingdom, Austrasia, Neustria, and Burgundy, developed its own mayor of the palace, and rivalry among these regional mayoralties frequently mirrored, and at times drove, broader conflict among the Frankish kingdoms themselves. The Austrasian mayoralty came to be dominated, across the seventh century, by a family descending from the union of two earlier aristocratic lines associated with the bishop Arnulf of Metz and the aristocrat Pippin of Landen, a family historians conventionally call Pippinid or Arnulfing, whose landed wealth, ecclesiastical connections, and military following in the Austrasian heartland provided a durable foundation for sustained political influence.

Merovingian exclusive claim to the royal title, though extraordinarily durable, was tested well before 751. In the mid-seventh century, the Austrasian mayor Grimoald reportedly set aside the young king Dagobert II, sending him into exile, and installed his own son, given the royal-sounding name Childebert, on the Austrasian throne, an episode modern historians often call "Grimoald's coup." The precise chronology and course of events remain debated, and the episode is known chiefly through later, sometimes conflicting, narrative and hagiographical sources, but the attempt itself is historically significant: it shows that at least one powerful mayor did try to substitute his own line for the Merovingian dynasty roughly a century before Pepin III succeeded in doing so, and that the attempt failed, with Merovingian kingship subsequently restored, suggesting just how resistant Frankish political culture remained to a non-Merovingian claimant even when a mayor held substantial practical power.

Against that background, the victory Pippin of Herstal, the Austrasian mayor of the palace, won over Neustrian and Burgundian forces at the Battle of Tertry in 687 takes on a more measured significance than older accounts sometimes suggest. Tertry greatly expanded Pippin's influence, allowing him to place his own supporters in positions of authority within Neustria and Burgundy and to exercise unprecedented influence across the reunited Frankish realm. It did not, however, overthrow the existing Merovingian political order or install an entirely new system of government; Pippin worked within, and adapted, the same royal and aristocratic institutions examined throughout this chapter, and Merovingian kings continued to reign, with genuine ceremonial and legal standing, for decades afterward. Later Carolingian era historical writing, composed after 751 to legitimize the new dynasty, tended to dramatize Tertry as the true beginning of Carolingian rule, projecting later certainty backward onto an outcome that was, in 687, still far from settled. What can be said with confidence is that Tertry marked an important stage in the rise of the Pippinid family, commanding growing aristocratic loyalty and controlling an increasing share of land and office, rather than a single decisive moment of takeover.

Pippin of Herstal's son, Charles Martel, consolidated and extended Pippinid influence after a period of succession conflict following Pippin's death in 714, defeating rival claimants and campaigning in Aquitaine, Frisia, and other peripheral regions through sustained military activity across the 720s and 730s. These campaigns, including the notable engagement near Poitiers traditionally dated to 732, though some historians argue for 733 or 734, in which Frankish forces halted a raiding expedition that had advanced from al-Andalus, strengthened Charles Martel's military reputation and his control over Frankish resources, though historians continue to debate the raid's longer term strategic significance, since such raiding activity continued in the region afterward. His campaigns in Aquitaine likewise expanded Frankish, and specifically Pippinid, influence there without securely integrating the region into Frankish government in the way the northern Frankish heartlands were governed; Aquitaine would remain a zone of contested and partial Frankish authority well beyond the period examined in this chapter.

Section 30.12

From Merovingian to Carolingian Power

By the time of Charles Martel's death in 741, the Pippinid family controlled the effective machinery of Frankish government so thoroughly that the Merovingian king himself had become, in practical terms, a largely ceremonial figure, retained on the throne because Merovingian royal blood continued to carry a distinctive legitimacy that even the most powerful Pippinid mayor had not yet claimed to possess in his own right. Charles Martel did not even bother to appoint a new Merovingian king following the death of Theuderic IV in 737, governing the Frankish realm for several years without any king on the throne at all, an arrangement that would have been unthinkable a century earlier and that demonstrates how far practical authority had shifted away from the Merovingian dynasty by the 730s.

Charles Martel's sons, Pepin III, generally known as Pepin the Short, and Carloman, initially continued to govern as joint mayors of the palace, restoring a Merovingian figurehead, Childeric III, to the throne in 743, a decision that suggests the Merovingian name still retained enough residual legitimacy to be worth invoking even at this late stage. Carloman withdrew from political life to enter a monastery in 747, leaving Pepin as sole mayor and effective ruler of the Frankish realm. In 751, Pepin took the decisive step that even Grimoald and the victors of Tertry had stopped short of: with the support of the Frankish aristocracy, Pepin deposed Childeric III, the last Merovingian king, sending him to a monastery, and assumed the Frankish throne in his place. Whether Pepin was also anointed as king at this precise moment remains uncertain and debated; a widely repeated later tradition credits the missionary bishop Boniface with anointing Pepin in 751, but this account rests on considerably later Carolingian era sources, and its reliability for events of that year has been seriously questioned.

What is securely documented is a separate and unambiguous anointing three years later, in 754, when Pope Stephen II traveled to Frankish territory and personally anointed Pepin, along with his young sons Charles, the future Charlemagne, and Carloman, reinforcing the new dynasty's legitimacy through explicit papal sanction and a ritual drawing on biblical precedent for royal consecration. This securely attested 754 anointing, discussed further in Chapter 31 in connection with Frankish relations with the papacy, mattered enormously for Carolingian legitimacy regardless of whatever may or may not have occurred in 751, since it publicly and repeatedly bound the new dynasty's claim to rule to the highest available religious authority in western Christendom.

This transition, though it represented a genuine change of dynasty, should not be understood as an abrupt or inevitable rupture with the Frankish political traditions examined throughout this chapter. Pepin's kingship rested on many of the same foundations that had sustained Merovingian rule: aristocratic support, landed wealth, and military leadership, now reinforced by the new element of church sanctioned anointing to help compensate for the new dynasty's lack of Merovingian blood. The Frankish kingdom that Pepin inherited, with its regional aristocratic networks, its complicated relations with neighboring peoples, its expanding monastic institutions, and its still incompletely integrated southern territories including Aquitaine, remained recognizably the product of the two centuries of Merovingian history examined in this chapter. Chapter 31 now turns to Pepin's son, Charlemagne, whose reign would transform this Frankish inheritance into a far more ambitious project of conquest, governance, and renewed western empire.

Historical Vocabulary
Partition and Regnum

Frankish custom treated the kingdom, or regnum, as a form of family inheritance divisible among a ruler's surviving sons, a principle historians call partition. This differed sharply from later medieval and modern ideas of an indivisible territorial state passed whole to a single heir, and it also differed from Roman imperial ideas of a single, unified imperial office. Partition did not, however, mean that the Frankish kingdom ceased to exist as a connected political world when divided; shared dynasty, law, aristocratic networks, and church institutions continued to link the divided kingdoms, as Sections 30.2 and 30.3 explain.

Historical Vocabulary
The Mayor of the Palace

Originally a household officer responsible for managing a king's estates and domestic affairs, the mayor of the palace became, across the seventh century, an increasingly powerful political and military office within each Frankish regional kingdom. The Pippinid family's growing dominance of this office, reinforced but not created outright by the Battle of Tertry in 687, examined in Sections 30.11 and 30.12, allowed them to exercise expanding practical authority within, rather than simply in place of, the existing Merovingian political order. By the early eighth century, Merovingian kings retained largely formal and ceremonial status while the Pippinid mayors commanded real political and military power. This development illustrates how office, once concentrated within a single powerful aristocratic family, could gradually eclipse the dynastic authority it had originally served, though the process was more incremental, and less complete before 751, than the phrase "eclipse" might suggest on its own.

Primary Source Window

Gregory of Tours, Histories, later sixth century

Creator: Gregory, bishop of Tours from 573 until his death around 594 · Date: composed substantially in the 580s and 590s · Type: A ten book narrative history of Gaul from creation to Gregory's own time, focused especially on sixth century Frankish political and religious affairs

Context: Gregory came from a prominent Gallo-Roman aristocratic and episcopal family and wrote from within, rather than outside, the political world he described, maintaining close relationships with several Frankish kings, including figures associated with the Austrasian and Burgundian courts. His work is our single most detailed narrative source for sixth century Frankish history, but it is also a partisan, moralizing account shaped by his own political relationships and religious commitments.

Paraphrase of themes recurring across Gregory's Histories Across many episodes, Gregory presents Frankish kings and queens as moral actors whose successes and failures reflect divine judgment, praising rulers and bishops who supported the church and who treated Gregory's own see favorably, while condemning rivals, including Fredegund, in strongly moralized terms as instigators of murder and disorder. This is a synthesis of recurring patterns across Gregory's narrative rather than a translation of any single continuous passage.

Analysis: Gregory's Histories remains indispensable for reconstructing sixth century Frankish political events, since no comparably detailed alternative narrative survives. At the same time, students must read Gregory critically: his portrayal of Fredegund, in particular, is shaped by his closer personal and political ties to figures within the Austrasian orbit, and his framework of moral and providential causation should not be mistaken for a neutral, disinterested account of political events. Historians therefore use Gregory's factual reporting, corroborated where possible by charters, church council records, and later continuations, while treating his moral judgments and characterizations as evidence of his own perspective rather than as established fact.

Historical Debate

Were the Later Merovingians Really "Do-Nothing Kings"?

The question: Later medieval and, until recently, much modern historical writing described the Merovingian kings who reigned after Dagobert I as rois fainéants, or "do-nothing kings," feeble figureheads incapable of governing effectively, whose weakness supposedly justified the Carolingian family's eventual seizure of the throne in 751. How accurate is this characterization, and where did it come from?

Interpretation A — The traditional view: genuine royal decline: Some older accounts argue that later Merovingian kings, often coming to the throne as children and dying young, genuinely lacked the personal capacity, experience, and authority to govern effectively, leaving a practical vacuum that capable Pippinid mayors of the palace filled through their own energy and ability, a vacuum that made the eventual change of dynasty in 751 a natural and, in some tellings, even a beneficial development for Frankish government.
Interpretation B — A Carolingian-shaped narrative requiring critical scrutiny: Most current historians argue that the "do-nothing king" characterization derives substantially from sources written under Carolingian patronage after 751, particularly later royal biography and historical writing produced to justify Pepin III's deposition of Childeric III. These historians point out that later Merovingian kings continued to issue charters, receive embassies, and participate in royal ritual, and that the real story is not simple royal incompetence but the structural concentration of landed wealth, military following, and administrative office within a single dominant aristocratic family, the Pippinids, a process examined in Sections 30.5 and 30.11 that made the mayoralty increasingly powerful regardless of any individual king's personal ability.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same basic narrative of declining royal initiative after Dagobert I, but differ sharply in how they explain that decline, whether as a matter of individual royal incapacity or as a structural consequence of aristocratic power concentration that later Carolingian sources exaggerated and moralized for their own dynastic purposes.

Why it matters: Accepting the traditional narrative uncritically risks treating the Carolingian seizure of power as an inevitable correction of Merovingian failure, rather than as a contingent political outcome shaped by specific aristocratic and ecclesiastical alliances examined throughout this chapter.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports treating the "do-nothing king" narrative with skepticism, then explain how the concentration of power in the mayoralty, rather than simple royal incompetence, better accounts for the political changes of the seventh and early eighth centuries.

Multiple Perspectives

Living within the Frankish World

Note: the entries below describe documented historical circumstances associated with each kind of person or community, drawn from surviving evidence discussed throughout this chapter, rather than invented first-person statements.

Documented circumstances of a Merovingian king: Surviving charters, narrative sources, and legal evidence show Merovingian kings distributing land and treasure to aristocratic followers, confirming church privileges, adjudicating disputes brought before the royal court, and traveling among royal estates rather than governing from a single fixed capital, patterns examined in Section 30.2.
Documented circumstances of a regional count: Charter and narrative evidence indicates that counts exercised royal judicial, fiscal, and military authority within a city and its territory, that office holding tended to remain within particular landholding families across generations, and that a count's local standing depended on both royal appointment and pre-existing family wealth and following, examined in Section 30.5.
Documented circumstances of a bishop such as Gregory of Tours: Episcopal correspondence, council records, and narrative sources show bishops defending church property and privileges against royal or aristocratic encroachment, participating in royal court politics, and, in Gregory's case, producing a detailed but partisan narrative shaped by his own ecclesiastical and political relationships, examined in the Primary Source Window above.
Documented circumstances of a rural household: Archaeological and limited documentary evidence suggests that most rural households remained primarily concerned with agricultural production, local obligations owed to a landlord, the church, or the crown, and the practical consequences of military campaigns passing through their region, regardless of which Merovingian king or regional kingdom claimed authority at a given moment, examined further in Section 30.9.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Clovis to the Later Merovingians

What Changed

  • Frankish territory expanded considerably beyond Clovis's original conquests, absorbing Burgundy, Thuringia, Provence, and looser authority over Bavaria and Alamannia
  • Political power shifted gradually from direct royal command toward aristocratic officeholders, especially the mayors of the palace
  • Christian institutions, including bishoprics and monasteries, expanded and deepened their presence across Frankish society
  • The Pippinid family rose from regional Austrasian aristocrats to the dominant political force across much of the Frankish realm

What Stayed the Same

  • Merovingian royal blood retained unique legitimacy until 751, even as practical authority shifted elsewhere
  • Kingship remained personal, itinerant, and dependent on land and treasure rather than bureaucratic administration
  • Kings and aristocrats remained locked in a relationship of mutual dependence rather than simple royal command
  • Regional political communities such as Austrasia, Neustria, and Burgundy persisted as meaningful, if shifting, frameworks throughout the period

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both real structural change and real continuity helps students avoid two opposite errors: treating 751 as an abrupt, unexplained rupture, or assuming that nothing of significance changed between Clovis and Pepin III.

Cause and Effect

The Growth of Aristocratic Power

Long-term causes

The Frankish kingdom depended structurally on aristocratic cooperation, since no king could personally govern such extensive territory, and royal offices such as count and duke tended, informally, to become hereditary within powerful families across generations.

Short-term causes

Repeated royal minorities, contested successions, and the practical need for regents and household officers to manage royal affairs during periods of weak or young kingship gave particular families, especially the Austrasian Pippinids, repeated opportunities to expand their influence.

Effect

By the early eighth century, the Pippinid mayors of the palace exercised effective control over Frankish government, military command, and the distribution of land and office, while Merovingian kings retained only formal royal status.

This chapter's caution

This outcome was not predetermined; it depended on specific political victories, including Tertry in 687, and on the Pippinid family's particular success in combining Austrasian landed wealth, military leadership, and ecclesiastical connections more effectively than rival aristocratic families elsewhere in the Frankish world.

Historical complexity: Other aristocratic families in Neustria and Burgundy pursued similar strategies of office concentration without achieving comparable success, a reminder that structural conditions created opportunity without guaranteeing any single family's triumph.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life in the Frankish Countryside

For most inhabitants of the Frankish kingdoms, daily life centered on agricultural labor, household production, and local community rather than on the dynastic politics that dominate this chapter's narrative sources. Peasant households cultivated grain, tended livestock, and produced textiles and other goods largely for local consumption, supplementing their own subsistence with obligations owed, in varying arrangements, to landlords, the church, or the crown. Villages typically centered on a small church, since parish structures, though not yet as fully developed as they would become in later medieval centuries examined in Part VII, increasingly organized rural religious life around a local priest and a nearby saint's shrine or relic.

Diet, housing, and material culture varied considerably by region and social status, but generally combined cereal grains, dairy products, and, where available, meat and fish, with timber remaining the dominant building material across most of Frankish territory outside major Roman era towns still using stone construction. Legal disputes among ordinary Franks and Gallo-Romans could be addressed through several overlapping mechanisms, including wergild compensation payments, local courts, negotiated settlement, and sworn oaths, rather than through any single dominant procedure, a flexible repertoire that, as Section 30.9 notes, also reveals a good deal about the hierarchical assumptions embedded in Frankish law. Understanding these ordinary patterns of rural life helps ensure that this chapter's necessary attention to kings, queens, bishops, and aristocrats does not obscure the experience of the great majority of the Frankish kingdom's population.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the political and social development of the Frankish world across the two and a half centuries that separated the death of Clovis in 511 from Pepin III's assumption of the Frankish throne in 751. Its central argument has been that repeated dynastic partition among Clovis's heirs did not dissolve the Frankish kingdom, but instead established a recurring pattern of division and reunification that should be understood as the normal operating rhythm of Merovingian government rather than as evidence of political failure.

The chapter examined Merovingian kingship itself as a personal, itinerant form of rule resting on dynastic legitimacy, royal landholding, and the reciprocal distribution of land and treasure to aristocratic followers. It traced the emergence of Austrasia, Neustria, Burgundy, and Aquitaine as distinct but shifting regional political communities, cautioning against reading these divisions as early versions of later national states. It examined the continued military and political expansion of Frankish power into Burgundy, Thuringia, Provence, and neighboring Alamannic and Bavarian territory, and it gave sustained attention to the aristocratic families, counts, and dukes whose landed wealth and local influence made them indispensable, and often difficult, partners of Frankish kings.

The chapter treated Merovingian queens, particularly Brunhild and Fredegund, as central political actors whose regencies and court factions shaped Frankish politics for decades, while cautioning students to read Gregory of Tours's partisan portrayal of these women critically rather than as neutral fact. It examined the gradual, uneven Christianization of Frankish society through bishops, church councils, and royal patronage, and it traced the growing importance of monasteries, particularly Columbanian foundations such as Luxeuil, as religious, economic, and political institutions. It also examined the social world beneath the level of kings and aristocrats, including rural production, dependent labor, coexisting legal traditions, and the wergild system for resolving disputes, and it placed the Frankish kingdoms within a wider network of relationships with Visigoths, Lombards, Saxons, Frisians, Bretons, Bavarians, and the eastern Roman world.

Finally, the chapter traced the growth of the mayoralty of the palace from a household office into an increasingly powerful center of Frankish political and military authority, noting the mid-seventh century Grimoald episode as an early and unsuccessful test of Merovingian dynastic exclusivity, the Pippinid family's expanding influence following the Battle of Tertry in 687, and Pepin III's deposition of the last Merovingian king in 751, followed by Pope Stephen II's securely documented anointing of the new dynasty in 754. The Historical Debate feature examined the traditional "do-nothing king" characterization of the later Merovingians and argued that this narrative reflects Carolingian political interests as much as historical reality, since the real story lies in the structural concentration of aristocratic power within a single dominant family rather than in simple royal incompetence.

These themes, dynastic partition without political collapse, the mutual dependence of kings and aristocrats, the significant political agency of royal women, the gradual and socially embedded character of Christianization, and the contingent rise of the Pippinid mayoralty, prepare students directly for Chapter 31's examination of Charlemagne and the Carolingian Empire, which inherited, and dramatically transformed, the Frankish political and religious world constructed across the Merovingian centuries examined here.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Merovingian
The Frankish royal dynasty descended from Clovis, ruling the Frankish kingdoms from the fifth century until 751.
Regnum
The Latin term for a Frankish kingdom, treated under Frankish custom as divisible family inheritance rather than an indivisible territorial state.
Partition
The Frankish practice of dividing a kingdom's territory among a ruler's surviving sons, a recurring pattern throughout Merovingian history.
Austrasia
The northeastern Frankish regional kingdom, centered on Reims, Metz, and the Rhine and Meuse valleys, whose aristocracy eventually produced the Pippinid dynasty.
Neustria
The northwestern Frankish regional kingdom, centered on Paris, Soissons, and the Seine basin.
Burgundy
The southeastern Frankish regional kingdom, incorporating former Burgundian territory along the Rhône and Saône valleys.
Aquitaine
The largely Latin speaking region of southwestern Gaul, more loosely integrated into Frankish royal authority than the northern kingdoms.
Itinerant kingship
A style of rule in which a king and his household moved seasonally among royal estates rather than governing from a single fixed capital.
Royal fisc
The accumulated landed estates belonging to the Frankish crown, used to support the royal household and reward followers.
Comitatus
A military following of armed retainers attached to a king or aristocrat; historians once traced this directly to an ancient Germanic warband tradition, but that lineage is itself a debated historiographical claim rather than settled fact.
Count
A royal official, typically appointed from local landholding families, exercising judicial, military, and fiscal authority within a city and its surrounding territory on the king's behalf.
Duke
A royal official exercising broader, more variable authority, often but not exclusively military, over a larger frontier or border region, rather than a simply higher tier composed of several fixed counties.
Mayor of the palace
Originally a household officer, this office grew into the most powerful political and military position within each Frankish regional kingdom.
Regency
Rule exercised on behalf of a minor or otherwise incapacitated king, frequently held by a queen mother such as Brunhild or Fredegund.
Wergild
A system of compensation payments intended to help resolve disputes arising from injury or killing, with amounts varying by the victim's social status, used alongside other mechanisms such as courts, negotiation, and sworn oaths.
Salic law
The law code associated with the Salian Franks, traditionally described as applying on a personal rather than strictly territorial basis alongside Burgundian and Roman law, though actual legal practice appears to have been considerably more variable and overlapping than this traditional model suggests.
Episcopal see
The area of ecclesiastical authority governed by a bishop, typically centered on a city.
Monastic rule
A set of guidelines governing the conduct and discipline of a monastic community, such as the rule introduced by Columbanus.
Immunity
A grant exempting church or monastic land from certain royal officials' jurisdiction, strengthening ecclesiastical institutional independence.
Villa
A royal or aristocratic agricultural estate, often serving as a residence during a king's itinerant travels.
Pippinid (Arnulfing)
The Austrasian aristocratic family, ancestors of the Carolingians, that came to dominate the Frankish mayoralty of the palace across the seventh and eighth centuries.
Anointing
A ritual of consecration with holy oil; Pope Stephen II securely anointed Pepin III and his sons in 754 to reinforce the new Carolingian dynasty's legitimacy, though a widely repeated tradition also credits an earlier 751 anointing that rests on less reliable, later evidence.
Do-nothing kings
A traditional, largely Carolingian-derived characterization of later Merovingian rulers as feeble figureheads, examined critically in this chapter's Historical Debate.
Frankish aristocracy
The landholding elite whose wealth, military followings, and office holding made them indispensable partners, and frequent rivals, of Frankish kings.
Gallo-Roman
Referring to the provincial population of Roman Gaul and their descendants, whose legal traditions and aristocratic families remained significant within Frankish society.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the two and a half centuries between Clovis's death and Pepin III's coronation were not simply a waiting period between one famous king and the next. They were, instead, a formative era in which the basic structures of early medieval western European government, personal itinerant kingship, aristocratic officeholding, ecclesiastical patronage, and monastic institution building, were tested, adapted, and, in important respects, permanently reshaped. The Carolingian Empire that Chapter 31 examines did not spring from nothing in 751; it grew directly out of the political, aristocratic, and religious world this chapter has described.

At the same time, this chapter has insisted throughout that Merovingian history should not be read backward from its ending. The eventual Pippinid triumph was not the predetermined outcome of inevitable Merovingian decline, but the contingent result of specific families, specific victories such as Tertry, and specific political choices made across many decades. Reading history in reverse, so that every earlier development seems to point directly toward a known later outcome, risks obscuring the genuine uncertainty and contingency that shaped events as they actually unfolded for the people living through them.

This chapter has also emphasized that Frankish political history cannot be separated from religious, social, and regional history. Bishops, monasteries, queens, counts, and ordinary rural households all shaped, and were shaped by, the dynastic politics that dominate our surviving narrative sources. Recognizing this interconnection equips students to read later chapters' discussions of Carolingian government, Christianization, and aristocratic power with appropriate historical depth, since the patterns established during the Merovingian centuries, partition and reunification, the mutual dependence of kings and aristocrats, and the gradual, uneven spread of Christian institutions, would continue to shape Frankish and later medieval European history for centuries to come.

Chapter 31 now turns to Charlemagne, grandson of Charles Martel and son of Pepin III, whose reign would carry the Frankish inheritance examined in this chapter toward a far more ambitious project of conquest, governance, and renewed western imperial authority. Understanding the Merovingian foundations traced here, the durable dynastic framework, the powerful regional aristocracies, and the expanding Christian institutions, is essential preparation for understanding what Charlemagne would build upon them, and what he would change.
