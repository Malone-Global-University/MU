Class, Gender, Family, Labor, and Social Change

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

A burial mound, a Roman tombstone set up by a freed slave, a manorial court roll, a parish register, a factory wage book, and a twenty-first century household survey record very different worlds. Each, however, answers the same questions: who worked, who owned, who obeyed, who cared for whom, and who could hope to change their place in society.

Most chapters of this textbook have followed rulers, armies, churches, ideas, and states. This chapter turns to the people who sustained those institutions and lived under them: farmers and artisans, enslaved and free workers, wives, widows, children, servants, factory operatives, clerks, pensioners, and migrants. It asks how Europeans organized the most basic features of social life, including the ownership of land and goods, the division of labor, the structure of households, the transmission of property from one generation to the next, and the relations between women and men. These arrangements rarely produced dramatic headlines, yet they determined for most people what a day, a year, and a lifetime contained.

The chapter is the fourth of five comparative chapters that conclude the series. Chapter 140 traced the changing role of Christianity and religious pluralism, Chapter 141 followed the long relationship among war, states, law, and sovereignty, and Chapter 142 connected empire, colonialism, migration, and diaspora. Chapter 143 asks how the political, religious, and imperial transformations examined in those chapters reached into households and workplaces, and how ordinary people, in turn, sustained, bargained with, evaded, or overturned the institutions that governed them. Chapter 144 will then reconsider Europe's changing place in the wider world.

A familiar narrative holds that European society moved in a single direction, from rigid hereditary hierarchy toward individual freedom, equality before the law, and prosperity. This chapter treats that story with caution. Legal emancipation, wage labor, mass schooling, democratic citizenship, and welfare provision did change lives profoundly, and no responsible account can deny it. But hierarchy repeatedly reappeared in new institutional forms. A Roman freedperson, a medieval serf, a journeyman in a guild town, a nineteenth century mill worker, a Soviet collective farmer, and a twenty-first century platform worker each experienced dependency, but their rights, obligations, and opportunities differed fundamentally and cannot be reduced to one category.

The chapter's second concern is the household. For most of European history, the household, rather than the individual wage earner or the factory, was the basic unit of production, consumption, reproduction, education, and care. Agricultural work, textile spinning, brewing, shopkeeping, childrearing, nursing the old and the sick, and the management of property all took place within households, and much of that work, especially women's, left few traces in conventional accounts of economic life. Placing households and unpaid work at the center changes how historians read the history of labor, class, and gender.

Third, the chapter insists on regional comparison. Western, northern, southern, central, eastern, and southeastern Europe did not follow a single social path from which some regions departed late. Serfdom weakened in some regions after the Black Death while it strengthened in others. Marriage and household patterns varied sharply, and industrial development, emancipation, and welfare provision followed different chronologies. Britain and France supply important evidence, but they are not the standard against which other societies are measured.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. It includes archaeology and burials, legal codes and inscriptions, wills and household inventories, manorial and tax records, guild statutes, parish registers, wage accounts, censuses, factory inspectors' reports, workers' memoirs and testimony, welfare statistics, and oral histories. Every such source reflects the purposes of those who created it, and occupational categories in particular conceal as much as they reveal. The chapter therefore pays attention not only to what happened but to how historians know it, and to whose labor and experience the surviving records tend to omit.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among class, legal status, estate, gender, household, and property as analytical categories, and explain why they cannot be applied identically to every period.
  2. Compare forms of dependent labor, including slavery, serfdom, tenancy, wage labor, and contractual service, across different periods and regions of Europe.
  3. Explain how households functioned as units of production, consumption, inheritance, and care, and how household structures varied regionally.
  4. Trace the demographic and social consequences of famine and plague, and explain why labor scarcity produced opposite outcomes in western and eastern Europe.
  5. Analyze the structure of early modern privilege and the limits of social mobility before and after the revolutionary era.
  6. Evaluate competing interpretations of women's work before and during industrialization, including the evidence and its limitations.
  7. Assess how industrialization formed new classes unevenly across Europe without eliminating older forms of work.
  8. Interpret primary sources concerning labor regulation and gender, distinguishing legal prescription from lived experience.
  9. Compare social provision, gender regimes, and mobility in western European welfare states and eastern European communist societies.
  10. Distinguish absolute from relative social mobility and formal equality from lived inequality.
  11. Connect contemporary inequality, care work, and household change to longer historical patterns without treating the present as an inevitable endpoint.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did changing forms of property, labor, and household organization shape opportunities and obligations for different groups across European history?
  2. Why did similar demographic shocks, such as the Black Death, strengthen peasant bargaining power in some regions and intensify coercion in others?
  3. To what extent was the household, rather than the individual, the basic economic unit for most of European history, and what changed as wage labor expanded?
  4. How have historians reconstructed the work of women, children, servants, and enslaved people when official records often ignored or misclassified it?
  5. Did industrialization create a new working class, reorganize an existing one, or both, and why did the answer differ among regions?
  6. How should historians distinguish improved living standards and social mobility from reductions in inequality?
  7. In what ways did welfare states and communist states respond differently to the social demands of the twentieth century, and what did they share?
  8. Why is it misleading to describe European social history as a steady progression from hierarchy toward equality?

Historical Context

The social history synthesized here has been examined chapter by chapter across the whole textbook. Chapters 4 through 6 traced farming, metallurgy, and the early differentiation of wealth and power in prehistoric Europe. Chapters 10, 15, and 18 examined Greek and Roman political orders, citizenship, slavery, and the household. Chapters 34, 42, 43, and 49 followed lordship, agricultural expansion, towns, and everyday life in medieval Europe. Chapters 50 through 52 examined famine, plague, and social unrest. Chapters 85 and 86 turned to population, consumption, gender, and family in the eighteenth century, and Chapters 87 through 90 followed the revolutionary assault on inherited privilege. Chapters 93 through 96, 101, and 102 examined industrialization, class formation, and mass politics, and Chapters 110, 114, 115, 123, and 126 through 130 examined war, economic crisis, authoritarianism, reconstruction, welfare, and social movements. Chapters 136 through 138 concluded the narrative with post-communist transformation, demographic change, and economic crisis.

Chapter 143 does not retell those narratives. It compares them. Its method is synthesis across time and space, and it relies on a particular vocabulary, introduced in Section 143.1, for describing social structures that changed over several thousand years. Some of that vocabulary is modern. The term "class," in its industrial sense, belongs largely to the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and applying it to a Roman landowner or a medieval peasant requires care. Other terms, such as "estate," "household," and "status," reflect the way earlier societies described themselves, though their meanings varied by language and period.

Social history also depends on kinds of evidence that differ from the chronicles and treaties that dominate political narratives. Archaeologists reconstruct inequality from burials, houses, and settlement layouts. Historians reconstruct household composition from tax lists, parish registers, and censuses, and reconstruct work from wage accounts, court records, guild statutes, and inventories. Statisticians and economic historians estimate wages, prices, mortality, and mobility from such sources, and these estimates carry uncertainty that students should keep in view. Occupational labels in particular deserve skepticism, since many households, especially women, combined several tasks that single labels could not capture.

Finally, the chapter's timeframe is uneven by design. It begins with prehistoric social differentiation, spends proportionally more space on the periods for which household and labor evidence is richest, and ends in the early twenty-first century. In accordance with the series' rules for contemporary history, developments after about 1990 are presented with exact dates where possible, distinguished from completed historical processes, and described without prediction. The historical information cutoff for this edition is 20 November 2026.

Division I · Social Hierarchy, Property, and Family in Early Europe · Section 143.1

How Historians Study Society: Class, Status, Gender, and the Household

Social historians begin with vocabulary, because the words used to describe inequality carry assumptions. In ordinary modern speech, "class" refers to a position within an economic order, defined by income, occupation, property, and education. Historians of nineteenth and twentieth century Europe often use it in that sense, sometimes with the more specific Marxist meaning discussed in Section 143.13. For earlier periods the term can mislead. A Roman senator and a Roman freedman might own comparable wealth, yet they differed in legal rank, political access, and social honor. A medieval knight and a wealthy merchant might hold similar incomes while occupying different places in a society organized by estates. Historians therefore distinguish legal status, meaning the rights and duties assigned by law or custom, from economic position, meaning wealth and control of resources, and from social standing, meaning the prestige conferred by birth, office, occupation, or lifestyle. These dimensions often overlapped, but they were not identical, and the relationship among them changed over time.

Gender is a similarly historical category. It refers not simply to biological sex but to the social expectations, legal rules, and economic roles assigned to women and men, which differed by period, region, religion, and social rank. A noblewoman, a peasant widow, a guild master's wife, and a female textile worker shared a gender but not a social position, and gender relations interacted with every other form of hierarchy. The same is true of age. Childhood, youth, adulthood, and old age were socially organized stages with distinct labor obligations, legal capacities, and vulnerabilities, and the life cycle structured work as powerfully as occupation did.

The household is the third foundational concept. In most European societies before the nineteenth century, and in many afterward, people were embedded in households that produced food and goods, consumed together, raised children, cared for the sick and old, and transmitted property. A household was not identical to a family. It might include a married couple and children, but also servants, apprentices, lodgers, enslaved persons, unmarried relatives, or co-resident kin. Conversely, a family might be dispersed across several households. Historians distinguish the household, defined by residence and shared economic life, from the family, defined by kinship and marriage, precisely because the two did not always coincide.

Historical Vocabulary
Class, Status, Estate, and Social Mobility

Class in its modern sense refers to groups defined by their position in an economic order, especially their relationship to property and labor. Status refers to the rank, honor, and legal privileges attached to a person's position, which may or may not correspond to wealth. An estate (French ordre or état, German Stand) was a legally defined social order, most commonly clergy, nobility, and commoners, each with distinct rights and obligations, though many regions recognized more or different orders. Social mobility is movement of individuals or groups between social positions, and historians distinguish absolute mobility, the total movement produced by changes in the occupational structure, from relative mobility, the degree to which a person's origins determine their chances compared with those of others.

These terms matter because applying one to the wrong period distorts interpretation. Calling a medieval peasant "working class" imports an industrial concept, while describing a nineteenth century clerk as a member of an "estate" ignores that the clerk's position depended on education, employment, and income rather than on legal birth rank.

These categories come to historians through sources that were never designed to answer their questions. Archaeologists infer inequality from burial goods, house sizes, and settlement patterns, while legal codes, inscriptions, wills, tax registers, parish baptismal and burial records, guild statutes, wage books, and household inventories supply information about property, kinship, and work. Censuses, from the nineteenth century, introduced standardized occupational categories, and twentieth century oral histories and surveys captured experiences that written records omitted. Each kind of source has biases. Elite writers tended to describe the poor and dependent as a problem or a type rather than as individuals. Tax and census officials counted what they could tax or classify, and they frequently recorded a married woman as dependent on her husband's occupation even when she worked at agriculture, trade, or manufacturing. Unpaid care work, which shaped nearly every household, rarely appeared at all.

The chapter that follows therefore uses a consistent set of comparative questions, introduced here and returned to in its conclusion. Was a person's position determined mainly by law, by property, by occupation, or by their interaction? Was labor free, bound, or coerced, and in what degree? Who owned property, and who controlled it in practice? What work counted as work in contemporary records, and what was left unrecorded? How far did legal equality translate into equal opportunity? These questions allow the long history of European society to be compared without imposing a single narrative of progress.

Section 143.2

Agriculture, Settlement, Property, and Social Stratification in Early Europe

Chapters 4 through 6 examined the spread of farming and the rise of metallurgy. For social history, the central question is what these changes meant for the distribution of resources and power. Hunting and gathering societies were not necessarily egalitarian, but their mobility limited the accumulation of material wealth, and many appear to have emphasized sharing and reciprocity. Farming created durable investments, including cleared fields, stored grain, livestock, houses, and tools, and it made possible both surplus and the unequal control of it. Agricultural settlement also tied households to particular land, increased the importance of inheritance, and made labor, especially of children and women, central to the household economy.

Archaeology suggests, however, that agriculture did not automatically produce hereditary hierarchy. Many Neolithic communities in Europe appear to have lived in villages or farmsteads with relatively modest differences in house size and grave goods, though the evidence varies widely by region and period. Monumental building, such as the megalithic tombs and enclosures of western and northern Europe, shows that communities could organize large collective labor without necessarily implying a rigid ruling class, although specialists debate who directed such labor and how. Social differentiation developed unevenly, and some communities seem to have actively limited the accumulation of individual wealth.

Differentiation became more visible in the Bronze Age. Metal production depended on access to copper and tin, which were unevenly distributed, and bronze weapons, ornaments, and tools circulated through long-distance exchange networks. Burial evidence from many regions includes individuals buried with weapons, ornaments, or imported goods, suggesting leaders, warriors, or elite lineages whose status was publicly displayed. Fortified settlements and hoards of metalwork point to competition and to control of valuable resources. Craft specialization, especially in metalworking, implies that some people derived their livelihood from skills rather than from farming alone, and that elites may have patronized or controlled such specialists.

Students should be cautious about reading these remains too literally. A rich burial may reflect the achieved status of an individual, an inherited position, the wealth a mourning group chose to display, or a ritual practice that does not map neatly onto everyday wealth. Evidence for gender is similarly complex. Skeletal analysis, including more recent DNA and isotopic studies, has revealed unexpected patterns in burial practice and in the movement of people between communities, such as evidence in some regions that women more often moved to their partners' communities after marriage, but such findings are specific to particular sites and times and do not support a single prehistoric model of gender relations. Archaeological interpretation is distinct from the documented legal institutions of later periods, and this chapter does not assume that prehistoric societies possessed anything resembling later law, class, or gender ideology.

The Iron Age societies discussed in Chapter 6 add further variety. Some communities in central and western Europe built large fortified settlements, often called oppida in their later phases, which suggest concentrated populations and political authority. Others remained dispersed farming communities. Many pre-Roman societies kept stratified orders that Greek and Roman writers described in ways shaped by their own assumptions, and those descriptions should be treated as outsiders' interpretations rather than direct evidence of how these societies understood themselves. The consistent lesson is that early European societies developed a range of property systems and social arrangements. The long history of European hierarchy therefore did not begin with a single transformation, but through multiple regional experiments in organizing land, labor, kinship, and authority.

Section 143.3

Greek and Roman Hierarchies: Citizenship, Slavery, and Labor

Greek and Roman societies organized inequality through law as well as wealth. In Greek city-states, the central distinction lay among citizens, resident foreigners (metics in Athens), and enslaved people. Citizenship carried political rights, such as participation in assemblies and courts, and in most cities it was restricted to adult free men of local descent, though the extent of participation and the property qualifications for office varied. Chapter 10 compared Athens and Sparta. In Athens, the democratic citizen body included farmers and artisans as well as the wealthy, while in Sparta a citizen elite relied on the agricultural labor of the helots, a dependent population bound to the land. Citizenship thus coexisted with large unfree populations, and the same city could be democratic for its citizens and exploitative toward those excluded.

Slavery was central, though its scale and role varied. In Athens, enslaved people worked in households, workshops, and the silver mines of Laurion, where conditions were notoriously harsh. Estimates of the enslaved population in classical Athens vary widely among scholars, and historians treat any single figure with caution. Roman society developed slavery on an even larger scale, especially after the conquests of Chapter 17 brought captives from across the Mediterranean and beyond. Roman slaves worked on rural estates, in mines and quarries, in urban workshops and households, and in specialized roles such as teachers, secretaries, physicians, and managers. Their conditions ranged from brutal agricultural gangs to positions of delegated authority in a master's business, though in all cases they were legally property and vulnerable to punishment, sale, and sexual exploitation.

Roman law also created a distinctive route out of slavery. Masters could free enslaved people, and freed persons (liberti) acquired citizenship in many cases, though they remained bound to their former masters by duties of respect and service, and some public offices remained closed to them. The sons of freedmen could rise further. Funerary inscriptions set up by freedpeople, which record trades and family relationships, are among the best evidence for the social lives of non-elites, though they were set up by those relatively successful enough to commemorate themselves, and therefore are not representative of the mass of enslaved people who never achieved freedom.

Among the free, land was the principal source of wealth and prestige in both societies. Greek and Roman elites derived status from landownership, and Roman political life was structured around a census ranking of citizens by property, which determined military and political roles. Small farmers formed the backbone of early republican armies, and Chapter 19 showed how debt, military service, and the growth of large estates strained the republic's social order. In the Empire, urban populations, especially in Rome itself, included artisans, traders, laborers, and many dependent on patronage, the exchange of favors and support between wealthy patrons and their clients. Patronage structured relations among unequals, tying the poor to the rich through obligation rather than formal contract.

Formal categories and lived economic relationships did not map neatly onto one another. A wealthy freedman could employ free citizens, and a poor free man could be economically dependent on a patron. Citizenship was extended across the Roman Empire over several centuries, culminating in the grant of citizenship to most free inhabitants in 212 CE, yet this expansion did not eliminate inequality, and later Roman law increasingly distinguished between honestiores and humiliores, giving the former milder punishments. By Late Antiquity, tenants called coloni were increasingly tied to the land by law and custom, a development whose precise legal meaning and chronology scholars still debate. Antiquity thus bequeathed to later Europe not a single social model but a repertoire, including citizen assemblies, juridical slavery, manumission, patronage, and tenancy.

Section 143.4

Families, Gender Relations, and Property in Classical Antiquity

The Greek oikos was both a household and an estate, comprising people, land, livestock, and tools, and its continuity across generations was a central civic and religious concern. The Roman familia similarly denoted those under the authority of the head of the household, the paterfamilias, including children, other dependents, and enslaved people. Neither institution corresponds to the modern nuclear family. They were property-holding and labor-organizing units in which authority was unequal and often legally concentrated in a senior male.

Marriage in both societies was typically arranged between families and linked to property transfer. In Greek practice, a bride's family commonly provided a dowry, which passed with her into the marital household but remained associated with her, and in Athens a woman could not normally conduct major legal transactions without a male guardian. Women of citizen families were generally expected to manage the household and were excluded from political life, though Greek societies differed. Spartan women, for example, are described by ancient sources as holding and inheriting more property than women in other Greek cities, although these sources were written by outsiders and need careful reading. Women also played significant roles in religious cults and festivals, which provided public visibility otherwise unavailable.

Roman law gave elite women more formal property capacity over time. Under the early forms of marriage a wife could pass into her husband's legal authority, but by the late Republic marriage without such transfer became common, allowing a woman to retain legal links with her own family and to hold property separately, although she continued to require a guardian in many matters until the legal requirement weakened under the Empire. Wealthy Roman women could own land, manage businesses through agents, and act as patrons, though they could not hold office. Divorce was legally accessible. Lawgiving also tried to shape reproduction: Augustus's marriage legislation, discussed in Chapter 21, offered legal privileges to citizens with children and penalized the unmarried and childless. The effect of these laws is debated, and they illustrate the interest of the state in family formation.

Everyday practice for non-elites is harder to see. Poor Greek and Roman women worked in agriculture, textile production, trade, hospitality, and domestic service, and inscriptions and papyri record women as shopkeepers, midwives, nurses, and artisans. Egypt under Roman rule supplies unusually rich documentary evidence in papyri, including marriage contracts, tax lists, and household registrations, which show a wider range of arrangements than literary sources do, although Egypt cannot stand for the entire empire. Children worked from an early age, and the Roman law of patria potestas, which gave fathers extensive authority over children, appears in practice to have been tempered by custom, mortality, and the possibility of emancipation. Because many fathers died while their children were still young, many Roman individuals reached adulthood legally independent.

The household also reproduced status. Citizens transmitted property to heirs, enslaved people lived within the same walls as free members, and adoption allowed elite families to secure heirs and political alliances, as the Julio-Claudian dynasty shows. Education, religious ritual, and care of the old occurred largely within households. The central conclusion for this section is that ancient families were neither uniform nor equivalent to modern nuclear households. They varied by city, period, wealth, and legal status, and their property rules and gender expectations were tools of social continuity as well as sources of vulnerability for women, children, and the enslaved.

Post-Roman Communities, Lordship, and Medieval Rural Life

The fragmentation of western Roman authority, examined in Chapters 27 and 28, changed the framework of rural society without erasing the agricultural communities that sustained it. Large Roman estates, villas, and the dependent tenants who worked them did not disappear overnight, but the taxation, markets, and legal institutions that had linked them to an empire weakened. In many regions landholding passed to Frankish, Gothic, Anglo-Saxon, and other elites, to churches, and to monasteries, and the relations between those who held land and those who worked it were reorganized through local custom, personal ties, and obligations of service. Historians debate how much continuity existed with late Roman estate structures, and the answer varies regionally, with greater continuity in parts of Italy and southern Gaul than in northern Britain.

Lordship, in the social sense used here, refers to the authority of those who held land and exercised rights over the people who lived on it. This chapter distinguishes it from the broader political structures examined in Chapter 141. A lord might collect rents in produce, money, or labor, hold a court that judged disputes, claim exclusive rights over mills and ovens, and offer protection in exchange. Peasants cultivated holdings that supported their own households while owing obligations that might include labor on the lord's own land, payments at inheritance or marriage, and the use of the lord's facilities. These obligations were negotiated and recorded in custom, and customary law proved resilient. Where evidence survives, such as in monastic estate surveys of the Carolingian period (for example, polyptychs recording tenants and dues), it shows considerable variety in the amount and kind of burdens, even on the estates of a single institution.

Monasteries were also major social institutions. They held land, employed or ruled dependent farmers, preserved records, and supported literate and charitable activity. They offered some women, especially those from elite families, alternative careers as abbesses and nuns, and some religious houses exercised considerable economic and political influence. Villages, meanwhile, developed communal institutions, including shared pasture, regulation of common fields, and local assemblies, which gave rural households some collective capacity to protect their interests.

Comparison with Byzantine and other eastern societies is essential. In the Byzantine Empire, discussed in Chapters 36 and 37, free village communities of smallholding farmers remained important for centuries, and the state treated them as a basis for taxation and military recruitment. Over time, aristocratic and monastic landowners expanded their holdings, and rulers periodically legislated to protect smallholders against encroachment, with mixed effect. The experience differed from western lordship because the imperial state retained greater fiscal capacity, yet in the later Middle Ages dependent peasants known as paroikoi became increasingly prominent on large estates. In the Slavic and steppe borderlands, discussed in Chapters 38 and 39, social orders also varied, and the Kievan Rus polities included princes, retinues, free and dependent rural populations, and enslaved persons.

Slavery also persisted. Although the dominant medieval image is of serfs rather than slaves, enslaved people continued to be bought, sold, and used in the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds, and in parts of Latin Europe, through the Middle Ages. Evidence from Italian port cities, Iberia, and the Black Sea trade shows enslaved domestic servants and laborers, many of them women, a point that complicates narratives in which slavery disappears after antiquity and returns only with Atlantic expansion, examined in Chapter 70. The social order of early medieval Europe was therefore not a simple pyramid of lords and serfs, but a patchwork of free smallholders, dependent tenants, enslaved persons, monastic dependents, and aristocratic households.

Section 143.6

Serfdom, Land, and the Unequal Geography of Peasant Obligation

The medieval peasantry was not one class. Some peasants were legally free and held land for rent. Others, in many western regions, were serfs, bound by hereditary dependency to a lord, who could be required to perform labor, pay fines on marriage or inheritance, and in some cases was restricted in movement. Between these categories lay wide variation. Many free tenants paid customary rents, small proprietors owned their plots, and landless laborers worked for wages or in return for subsistence. Prosperous peasants, sometimes called yeomen or by regional equivalents, could rent or purchase additional land, employ labor, and send children to school. Historians accordingly avoid portraying all peasants as uniformly impoverished and immobile.

Historical Vocabulary
Serfdom, Tenancy, and Peasant Dependency

Serfdom is a term that historians apply to forms of hereditary rural dependency in which cultivators held land from a lord and owed labor, payments, and legal subjection, often with restrictions on leaving the land or marrying without permission. It was not slavery, because serfs were not typically owned as chattels to be sold apart from their land in the same way, though in some regions, notably in the Russian Empire in the eighteenth century, serfs could be bought and sold with and sometimes without land, which narrowed the distinction. Tenancy describes holding land by agreement with a landlord in return for rent, which could be fixed, share-based (sharecropping), or customary, and tenants could be free. Dependency is the broader category covering any relationship in which a person's livelihood or legal capacity depended on another's authority.

"Serfdom" was not a single system. Its meaning in thirteenth century England, eighteenth century Bohemia, and nineteenth century Russia differed in law, burden, and enforcement, which is why historians discuss serfdoms in the plural.

Geography mattered. In much of western Europe, personal serfdom had declined by the later Middle Ages, particularly in England, northern France, and the Low Countries, where commutation of labor services into money rents, the growth of markets, and peasant bargaining weakened lordly claims, though other burdens persisted. In parts of Germany, Spain, and elsewhere, forms of dependence persisted in modified form. In Scandinavia, relatively free peasant landholding was more common, and Sweden's peasants had representation in the Riksdag. Southern Europe presented its own arrangements, including large estates worked by laborers or tenants and, in central Italy, the sharecropping system known as mezzadria, in which families farmed a landlord's holding in return for a share of the produce.

In central and eastern Europe, rural dependency took a different course. From roughly the sixteenth century, landowners in Poland-Lithuania, Bohemia, Hungary, the Baltic provinces, Brandenburg and eastern German territories, and Russia increased their demands on peasants, tied them more tightly to the land, and expanded demesne production for export markets, especially grain sold to western Europe. The phrase "second serfdom" is commonly used for this development, though historians debate it. Some emphasize that the term implies a revival of an earlier medieval condition when in fact the new regimes were distinct, and others point to major regional differences, including in the strength of the state, the role of towns, and the bargaining power of landlords, which explain why the intensity of dependency varied. In Russia, legal enserfment was consolidated in the Law Code of 1649, and serfdom persisted until the emancipation of 1861, which Section 143.12 addresses.

Peasants were not passive. They negotiated, litigated, petitioned, fled, withheld labor, hid produce, and, at times, rebelled. Manorial court rolls in England and parallel records elsewhere show peasants contesting obligations in court. The Jacquerie of 1358 in France and the German Peasants' War of 1524–1526 were large uprisings, with demands that combined economic grievances, claims about customary rights, and religious arguments, and they were met with brutal suppression. Later rebellions, such as the Pugachev revolt in the Russian Empire (1773–1775), likewise show that coerced rural populations could mount large-scale resistance. Yet the same sources record countless quieter strategies, including appeals to princes or kings over the heads of local lords, which in some states gave rulers a political reason to regulate lordly claims, as Habsburg reformers did in the eighteenth century.

Southeastern Europe supplies a further comparison. Under Ottoman rule, rural society included smallholding peasants, many of them Christian, who paid taxes and dues to the state and to holders of revenue grants, and who in some periods enjoyed greater legal protection against arbitrary exaction than many peasants elsewhere. Over time, large private estates (çiftliks) expanded, especially in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, increasing the dependence of cultivators. This variety is the key point of the section: the geography of rural dependency in Europe was uneven, historically changing, and shaped by markets, states, demography, and the bargaining of peasants themselves.

Section 143.7

Towns, Guilds, Merchants, and Urban Social Divisions

Chapter 43 traced the commercial revival and the growth of towns. For the social historian, medieval towns were spaces of both opportunity and exclusion. Urban populations included merchants and bankers, master artisans, apprentices and journeymen, wage laborers, domestic servants, clergy, students, soldiers, beggars, and migrants from the countryside. A common saying that town air made one free reflects the fact that in many regions serfs who lived in a town for a year and a day might acquire freedom, though that principle was not universal and was unevenly enforced. Towns offered chances for mobility, but their governing elites were often narrow oligarchies of wealthy families who monopolized office and political privileges.

Guilds were central to urban economic life. They were associations of craftspeople or merchants that regulated entry, set standards of quality, controlled prices and training, provided mutual aid, and held religious and social functions. A boy or girl entered a trade as an apprentice, usually living in a master's household for several years, and then, in many places, worked as a journeyman before attempting to become a master. The system promised stability and skill, and it also served to restrict competition. Economic historians debate its effects. Some emphasize that guilds provided training, quality assurance, and social insurance in societies lacking public institutions, while others, including Sheilagh Ogilvie, argue that they protected insiders at the expense of outsiders and slowed innovation. The evidence suggests that guild strength and function varied considerably among cities, trades, and periods.

Access to guilds and trades was unequal. Women participated in the urban economy as brewers, bakers, spinners, weavers, retailers, nurses, and moneylenders, often as wives or widows working within a master's household, and some guilds admitted women as members or allowed widows to continue a deceased husband's workshop. Judith Bennett and others have shown, however, that women's opportunities in many trades narrowed in the later medieval and early modern centuries, as guilds and urban regulation increasingly restricted women's independent participation. Religious minorities also faced restrictions. Jews were often excluded from guilds and from many forms of landholding, and many were concentrated in trades such as moneylending, medicine, and trade in which their legal status provided limited and revocable protection. Migrants and the poor likewise lacked full membership in the civic community, and charitable institutions such as hospitals and confraternities supported some while distinguishing between the "deserving" and "undeserving" poor.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Urban Household: Work, Money, Food, and Survival

A master craftsman's household in a late medieval or early modern town was typically both home and workplace. Production often took place on the ground floor or in an adjoining workshop, and family members, apprentices, journeymen, and servants worked and sometimes ate together. A master's wife frequently managed accounts, supervised servants, sold goods, and ran the household, tasks essential to the enterprise even where guild regulations did not recognize them as separate trades. Apprentices, often adolescents, depended on the master for lodging, food, and training, and their treatment depended heavily on individual masters and on the contract that bound them.

Diets varied by wealth and region. Bread and grain-based dishes, supplemented by vegetables, dairy, fish, and, for the better off, meat, formed the basis of most diets, with regional staples and drinks, such as beer in northern towns and wine in southern ones. Housing was often crowded, with several households sharing buildings, and fuel, water, and food were purchased in markets. Wages, whether paid in cash or partly in kind, rose and fell with harvests and demographic change, and a harvest failure could push artisans' households into poverty. Households coped through multiple strategies: taking in lodgers, sending children to work, borrowing, pawning goods, relying on neighbors, and turning to charity. The poor, widows, and elderly without kin were particularly vulnerable. These patterns remind students that urban growth did not abolish older hierarchies, but reproduced them in new forms, with distinctions of wealth, citizenship, gender, and religion structuring everyday survival.

Differences among towns also mattered. Great commercial and financial centers such as Venice, Florence, Bruges, Antwerp, and the Hanseatic cities hosted wealthy merchant elites and large wage-earning populations, particularly in textile production, while small market towns served surrounding agricultural districts and had limited internal differentiation. Urban revolts, such as the Ciompi uprising in Florence in 1378, in which wool workers without guild representation demanded political voice, show that excluded workers could challenge civic hierarchies, though such challenges were frequently suppressed. Urban society, in short, combined collective institutions with sharp inequality, and its history cannot be told as the simple replacement of rural hierarchy by civic freedom.

Section 143.8

Marriage, Kinship, Inheritance, and Medieval Household Organization

The Church claimed increasing authority over marriage during the Middle Ages, insisting from the twelfth century on that valid marriage required the consent of both parties, and gradually promoting public ceremony and registration, with the decrees of the Council of Trent, discussed in Chapter 65, requiring formal publicity in Catholic regions in the sixteenth century. Secular law governed property and inheritance, and local custom shaped practice. Families used marriage to form alliances, transfer property, and secure heirs, yet couples also made choices, and church courts recorded disputes over promises of marriage that show the interplay of family strategy and individual preference.

Inheritance rules shaped households and mobility. In some regions, property passed to a single heir, often the eldest son, with others receiving smaller portions, apprenticeships, dowries, or the option to migrate, which tended to produce a mobile population of younger children and unmarried adults. In other regions, partible inheritance divided land among children, producing small plots and, over time, pressure to supplement income through craft work or labor. Dowries, widely used in southern and western Europe, transferred property to daughters at marriage, while in some eastern and southern regions, bride-wealth and other arrangements prevailed. Widows might hold rights to a portion of their husband's property, known as dower or by regional terms, and widowhood, which was common, could give women unusual legal and economic independence or leave them dependent on others, depending on custom, wealth, and the presence of children.

One of the most influential models of European household history was proposed by the demographer John Hajnal in 1965. Hajnal observed that in much of northwestern and central Europe, men and women married relatively late (often in their mid-twenties), and a significant proportion never married, while in eastern Europe and much of the non-European world marriage tended to occur earlier and was more nearly universal. He located the dividing line, roughly, between Saint Petersburg and Trieste. Later work associated with the Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure, led by Peter Laslett, added that in northwestern Europe households were mostly small and nuclear, with life-cycle service, in which adolescents lived and worked in other households before marrying, as a widespread institution. The model has been influential in explaining why northwestern Europe may have had relatively flexible population responses to economic conditions.

Scholars have also qualified it substantially. Research has shown considerable variation within the supposed regions: complex and joint households were common in parts of central Italy and southern France, while Balkan societies and parts of Russia and eastern Europe included extended kin groups, such as the zadruga in South Slavic regions and large peasant households in Russia, though historians debate how typical and how persistent these were. David Reher and others have argued that southern Europe's family system emphasized strong ties among generations, while northern systems emphasized the independence of the nuclear unit. Others note that economic status, local inheritance customs, and ethnic or religious differences cut across regional patterns. The model remains useful as a starting point, but it should not be taken to mean that Europe divided into two uniform family systems, nor that "western" patterns were normal and others deviant.

Division III · Demographic Change, Early Modern Work, and Social Transformation · Section 143.9

Plague, Demographic Crisis, and the Reordering of Work

Chapter 50 examined the environmental and demographic catastrophe of the fourteenth century. Here the focus is on its social consequences. By the early fourteenth century, population growth in many regions had pressed on available land, and harvest failures during the Great Famine of 1315–1317 caused widespread hunger and elevated mortality in northwestern Europe, though the precise death toll is uncertain and varied by locality. The Black Death, which reached European ports in 1347 and spread through the continent over the following years, killed a large proportion of the population. Scholars' estimates for Europe as a whole vary substantially, often placing mortality from the first Black Death wave at roughly one-third to one-half of the population, with some reconstructions suggesting even higher losses. Regional and local differences were considerable, and recurrent plague outbreaks over subsequent centuries delayed recovery in many places.

The immediate effect in many regions was labor scarcity. With fewer workers, landlords and employers competed for labor, and wages rose, while land became relatively abundant and rents and land prices in many places fell. Peasants could negotiate better terms, move to more favorable holdings, or abandon the lands of harsh lords, and villages were deserted in significant numbers in some regions. Landlords responded in different ways. In England, the Ordinance of Labourers (1349) and the Statute of Labourers (1351) attempted to freeze wages at pre-plague levels and restrict mobility, and they were enforced, with varying success, through local courts, though market pressures made full enforcement impossible. Similar wage and price regulation appeared in France, Castile, Aragon, and Italian cities, and they show governments responding to the interests of employers and property-owning classes.

The English Peasants' Revolt of 1381 illustrates how plague-era conditions interacted with specific political pressures. Its immediate trigger was the poll taxes of the late 1370s and 1380, but it drew on grievances about labor regulation, the residual burdens of serfdom, and local conflicts with lords and officials, and participants included not only villeins but also artisans and some wealthier villagers. Historians caution against treating it as an inevitable result of the plague. It occurred in a particular political setting, during the minority of Richard II and after costly wars, and comparable crises elsewhere, such as the Jacquerie of 1358 or the Ciompi revolt of 1378, had different causes and outcomes. After 1381, serfdom in England continued to decline, though largely through gradual shifts in landholding and enforcement rather than through the revolt's direct success.

The most striking comparison is between western and eastern Europe. In much of the west, the combination of labor scarcity, peasant bargaining power, and the decline of customary lordly authority contributed to the weakening of serfdom and the spread of leasehold and money rent, though the process was far from uniform. In parts of the east, where towns were fewer, landlords more politically powerful, and later growth of western grain markets offered profitable export opportunities, landowners tightened control. Legislation restricting peasant mobility, such as measures in Poland-Lithuania in the late fifteenth century, and the growth of labor service on demesne farms, as discussed in Section 143.6, show intensifying coercion. Historians debate how far demography, markets, state power, or the balance of political forces explains this divergence, and many stress several of these together. The lesson is that demographic shocks do not produce fixed social outcomes. Institutions, political power, and economic opportunity determine how societies respond.

Landowners, Peasants, Wage Workers, and Households after the Black Death

Section 143.10

Early Modern Households, Gendered Labor, and Commercial Production

Between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, Europe's economy became more commercial, but production remained largely household-based. Farm households combined cultivation with spinning, weaving, dairying, and small-scale trade. In many rural regions, merchants supplied raw materials to households and collected finished goods, a method called the putting-out or domestic system, widely used in textiles in Flanders, England, parts of Germany, Bohemia, Silesia, northern Italy, and elsewhere. Historians once described the resulting growth of rural manufacturing as "proto-industrialization," a concept that stressed its role in preparing later factory development, though subsequent research showed that regional patterns varied and that rural industry did not always lead to industrialization.

Households also served as the principal site of consumption and care. Women, children, and men performed different but overlapping tasks, and the sexual division of labor varied by region and crop. In many farming regions, women milked, tended poultry and gardens, harvested, and spun, while men plowed and managed larger livestock, though the line shifted with local custom and need. In towns, women ran shops and market stalls, brewed, sold food, nursed, and worked as laundresses and domestic servants. Domestic service was among the largest occupations, especially for young women, and a significant share of the population in many regions spent part of their youth as servants. Wage work for women existed in agriculture, textiles, and services, though women's wages were commonly lower than men's for comparable work.

The commercial expansion of the early modern period also altered consumption. Chapter 73 examined the impact of empire on European consumption, and Chapter 85 examined consumer society in the eighteenth century. Households increasingly purchased sugar, tea, tobacco, cotton goods, ceramics, and clocks, many of them products of colonial trade and, in the case of sugar and cotton, of Atlantic slavery. The historian Jan de Vries proposed the concept of an "industrious revolution," arguing that in northwestern Europe from the seventeenth century households reallocated time from leisure and household production toward market work in order to buy more goods, with women and children's labor important in that shift. The concept has stimulated extensive research and criticism. Evidence for changing working hours and consumption is limited, and scholars question how far desire for goods, as opposed to economic necessity, drove the change.

Recent economic history has also emphasized the scale of women's work. Studies of wage accounts, court depositions, household inventories, and tax records indicate that women's participation in agricultural, manufacturing, and service work was substantial in the early modern period, even though contemporary records often described women by marital status rather than occupation and omitted wives' work. This means that older accounts which dated women's entry into productive economic life to industrialization were mistaken. At the same time, evidence of participation does not mean equality. Women were often paid less, restricted from certain guilds and offices, limited in legal capacity if married, and heavily burdened with unpaid domestic and childcare work.

Historical Debate

Women's Work before and during Industrialization

The question: Did industrialization reduce women's economic participation, expand it, or change its organization, visibility, valuation, and documentation?

Interpretation A — Industrialization displaced or marginalized women's productive work: Early twentieth century historians, notably Alice Clark in her study of seventeenth century women, argued that as production moved out of the household and capitalist enterprise grew, women lost access to skilled, home-based work and were increasingly confined to lower-paid or domestic roles. Related arguments about the growth of the male-breadwinner ideal and the separation of home from workplace suggest that industrial society narrowed women's economic scope, particularly for the middle classes, whose wives and daughters were increasingly expected to withdraw from paid work.
Interpretation B — Continuity and transformation within the family economy: Other historians, including Ivy Pinchbeck, who emphasized new opportunities in factory work, and later scholars such as Joan Scott and Louise Tilly, argued that working-class women's labor continued to be shaped by family needs and that women moved between paid and unpaid work through the life cycle. More recent quantitative research, using wage records and employment registers, suggests that women's work was substantial before industrialization and that apparent declines in female participation in census data reflect, in part, changes in how census officials defined and recorded occupations, especially for married women combining several tasks.

Evidence: Both sides rely on household account books, wage records, employment registers, factory inspectors' reports, court depositions, and censuses. Each has limits. Early modern sources often underrecord married women's work, nineteenth century censuses used shifting and gendered definitions of occupation, and many women's tasks, such as boarding house keeping, outwork, seasonal agriculture, and unpaid family labor, did not enter formal categories. Regional differences are also large, since industrial textile districts, mining areas, and agricultural regions produced distinct patterns.

Why it matters: If women's work was already extensive, then industrialization did not "introduce" women to production, and historians must ask how its organization, location, supervision, and remuneration changed. If census figures understate women's labor, then measures of economic growth and of the "working class" itself are affected.

Then assess which interpretation, or which combination, you find most persuasive.

Section 143.11

Estates, Legal Privilege, Property, and the Geography of Inequality

Early modern European states were societies of unequal legal rights. In many, the population was understood as divided into orders or estates, commonly those who prayed, those who fought, and those who worked, though real societies were more complex. Rights to office, exemption from taxation, and judicial privileges were attached to birth or corporate membership. The clergy held extensive property and its own courts in Catholic states and, after the Reformation, shifted in role in Protestant ones. Nobles enjoyed privileges that varied widely. In France, the nobility and clergy were exempt from some taxes, such as the taille, though they were subject to others. In Hungary and Poland-Lithuania, the nobility formed a much larger proportion of the population, with political rights in diets and parliaments, though that group ranged from magnates to poor gentry. In England, the peerage was small, but a wider gentry class dominated local government, and the line between noble and commoner was legally less sharp than elsewhere. In Spain, a large number of people claimed the status of hidalgo, which carried fiscal and symbolic privilege, while in Russia, Peter the Great's Table of Ranks of 1722 tied noble status to state service, and the Charter to the Nobility of 1785 confirmed noble privileges.

The Dutch Republic offers a contrast. Its political and economic life was dominated by urban merchant oligarchies, the regents, rather than by a landed aristocracy, and its provinces were governed through assemblies of cities and nobles, though a stadtholder from the House of Orange held important powers. Wealth and legal privilege did not map neatly onto each other. Commercial success could produce enormous fortunes without conferring noble status, and rich merchants often sought to purchase land, offices, or titles that would translate economic success into social honor. In France, the sale of offices created a robe nobility, and wealthy commoners could join the privileged orders through office, marriage, or purchase, while in Britain landed society absorbed commercial wealth through investment in land and marriage. Hereditary privilege and commercial wealth therefore coexisted and interacted.

Inequality was not limited to the distinction between nobles and commoners. Rural society included prosperous farmers, small tenants, cottagers, and landless laborers, and the proportion of landless wage workers grew in some regions, notably in England with the enclosure of open fields and commons, though enclosure's pace and effects remain debated. Urban poverty was substantial, and states and local governments introduced poor relief, such as the English Poor Laws, and sought to regulate vagrancy. Religious minorities faced restrictions on property, residence, and occupation, and women's legal capacity was limited by marriage law in many jurisdictions. Education was a route to advancement, but access depended on wealth, gender, and confession. Universities and grammar schools were open to few women.

Mobility existed but was limited and unequal. Ambitious individuals could rise through commerce, officeholding, military service, the church, education, advantageous marriage, or migration, and these routes enabled a minority of families to rise in rank across generations. But hereditary privilege, patronage, and restricted access to capital and education constrained most people. Downward mobility was also common, through debt, mortality of breadwinners, harvest failure, or the division of small inheritances. An important caution for students is not to equate the existence of a few celebrated careers with open opportunity. The distribution of wealth in early modern Europe, where tax records allow estimates, was generally highly unequal, and in many regions inequality appears to have increased between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, though measures vary by place and source.

Section 143.12

Revolutions, Emancipation, and the Rewriting of Social Privilege

The French Revolution, examined in Chapters 88 and 89, attacked the legal foundations of estate society. On the night of 4 August 1789 the National Assembly voted to abolish feudal and seigneurial privileges, and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen proclaimed legal equality. In practice, the dismantling of seigneurial dues was complex. Some obligations were initially made redeemable by payment, and a more radical abolition without compensation came in 1793 under revolutionary pressure. Church lands were nationalized and sold, redistributing property, though to buyers who were often wealthier peasants, townspeople, and bourgeois. The Le Chapelier law of 1791 banned guilds and workers' combinations alike, establishing a legal framework in which labor was a contract between individuals and collective organization was prohibited, a restriction that lasted for decades. French Jews were granted civil rights in 1790 and 1791, an emancipation later extended by French conquest and influence into other parts of Europe, and then contested or reversed after 1815 in some places.

The limits of revolutionary emancipation are equally important. Women were declared citizens in some respects but denied political rights. In 1791, Olympe de Gouges published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen, arguing for women's equal rights, and she was executed in 1793, while the revolutionary government banned women's political clubs the same year. The Napoleonic Civil Code of 1804, discussed in Chapter 90, established equality before the law, secured property rights, abolished primogeniture in favor of partible inheritance in much of France, and reinforced a strong form of contract law. It also placed married women under their husbands' legal authority, restricted their capacity to manage property or enter contracts without consent, and treated adultery and divorce asymmetrically. The Code spread through conquest and imitation to the Rhineland, Italy, the Low Countries, parts of Spain, Poland, and beyond, and it influenced much of continental European law long after Napoleon.

Legal reform affected different regions differently. In Prussia, the October Edict of 1807, part of the reforms that followed military defeat by France, abolished hereditary personal serfdom and loosened restrictions on the sale of land, but the terms of releasing peasants from obligations often favored estate owners, and the Junker landowning class retained substantial social and political power. In the Habsburg monarchy, Joseph II's patent of 1781 abolished personal serfdom in some lands, but robot labor dues continued until the revolution of 1848, when serf obligations were formally abolished across the monarchy. In Denmark, the adscription system was loosened in 1788. In Russia, emancipation arrived by decree in 1861 under Alexander II, but peasants had to pay redemption payments over decades and remained bound in many ways to communes, and many considered the settlement inadequate. In the Danubian principalities, the land reform of 1864 under Alexandru Ioan Cuza freed peasants from labor dues with compensation to landowners, and Ottoman reforms, including the Land Code of 1858, reorganized landholding in the Empire's provinces. The abolition of rural dependency was thus prolonged, running from the 1780s to the 1860s and beyond, and its terms often burdened former dependents.

For many people, emancipation therefore changed legal status without ending economic inequality. Former serfs frequently owned too little land to subsist, and landowners retained large estates, forests, pastures, and, in many places, the social authority of the old order. Wage labor and rural poverty persisted, and rural overpopulation encouraged emigration, especially from the later nineteenth century. Where property was redistributed more broadly, as in parts of France, it fostered a durable class of smallholders. Revolutionary legal equality altered how privilege was justified and organized, but it did not by itself generate equal opportunity. Property, gender, education, and local power continued to shape lives, and these continuing inequalities set the stage for the industrial class formation that followed.

Division IV · Industrialization, Class Formation, and Mass Society · Section 143.13

Industrial Labor, Urbanization, and the Formation of Modern Classes

Chapters 93 through 96 traced industrialization from Britain across the continent. The social consequence examined here is the reorganization of work and class. Factories concentrated production under a single roof, powered first by water and then by steam, and subordinated workers to time discipline, supervision, and machine rhythms. Coal mining, iron and steel, shipbuilding, railways, and later chemicals and engineering drew large labor forces. Wages, rather than household production or customary dues, became the dominant form of income for many, and capital, in the form of machinery, factories, and credit, became more significant relative to land as a source of wealth. Towns and cities grew rapidly. In England and Wales the census of 1851 is often cited as the first in which more than half the population was recorded as living in urban places, though definitions of "urban" are debated.

Historical Vocabulary
Bourgeoisie, Proletariat, and Wage Labor

Bourgeoisie originally referred to the burghers or townspeople of medieval and early modern towns. In nineteenth century usage, especially in the writings of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, it came to mean the class owning capital and the means of production. In everyday speech it more loosely described the middle classes, including professionals, merchants, and managers. Proletariat, a term borrowed from Roman usage, designated in Marxist analysis the class of wage earners who owned no productive property and lived by selling their labor. Wage labor is work performed in exchange for money payment under an employment relationship, as distinct from labor owed to a lord, performed by an enslaved person, or produced within an independent household enterprise.

These categories were analytical constructs as well as social descriptions. Actual workers might be factory operatives, outworkers, miners, skilled artisans, dockers, or domestic servants, with different incomes, skills, loyalties, and political outlooks. Historians therefore use "working class" with attention to its internal diversity and to the fact that class identity was formed through experience, organization, and political language, not simply assigned by occupation.

The industrial working class was not new in the sense of appearing from nowhere. Many early factory workers were recruited from agricultural districts, from domestic industry, from among former artisans, and from migrants, including, in Britain, large numbers of Irish emigrants after the famine of the 1840s. Meanwhile, the persistence of older forms of work is as important as the rise of the factory. Agriculture remained the largest source of employment across most of Europe well into the nineteenth century and in much of southern and eastern Europe well into the twentieth. Domestic service was among the largest occupations for women, small workshops continued to employ many artisans, and informal work, such as street trading, casual labor, and piecework at home, coexisted with factory production. The middle classes, too, were diverse: they included industrial employers, bankers, merchants, lawyers, doctors, teachers, civil servants, and a growing body of clerks and shopkeepers.

The pace and form of industrialization varied. Britain led, with coalfields, textile districts, and colonial markets. Belgium industrialized early around coal and iron in the Sambre and Meuse valleys, and parts of northern France, Saxony, Silesia, and the Rhineland followed. German industrial growth accelerated after unification in 1871, with the Ruhr as a center of coal and steel, while northern Italy industrialized later, around Milan, Turin, and Genoa, and Scandinavia experienced rapid industrial growth in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In the Russian Empire, industrial development concentrated in certain regions, such as Saint Petersburg, Moscow, and the Donbas, with workers often retaining ties to villages, and rural peasants still formed the large majority of the population. Habsburg industry was regionally concentrated in Bohemia, Austria, and parts of Hungary. Industrialization was thus uneven geographically and chronologically, and later industrializers did not follow Britain's path in detail.

Class formation involved conflict and bargaining. Employers, often drawing on liberal doctrines of contract and market freedom, resisted regulation and collective organization, while workers sought higher wages, shorter hours, and safer conditions. The relationship between employer and worker was legally framed as free contract, yet in practice differences in bargaining power were large, and many states retained laws, such as master and servant legislation in Britain, that penalized workers' breaches of contract more severely than employers'. The resulting struggles, examined in Section 143.15, shaped political life across Europe.

Section 143.14

Women, Children, and the Household Economy under Industrialization

Industrialization changed how families earned incomes, but it did not simply remove production from the home or women from paid work. In textile districts, women and children formed large proportions of the early factory labor force, and in mining and metalworking regions male employment predominated, with women's income coming from other sources, such as laundry, lodging-keeping, trade, or outwork. Domestic industries persisted long after the introduction of factories, and in some regions, such as the Silesian weaving districts, they declined painfully, as the weavers' uprising of 1844 showed. Many households relied on income from several members and several sources, a family economy in which wages, garden produce, boarding, borrowing, and charity combined.

Children worked in factories, mines, workshops, agriculture, and domestic service. Their labor was often valued for its low cost and, in some tasks, for supposed suitability to small bodies. Public concern about the conditions of child labor, expressed in parliamentary inquiries, factory inspectors' reports, and writings by reformers, led to legislation. Britain regulated textile factories in the early nineteenth century, with the Factory Act of 1833 as a landmark of effective enforcement through inspectors. Prussia limited child labor in factories in 1839, France passed legislation in 1841, and the Mines Act of 1842 in Britain banned women and young boys from underground work. Enforcement was often weak and varied by country and industry. In the later nineteenth century, compulsory schooling, introduced in various forms in Prussia, Britain (1870 and after), France (the Ferry laws of 1881–1882), and elsewhere, gradually changed childhood from a period of work toward one of education, though in agricultural regions children's seasonal labor continued.

Gender ideology and economic practice interacted. Middle-class social commentators, reformers, and some labor leaders promoted a domestic ideal in which men were breadwinners and women were homemakers, a doctrine sometimes called "separate spheres" and, in its economic form, the male-breadwinner or family wage ideal. It influenced law, wage-setting, and trade union demands, and it shaped employment practices, such as marriage bars that required women to leave certain jobs, including in teaching and the civil service, on marriage in some countries. But it described the lives of the better-off far more than those of most working families, which often needed more than one income. Working-class wives took in washing and lodgers, did piecework, worked in factories or fields, and sold goods, and widows and unmarried women, who lacked a male earner, frequently supported themselves and their dependents. Women's pay was typically lower than men's, justified by claims that women's wages supplemented a family income and that women's skills were less valuable, and occupational segregation reinforced this gap.

Domestic service, one of the largest sources of employment for young women, shows the porous boundary between household and labor market. Servants lived within employers' homes, subject to their supervision, and their work was both paid employment and a form of dependency. At the same time, unpaid domestic labor, including cooking, cleaning, nursing, and childrearing, remained crucial to household and economic life, though not counted in production statistics. Both the transformation of women's work and its continuity are central to understanding industrial society, and as Section 143.10 showed, historians continue to debate how to measure them.

Regulating Labor and Gender: Three Identifiable Primary Sources, 1832–1957

Sources: (1) Factory Act, 1833, 3 & 4 William IV, chapter 103, particularly sections 1, 2, 8, 17–21; (2) testimony of Elizabeth Bentley, a former flax-mill worker from Leeds, before the House of Commons committee chaired by Michael Thomas Sadler in 1832, published in Parliamentary Papers, 1831–1832, volume XV, page 195; (3) Article 119 of the Treaty establishing the European Economic Community, signed in Rome in 1957. Source types: statute, recorded parliamentary witness examination, and founding treaty article.

Context: British parliamentary investigations into textile-factory conditions in the early 1830s helped shape public debate over child labor and factory regulation. The evidence given to the Sadler committee must be read critically, since the chairman favored shorter working hours and some questions were leading. The Factory Act of 1833 imposed new restrictions and established inspectors. More than a century later, the six founding members of the European Economic Community included equal pay for women and men in Article 119, partly in response to concerns about competitive disparities among national labor laws.

Document descriptions below are paraphrases, not quotations

Document 1, Factory Act (1833): The Act regulated specified mechanized textile mills, banned most employment of children under nine, introduced a staged reduction of work for children below thirteen to a maximum of nine hours per day and forty-eight per week, and limited workers under eighteen more generally to twelve hours per day and sixty-nine per week. It required schooling for children within the restricted age group and created an inspectorate. The transition rules took effect in stages, so the final under-thirteen threshold was not immediate.

Document 2, Elizabeth Bentley's testimony (1832): Bentley told the committee that she had begun work at six years old in a Leeds flax mill. She described ordinary days extending from early morning to evening, still longer shifts when production was busy, limited meal breaks, and physical punishment when children fell behind. This is her reported personal experience as recorded in a reform-oriented parliamentary inquiry, not a statistical description of every factory.

Document 3, Treaty of Rome, Article 119 (1957): The treaty required member states to establish and maintain equal pay for women and men performing equal work. It addressed pay at time rates and piece rates, and defined remuneration broadly enough to include certain additional benefits supplied by employers.

Analysis: The law shows how the state sought to limit child labor and establish enforcement powers; Bentley's account provides a named worker's testimony about conditions the law aimed to change; Article 119 expresses a later principle of sex equality within a common economic market. The three documents do not prove how consistently laws were enforced or how representative the recorded testimony was. In 1976, the European Court of Justice held in the second Defrenne case that Article 119 could be relied on directly before national courts in appropriate circumstances. Teachers and students should compare the documentary claims with inspectors' records, wage books, and other worker testimony.

Section 143.15

Organized Labor, Socialism, Collective Action, and Social Reform

Workers organized for mutual aid, higher wages, and political voice, often in the face of legal prohibition. Early associations included friendly societies, which pooled funds against sickness and burial costs, craft societies of skilled workers, and cooperatives, such as the Rochdale consumers' cooperative founded in 1844. Governments initially restricted combinations of workers. Britain's Combination Acts were repealed in 1824–1825, though trade union activity remained legally precarious, and France's Le Chapelier law remained in force until the Waldeck-Rousseau law of 1884 legalized unions. In Germany, restrictions loosened unevenly in the 1860s. Trade unions grew in the later nineteenth century and expanded rapidly around the turn of the century, with the German free trade unions, linked to the Social Democratic Party, enrolling more than two million members by 1913.

Competing currents shaped the labor movement. Reformist socialists and trade unionists sought gradual improvement through legislation and collective bargaining. Marxist parties, such as the German Social Democratic Party (SPD), founded in its unified form in 1875, combined revolutionary theory with practical political and union work. Anarchists and syndicalists, strong in Spain, Italy, and France, emphasized direct action and the general strike. Christian trade unions and social movements, encouraged in Catholic regions by Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Rerum Novarum (1891), sought social reform on religious foundations, while liberal reformers emphasized education, thrift, and limited regulation. Employers resisted through lockouts, blacklists, company unions, and, at times, armed force, while some adopted paternalist welfare schemes to secure loyalty. Strikes ranged from local disputes to large national events, and states experimented with arbitration and labor law. Chapter 102 examined the political dimension, including mass parties and suffrage, and this section concentrates on their social and economic aspects.

State intervention expanded in response. Factory legislation, discussed above, was followed by laws on hours, safety, and employer liability. Germany under Bismarck, partly to undercut socialism while also persecuting the movement through the anti-socialist laws of 1878–1890, introduced compulsory sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884), and old-age and disability insurance (1889), a pioneering social insurance system. Other states followed with varying schemes. These measures showed that states could assume new responsibilities for workers' welfare, motivated by humanitarian concern, social stability, military and economic considerations, and political competition.

Women and labor organization had a complicated relationship. Women workers participated in strikes, unions, and socialist organizations, and women such as Clara Zetkin in Germany became prominent leaders. Yet many craft unions excluded women, fearing wage competition and sharing the male-breadwinner ideal, and women's wages and organizational needs were frequently subordinated within mixed movements. Migrant workers and workers from minority groups also faced exclusion or hostility from some unions, while in other cases union solidarity crossed national and ethnic lines. In agriculture, where much of Europe's workforce was employed, organization was weaker and slower, though rural labor unions and peasant movements emerged in places such as Italy and Andalusia.

Section 143.16

Public Health, Housing, Education, and Demographic Transition

Rapid urbanization produced severe sanitary problems. Industrial cities experienced overcrowding, polluted water, inadequate sewage, and epidemic disease, including cholera outbreaks across Europe in 1832, 1848–1849, and later years. Mortality and life expectancy varied sharply by neighborhood and occupation: the poor lived in damp, crowded housing, with exposure to occupational hazards such as lead, mining accidents, and respiratory disease, while wealthier districts had earlier access to clean water and healthier conditions. Reformers, including Edwin Chadwick in Britain, whose 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the laboring population influenced the Public Health Act of 1848, argued that disease was linked to environmental conditions. Over the following decades, municipalities built water supply and sewer systems, passed building regulations, and expanded public health administration, and the acceptance of germ theory in the later nineteenth century strengthened their basis. The scale and speed of action varied. Hamburg's severe cholera epidemic of 1892, which struck harder than in neighboring cities with more advanced filtration, is a frequently cited example of the cost of delay.

Housing reform followed, from philanthropic model dwellings to municipal and cooperative housing programs, and the large-scale rebuilding of Paris under Georges-Eugène Haussmann, who displaced many poorer residents from central districts, shows how urban improvement could also reorganize social geography. Access to healthcare grew through hospitals, mutual funds, and eventually insurance, though in unequal fashion. Occupational health, working hours, and workplace safety remained contested, and employers' liability laws developed slowly.

Education expanded in parallel. Protestant regions, especially Scandinavia and parts of Germany, achieved high literacy relatively early through church-supported instruction, while literacy was far lower in much of southern and eastern Europe in the early and mid nineteenth century. At Italian unification, for instance, a majority of the population was unable to read. States increasingly introduced compulsory elementary education, expanding teacher training, school buildings, and curricula that served civic and national purposes as well as individual opportunity. Secondary and university education remained restricted by cost, gender, and social background, but expanding administrative, commercial, and professional occupations created opportunities for educated people, including, gradually, women in teaching, nursing, clerical work, and, later, the professions. Mass literacy also enabled newspapers, political pamphlets, and workers' libraries, and it widened participation in political and cultural life.

These changes accompanied the demographic transition, the shift from high birth and death rates toward low ones. European mortality began to fall, in some regions from the later eighteenth century and more clearly in the nineteenth, owing to a mixture of improved nutrition, public health, and other factors whose relative weight historians debate. Fertility decline followed, but the timing varied sharply. In France, birth rates began to fall as early as the late eighteenth century, whereas in England they fell substantially from the 1870s, in Germany somewhat later, and in much of eastern and southern Europe in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Explanations emphasize changing costs and benefits of children as schooling expanded, declining infant mortality, changing norms, access to contraception, and economic conditions, but no single cause accounts for all regions. Ireland's pattern, with late marriage, heavy emigration, and population decline after the famine of 1845–1852, shows that demographic change was shaped by particular political and economic circumstances.

Taken together, mass schooling, public health, and occupational diversification expanded opportunities for many families, and literacy and skills created routes for upward mobility, especially for the children of skilled workers and small proprietors. Yet significant inequalities persisted. Child mortality, housing quality, schooling, and life expectancy continued to correlate with class, gender, and region, and rural and urban differences remained wide. Improved living standards, in other words, did not eliminate inequality, a distinction that will recur in the discussion of the twentieth century.

Division V · War, Welfare, Gender, and Contemporary European Society · Section 143.17

War, Displacement, and the Social Consequences of Mass Mobilization

The military narratives of Chapters 108 through 123 are not repeated here. This section asks what total war, economic crisis, and political violence did to households and social structures. World War I mobilized millions of men and removed them from farms, workshops, and families, leaving women, children, and the elderly to sustain households. Women entered munitions factories, transport, offices, and agriculture in large numbers, state rationing and price controls regulated consumption, and governments assumed responsibility for housing, food supply, and pensions for the wounded and bereaved. The war left large numbers of widows, orphans, and disabled veterans. In many countries, women's wartime employment contracted after 1918 as men returned, though some new patterns, notably in clerical and service work, persisted. Civilians in blockaded or occupied areas, such as Germany during the "turnip winter" of 1916–1917 and occupied Belgium and northern France, experienced hunger and hardship.

The interwar period combined political upheaval with economic insecurity. The German hyperinflation of 1923 eroded the savings of many middle-class households while benefiting some debtors, and the Great Depression after 1929 produced mass unemployment, with roughly six million registered unemployed in Germany at the peak in 1932, and severe hardship in Britain's industrial regions, Central Europe, and agricultural economies hit by collapsing prices. Governments responded in different ways: unemployment relief, public works, protective tariffs, and in some places social experimentation, as in Sweden's social-democratic governments, while in others the crisis fueled authoritarian movements. Agricultural populations in southern and eastern Europe, still the majority in many states, faced poverty, indebtedness, and land hunger, and land reforms after 1918 in several successor states, such as Romania and Czechoslovakia, redistributed some estates with mixed consequences.

Distinct political systems pursued distinct social policies, and they should not be treated as equivalent. Fascist Italy promoted pronatalist policies, corporatist labor organization that suppressed independent unions, and leisure organizations such as the Dopolavoro. Nazi Germany combined welfare provision and employment programs for people it classified as members of the national community with systematic persecution, dispossession, and eventually murder of Jews, Roma, disabled people, and others, as Chapter 121 examined, and it relied on massive forced labor of foreign civilians and prisoners during the war, numbering well over ten million by most estimates. The Soviet Union under Stalin forced rapid industrialization and collectivization of agriculture from 1929, uprooting rural households and contributing to famine in 1932–1933 in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, and other regions, expanded women's employment and schooling, imposed political repression and forced labor in the Gulag system, and enacted pronatalist family legislation in the 1930s. Democratic states such as Britain, France, and the Scandinavian countries expanded social insurance, though with severe interwar limits, and their wartime and postwar planning drew on these experiences.

Cause and Effect

War, Displacement, and the Transformation of Social Policy

Long-term causes

Nineteenth century industrialization and urbanization had already created demands for social insurance, public health, and labor regulation, and mass politics gave workers and women organized political voice. States had also built administrative capacity for taxation and conscription.

Short-term causes

Total mobilization in 1914–1918 and 1939–1945, economic depression, mass unemployment, bombing, occupation, and the political need to secure public support in prolonged war made governments responsible for food, housing, jobs, and the care of the bereaved and wounded.

Event or transformation

Wartime and postwar governments, including Britain's coalition government with the Beveridge Report of 1942 and France's social security ordinances of 1945, committed to wider social protection, while in the east communist authorities built new systems of employment and provision under party control.

Immediate effects

Millions of dead, displaced, and bereaved people required housing, pensions, and care. Women's wartime labor changed expectations in some sectors, while homes, cities, and infrastructure lay in ruins, and tens of millions had to be resettled, repatriated, or sustained as refugees.

Long-term consequences

The postwar welfare and employment systems of both halves of Europe rested on wartime experience, as did new assumptions that states were responsible for social security. Memory of unemployment and war also shaped popular expectations for decades.

Historical complexity

War did not directly produce welfare states. Prewar reform movements, party politics, economic conditions, Cold War competition, and national traditions all shaped outcomes, and similar wartime pressures led to very different institutions in different countries.

Why one cause is not enough: The same war destroyed Jewish communities, uprooted millions, and enabled new social contracts. Social policy cannot be explained as a simple reward for sacrifice, since those most harmed by Nazi rule, including the Roma and surviving Jews, often faced obstacles in obtaining compensation and restitution.

World War II and its aftermath displaced populations on a scale unmatched in modern Europe. Beyond military deaths, civilian mass murder, bombing, and famine, millions were deported, enslaved as laborers, expelled, or forced to flee. During approximately 1944–1950, at least twelve million ethnic Germans fled or were expelled from east-central and eastern Europe (estimates vary by definition and source), and many others, including Poles, Ukrainians, and Hungarians, were transferred or resettled in changing borders. Allied authorities and relief organizations cared for several million displaced persons in Germany and Austria, including survivors of concentration camps and former forced laborers. Housing shortages, destroyed cities, widowhood, orphanhood, and family separation shaped the immediate postwar years, and women often bore the burden of rebuilding households and clearing rubble, a role later memorialized in Germany as that of the "rubble women," a figure whose scale and meaning historians continue to examine. Postwar reconstruction thus began amid profound social dislocation, setting the stage for the welfare and employment systems of the following decades.

Section 143.18

Welfare States, Mass Consumption, and Postwar Social Mobility

Chapters 126 through 128 examined postwar reconstruction. In western Europe, economies grew rapidly from the late 1940s to the early 1970s, unemployment was low, real incomes rose, and states expanded social insurance, healthcare, housing, and education. Britain introduced the National Insurance system and the National Health Service in 1946–1948, France created its social security system in 1945, the Scandinavian states developed comprehensive provision, and West Germany combined a "social market economy" with contribution-based insurance, including pension reform in 1957. In The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism (1990), the sociologist Gøsta Esping-Andersen influentially distinguished liberal, conservative-corporatist, and social-democratic welfare regimes, emphasizing decommodification, social stratification, and the relationship among states, markets, and households. His original classification did not adequately capture unpaid care, gender differences, or the economic dependence of family members, limitations later emphasized by feminist scholars. Subsequent research also proposed a distinct southern European welfare pattern in which family networks bore much of the care burden and public provision developed differently in Spain, Portugal, Greece, and Italy; the boundaries and adequacy of these typologies remain debated.

Social-democratic systems, as in Sweden, aimed at universal provision and, increasingly, public childcare supporting women's employment. Christian-democratic systems, as in West Germany and Italy, emphasized the family and tied benefits to employment, which reinforced the male-breadwinner model. The liberal model, more evident in Britain after the early postwar period, relied more on means-tested assistance and market provision. Mass consumption grew. Households acquired refrigerators, washing machines, televisions, and cars, and consumer culture reorganized domestic work and leisure, though it did not by itself reduce the amount of housework in all cases. Western European economies also recruited migrant workers from southern Europe, Turkey, North Africa, and former colonies, discussed in Chapters 128 and 137, whose access to housing, citizenship, and social benefits was often limited.

In eastern Europe, communist states, as Chapters 124 and 125 explained, nationalized industry, collectivized agriculture in most countries (Poland and Yugoslavia were notable partial exceptions in retaining substantial private farming), and made full employment a political commitment, including a duty to work. Housing, basic foods, transportation, healthcare, and education were heavily subsidized, and workplaces often provided benefits ranging from childcare to holidays. Women entered the labor force in very high proportions, supported by state childcare in many countries, while household labor still fell disproportionately on them, a double burden. Systems differed substantially: Hungary after 1956 allowed more private economic activity, Yugoslavia developed worker self-management outside the Soviet bloc, Poland retained a strong Church and private farming, and East Germany built a large social apparatus. Shortages, queues, informal networks, and privileges for the party elite and its officials shaped everyday life, and authorities limited independent associations and speech. It is therefore misleading to treat the "Eastern Bloc" as socially uniform.

Mobility is a major question for both halves of Europe. The expansion of education, growth of white-collar and professional work, decline of agriculture, and growth of industry and services offered many people employment higher in status than their parents'. Historians distinguish this absolute mobility, produced by changes in the economy, from relative mobility, which asks whether children of different backgrounds had more equal chances. In western Europe, comparative studies suggest that educational expansion widened access, as with the Robbins Report in Britain in 1963 and the growth of universities, but that class origin continued to influence educational and occupational outcomes. In communist Europe, regimes initially promoted workers' and peasants' children through preferential admissions and elite replacement, producing striking upward mobility, but over time educated families, party membership, and networks tended to reproduce advantage, and scholars continue to debate the degree to which mobility patterns differed from those in market economies. Both systems thus changed life chances substantially while leaving significant inequalities intact.

Section 143.19

Feminism, Family Change, and the Transformation of Social Citizenship

Women's political rights arrived at different times: Finland granted women full suffrage in 1906, Norway in 1913, Denmark in 1915, Germany and Britain in 1918 (with Britain's electoral age initially higher for women, equalized in 1928), France in 1944, Italy in 1945, and Switzerland at the federal level only in 1971. Legal equality within the family lagged further. Until reforms in the 1960s and 1970s, married women in many countries needed their husbands' consent for employment, bank accounts, or other transactions, or were legally subordinate in family matters. In West Germany, for example, civil law until 1977 defined a wife's responsibility for the household in ways that conditioned her paid employment, and in France reforms of 1965 allowed married women to work and open bank accounts without their husbands' permission.

The women's movements of the 1960s and 1970s, examined in Chapter 130, took varied forms across Europe. They campaigned for equal pay, access to contraception and abortion, divorce reform, protection from domestic violence, childcare, and an end to occupational discrimination. Legislative change followed unevenly. Britain's Abortion Act of 1967 and France's Veil law of 1975 legalized abortion under conditions, Italy enacted divorce legislation in 1970 and abortion legislation in 1978, and voters subsequently rejected attempts to repeal these laws in referendums held in 1974 and 1981 respectively. Spain legalized divorce in 1981 after the end of the Franco dictatorship. In Ireland, where the Catholic Church held strong influence, voters approved a constitutional amendment permitting divorce in a 1995 referendum; the Family Law (Divorce) Act was enacted in 1996 and came into operation on 27 February 1997. Ireland enacted legislation permitting abortion in specified circumstances in 2018, following a referendum that year. Equal pay and equal treatment directives of the European Communities from the 1970s required member states to adapt law, building on Article 119. Education expanded, and women began to outnumber men among university students in many countries by the end of the twentieth century.

Communist and post-communist societies followed other paths. Communist regimes proclaimed women's equality, mobilized women into employment and education, and provided childcare and maternity leave in many countries, and the female employment rate was very high. Yet political leadership remained male-dominated, domestic work was still largely borne by women, and official equality coexisted with censorship of independent feminist organizations. Abortion policy varied: it was liberalized in the Soviet Union in 1955 and in much of the bloc, but Romania, under Decree 770 of 1966, banned most abortions and contraception as part of a coercive pronatalist policy, with grave consequences for women's health and lives. After 1989, many post-communist states reduced public childcare and experienced declining female employment, and debates over gender and family policy became linked to national and religious politics. Poland, for example, enacted a restrictive abortion law in 1993, and a Constitutional Tribunal ruling in 2020 tightened it further, prompting large protests.

Family forms diversified across Europe. Cohabitation, divorce, births outside marriage, single-parent households, and later childbearing became more common, with Nordic countries leading, and registered partnerships and then marriage for same-sex couples were introduced beginning with Denmark's registered partnership of 1989 and the Netherlands' marriage law of 2001, though many states in central and eastern Europe did not follow. Employment of women rose, but pay gaps, occupational segregation, and unequal division of unpaid care persisted, with the average gender pay gap in the European Union remaining in the low double digits in percentage terms and differing widely among states. Laws did not produce identical outcomes for all women: class, region, migration background, religion, and access to childcare and services shaped how far formal equality translated into real opportunity. The history of gender relations in this period is thus one of contested and uneven change, rather than steady convergence.

Section 143.20

Inequality, Care, Work, and Everyday Life in Contemporary Europe

The end of communist rule in 1989–1991, examined in Chapters 132 and 136, opened a period of economic restructuring. Privatization, the collapse of many state enterprises, and the integration of markets caused sharp output declines and rising unemployment in much of central and eastern Europe in the early 1990s, with large regional differences: Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary, and the Baltic states recovered more quickly than many of the successor states of the Soviet Union and parts of southeastern Europe. Income inequality widened in many post-communist societies, property ownership shifted, with privatized housing creating high owner-occupation rates, and the sharp fall of birth rates and rise of emigration, especially after EU enlargement in 2004 and 2007 opened labor mobility, changed households and communities, leaving many children raised partly by grandparents or relatives while parents worked abroad. In eastern Germany, unification and the work of the Treuhand privatization agency brought widespread job losses and lasting differences in wealth and income compared with the west.

Across Europe, the structure of work changed. Manufacturing declined as a share of employment in many western economies, services expanded, and the educational level of the workforce rose. Fixed-term, part-time, and agency contracts grew, and the global financial crisis and the eurozone crisis of 2008–2013, discussed in Chapter 138, produced high unemployment, especially among the young, with more than half of labor-force participants under twenty-five unemployed in Greece and Spain at the peak in 2013. More recently, platform-mediated work, such as delivery and ride-hailing services, has raised questions of whether workers are employees or independent contractors. The European Union adopted Directive (EU) 2024/2831 on platform work in October 2024, with a deadline of 2 December 2026 for member states to transpose its requirements into national law. At the historical cutoff of 20 November 2026, that deadline had not yet arrived, and the directive's longer-term effects could not be assessed. These developments recall earlier debates over dependency and contract, though they occur in a very different institutional setting.

Aging populations and care work are central contemporary concerns. Lower fertility and longer life expectancy have raised the proportion of older people, particularly in southern and eastern Europe and in Germany, increasing demand for pensions, healthcare, and long-term care. Much of this care is still provided unpaid by family members, most often women, or by paid carers, many of them migrants, including from central and eastern Europe working in western households, a pattern sometimes described as "care chains" in which the care of one household is supplied at the expense of another. Differences in public provision, such as childcare, eldercare, and parental leave, produce differing burdens by country and income.

Housing and wealth illustrate persisting inequality. In many cities, rising rents and property prices have strained younger and lower-income households, while inherited wealth and home ownership affect the opportunities of later generations. Inequalities of income and wealth vary widely among European states, with Nordic countries generally showing less income inequality than southern or some eastern states, but differences within countries, between regions such as northern and southern Italy or western and eastern Germany, remain large. Class, gender, age, migration background, ethnicity, disability, and region intersect, and the experiences of, for example, a young Roma woman in rural southeastern Europe, a retired industrial worker in a declining coalfield, and a highly paid professional in a capital city, cannot be reduced to one dimension of advantage or disadvantage. Statistics on contemporary inequality are subject to revision and to differences in definition, and contemporary social developments are ongoing rather than completed historical processes.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Social Hierarchy, Property, Work, and Families across European History

What changed

  • Legal categories shifted from citizen, noncitizen, and enslaved, through lordship, serfdom, and estates, to formal legal equality and contractual employment
  • Work moved, for many, from household-based farming and crafts toward wage employment in factories, offices, and services, and from agriculture to industry and then services
  • Women gained political rights, access to education, and wide participation in paid work, and household forms diversified
  • States assumed responsibility for health, schooling, pensions, and unemployment, in contrast to earlier reliance on kin, church, and local charity

What remained continuous

  • Property, in land, capital, housing, and inheritance, remained closely tied to social advantage and to political influence
  • Households continued to organize production, consumption, and care, and unpaid work, mostly by women, remained essential and undercounted
  • Inequality of opportunity persisted, with family background affecting education, wealth, and career, even as absolute living standards rose
  • Regional differences, between east and west, north and south, and city and countryside, persisted in new forms

Who experienced change most directly? Enslaved and dependent people, whose status changed through emancipation, women, whose legal and economic position shifted markedly in the twentieth century, and rural populations leaving agriculture. Who experienced greater continuity? Owners of substantial property, whose advantages were often transmitted across generations despite changing legal forms, and many carers and low-paid workers, whose burdens persisted under new institutional arrangements.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two errors: assuming that European society has progressed steadily from hierarchy to equality, and assuming that nothing fundamental has changed. Institutions for organizing work, family, and property were reorganized repeatedly, yet new inequalities emerged from the reorganization.

The chapter's central question can now be restated. How much has European social life been transformed, and which forms of inequality, dependence, household organization, and social obligation have persisted or reappeared in different forms? The evidence suggests that the transformation was profound in legal status, the nature of work, life expectancy, education, and the position of women, and that persistence is equally evident in the importance of property, the centrality of households and unpaid care, and unequal chances of advancement. Contemporary Europe is not the predetermined culmination of this history. It is a historically contingent configuration, produced by choices, conflicts, and constraints that could have developed differently.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has synthesized the history of European society across five divisions, asking how changing forms of property, labor, household organization, and legal and social hierarchy shaped the lives of Europeans from prehistory to the early twenty-first century. Its central argument is that social hierarchy repeatedly changed its institutional forms without disappearing. Ancient legal status, medieval dependency, early modern estate privilege, industrial class structure, and contemporary economic inequality are historically distinct, yet each organized access to land, labor, protection, and opportunity in unequal ways.

Division I showed that social differentiation in prehistoric Europe developed unevenly, with agriculture and metallurgy creating possibilities for inequality that different communities realized in different ways. Greek and Roman societies organized hierarchy through citizenship, slavery, patronage, and landownership, and their households, the oikos and the familia, were institutions of production, authority, and inheritance rather than nuclear families. Ancient women's property rights and autonomy varied by city, rank, and period, and legal prescription differed from lived practice.

Division II examined medieval society. After the fragmentation of western Roman authority, lordship and customary obligation organized rural life, while in Byzantium free villagers and large estates coexisted under a stronger fiscal state, and enslavement persisted in the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds. Peasants were a varied group, and serfdom was not a single system, with the geography of dependency diverging between western Europe, where it generally weakened, and central and eastern Europe, where it later hardened. Towns offered opportunities and exclusions through guilds, citizenship, and gender and religious restrictions. Marriage and household patterns, including the debated European Marriage Pattern, varied considerably across regions.

Division III showed how the Great Famine and the Black Death reordered work. Labor scarcity increased bargaining power and weakened dependency in much of the west, while in parts of the east landowners with greater political power and export opportunities intensified coercion, demonstrating that demographic shocks do not determine social outcomes. Early modern households combined agriculture, domestic manufacturing, service, and care, and women's work was substantial though underrecorded. Estate-based societies distributed privilege unevenly and permitted limited mobility. Revolutions then altered legal privilege and property relations, yet emancipation, in France and in the later abolitions of serfdom in Prussia, the Habsburg lands, Russia, and elsewhere, left women's subordination, rural poverty, and economic inequality largely intact.

Division IV traced industrialization and class formation. The industrial working class formed unevenly, alongside persisting agriculture, domestic service, and small workshops. Women's and children's work was reorganized rather than simply created or removed, and the male-breadwinner ideal described the aspirations of some households far more than the realities of most. Workers' organizations, socialist and Christian movements, and state reforms gradually changed the framework of labor relations and introduced social insurance, while public health, housing reform, mass schooling, and demographic transition improved living conditions and expanded some opportunities without eliminating inequality.

Division V examined the twentieth century. Total war, economic crisis, authoritarian rule, genocide, and displacement transformed households and made states responsible for social security in new ways, though democratic, fascist, Nazi, and communist systems pursued very different social policies. After 1945, western welfare states and eastern communist states expanded employment, education, and provision, with divergent models of gender, family, and political authority, and both produced substantial absolute mobility while leaving relative inequalities in place. From the 1960s, feminist movements and legal reforms transformed the position of women and the diversity of households, with outcomes that varied among western, southern, communist, and post-communist societies.

The post-1989 period brought restructuring, migration, and new forms of precarity. Aging populations, care work, housing costs, regional disparities, and changing employment contracts mean that social class, gender, age, migration background, and region intersect in complex ways. The treatment of this recent period is necessarily provisional, since developments are ongoing and historians cannot yet know their long-term consequences.

Across these divisions, four conclusions recur. Hierarchy changed form but persisted. Households and unpaid work belong at the center of economic history. Gender relations changed through contested, uneven processes rather than steady progress toward equality. And social mobility and rising living standards must not be confused with the elimination of inequality. Regional comparison, particularly between western and eastern Europe and between northern and southern Europe, shows that no region represented a standard from which others deviated.

The chapter also demonstrated how historians know these things. Archaeological, legal, fiscal, demographic, and testimonial evidence each illuminate different aspects of social life and each contains silences, especially about women, dependents, and the poor. Debates such as that over women's work and industrialization show how the definition of work in records shapes historical conclusions.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Class
In modern usage, a group defined by its position in an economic order, especially its relation to property and labor; applied cautiously to earlier periods in which legal status and honor were as important as wealth.
Legal status
The rights, privileges, and obligations assigned to a person by law or custom, such as citizen, freedperson, noble, serf, or subject.
Estate
A legally defined social order in medieval and early modern societies, commonly clergy, nobility, and commoners, each with distinct rights and duties.
Social mobility
Movement of individuals or families between social positions, measured within a lifetime or across generations.
Absolute mobility
Movement produced by changes in the occupational structure, such as the decline of agriculture and the growth of white-collar work.
Relative mobility
The degree to which a person's social origins affect their chances of reaching a given position compared with those of others.
Household
A group sharing residence and economic life, which might include kin, servants, apprentices, lodgers, or enslaved persons, and which differs from the family defined by kinship.
Oikos
The Greek household and estate, comprising people, land, and property, whose continuity across generations was a civic and religious concern.
Familia and paterfamilias
The Roman household under the legal authority of its senior male head, who held extensive powers over dependents and property.
Metic
A resident foreigner in a Greek city, notably Athens, who lacked citizenship but participated in economic life and owed obligations.
Patronage
The exchange of support and favors between wealthy patrons and dependent clients, a structuring relationship in Roman society and later.
Dowry
Property given by or on behalf of a bride at marriage, widely used in southern and western Europe, often protected for the woman's later support.
Lordship
The authority of landholders over people living on their land, including rights to dues, labor, justice, and protection.
Serfdom
Hereditary rural dependency in which cultivators held land from a lord and owed labor, payments, and legal subjection; its forms varied widely by region and period.
Second serfdom
A conventional term for the strengthening of peasant obligations in parts of central and eastern Europe from about the sixteenth century, a label debated by historians.
Sharecropping
Tenancy in which cultivators give a share of the harvest to the landowner, as in the mezzadria of central Italy.
Guild
An association of craftspeople or merchants regulating training, quality, prices, and membership, and providing mutual aid.
European Marriage Pattern
John Hajnal's model of late marriage, high celibacy, and small households in northwestern Europe, a useful but heavily qualified generalization.
Life-cycle service
The practice of adolescents and young adults living and working as servants in other households before marrying.
Putting-out system
Manufacturing in which merchants supplied materials to households and collected finished goods, widespread in early modern textiles.
Industrious revolution
Jan de Vries's thesis that northwestern European households increased market work to purchase more goods from the seventeenth century.
Bourgeoisie
Originally townspeople; in nineteenth century usage, the propertied and professional middle classes, and in Marxist analysis, the owners of capital.
Proletariat
In Marxist analysis, the class of wage earners owning no productive property and living by selling their labor.
Wage labor
Work performed for money payment under an employment relationship, distinct from enslaved, bound, or independent household labor.
Male-breadwinner ideal
The norm that men should earn the family income while women manage the household, influential in law and wage policy though often unrealistic for working families.
Demographic transition
The historical shift from high birth and death rates toward low ones, occurring at different times and for different reasons across Europe.
Welfare state
A system in which public institutions provide social insurance, healthcare, pensions, and other support; its forms differ by country.
Social citizenship
The idea that membership in a political community includes entitlement to a basic level of economic and social security.
Care work
Paid and unpaid labor of raising children and looking after the sick, disabled, and elderly, historically performed mostly by women.
Double burden
The combination of full-time paid employment and primary responsibility for household work, common for women in communist and other societies.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 143 matters because it returns the history of Europe to the people who lived it. The political, religious, and imperial transformations synthesized in Chapters 140 through 142 reached ordinary lives through land, work, marriage, inheritance, wages, and care, and ordinary people in turn shaped those transformations by cultivating, resisting, bargaining, migrating, and organizing. A history confined to states and ideas would omit the daily labor that made them possible.

The chapter has also shown that Europe's social history cannot be told as a single national or regional story. The weakening of serfdom in the west and its hardening in parts of the east, the varied household systems of the north, south, and east, the different timing of industrialization, and the contrasting welfare and gender regimes of the twentieth century reveal a continent of many social formations, linked by trade, law, migration, and ideas, and compared fruitfully but not ranked. No single region served as the norm for the rest.

Within Volume VI, the chapter adds a social foundation to the narrative of Cold War division, integration, and contemporary change. The welfare states, communist societies, feminist movements, and post-communist restructuring examined in earlier chapters rested on older patterns of property, household, and labor, and they reorganized those patterns without erasing them. The persistence of inequality and of unpaid care, in forms that differ from those of earlier centuries, is a reminder that contemporary Europe is a historically contingent order, not the destined completion of a long progress.

Chapter 144 now turns to Europe and the wider world, reconsidering Europe's changing boundaries, global connections, power, and identity. The social history synthesized here supplies part of that foundation, because the labor, households, migrations, and inequalities of European societies were bound up with the empires, markets, and conflicts that connected the continent to other regions. Understanding how Europeans lived, worked, and cared for one another is essential to answering the series' final question of what Europe has meant, and for whom.
