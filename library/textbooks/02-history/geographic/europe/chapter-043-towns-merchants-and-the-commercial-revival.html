Towns, Merchants, and the Commercial Revival

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between about 1000 and 1300, a growing share of what European farmers, shepherds, miners, and artisans produced passed through a market, a town, or a merchant's hands before it reached its final user. Commerce did not appear from nothing in these centuries, but it thickened, specialized, and connected distant regions in ways that changed the relationship between the countryside and the town.

Consider a bale of wool leaving an English estate in the later thirteenth century. It may first have been sold to a regional merchant at a market town, carried by cart to a port, shipped across the North Sea, sold again in a Flemish city, woven, fulled, and dyed by skilled artisans, and then carried south to the fairs of Champagne, where Italian merchants might buy the finished cloth and pay for it through credit arrangements that were settled on a fixed schedule. Each step involved different people, different laws, different coins, and different risks. Almost none of those people would have said that they were taking part in a "commercial revival," and yet the chain of exchanges that connected an English sheep to an Italian customer would have been much harder to assemble two centuries earlier.

This chapter studies how that chain, and thousands of shorter and humbler ones like it, came to exist. Chapter 42 examined the agricultural expansion, population growth, land clearance, and denser rural settlement of the High Middle Ages. This chapter takes the surplus and the people produced by that expansion as its starting condition and asks how they fed an increasingly interconnected urban and commercial economy. Its assignment is precise: to trace urban growth, fairs, guilds, banking, Mediterranean and Baltic commerce, and expanding regional markets. The larger question is not simply why towns grew, but how towns, merchants, markets, credit, transport, institutions, and regional specialization began tying different parts of Europe together between roughly 1000 and 1300.

The chapter's central argument is that Europe's commercial economy expanded through the interaction of demographic growth, agricultural surplus, urbanization, regional specialization, improved commercial institutions, and widening trade networks. This was neither the sudden rebirth of commerce after centuries of isolation nor the emergence of a modern capitalist economy. It was a gradual and regionally varied intensification of exchange. Commerce around the Mediterranean had never disappeared, local markets had never vanished, and the great majority of Europeans remained rural cultivators in 1300. What changed was density, scale, institutional sophistication, and the degree to which producers in one region depended on buyers, suppliers, and credit in another.

The story is also Europe-wide rather than Italian or Flemish alone. Mediterranean ports such as Venice, Genoa, and Pisa traded with Byzantine, Egyptian, Syrian, and North African partners. Northern towns such as Lübeck, Bruges, and London built networks around timber, grain, fish, furs, wax, wool, and cloth. Iberian ports linked Christian and Muslim economies. Central and eastern European towns, many founded or reorganized in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, connected mining districts, grain lands, and forests to wider markets. Jewish merchants and financiers, Muslim traders, Greek and Italian communities in Constantinople, and Germans in Baltic ports all belonged to this world, as did the women who brewed, spun, sold, and lent in local markets.

Historians reconstruct this world from evidence quite different from the chronicles and charters that dominate political history. Notarial contracts, customs and toll registers, town charters, guild statutes, fair ordinances, merchant letters, coin hoards, and the excavated remains of ships and warehouses allow historians to follow goods and credit with unusual specificity. Each of these sources has limitations, and the chapter introduces them as it proceeds.

Within Part VII, this chapter occupies the second place in a sequence that begins with Chapter 42 and moves on through Chapter 44, which examines church reform and the investiture conflict, and Chapter 45, which turns to monarchy. The commercial and urban developments examined here shaped the fiscal resources of rulers, the institutional world of the church, and the social composition of later medieval Europe, but this chapter treats them principally as economic and institutional transformations. Within Volume II, it forms part of a larger account of a Europe that became denser, more connected, and more internally differentiated between the Carolingian world examined in Chapter 31 and the crises of the fourteenth century examined beginning in Chapter 50.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians describe a "commercial revival" after 1000 and why the term can mislead if it implies the disappearance of earlier commerce.
  2. Trace the demographic, agricultural, political, and institutional causes of urban growth and distinguish expanded older towns from newly founded ones.
  3. Compare the principal types of medieval town, including Italian communes, episcopal towns, royal boroughs, castle towns, ports, and planned frontier towns.
  4. Analyze how charters, privileges, and communal institutions shaped urban commerce without assuming that towns were simply islands of freedom.
  5. Describe the commercial hierarchy from village market to interregional trade and explain why ordinary regional exchange was more important than luxury trade.
  6. Explain the functions, chronology, and limits of the Champagne fairs within the wider European economy.
  7. Distinguish merchant guilds from craft guilds and assess both their economic benefits and their exclusionary effects.
  8. Compare Mediterranean and northern commercial systems, including their goods, institutions, and relationships with Byzantine, Muslim, and Slavic societies.
  9. Interpret the development of money, credit, partnerships, and banking without describing medieval merchant-bankers as modern commercial banks.
  10. Evaluate the debate over whether the period witnessed a "commercial revolution" and identify the evidence on which each position depends.
  11. Assess regional specialization and the persistent barriers to integration, and explain why Europe in 1300 was more connected but not a modern economy.
  12. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of merchant and administrative records as historical evidence.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

The commercial world of the eleventh century inherited several distinct legacies. The Roman Empire had linked the Mediterranean basin through shipping, roads, a common coinage, and a dense network of cities, as examined in Chapter 22. Those arrangements did not survive intact the political fragmentation examined in Chapters 27 and 28, but the Mediterranean continued to carry goods and people, and the eastern Roman state described in Chapters 36 and 37 maintained a monetary economy centered on Constantinople. In the seventh and eighth centuries, the expansion of Islamic political power created new commercial systems around the southern and eastern Mediterranean, linking Egypt, Syria, North Africa, Sicily, and al-Andalus to the Indian Ocean and Central Asian trade. Latin Europe lay at the western and northern margins of these systems, but not outside them.

Within the Latin West, the Carolingian world examined in Chapter 31 relied heavily on great estates, royal and ecclesiastical households, and a silver penny that circulated in modest quantities. The Viking and Scandinavian expansion examined in Chapter 32 created, alongside its violence, durable trading routes across the North Sea, the Baltic, and the rivers of eastern Europe toward the Black Sea and Caspian, a connection that also shaped the formation of Kievan Rus examined in Chapter 39. Markets and trading settlements, including Dorestad, Hedeby (also called Haithabu), and Italian ports, existed long before the High Middle Ages. The manorial and seigneurial arrangements discussed in Chapter 34 meant that much production was consumed within estates or rendered as dues, but lords also sold surpluses and purchased what their estates could not supply.

The decisive new condition after about 1000 was growth, examined in Chapter 42. Rising population, clearance of woodland and marsh, improved cultivation in some regions, and denser rural settlement increased the number of people able to buy and sell, the volume of grain and other produce available to be marketed, and the number of rural households able to support themselves partly through craft or wage labor. Greater political stability in many regions, including the consolidation of kingdoms and principalities traced in Chapter 33 and the Christianization of Scandinavia and central Europe in Chapter 35, reduced some risks of travel and made it easier for rulers to protect markets and tax commerce. Chapters 44 to 46 examine how church reform and the growth of monarchies and the Empire altered authority; here those developments matter chiefly as the political setting in which towns negotiated their rights.

Geography, as Chapter 2 emphasized, shaped the possibilities. Navigable rivers such as the Rhine, Rhône, Seine, Po, Elbe, Vistula, and Dnieper carried bulky goods more cheaply than roads could. The Mediterranean, North Sea, and Baltic provided maritime highways, and the Alpine passes linked Italy to the north. Coal was not yet central, but timber, salt, grain, wine, metals, wool, and fish were all unevenly distributed, and that unevenness gave regions reasons to exchange. Money also mattered. Silver pennies, or deniers, predominated in the Latin West for most of the period, with gold coinage associated more with Byzantine and Islamic worlds until Italian cities and others began minting gold in the thirteenth century.

Students should note one methodological point at the outset. Much of what is known about commerce comes from records produced when growth generated documentation: notaries recording contracts, towns issuing charters, rulers collecting tolls, merchants keeping accounts. A rise in surviving documents can therefore exaggerate a rise in economic activity itself. Historians correct for this by combining written evidence with archaeology, coin finds, and the evidence of buildings and landscapes, a challenge discussed further in the Historical Methods feature in Section 43.5.

Section 43.1

Europe's Commercial Economy around 1000

It is tempting to imagine that trade vanished with the western Roman Empire and returned, as if from hibernation, in the eleventh century. The evidence does not support that picture. Around 1000, local markets already operated in many regions, supplying towns, monasteries, and lordly households. Mediterranean commerce retained substantial continuity, with Byzantine and Islamic commercial systems at its center. Venice and Amalfi were already active ports, and Venice in particular had long served as an intermediary between the Byzantine world and the Latin West. In the north, the seas and rivers carried exchange among Frisian, Scandinavian, Anglo-Saxon, Frankish, and Slavic communities. Monasteries and great estates exchanged surpluses of grain, wine, salt, and cloth. The Jewish merchant communities whose letters survive in the Cairo Geniza, a storeroom of documents from medieval Cairo, show an active Mediterranean commercial world in the eleventh century that connected Egypt, North Africa, Sicily, and the Levant.

What changed after 1000 was not the existence of trade but its acceleration, spread, specialization, and density. More people lived in towns, more producers sold to markets rather than only to their own lords, more coin circulated, and more regions came to depend on goods and services from elsewhere. The change was cumulative rather than sudden, and it unfolded at different speeds in different places. Northern Italy and Flanders experienced intense urban growth early. Parts of eastern and central Europe saw their greatest period of town founding in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. In other regions, such as much of the interior of Iberia or the northern fringes of Scandinavia, towns remained few and small.

Historians have long described this transformation as a "commercial revival," a phrase that this chapter retains but treats with care. The word revival is useful when it points to the renewed growth of towns and the expansion of commerce in Latin Europe, especially in the north and west where urban life had contracted most sharply after antiquity. It is misleading when it implies that commerce had been dead, that all medieval economic life before 1000 was local and subsistence-based, or that the Mediterranean world was economically inert. A better formulation is that the High Middle Ages witnessed a phase of rapid expansion within a commercial economy whose roots lay in the earlier medieval and late Roman worlds.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Commercial Revival

Commercial revival is a conventional phrase for the expansion of towns, markets, merchant activity, and monetary exchange in Latin Europe between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. The older and more dramatic phrase "commercial revolution," associated especially with twentieth-century historians of medieval economic life, emphasized rupture and rapid change.

The term matters because it names a real and substantial transformation. It must be used with qualification because medieval commerce did not begin or end in these centuries. Both "revival" and "revolution" can imply a sharper break than the evidence supports, a problem examined directly in the Historical Debate in Section 43.8.

Section 43.2

Why Towns Grew

Urban growth began with the countryside. As Chapter 42 explained, rising agricultural output and a larger rural population created more people who could live without producing their own food and more surplus that could be sold. Towns depended on that surplus. A town of several thousand residents needed grain, fuel, timber, meat, fish, and drink every week, and it could obtain them only from a surrounding region that was productive enough and accessible enough to supply them. Rural specialization reinforced the pattern. Households and estates that could sell wool, wine, grain, flax, or livestock were more likely to purchase tools, cloth, salt, and other goods from market towns. Monetization, meaning the growing use of money in payments and rents, strengthened these links, since a peasant or lord who needed coin had reason to market produce.

Other drivers worked alongside demography. Greater political stability in many regions reduced some of the risks of storing and moving goods. Merchants themselves were agents of growth, since their search for profit encouraged new routes and trading settlements. Rulers and lords encouraged markets because tolls, rents, and fees from commerce were valuable sources of income, and many granted market rights and privileges in the hope of attracting settlers. Ecclesiastical centers, including cathedral and monastic sites, attracted pilgrims, clergy, servants, and craftspeople. Castles and administrative centers drew soldiers, officials, and suppliers. Improved transport, including bridges, cleared roads, canalized streams, and Alpine routes, including the Gotthard route that became passable in the early thirteenth century, widened the markets that producers could reach.

A crucial distinction is between old towns that expanded and new towns deliberately founded. Many cities of Italy, southern France, Iberia, the Rhineland, and the Danube had Roman origins or earlier medieval foundations and grew within or beyond ancient walls, sometimes on the basis of a bishop's seat or an old market. Others, particularly in northern and central Europe, were new creations. Lords and rulers established planned towns in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, including Freiburg, Lübeck, many English boroughs, the bastides of southwestern France, and numerous German-law towns across Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, and the Baltic. These foundations often offered settlers plots, legal privileges, and market monopolies. Students should not imagine every medieval town as a Roman city simply returning to life. Many had no Roman past at all, and even those that did were frequently transformed by new walls, markets, churches, and populations.

Section 43.3

What Was a Medieval Town?

The word "town" covered an extraordinary range of places. A great commune such as Milan or Genoa might house a hundred thousand people or fewer, with scholarly estimates for the largest cities around 1300, including Paris, Milan, Venice, Genoa, and Florence, ranging widely and remaining uncertain. At the other end, thousands of small market towns held a few hundred or a couple of thousand inhabitants and might be barely distinguishable from large villages except for a market, a charter, and a concentration of artisans. Historians generally define a medieval town by a combination of features rather than a single test: a concentrated population engaged substantially in non-agricultural work, a market, a degree of legal distinctiveness, and often walls or other marks of separate status.

Types of town differed with origin and function. Italian communes were large, politically self-governing cities whose commercial classes often ruled them. Episcopal towns grew around a bishop's seat and cathedral, with the bishop sometimes the principal lord. Royal boroughs in England and similar royal towns in France and elsewhere owed their privileges to the crown. Castle towns grew in the shadow of fortifications, supplying garrisons and lords. Market towns served rural districts and were often the commonest form. Ports such as Venice, Genoa, Bruges, London, Lübeck, Barcelona, and many smaller harbors depended on shipping, warehousing, and the processing of cargoes. Cathedral cities combined religious, administrative, and commercial roles. Planned frontier towns in eastern and central Europe, many organized under German or other imported legal models, were designed to attract settlers to newly cultivated or newly pacified lands.

Physically, towns were crowded and mixed. Walls defined many of them, though suburbs often spread beyond. Markets occupied squares or streets, and churches, hospitals, and monastic houses held significant property. Workshops lined streets, often combining production and sale on the same premises. Bridges, quays, and harbors structured the movement of goods. Gardens, orchards, and pastures survived within and around many towns, since urban households kept animals and grew food. Towns also drew on surrounding agricultural hinterlands. A town was never economically independent of the countryside. Urban growth depended on constant flows of food, fuel, raw materials, people, and money from rural regions, and migration from villages to towns was one of the principal sources of urban population because high urban mortality meant that towns often could not sustain their numbers through births alone.

Section 43.4

Urban Liberties, Charters, and Communes

Urban growth was institutional as well as economic. Merchants and artisans needed predictable rules for buying, selling, owning property, resolving disputes, and moving goods, and lords and rulers who wanted the revenue of thriving markets were often willing to grant them. The result was a wide range of charters, written grants defining a town's rights and obligations. A charter might confirm the right to hold a market or fair, exempt townspeople from certain tolls, fix rents and dues, establish a town court, allow inhabitants to inherit and sell property freely, or recognize some degree of self-government. Charters such as the customs of Lorris, first granted in the reign of Louis VI and confirmed by Louis VII in 1155, and those that structured the new German-law and bastide towns, transmitted models of urban privilege across regions.

Some towns went further and became communes, associations in which inhabitants swore oaths to defend one another and to govern together. The communes of northern and central Italy, emerging from the late eleventh century, evolved councils, magistrates, and courts that often controlled the city's surrounding territory. Communal movements also arose in parts of France, Flanders, and the Rhineland, sometimes through negotiation and sometimes through conflict with a bishop or lord. Struggles over urban liberties were often fierce. Townspeople might purchase privileges, extract them in moments of lordly weakness, or lose them when rulers regained strength. The political history of Italian communes belongs to Chapters 46 and 56, and the larger relationship between towns and monarchies to Chapters 45 and 46; here the point is that commerce flourished where rules were clear enough to give merchants confidence.

Historians have long been tempted by a simple formula in which town air meant freedom, the countryside feudal oppression. That formula captures something real, since in many places a person who lived in a town for a set period could escape certain servile dues, and townspeople often enjoyed legal protections unavailable to dependent peasants. But it obscures the realities of urban life. Towns were governed by oligarchies, typically wealthy merchants and property holders who controlled councils and often excluded poorer artisans, laborers, women, and outsiders. Townspeople paid taxes, obeyed guild regulations, and answered to urban courts. Tensions among patricians, craftspeople, and the poor produced conflicts examined in later chapters, including Chapter 52. Urban liberty was a bundle of specific privileges that benefited some residents far more than others.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Liberties and Commune

Liberties (or franchises) were specific legal privileges granted to a town or its inhabitants, such as exemption from certain tolls, the right to hold a market, or the right to be judged in a town court. They were not a modern, general concept of individual freedom. A commune was a sworn association of townspeople that sought collective self-government. Not every town with liberties was a commune, and communes differed widely in power.

The distinction matters because the history of towns is easily misread as a simple story of liberation from lords. In reality, urban liberties were negotiated, limited, and unequally shared.

Section 43.5

Markets and the Growth of Regional Exchange

Consider an ordinary market day in a market town of the thirteenth century. Villagers from the surrounding district arrive with grain, eggs, poultry, cheese, yarn, or livestock and sell to townspeople, to artisans who need raw materials, and to dealers who gather produce for larger markets. The same villagers buy salt, iron tools, cloth, pots, and sometimes a little imported wine or spice. Market days were fixed, often weekly, partly so that people from villages within a day's round trip on foot or by cart could attend and return home. Officials of the lord or town checked weights and measures, collected stallage and tolls, and punished fraud. Regulating the price and quality of staples such as bread and ale was a familiar concern, and English legislation of the thirteenth century addressed it directly. Most medieval trade looked like this, not like the great fairs or the Venetian galleys that dominate popular images of medieval commerce.

Regional trade moved a range of goods. Grain from the fertile districts around Paris traveled by the Seine and its tributaries toward the capital. Wine from Gascony reached England by sea, and wine from the Rhine and Moselle valleys reached northern towns by river. Salt from Atlantic coastal pans and inland brine springs was carried considerable distances, and timber and fish moved along rivers and coasts. Ironworking districts supplied tools, and potters' kilns supplied distinctive wares that archaeologists can trace across regions. Wool and cloth moved between producing districts and finishing towns. Many of these goods were bulky and low in value relative to weight, so they traveled by water where possible, which is one reason river and coastal towns grew so strongly.

The result was a commercial hierarchy that is arguably the chapter's most important economic concept. At the base stood villages and their local markets. Above them were regional market towns where nearby producers brought goods. Larger towns collected produce and redistributed manufactured goods, and at the top stood major commercial centers, including great ports and fair towns, linked to interregional and international routes. Take grain as an example: a peasant sold surplus to a local dealer, who sold to a market-town merchant, who shipped it to a port, from which it might be carried to a densely populated textile city whose workers could not feed themselves from their own fields. A transaction at the top rested on a long chain of small transactions below it. The hierarchy also operated in reverse, bringing imported goods, coin, and credit down into regional towns and eventually into villages. Growth at one level encouraged growth at the others.

Commerce also reached into the countryside in ways that blur the line between rural and urban economies. Cottagers spun yarn and wove for merchants, peasants sold produce to raise cash for rents and taxes, and the monetization of dues meant that many rural households needed coin and therefore needed markets. At the same time, much produce was still consumed within households or rendered to lords, and many villages participated in markets only intermittently. The market economy was substantial, but it did not replace subsistence agriculture; it overlaid and drew upon it.

Markets grew within a framework of privilege. A lord or ruler who held a market right normally expected that rivals would not open a market close enough to draw away its trade, and disputes over rival markets reached royal courts in England and elsewhere. Markets were therefore as much instruments of revenue and lordship as expressions of free exchange. The same framework shaped how disputes were settled. Fairs and larger markets had courts able to resolve quarrels quickly so that visiting merchants could continue their journeys, like the English fair courts popularly known as piepowder courts. Older scholarship described such courts as expressions of a universal law merchant, lex mercatoria, shared by traders across Europe. Recent legal historians dispute that picture, finding little evidence of a coherent or autonomous trans-European merchant law and emphasizing that commercial customs and procedures were strongly local and embedded in particular jurisdictions, even though some practices traveled between markets with the merchants who used them.

Women were prominent in local markets as brewers, bakers, spinners, sellers of food and cloth, and small-scale lenders, though they were often excluded from the higher levels of long-distance commerce and from many guilds. Their work is documented unevenly, usually through the regulations and fines that governed it, so the scale of female participation is easier to glimpse than to measure.

Historical Methods

What Merchant Records Tell Us, and What They Do Not

Commercial history rests on a different evidentiary base from the agricultural and demographic history of Chapter 42. Historians use notarial registers, especially from Italian ports, which preserve contracts for partnerships, loans, sales, and shipping. They use customs accounts and toll registers, which record goods passing particular points and sometimes the sums collected. They use town charters, guild statutes, and fair ordinances, which show the rules that regulated trade. Merchant letters and account books become more common in the thirteenth century, especially in Italy. Coin hoards and finds show where money circulated. Archaeology adds excavated warehouses, harbors, workshops, and shipwrecks, including the eleventh-century ship excavated at Serçe Limanı off the southwestern coast of Anatolia, which carried a cargo including glass.

Each source type has limits. Contracts record intentions and agreements, not always completed voyages. Customs records document goods that were taxed and may miss exempt or smuggled cargo. Charters describe privileges that may have been disputed or ignored in practice. Surviving documentation is geographically uneven, with Italian notarial material exceptionally rich and many regions poorly documented. The rise in surviving records after about 1100 also partly reflects growing literacy and administration rather than commerce alone. Women, the poor, small producers, and most rural traders appear only indirectly, through the rules designed to regulate them.

Section 43.6

Fairs and the Champagne System

Fairs were periodic markets, held at fixed times and places, that drew merchants from long distances and operated under special legal protection. Many towns held local fairs, but the fairs of Champagne became the best-known meeting place of the commercial worlds of northern and southern Europe. They were held in a cycle among four towns in the territory of the counts of Champagne: Lagny, Bar-sur-Aube, Provins, and Troyes. Six fairs, traditionally listed as those of Lagny in January, Bar-sur-Aube in mid-Lent, Provins in May, Troyes in the summer, Provins again in September, and Troyes in the autumn, meant that merchants could find a fair under comital protection at some point in almost every season. Historians treat the cycle as essential to the system's power, since the fairs did not compete with one another but formed a continuous sequence.

Each fair was itself divided into stages. Historians commonly describe an opening period for setting up and registering goods, then sales of cloth, then sales of other goods sold by weight such as leather, furs, and spices, and finally a period devoted to payments and the settlement of accounts. The last stage mattered as much as the first. Merchants who had bought and sold on credit could balance their obligations against one another and pay the remainder in coin or by transferring credit, which reduced the amount of money that had to be carried. Money changers set up their tables to exchange the many coinages that arrived, and documents drawn up and sealed under the authority of the fair gave debts a security that private agreements often lacked, as historians of the fairs have emphasized.

The counts of Champagne encouraged this system because it served their interests. They offered safe conduct to merchants traveling to and from the fairs, appointed officials to guard the fairs and enforce obligations, maintained special courts, and collected fees and tolls in return. They also needed to make the fairs attractive to merchants who had alternatives. Merchants from Flanders, Artois, Brabant, and other northern regions brought cloth, and the cloth of towns such as Arras, Ypres, and Ghent was prominent in the trade. Italian merchants, from cities including Genoa, Piacenza, Siena, Lucca, and Florence, purchased northern cloth and brought spices, silks, and other products of the Mediterranean and eastern trade. Champagne's location, between the manufacturing towns of the north and the commercial cities of Italy, and the security that its rulers offered, explain its prominence better than any special fertility or manufacturing strength of its own, although Provins and Troyes also had local cloth industries.

The Champagne fairs should not be treated as the single creator of European commerce. They flourished above all in the later twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and they were one node in a larger system of regional fairs and urban markets that included major fairs in Flanders and elsewhere. From the later thirteenth century their relative prominence declined. Direct sea links between the Mediterranean and the North Sea offered an alternative to the overland route, and Genoese merchants maintained a regular connection with the Flemish coast from at least 1277. Italian firms increasingly stationed resident agents in northern cities, which reduced the need to meet in Champagne at fixed intervals. Political change mattered as well. The marriage of Joan, heiress of Champagne and Navarre, to the future Philip IV in 1284 prepared a dynastic union, and when Philip became king in 1285 Champagne came under the French crown, which brought its fairs into a state increasingly eager to tax and regulate commerce. Conflict between France and Flanders around 1300 further disrupted traffic. The fairs continued to operate, but their central role in international commerce diminished, a process that shows how commercial systems could change when transport, institutions, and politics changed together.

Section 43.7

Guilds: Merchants, Crafts, and Regulation

Guilds were associations of people sharing an occupation or commercial interest, and they appear across much of Europe in varied forms. It helps to separate merchant guilds from craft guilds. Merchant guilds organized traders, often to defend their privileges, regulate trade within a town, secure advantages in markets, and negotiate collectively with rulers or foreign authorities. In some towns, as in parts of England and Flanders, the merchant guild was closely tied to the town's governing elite. Craft guilds organized artisans in particular trades, such as weavers, fullers, dyers, bakers, butchers, goldsmiths, and metalworkers, and were especially associated with the larger and more manufacturing-oriented towns.

The functions of guilds varied but often included regulating entry into a trade, setting quality standards, supervising apprenticeship, protecting members' access to materials and markets, settling disputes among members, supporting members in need, funding religious observance and burial, and representing the trade before rulers and town councils. Revenue came from entry fees, fines, and subscriptions. Guild officers, variously called jurors, wardens, or by other names, inspected work, and offenders could be fined or have goods confiscated. Apprentices learned a trade under a master for a term of years, journeymen worked for wages, and masters ran workshops, though the exact structure differed greatly by place and date.

The best evidence for how a large city organized its crafts is the Livre des métiers of Paris, compiled around 1268 under Étienne Boileau, the provost of Paris appointed under Louis IX. Boileau did not invent guilds. Representatives of the trades presented their own rules, and the resulting register records the statutes of roughly one hundred crafts. Their provisions are revealing. The goldsmiths' statute, for example, allowed anyone who knew the craft to practice it in Paris provided he followed its customs, required work in gold to meet the Paris standard, and forbade work at night except on commissions for the king, the queen, their family, or the bishop of Paris. It also exempted goldsmiths from customs duties on goods bought or sold for their trade, provided for inspection by trusted members of the craft who could bring offenders before the provost, and tied the trade to a confraternity whose funds paid for a meal for the poor of the Hôtel-Dieu each Easter. Other statutes limited the number of apprentices a master could take, though one early statute for mail makers allowed as many as a master wished. Regulations for weavers specified the number of threads in the warp and the width of the finished cloth. Many trades banned night work, which the statutes justified largely by concern for quality, and the purchase of the right to practice was required in some trades but not others.

These provisions illustrate both the functions and the ambiguities of guilds. Standards protected customers and the reputation of the trade, but entry rules, fees, and limits on apprentices protected established masters from competition. Guilds provided mutual aid and religious fellowship, but they also policed members and served the town and the crown as instruments of supervision and taxation. They should not be romanticized as medieval trade unions or as institutions defending the common worker. Guild regulation could exclude outsiders, migrants, poorer artisans, and women, although a few trades, notably some in silk working, were largely organized by women, and widows in many places could continue a deceased husband's workshop. Membership in guilds in Christian towns often rested on Christian oaths and religious confraternities, which excluded Jews, though the extent of exclusion varied by region and period. Jewish economic activity was diverse, including trade, crafts, medicine, and credit, and was shaped by legal restrictions and by the protection or exploitation of rulers. Guild importance also varied enormously. In some towns, guilds dominated government and production. In others, they were weak, late, or absent. Tensions between merchants who controlled the sale of cloth and the artisans who made it, which emerged in Flanders and elsewhere, belong to the social conflicts of Chapter 52. The broader experience of artisans and townspeople belongs to Chapter 49; here guilds matter as one of the institutions that structured production and commerce.

A master artisan in an established craft guild: Guild rules protect the quality and reputation of the trade, guarantee training, and prevent undercutting by unskilled or unregulated producers. The system secures the master's livelihood and offers mutual aid and religious fellowship.

Section 43.8

Mediterranean Commerce: Venice, Genoa, Pisa, and Wider Networks

The Mediterranean was one of the largest and most sophisticated commercial arenas available to Latin merchants. Venice, Genoa, and Pisa rose as maritime powers whose ships, merchants, and governments worked closely together, and their ships served both as commercial carriers and as naval forces. Venice, which had long been politically tied to Byzantium before gradually asserting its independence, obtained major privileges in the Byzantine Empire. In 1082 Emperor Alexios I Komnenos, in return for Venetian naval support against the Normans of southern Italy, exempted Venetian merchants from many duties in imperial ports and confirmed them a quarter in Constantinople on the Golden Horn. Pisa and Genoa obtained comparable, though at first more limited, privileges in the twelfth century. The Byzantine government used these concessions to purchase naval aid and balance rival powers, but they also gave Italian merchants advantages over Greek merchants that provoked resentment, a tension that Chapter 41 considers in connection with the end of Byzantium.

Italian merchants worked in an arena that already contained Byzantine, Greek, Jewish, Armenian, and Muslim merchants. Alexandria, the principal Egyptian port, received spices and other goods from the Red Sea and Indian Ocean routes, and Italian cities negotiated treaties with Egyptian rulers securing lodgings, warehouses, and fixed duties. In North Africa, ports such as Tunis, Bougie, and Ceuta attracted Genoese and Pisan merchants, and the Genoese contract of 1163 in the Primary Source Window below sends silk and paper to Tunis. Sicily and southern Italy, under Norman rule, combined Greek, Latin, and Muslim populations and linked Mediterranean routes. Amalfi, once prominent, declined after the Norman conquest of southern Italy and a Pisan raid in the twelfth century. Iberian ports such as Barcelona and Valencia joined the networks, and the conquest of Mallorca in 1229 added a major island base. The Cairo Geniza documents show that Jewish merchants were important and highly active participants in eleventh-century Mediterranean commerce; Italian commercial predominance developed later within an already active and religiously diverse system.

Merchants worked through fondaci in Italian and funduq in Arabic, which housed foreign merchants, stored their goods, and subjected them to regulation by local authorities or, in some cases, their own consuls. Consuls of Italian cities settled disputes among their own merchants abroad. Brokers and interpreters mediated transactions. The goods that crossed the Mediterranean included spices, pepper, silk, sugar, cotton, alum, dyes, and luxury textiles from the east; grain, wine, oil, and salt moved in many directions; and timber, iron, and other metals flowed from Latin Europe to regions short of them. The trade in timber and iron to Egypt, which lacked abundant forests, illustrates how commerce could cut across hostility. The Third Lateran Council in 1179 forbade supplying Muslims with arms, iron, and wood for galleys, yet such bans were difficult to enforce and merchants repeatedly sought ways around them or secured exemptions.

The trade also included enslaved people. Mediterranean slavery drew victims from many regions, including the Black Sea coasts and the Balkans, and in the later thirteenth century and after Genoese and Venetian merchants became increasingly prominent in this traffic. Muslim and Christian societies both held enslaved people, and church teaching in principle discouraged the enslavement of fellow Christians, though in practice the rule was applied unevenly. Slavery was a real and often brutal component of Mediterranean commercial life and should not be omitted from an honest account of these networks.

The Mediterranean economy demonstrated that political and religious boundaries, though real, were not impermeable economic walls. Latin Christian merchants traded extensively with Byzantine Greeks and with Muslim merchants and rulers, often under treaties and commercial agreements, even in periods of conflict or crusading. Muslim and Jewish merchants operated in Christian ports and Iberian cities. At the same time, commerce could be a source of conflict. Italian cities competed fiercely, fought one another at sea, and sometimes pressed their advantages at the expense of local merchants. Crusading, examined in Chapter 47, opened new opportunities for Italian cities in the eastern Mediterranean, and as Chapter 41 explained in connection with the Fourth Crusade, tied commerce to violent political change. This section addresses the commercial networks rather than crusading ideology and warfare, which belong to Chapter 47.

Behind the merchants stood the governments of the Italian cities. Venice, Genoa, and Pisa negotiated treaties, appointed consuls to represent their merchants abroad, and mobilized fleets to protect or enforce their interests. Ships were often owned in shares, as the Venetian contract of 1073 shows when two partners buy two shares in a vessel, which spread the cost and risk of ownership among many investors. Shipbuilding was concentrated in specialized yards, among which the Venetian Arsenal, traditionally dated to the early twelfth century, became the most famous. Voyages were seasonal, since winter weather closed many routes, and they faced storms, piracy, and the danger of seizure in disputes between cities. The profits of this commerce flowed back into the home cities, where they funded buildings, fleets, and the political ambitions of merchant elites. That connection between commerce and urban politics is one reason that the political histories of Venice and Genoa, treated in later chapters, cannot be separated from their economic histories.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was There a Medieval Commercial Revolution?

The question: Did the period from roughly the tenth to the early fourteenth century witness a "commercial revolution," a rapid and fundamental transformation of European economic life, or a more gradual and regionally uneven intensification of existing exchange?

Interpretation A: A dramatic transformation. Influenced by the Belgian historian Henri Pirenne, whose argument that Islamic expansion had cut Europe off from Mediterranean commerce and that trade and towns revived dramatically in the eleventh century became famous in the early twentieth century, and developed in modified form by later scholars such as Robert Lopez, who wrote of a commercial revolution of the Middle Ages, this view emphasizes dramatic urban growth, the expansion of merchant communities, monetization, new contract forms, fairs, and long-distance commerce, and sees these changes as together decisive.
Interpretation B: Gradual change and substantial continuity. Later research, drawing on archaeology, numismatics, and the study of earlier sources, has challenged Pirenne's thesis about a collapse of Mediterranean commerce, shown considerable commercial activity before 1000, emphasized how regionally uneven growth was, and stressed that agriculture remained dominant and that many practices associated with the "revolution" evolved gradually from earlier forms.

Evidence: The first interpretation relies on the scale of urban growth, the multiplication of markets and fairs, the surviving notarial contracts, and the visible institutional innovation. The second relies on evidence of earlier trade, including Mediterranean contacts in the early Middle Ages, the Cairo Geniza, coin finds, and the gradual development of institutions, and on the recognition that rising documentation partly reflects better recording.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians understand the relationship between earlier and later medieval economies and the degree to which the High Middle Ages were a break or a continuation. Most scholars today would say that "commercial revolution" can identify the scale of the transformation but becomes misleading if it implies a sudden rupture, a uniform process, or a modern capitalist economy.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports the idea of dramatic change and one that supports continuity. Explain what additional evidence, such as archaeological data from a particular region, would help you decide how to characterize the period.

Section 43.9

Northern Seas and the Baltic

North of the Alps, a distinct but increasingly connected commercial system developed around the North Sea and the Baltic. The North Sea linked England, Flanders, the Low Countries, the Rhine mouth, northern Germany, and Scandinavia. The Baltic connected northern Germany, Scandinavia, Poland, the eastern Baltic coast, and, through river routes, the Rus lands, with Novgorod the great trading city of the northwest. Ports and towns included Bruges and other Flemish centers, London and the English east coast, Hamburg, Lübeck, Visby on the island of Gotland, Scandinavian harbors, and Novgorod. The goods carried fall into two groups that students should not confuse. The first was bulk staples: grain, timber, salt, and fish, especially herring, which were cheap per unit but required many ships and supplied basic necessities to regions with less timber, grain, or fish. The second was higher-value goods: furs and wax from the forests of the northeast, amber, metals, and cloth. The cloth was a manufactured product of Flemish and English towns rather than a raw material, and furs and wax could be valuable in proportion to their weight.

One of the clearest examples of how these goods interlocked is herring. Large autumn herring fisheries at Scania, in southern Scandinavia, drew merchants and fishermen from many regions, and the fish had to be salted quickly to be marketed inland. The salt came largely from Lüneburg and was carried to Lübeck, where it was shipped across the Baltic. The salt and herring trade thus linked an inland mining and brine district, a port town, a fishery, and distant consumers who ate fish on fast days. Timber, grain, and wax from the eastern Baltic and furs from the Rus lands flowed in the other direction toward the west, in exchange for cloth, salt, metals, and manufactured goods.

The commercial growth of northern Germany was closely connected to eastward settlement and town foundation. Lübeck, founded in 1143 by Count Adolf II of Holstein and refounded in 1159 under Henry the Lion, duke of Saxony and Bavaria, became a gateway between the North Sea and the Baltic, and its legal customs became a model for other towns. Riga, founded in 1201 by Bishop Albert in the context of the Livonian mission and crusade treated elsewhere in this textbook, extended the German commercial presence along the eastern Baltic. German merchants established a presence on Gotland at Visby and trading posts at Novgorod, where they maintained a walled compound, while merchants from the Rhineland and other regions traded in England and Flanders, and merchants of Cologne held a privileged place in London by the later twelfth century. Ships called cogs, capacious sailing vessels with high sides, became common on northern routes in the thirteenth century. Navigation was seasonal, since winter ice and storms closed many routes, and the rhythms of fairs, fisheries, and shipping were fixed by the sailing season.

In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, associations of merchants cooperated to secure privileges abroad and protect shipping, and towns such as Lübeck and Hamburg entered agreements, including a well-known alliance of 1241 providing for joint action to protect merchants against robbery, that helped lay foundations for later cooperation. A chronological caution is needed. Students should not project the mature Hanseatic League back into the eleventh or twelfth century. The institutionalized Hanseatic world, with its network of member towns, common privileges, and diplomatic and economic power, belongs principally to the later Middle Ages and is treated in Chapter 54. In the period of this chapter, one sees commercial networks and merchant associations, partly informal and shifting, out of which that later system developed. Lübeck and other northern towns became increasingly connected in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, laying foundations for the more institutionalized networks that Chapter 54 examines. The northern system was also not simply German. Scandinavian, Flemish, English, Rus, Slavic, and Baltic peoples participated, and eastward settlement involved complex interactions among settlers, local populations, rulers, and the church, aspects of which are discussed in Chapter 35.

At the western end of the northern system, Flanders held a special position. Bruges, which lay inland but had access to the North Sea through the Zwin estuary, increasingly relied on outports and transshipment points, especially Damme and later Sluis, and became in the thirteenth century a hub where northern merchants met Italian and other southern merchants, and foreign merchants were lodged and granted privileges there. Norwegian dried cod, known as stockfish, was shipped through Bergen to markets as far away as Flanders and England and illustrates how far commodities of the northern fisheries traveled. At the eastern end, Novgorod negotiated treaties with German and Gotlandic merchants in the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries that regulated their access to the city, their legal protections, and the terms of their trade, and Novgorod itself was a Rus city with its own rulers, institutions, and interests, not merely a trading post for others. Seen from the northern system, the Baltic and North Seas were not peripheral to a Mediterranean core. They were a distinct commercial region whose goods, ships, and institutions supplied much of what western Europe consumed.

Section 43.10

Money, Credit, Partnerships, and Banking

Commercial growth created practical problems. Moving large quantities of coin over long distances was dangerous, slow, and expensive. Merchants dealt with many different coinages, since rulers, bishops, and cities minted their own, and weights and silver content varied. Trust was difficult to establish across distance, and courts and enforcement differed from place to place. The responses to these problems were gradual and cumulative rather than the invention of a single system. Silver pennies remained the everyday coin of the Latin West, and larger silver coins such as the Venetian grosso, introduced around 1200, eased larger payments. Gold never disappeared from the wider Mediterranean, where Byzantine and Islamic gold coins circulated and Latin merchants encountered them, and Frederick II issued gold augustales in southern Italy from 1231. In 1252 Genoa and Florence struck gold coins, the genovino and the florin, and Venice followed with the ducat in 1284. These durable, high-value coins were increasingly important for large payments over long distances.

Money changers appraised and exchanged coin, and many also took deposits and made loans. Merchants kept written accounts and used contracts drawn by notaries, professionals who produced legally recognized documents and whose registers survive in large numbers in Italian archives. The contract of exchange had a double function. It allowed a sum to be paid in one place and repaid in another, in a different currency, which transferred funds without carrying coin, and because repayment came later, it could also function as credit. Genoese notarial records of the twelfth century already preserve such exchange contracts, early forms of the instruments that matured into bills of exchange. The sea loan, in which repayment depended on the ship or cargo arriving safely, combined credit with maritime risk allocation. Gregory IX's decretal Naviganti, incorporated into the Liber Extra in 1234, treated profit above the principal on such a risk-bearing loan as usurious.

Partnerships were especially important. The commenda and related contracts allowed wealthy investors, including widows, clergy, and others who did not travel, to participate in trade, and allowed young or poorer merchants to access capital. The Primary Source Window shows how the terms varied: the Venetian contract of 1073 divided profits in equal halves where both parties contributed capital, while the Genoese contract of 1163 gave the traveling merchant, who contributed no capital, one quarter of the profit. A related contract, the societas, joined partners who each contributed capital, as when one Genoese partner in 1163 supplied twice as much as the other and the two divided the profit equally. Longer-term partnerships, often among family members, developed into firms that could operate across several cities. Italian merchant firms from cities such as Genoa, Siena, Lucca, and Florence built extensive networks, lent to rulers, handled the proceeds of papal and royal revenues, and traded in wool and cloth. The Riccardi of Lucca, for example, lent heavily to Edward I of England in the later thirteenth century. Religious orders such as the Templars also safeguarded and transferred funds, a financial role distinct from their military one.

Students should avoid describing medieval merchant-bankers as modern commercial banks. Medieval banking grew from the overlapping activities of merchants, money changers, financiers, deposit takers, and lenders, and the same firm might trade goods, change money, take deposits, and lend to rulers. The mature, widespread use of bills of exchange and more complex banking institutions belongs substantially to the later medieval commercial world, so it would be a mistake to imagine a fully formed fourteenth-century banking system in 1150. Church doctrine on usury, condemned in various forms by medieval councils, shaped the moral and legal environment of credit. Some arrangements were structured to fit its limits, while others had independent commercial purposes, including risk sharing, currency exchange, and the practicalities of long-distance payment. Historians disagree about how far particular contracts were evasions of doctrine and how far they were ordinary commercial instruments, and the theology of usury belongs principally to discussions of learning and religion, including Chapter 48.

Lending was carried out by many groups. Jewish lenders operated in many regions under varying restrictions and royal or lordly protection, often vulnerable to sudden expulsion or confiscation. Christian lenders were also active, including pawnbrokers and financiers known in northern Europe as Lombards and Cahorsins, from northern Italy and Cahors, as well as monastic houses and local lenders. Moneylending arose from combinations of demand for credit, legal restrictions on other occupations and on interest, royal and lordly interests, and community networks, and no single cause explains it.

Practical record keeping underpinned all of this. Merchants who traded across distances needed to track what they had sent, to whom they owed money, and what profits they were due, and Italian merchants in particular kept increasingly sophisticated written accounts by the thirteenth century. Reputation mattered as much as law, since a merchant known to default would find credit hard to obtain, and families and neighborhood or city communities often provided the networks of trust within which credit circulated. Insurance was also developing, though formal marine insurance contracts appear mainly in the fourteenth century, and earlier merchants spread risk by dividing cargoes among several ships, forming partnerships, and using the sea loan. These practices show that medieval commerce was not unsophisticated, but they also show how dependent it was on personal relationships and local institutions compared with later systems of impersonal finance.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Commenda

The commenda (plural commendae) was a Mediterranean commercial contract in which an investor, sometimes called the sedentary or stationary partner, entrusted capital to a traveling merchant who carried goods or money on a voyage and shared the resulting profit according to the agreed terms. In the unilateral form the investor typically bore the financial risk of loss of capital, while the traveling partner risked labor and effort; in bilateral forms, such as the Venetian collegantia, both parties contributed capital.

It matters because it let capital and commercial skill be combined without requiring each investor to travel. Modern equivalents such as the limited partnership are imperfect parallels. The commenda arose in a world of notaries, ship cargoes, and personal trust, and was not a corporation or a bank.

Paraphrase: Venice, August 1073 Giovanni Lissado acknowledges that he has received 200 Venetian pounds from Sevasto Orefice and has himself invested 100 pounds. With the combined capital they hold two shares in a ship commanded by Gosmiro da Molino, and Giovanni undertakes to carry the whole venture to Thebes in that ship and to work with it as well as he can. Profit is to be divided in exact halves. If the goods are lost to the sea or to hostile people, and that is proved, neither side may claim anything from the other, and any remainder is shared in proportion to the investment. If Giovanni fails to keep the agreement, he promises to repay both capital and profit in double from his land and house. Witnesses and the notary are named.
Paraphrase: Genoa, 7 October 1163 Ingo Bedello declares publicly that he is carrying goods worth 41 Genoese pounds and 6 shillings belonging to Guglielmotto Ciriolo, invested in silk and paper, to Tunis and then to Genoa. On his return he will put the proceeds into Guglielmotto's hands or those of his messenger, and after the capital is deducted he is to receive one quarter of the profit. Guglielmotto contributes only the original money and bears no expenses. A separate entry of 29 September 1163 records a partnership in which Stabile contributes 88 pounds and Ansaldo 44; Ansaldo carries it to Tunis or wherever his ship goes, and after capital is deducted the profit is divided in half.

Analysis: The contracts show how medieval merchants combined capital and labor, how they allocated risk, and how notaries gave transactions legal form. The shares are instructive. Where the traveling merchant also invested, as in Venice, the profit was split evenly, whereas the Genoese traveler who invested nothing received a quarter. The destinations, Thebes and Tunis, and the goods, silk and paper, show the reach of the trade across Byzantine and Muslim markets. They also show that investors included people who did not travel. The contracts do not show how voyages turned out, what prices were obtained, or the experiences of crews, local partners, or the Byzantine and North African merchants on the other side of the transactions. The surviving documents come from cities with exceptionally rich notarial records, which makes it easy to overstate how typical Venice and Genoa were of commerce elsewhere in Europe.

Questions for students:

  1. What does each contract say about how merchants raised and shared capital and risk?
  2. Who created these records, and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from them about trade routes, goods, and trust?
  4. What can historians not establish from the contracts alone?
Section 43.11

Commercial Regions and Economic Specialization

As commerce deepened, regions increasingly specialized in what they produced for markets beyond immediate local consumption. The clearest case is the link between England and Flanders. England produced wool that was prized for its quality, and monastic houses, great lords, and many smaller sheep farmers sold it to merchants. Cistercian monasteries were among the large producers, and some sold their wool years in advance to Italian merchants. The wool was shipped to Flanders, where cities such as Ypres, Ghent, Bruges, and Douai organized large numbers of weavers, fullers, and dyers, and where imported wool supported a large urban population. Flanders in turn depended on imported food, especially grain from neighboring regions of northern France and elsewhere around the North Sea; occasional Baltic grain imports are documented by the later thirteenth century, but they did not yet dominate Flemish supply, and the urban population could not feed itself from its own territory. The relationship made both regions vulnerable to disruption. The English crown recognized the importance of wool revenue, imposing a regular customs duty on wool exports in 1275, and interruptions to the wool trade, such as the Anglo-Flemish wool embargo of 1270 to 1274, hurt Flemish cities and their workers, showing how political decisions in one region could affect the economy of another.

Northern Italy combined urban manufacturing, finance, and Mediterranean commerce. Florentine merchants imported finished northern cloth and finished it further, including dyeing, in workshops governed by the guild known as the Calimala, and sold it widely. Champagne contributed fairs and interregional exchange. The Baltic and North Sea regions supplied bulky commodities, and the Mediterranean carried maritime long-distance commerce. Each region's specialization depended on the others. Flemish weavers needed English wool, Italian merchants needed northern cloth, and northern towns needed Baltic grain and Mediterranean spices.

Iberia presented a distinctive pattern. Its regional trade connected Christian, Muslim, and Mediterranean economies, with ports such as Barcelona and Valencia participating in trade with Italy, North Africa, and the eastern Mediterranean, and Muslim al-Andalus and its successors linking Iberia to the wider Islamic world. Christian conquests in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, including Mallorca in 1229 and Seville in 1248, brought major commercial centers under Christian rule and created mixed populations of Christians, Muslims, and Jews with varying legal status. Central and eastern Europe saw the development of mining, grain, timber, and salt economies and expanding urban networks. Silver mining at Freiberg and later Kutná Hora, salt production in Poland, and the growth of Kraków, Wrocław, and Buda in the thirteenth century, often with settlers brought in under German law, tied eastern regions into broader European exchange. The Mongol invasions of 1241, which devastated parts of Poland and Hungary, encouraged rulers to bring in settlers and found or refound towns, a connection between catastrophe and urban development that students should note.

This specialization should not be mistaken for the emergence of a unified European market. Barriers remained substantial. Tolls were levied at innumerable points, currencies and weights and measures differed, roads were poor, many rivers and seas could be navigated only seasonally, and local custom and political boundaries constrained trade. Transport costs were high, especially overland, and security was uncertain. Rulers sometimes restricted exports or imposed monopolies for fiscal reasons. Merchants therefore built personal networks, relied on privileges negotiated with rulers and towns, and exploited differences in prices among markets, a pattern very different from the integrated national and continental markets of later centuries.

Regional Variation

Regional Variation: Six Commercial Environments, c. 1250

Italian maritime cities

Large, politically self-governing, and oriented to the sea; commerce, shipbuilding, finance, and manufacturing were closely linked to city government.

Flanders

Dense towns organized around cloth production, dependent on imported wool and on access to the North Sea; tensions among merchants, patricians, and artisans were frequent.

Champagne

A region whose prominence depended on fairs under comital protection, less on large-scale production, and vulnerable to changes in routes and political control.

English market towns

A dense network of boroughs and markets tied to wool and grain, supervised by crown and lords, with London as a growing center.

Baltic ports

Towns such as Lübeck and Visby handling timber, fish, grain, wax, and furs, often founded or reorganized by settlers and tied to eastward expansion.

Mediterranean Iberia

Ports in the Crown of Aragon and neighboring regions linked to Italy, North Africa, and the Levant, with Christian, Muslim, and Jewish communities under varied legal status.

Why the pattern matters: No single region defines the European commercial norm. Italy was not simply ahead of a backward north, nor Flanders a model for the rest. Each combined geography, politics, and production in different ways.

Everyday Commercial Life

Everyday Commercial Life: Following a Bale of Wool

Imagine, as a generalized illustration drawn from the patterns described by historians rather than a record of one shipment, a lot of wool from a thirteenth-century English estate. The wool is sheared, sorted, and packed. A local or regional merchant purchases it, sometimes in advance through a contract, and arranges transport to a port, paying tolls and fees along the way. A shipper carries it across the North Sea, where it is inspected and taxed. In a Flemish city, it is sold to cloth makers or merchants, then cleaned, carded, spun, woven, fulled, and dyed by workers whose labor, often including women spinners, spreads across city and surrounding countryside. Finished cloth may be sold locally or carried to fairs or ports. Italian merchants buy cloth, ship it south, finish or dye it further, and sell it in Mediterranean markets, perhaps paying through credit arrangements settled at a fair or through exchange between firms.

The sequence shows how many different people, from shepherds to carters, dockworkers, weavers, notaries, and financiers, depended on the same flow of goods, and how credit and trust enabled a chain of transactions across long distances. It also shows how much of the economic life of commerce was unglamorous, based on processing and carrying bulky goods rather than only trading luxuries.

Section 43.12

A More Connected Europe, Not a Modern Economy

By about 1300, Europe possessed more towns, more markets, more coin in circulation, larger merchant communities, denser trade routes, more sophisticated partnerships and credit, expanding craft production, stronger regional specialization, and deeper links between countryside and city than it had around 1000. These changes mattered on their own terms. They altered the economic geography of the continent, created new groups of wealthy and influential townspeople, gave rulers and churches new sources of revenue, and shaped the world in which later developments occurred, including the Renaissance city-states examined beginning in Chapter 56 and the commercial empires examined in Part XI.

The conclusion must nonetheless reject teleology. Europe in 1300 had not become capitalist in the modern sense, and the High Middle Ages were not an embryonic Industrial Revolution, a transformation examined in Chapter 93. Agriculture remained the dominant activity, and most people lived in the countryside, often subject to customary obligations and lordship, as discussed in Chapter 34. Transport remained expensive, markets were fragmented, political privilege mattered heavily, and commerce coexisted with religious restrictions, local regulation, and customary obligations. Many of the institutions that supported commerce, including guilds, privileges, tolls, and corporate towns, restricted as much as they enabled. Debates about the development of capitalism, examined in Chapter 96, should not be projected back onto the thirteenth century.

The commercial economy was also vulnerable. Commerce depended on political security, on stable coinage, and on a growing population. The fourteenth-century famines and the Black Death examined in Chapter 50 would transform the demographic base, and later chapters show how towns, merchants, and workers responded. In 1300, however, the trends of the preceding centuries had changed the relationship between countryside, town, and long-distance trade. Chapter 42 explained how Europe produced more. This chapter has shown how more of that production began circulating through towns, markets, merchants, and increasingly connected commercial networks. Chapter 44 turns to the religious and political authority that would increasingly contend over a society that was growing richer, more urban, and more complex.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Agricultural Surplus to Regional Integration

Long-term causes

Agricultural expansion and population growth (Chapter 42); inherited Mediterranean and northern trade networks; growing monetization; regionally uneven political stabilization.

Short-term causes

Rulers' and lords' desire for tolls and rents; merchants' pursuit of profit; new town foundations; improved routes such as the Gotthard; the political protection offered by Champagne's counts.

Event or transformation

Growth of towns, markets, fairs, guilds, partnerships, and credit, and the linking of Mediterranean, North Sea, and Baltic systems.

Immediate effects

Larger urban populations, regional specialization, more coin, new merchant elites, new legal privileges, and heightened competition among towns and rulers.

Long-term consequences

A denser commercial network, new sources of royal and urban wealth, intensified relations between countryside and town, and institutions that later periods would adapt or contest.

Historical complexity

Causation ran in both directions. Towns drew on rural surplus but also stimulated rural production, so countryside and town shaped one another. No single cause, whether population, merchants, or rulers, explains the whole.

Feedback: Agricultural surplus enlarged markets and encouraged town growth; town growth increased demand and encouraged specialization; specialization increased trade and demand for credit and transport; and improved credit and transport, in turn, allowed rural producers to market more, reinforcing the cycle.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Commerce, c. 1000 to c. 1300

What changed? The number and size of towns, the density of markets, the use of money, the scale of merchant firms, the sophistication of contracts and credit, the volume of long-distance trade, and the connections among Mediterranean, North Sea, and Baltic networks all grew substantially.

What remained continuous? Agriculture remained dominant; lords, churches, and rulers retained great power; transport was slow and costly; political fragmentation and local custom limited integration; and the Mediterranean's earlier commercial connections persisted.

Who experienced change most directly? Townspeople, merchants, artisans, migrants to cities, rural producers near major markets, and rulers who gained new revenues experienced change most directly, as did enslaved people caught in expanding Mediterranean trade.

Who experienced greater continuity? Many peasants in remote regions, rural households outside market networks, and communities with limited access to towns continued customary patterns of life with comparatively little change.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows how a transformation can be profound without being total, and helps students avoid treating the High Middle Ages as either an economic dead zone that suddenly woke or a premature version of modern capitalism.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that between about 1000 and 1300, Europe's commercial economy expanded through the interaction of demographic growth, agricultural surplus, urbanization, regional specialization, improved commercial institutions, and widening trade networks. It began from the premise that commerce had not disappeared after Rome. Local markets, Mediterranean trade, Byzantine and Islamic commercial systems, and northern sea routes all existed around 1000, and the important development was acceleration, specialization, and increasing density rather than commerce appearing from nothing.

Towns grew because rural expansion, examined in Chapter 42, supplied food, people, and demand, because rulers, lords, churches, and merchants had reasons to encourage markets, and because transport, security, and monetization improved in many regions. Historians must distinguish older towns that expanded from new towns deliberately founded, and must recognize the great variety of medieval towns, from Italian communes to castle towns, ports, cathedral cities, and planned frontier towns. All depended on their rural hinterlands.

Urban growth was institutional. Charters, market rights, toll exemptions, town courts, and communes gave merchants and artisans predictable rules, but they did not create a simple opposition between urban freedom and rural oppression. Towns were governed by oligarchies, guilds, and councils, and their liberties were specific, negotiated, and unequally shared.

Most medieval trade was regional and ordinary. A commercial hierarchy ran from village markets through regional towns and major centers to interregional routes. Fairs, especially the Champagne fairs of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, connected northern and southern commercial worlds and provided settlement points for credit, but they were one element in a broader system and declined in relative importance as direct maritime links and changing politics offered alternatives.

Guilds organized merchants and craftspeople, regulated standards, training, and access, and supported members, but they also restricted competition and excluded outsiders, women, and minorities. Their importance varied greatly from town to town. Mediterranean commerce, led by Venice, Genoa, and Pisa, linked Latin Europe to Byzantine, Egyptian, Syrian, North African, and Iberian markets and demonstrated that political and religious boundaries were permeable to trade, while also including the sale of enslaved people. Northern commerce, centered on the North Sea and Baltic, handled bulky goods and laid foundations for the later Hanseatic world examined in Chapter 54.

Money, credit, and partnerships made long-distance trade workable. Money changers, notaries, the commenda, and Italian merchant-bankers created flexible financial arrangements, though medieval banking should not be equated with modern commercial banking and the mature use of bills of exchange belongs largely to later centuries. Regional specialization in Flanders, England, northern Italy, Champagne, the Baltic, Iberia, and central and eastern Europe tied regions together, but tolls, currencies, transport costs, and political boundaries kept the continent fragmented.

The historiographical debate over a "commercial revolution" illustrates how historians weigh evidence of dramatic growth against evidence of earlier commerce and gradual change. The most defensible view is that the term identifies the scale of transformation but misleads if it implies sudden rupture. By 1300, Europe was more connected, more urban, and more monetized, but it was not capitalist in the modern sense, and agriculture and lordship remained fundamental. The transformation is significant on its own terms and set conditions for later developments, including the crises examined from Chapter 50 onward.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Commercial revival
The expansion of towns, markets, and merchant activity in Latin Europe between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, a term that must not imply that earlier commerce had vanished.
Monetization
The growing use of money in payments, rents, wages, and exchange, replacing or supplementing payments in kind and labor.
Hinterland
The rural region surrounding and supplying a town or port with food, fuel, and raw materials.
Borough
A town, especially in England, with defined privileges such as a market and a court, often granted by a king or lord.
Charter
A written grant defining the rights, privileges, and obligations of a town, community, or institution.
Liberties
Specific legal privileges, such as tax or toll exemptions and rights to self-government, enjoyed by a town or its inhabitants.
Commune
A sworn association of townspeople seeking collective self-government, especially prominent in northern and central Italy.
Burgher
A full townsman with citizenship rights, often a merchant or property owner, as distinct from poorer or non-citizen residents.
Market right
The legal authority to hold a market, usually granted by a ruler or lord and often protected from nearby competitors.
Toll
A payment charged on goods or people passing a bridge, road, river point, or market, a source of revenue and a barrier to trade.
Fair
A periodic market held at fixed times and places, often protected by special privileges and drawing long-distance merchants.
Safe conduct
A ruler's guarantee of protection to travelers or merchants going to or from a market or fair.
Law merchant (lex mercatoria)
A phrase for a supposed body of merchant law shared across medieval Europe. Historians now treat it as a contested historiographical concept, since commercial customs were strongly local even when practices traveled between markets.
Merchant guild
An association of traders that regulated commerce and defended privileges within or across towns.
Craft guild
An association of artisans in a trade that regulated training, standards, and access to the occupation.
Apprenticeship
A fixed term of training under a master craftsperson, a core part of guild structure.
Entrepôt
A trading center where goods are stored, exchanged, and redistributed, such as Constantinople or Bruges.
Fondaco
A trading quarter or warehouse-hostel where foreign merchants lived and traded under special regulations.
Commenda
A partnership contract in which an investor entrusts capital to a traveling merchant and profits are divided as agreed.
Notary
A professional who drew up legally recognized contracts and kept registers, key to Italian commercial records.
Money changer
A specialist who appraised and exchanged coins and often also took deposits and made loans.
Merchant-banker
A medieval merchant whose activities included trade, exchange, deposit, and lending, not equivalent to a modern commercial bank.
Usury
In medieval Christian doctrine, lending at interest in ways condemned by the church, which shaped how contracts were structured.
Bill of exchange
A payment instrument allowing funds to be paid in one place and currency and received in another; its mature use is largely later medieval.
Specialization
Concentration of a region or town on producing particular goods for sale in wider markets.
Bastide
A planned town founded in southwestern France in the thirteenth century, typically with a regular layout and privileges.
German-law towns
Towns in central and eastern Europe organized according to legal models derived from German towns such as Magdeburg or Lübeck.
Cairo Geniza
A storeroom of medieval documents in Cairo whose letters and contracts illuminate Jewish and Mediterranean commerce, especially in the eleventh century.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a continent became more connected without becoming unified. The commercial and urban expansion of the High Middle Ages linked English sheep farmers, Flemish weavers, Champagne fair officials, Genoese notaries, Venetian shipowners, Baltic timber merchants, and central European miners in a web of exchange. Yet it did so through privileges, local customs, competing rulers, and fragile institutions, not through a single authority or a single market. In that respect, it reveals a recurring feature of European history: integration and fragmentation advanced together.

The chapter also reveals how economic change interacts with political and social change. Towns created new groups, new institutions, and new tensions, and rulers and churches found in commerce both revenue and rivalry. These developments set the stage for the contests over authority examined in Chapters 44 to 46 and for the social conflicts examined in Chapter 52. At the same time, the chapter shows that commerce operated across religious and political boundaries, with Latin, Byzantine, Muslim, and Jewish merchants and financiers all participating, a reminder that the history of Europe cannot be written as the history of one church, one language, or one region.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the argument of Volume II by showing that medieval Europe was neither static nor simply a prelude to something else. Its commercial networks, financial practices, and urban institutions developed on their own terms and carried forward into the later medieval, Renaissance, and early modern periods, but they were not a straight path to modern capitalism. Chapter 44 now turns from commerce to the reform of the church and the conflict over authority that accompanied Europe's growing wealth and complexity.
