Sparta, Athens, and Competing Political Orders

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Two Greek communities, shaped by the same Archaic world of aristocratic competition, hoplite warfare, colonization, and civic experimentation, built strikingly different answers to a single question: who should hold political power, and at whose expense should that power rest? Neither answer was inevitable, and neither should be mistaken for the whole of Greek political life.

Imagine a traveler moving overland from the Isthmus of Corinth into the Peloponnese sometime around 500 BCE, bound first for the valley of the Eurotas River in the southern region called Laconia, and later for the plain of Attica further to the northeast. In Laconia, the traveler would find a community of citizens who lived, dined, trained, and served in arms together under a peculiar constitutional arrangement combining two kings, a council of elders, five annually elected overseers, and an assembly of citizen soldiers, all sustained by the labor of a substantially larger subordinated population than the citizen body itself. In Attica, the same traveler would find a community that had recently reorganized its political life around ten newly created tribes, a council of five hundred citizens chosen from every corner of the countryside, and an assembly open to a much broader circle of adult male citizens than Sparta's ruling elite, a community still working out, in fits and starts, what a genuinely broad citizen government might look like in practice.

Chapter 9 introduced the general institutions of the Archaic Greek polis: aristocratic competition for honor and office, the spread of hoplite warfare and its debated relationship to political claims, the varied and locally specific pressures, including but not limited to land and population, that drove different communities to send out colonies, and the recurring appearance of tyranny as one Archaic outcome of elite political competition, an outcome that sometimes drew wider popular backing but was not simply a movement of the people against the aristocracy as such. Sparta and Athens both emerged from this same world of shared institutions and shared pressures, though each experienced internal conflict in its own distinctive form. Both possessed aristocracies who once dominated political life, and both eventually developed distinctive citizen assemblies, along with a dependence, in different ways and to different degrees, on the labor of people excluded from citizenship altogether. Yet the two communities arrived at dramatically different arrangements for distributing political power, defining citizenship, organizing military service, and structuring social hierarchy.

This chapter asks why. It is tempting, and historically misleading, to reach immediately for a simple contrast: Sparta as a militarized, closed, backward looking oligarchy, and Athens as an open, progressive, freedom loving democracy. Ancient authors themselves sometimes wrote in exactly these terms, and modern popular culture has often exaggerated the contrast further still. This chapter resists that temptation. Sparta's citizen body, however small and however dependent on coerced labor, participated meaningfully in political deliberation through its assembly and through elected officials who could be held accountable. Athens's expanding citizenship, however genuinely significant, excluded women, resident foreigners, and a large population of enslaved people from any formal political voice at all, and its most celebrated democratic institutions were not yet in place by the point at which this chapter ends. Both systems rested on hierarchy. Both systems rested on labor performed by people who could not vote. The differences between them, real and historically consequential as they were, existed within a shared Greek world of unequal citizenship, not between one free society and one unfree society.

This chapter also confronts, more directly than most chapters in this textbook, a serious problem of evidence. Much of what later readers think they know about Sparta comes not from Spartans themselves, who left behind comparatively little of their own writing, but from Athenians, other outside observers, and, above all, later writers such as the biographer Plutarch, composing many centuries after the events they described. The famous lawgiver Lycurgus, credited by ancient tradition with founding the entire Spartan system in a single decisive act, cannot be securely dated, and ancient accounts disagreed sharply over his lifetime, activities, and the reforms attributed to him. Athens, by contrast, is unusually well documented for a Greek polis of this period, but even Athenian evidence for the seventh and sixth centuries BCE is patchy, retrospective in places, and shaped by later democratic memory that had its own reasons for emphasizing certain reformers over others. This chapter treats these evidentiary problems as part of the history itself, not as footnotes to be skipped past on the way to a tidy narrative.

The chapter proceeds, after establishing the two poleis side by side, to examine Sparta first: its formation through conquest in Laconia and Messenia, its distinctive multi part constitution of kings, council, overseers, and assembly, the persistent uncertainty surrounding Lycurgus and the so called Great Rhetra, and the social structure of Spartiate citizens, free but politically subordinate perioikoi, and the coerced Messenian and Laconian population known as helots. It then turns to Athens: the aristocratic and legal world before Solon, Solon's own reforms and their contested meaning, the tyranny of Peisistratus and his sons, and the reorganization of Attica carried out by Cleisthenes after the expulsion of the last tyrant. The chapter closes with a direct comparison of citizenship, labor, and political participation in the two systems, one designed to leave students with a clear and historically responsible understanding of what Sparta and Athens actually were, politically and socially, on the eve of the Persian Wars examined in Chapter 11.

Nothing in this chapter should be read as though Sparta and Athens represent the entire Greek political world. Hundreds of other poleis, examined more briefly in Chapter 9 and referenced at points throughout this Part, developed their own combinations of oligarchy, tyranny, and citizen government. Sparta and Athens receive sustained attention here because their political systems became exceptionally influential and substantial literary traditions survive about both, although the evidence for early Sparta is unusually indirect and retrospective, because they will dominate the narrative of the Persian Wars and the Peloponnesian War examined in the following two chapters, and because comparing them directly illuminates, with unusual clarity, the range of political possibilities available within a shared Archaic Greek civic culture.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Sparta's conquest of Laconia and Messenia shaped its distinctive social and political order.
  2. Describe the institutional structure of the Spartan constitution, including the dual kingship, the Gerousia, the ephorate, and the citizen Assembly.
  3. Evaluate the historical reliability of the Lycurgus tradition and the Great Rhetra, and explain why historians describe Spartan evidence as unusually retrospective.
  4. Distinguish Spartiates, perioikoi, and helots as legally and socially distinct populations within Spartan society.
  5. Analyze the social, economic, and civic position of Spartan women in comparison with common assumptions about women elsewhere in the Greek world.
  6. Trace the development of Athenian political institutions from aristocratic rule through Draco's laws and Solon's reforms.
  7. Explain the nature and limits of Solon's reforms, including the seisachtheia and changes to debt bondage, without overstating their democratic character.
  8. Describe the tyranny of Peisistratus and his sons and its relationship to earlier aristocratic politics.
  9. Explain how Cleisthenes reorganized Attica through the deme and tribal system and how this reorganization related to later Athenian democracy.
  10. Compare citizenship, political participation, labor, and social hierarchy in Sparta and Athens without treating either as representative of Greek civilization as a whole.
  11. Identify the kinds of evidence, contemporary and retrospective, that allow historians to reconstruct Spartan and Athenian institutions, and assess the limitations of each.
  12. Avoid common anachronistic or oversimplified claims about Spartan militarism, Athenian democracy, and the status of women, foreigners, and enslaved people in both societies.

Historical Context

Chapter 9 established the general institutional world of the Archaic Greek polis, roughly from the eighth through the sixth centuries BCE. Aristocratic families competed for honor, office, and influence within an emerging framework of shared citizen institutions. The spread of hoplite warfare, fought by citizens who could afford their own bronze armor and who fought shoulder to shoulder in a close formation called the phalanx, has traditionally been linked to a broadening claim to political voice among propertied men, though the precise relationship between hoplite service and political rights is debated among historians rather than a settled causal chain. Many poleis sent out colonies across the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds for a variety of locally specific reasons, which could include land pressure in some cases but also trade, exile, factional conflict, and other motives that varied from community to community, and unresolved domestic tensions, particularly over debt, land, and access to office, produced, in many poleis, a period of tyranny in which a single individual seized power outside traditional aristocratic channels through elite political competition, sometimes drawing wider popular backing in the process.

Both poleis experienced internal conflict, but in different forms: Athenian tradition preserves a severe crisis involving debt, landholding, and political access, addressed in stages by Draco and Solon, while Spartan tensions were shaped especially by conquest, status, landholding, and the ongoing management of a large dependent population. Sparta and Athens both belong to this general Archaic pattern, but each developed a markedly distinct trajectory within it. Sparta, located in the region of Laconia in the southeastern Peloponnese, took an unusual path by comparison with most other poleis examined in Chapter 9. While Sparta did participate to some degree in overseas settlement, Spartan tradition crediting the community with founding the colony of Taras in southern Italy, conquest much closer to home had by far the greater and more lasting consequence for Spartan society. Sparta expanded by conquering and subjugating neighboring Greek populations, first within Laconia itself and later, in the region of Messenia to the west, across the Taygetus mountain range. This conquest produced a social structure unlike that of any other well documented Greek polis: a small citizen body sustained by the compelled agricultural labor of a much larger subjugated population, organized around institutions that combined kingship, aristocratic council, elected oversight, and citizen assembly in a distinctive balance.

Athens, located in the region of Attica in central Greece, followed a path that, in its broad outlines, is more typical of Archaic poleis generally, though its ultimate scale and later fame make it exceptional in the depth of its documentation. Attica was unified, according to Athenian tradition, at an early and imprecisely dated period through a process later Athenians called synoikismos, the bringing together of separate communities into a single political unit centered on the city of Athens. Aristocratic families, holding the annual offices called archonships and dominating the aristocratic council known as the Areopagus, controlled political life through the seventh century BCE, presiding over a population increasingly strained by debt, land shortage, and unequal access to justice. Athens's subsequent political history included the publication of Draco's laws late in the seventh century, the more far reaching reforms of Solon early in the sixth century, a period of tyranny under Peisistratus and his sons later in the sixth century, and finally the reorganization of Attica's political structure carried out by Cleisthenes after the expulsion of the tyrant Hippias in 510 BCE.

Readers should bear in mind, throughout this chapter, the caution already established in Chapter 9: the Archaic Greek world was not a single uniform civilization moving along one predetermined path toward either oligarchy or democracy. Sparta and Athens represent two documented and historically influential outcomes among many possible Archaic trajectories, not the two poles between which every other Greek polis can simply be arranged. This chapter treats them as important and well evidenced case studies in comparative Greek political development, not as the complete story of Archaic Greek politics.

Evidence for this period presents particular challenges that differ significantly between the two poleis. Athens benefits from a comparatively rich, though still incomplete and sometimes contradictory, body of evidence: inscriptions, later constitutional treatises including material associated with the philosopher Aristotle's school, the historian Herodotus writing in the fifth century BCE, and the poetry of Solon himself, some of which survives in quoted fragments preserved by later authors. Sparta, by contrast, generated remarkably little surviving documentation of its own. Spartan citizens did not cultivate the kind of public inscriptional culture, dramatic literature, or historical writing that flourished at Athens, and much of what later readers know about Spartan institutions comes from outside observers, sometimes admiring and sometimes hostile, and especially from the biographer Plutarch, writing in the late first and early second centuries CE, many centuries after the events and institutions he described. Historians of Sparta must therefore work with what specialists sometimes call the Spartan mirage, a body of later tradition that reflects, in complicated ways, both genuine institutional memory and the political and philosophical purposes of the outside observers who transmitted it.

Section 10.1

Two Poleis, Two Political Orders

Sparta and Athens shared a great deal by the standards of the wider Archaic Greek world described in Chapter 9. Both communities possessed aristocratic families who once monopolized political office, both fought as hoplites in the close ordered phalanx formation, and both, at different points and to different degrees, developed institutions that gave a defined citizen body a formal role in political decision making. Both also experienced serious internal conflict, but in different forms: Athenian tensions centered prominently on debt, landholding, and political access, whereas Spartan tensions were shaped especially by conquest, status, landholding, and control of a large dependent population. Neither community's history can be understood as a simple, isolated development; both belong to the shared Archaic pattern of aristocratic competition, hoplite military service, and civic experimentation.

Yet the specific arrangements the two communities constructed differed sharply. Sparta's citizen body, the Spartiates, remained a comparatively small and closed group throughout the period examined in this chapter, sustained economically by the compelled labor of a much larger subordinated population and organized politically around a distinctive combination of hereditary dual kingship, an aristocratic council of elders, a board of annually elected overseers called ephors, and a citizen Assembly with more limited deliberative initiative than its Athenian counterpart would eventually possess. Athens, by contrast, moved gradually and unevenly, through the reforms of Solon, the tyranny of Peisistratus, and the reorganization carried out by Cleisthenes, toward a system in which a progressively wider circle of adult male citizens gained formal political standing, organized not primarily by inherited aristocratic clan or existing regional loyalty but by a deliberately constructed system of demes and tribes that many historians interpret as helping to cut across older lines of local and family power.

This chapter avoids describing this contrast in the simplified terms that later observers, ancient and modern alike, have sometimes used: Sparta as an unfree military state and Athens as a free democratic one. Such a contrast obscures too much on both sides. Sparta's Spartiate citizens genuinely participated in an Assembly that voted on major questions of war, peace, and policy, and Sparta's ephors could call the kings themselves to account, features that fit uneasily with any simple image of totalitarian royal or aristocratic rule. Athens's political system, even after the reforms examined in this chapter, excluded the majority of the population living within Attica, including all women, a substantial population of resident foreigners, some later formally categorized as metics, and a large population of enslaved people, from any formal political voice whatsoever, and its most celebrated democratic institutions, associated with the fifth century statesman Pericles and examined in Part II's later chapters, did not yet exist in the period covered here. Both systems were built upon exclusion and, in differing forms and degrees, upon coerced or dependent labor. The genuine historical differences between them, explored throughout the remainder of this chapter, existed within that shared framework of unequal citizenship rather than across a simple line separating tyranny from freedom.

Section 10.2

Sparta: Geography, Conquest, and the Formation of the Spartan System

Sparta developed in the region of Laconia, a fertile valley formed by the Eurotas River and enclosed by the Taygetus range to the west and the Parnon range to the east, in the southeastern Peloponnese. Sparta was not entirely absent from the overseas colonization movement described in Chapter 9; Spartan tradition, for example, credited the community with founding the colony of Taras, later Tarentum, on the southern coast of Italy. Even so, conquest within the Peloponnese itself, rather than settlement overseas, had by far the larger and more lasting consequences for Spartan social and political structure. According to later tradition, Sparta was formed through the union, at an uncertain early date, of several Laconian villages, and Spartan power subsequently expanded across the rest of Laconia, subordinating neighboring communities and populations to Spartiate control.

The most consequential phase of this expansion was Sparta's conquest of Messenia, the fertile region lying west of the Taygetus range. Ancient tradition, transmitted especially through later poets and historians, describes two major Messenian Wars, generally placed by later chronographers in the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, through which Sparta subdued the Messenian population and reduced much of it to the coerced agricultural labor status later called helotage. Historians today treat this traditional chronology with considerable caution. The precise dating, the number and character of the conflicts, and even some details of the conquest itself rest on later poetic and historical tradition, including the poetry of the seventh century Spartan poet Tyrtaeus, composed to encourage Spartan resolve during a period of Messenian resistance, rather than on contemporary administrative record. What is reasonably well established is that by the sixth century BCE, Sparta controlled an unusually large territory for a single Greek polis, encompassing both Laconia and Messenia, and that this territory was worked overwhelmingly by a population, the helots, who lacked citizenship and who could be compelled to labor for Spartiate landholders under conditions that ancient sources describe as harsh and, at least periodically, dangerous for the ruling minority to maintain.

This pattern of conquest based territorial expansion, rather than overseas colonization, shaped Spartan society in ways that recur throughout this chapter. The economic foundation of Spartiate citizenship rested heavily on agricultural production from helot worked estates, while perioikic communities, examined in Section 10.6, participated more actively in craft production and exchange, activities from which Spartiate citizens were, according to tradition, largely excluded or discouraged. This economic arrangement freed Spartiate citizens, at least according to later idealized tradition, from the need to engage personally in trade or craft, permitting a form of citizenship organized substantially around military training, communal dining, and civic participation. At the same time, this same economic base created a permanent internal security concern: a Spartiate citizen population that remained relatively small, controlling and depending upon a helot population that ancient sources, including the fifth century historian Thucydides, describe as considerably larger and, at times, dangerously restive. Much of the distinctive character of Spartan institutions examined in the following sections, including its unusual emphasis on military training, discipline, and internal cohesion among citizens, developed, at least in part, as a response to this underlying structural condition, though historians caution against reducing every Spartan institution to a single security driven explanation.

Section 10.3

The Spartan Political Order: Kings, Gerousia, Ephors, and Assembly

Sparta's political order combined several distinct institutions whose respective powers, and whose relationships to one another, must be understood carefully rather than reduced to a single label such as monarchy, oligarchy, or military dictatorship. At the apex stood a dual kingship, an arrangement in which two hereditary royal lines, later tradition calls them the Agiads and the Eurypontids, held kingship jointly and continuously, rather than in succession. The two kings held important religious, military, and judicial responsibilities. In earlier periods, both kings customarily commanded Spartan armies on campaign, though from the late sixth century onward, and especially from the early fifth century, Sparta increasingly sent only one king on a given campaign, a change historians associate with growing concern about the political risks of joint royal command. The kings also held religious authority as chief priests of the state cult and possessed certain judicial powers, particularly in matters concerning inheritance and family law.

Alongside the kings stood the Gerousia, a council composed of the two kings together with twenty eight additional members, each a Spartiate citizen at least sixty years of age, elected to the position for life by the citizen Assembly. The Gerousia played a major probouleutic role, alongside the ephors, in deliberating on major questions of policy and in shaping and preparing specific proposals, called probouleumata, to be placed before the citizen Assembly for a final decision. This arrangement gave a relatively small, elite, and permanently entrenched body, working together with the annually elected ephors, substantial influence over what questions the wider citizen body would actually be asked to decide, a significant check on the scope of popular deliberation even where the Assembly itself retained a formal vote.

A third and, in many respects, especially distinctive Spartan institution was the ephorate, a board of five ephors elected annually from the full Spartiate citizen body. The ephors held wide ranging powers: they presided over meetings of the Gerousia and Assembly, exercised significant judicial authority in civil cases, managed important aspects of Sparta's relations with the helot population, including, according to some ancient testimony, an annual ritual declaration of war against the helots that provided a legal basis for killing helots suspected of resistance without formal trial, and, remarkably, held authority to call the kings themselves to account, including the power to bring formal charges against a king before the Gerousia. This capacity to check royal power through an annually elected board drawn from the wider citizen body represents one of the more genuinely distinctive features of the Spartan constitutional order, one that complicates any simple description of Sparta as an unaccountable royal or aristocratic state.

Finally, the Spartan Assembly, composed of all full Spartiate citizens over a minimum age, met periodically to vote on proposals brought before it by the Gerousia, including questions of war, peace, treaties, and the election of Gerousia members and ephors. Ancient evidence, and modern scholarly reconstruction based on it, suggests that the Assembly's practical initiative was considerably more limited than that of its later Athenian counterpart: the Assembly generally voted on prepared proposals rather than initiating extended open debate, and voting itself, according to later description, was conducted by acclamation, with the volume of shouting determining the outcome rather than a formal count of individual votes, a method ancient critics themselves sometimes found imprecise. Even so, the Assembly's approval remained constitutionally necessary for major decisions, and Spartan governance is best understood as an interaction among these four elements, kings, Gerousia, ephors, and Assembly, rather than as the unchecked rule of any single office or body.

The traditional textual anchor for this constitutional arrangement is a document known as the Great Rhetra, an oracular pronouncement, associated by later tradition with the god Apollo at Delphi and with the lawgiver Lycurgus, that outlines, in compressed and somewhat obscure language, the basic institutional framework of kings, Gerousia, and popular assembly described above. The Great Rhetra survives only through a much later quotation, preserved in the biographer Plutarch's Life of Lycurgus, composed many centuries after the institutions it purports to describe. Historians accordingly treat the Great Rhetra as a document of genuine historical importance for understanding how later Spartans, and later Greek authors more generally, remembered and explained the origins of their constitutional order, while remaining appropriately cautious about treating it as a securely dated, verbatim record of a specific Archaic enactment. Section 10.4 examines this evidentiary problem, and the associated figure of Lycurgus, in detail.

Section 10.4

Lycurgus, the Spartan Mirage, and the Problem of Evidence

Ancient Greek tradition, particularly as it survives through much later authors, attributed the entire framework of Spartan institutions, the dual kingship's regulation, the Gerousia, the ephorate, the Great Rhetra, the common messes, the educational system known as the agōgē, and even Spartan land distribution, to a single lawgiver named Lycurgus, said to have received divine sanction from the oracle at Delphi for his reforms. This tradition exercised enormous influence over how later Greeks, Romans, and, eventually, early modern and modern readers understood Sparta, and it remains deeply embedded in popular understanding of Spartan history today.

Careful historical examination, however, reveals substantial problems with treating the Lycurgus tradition as reliable history in any straightforward sense. Lycurgus cannot be securely dated. Ancient authors themselves proposed widely varying dates for his activity, in some cases separated by more than a century. Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, does treat Lycurgus as a genuine Spartan lawgiver, but even he reports competing traditions about the origin of Sparta's laws, some crediting the Delphic oracle and others tracing the laws to Crete, a sign that uncertainty surrounded the figure from an early point. It is Plutarch, writing many centuries later, who states most explicitly that virtually nothing about Lycurgus's life and dates could be established with confidence, opening his biography by cataloguing the many contradictory accounts already circulating in his own time. No contemporary seventh or eighth century source describing Lycurgus survives, and the fullest ancient account of his life, Plutarch's Life of Lycurgus, was composed roughly seven to eight centuries after the period in which Lycurgus is traditionally supposed to have lived, drawing on a range of earlier sources of highly uneven reliability, some themselves already legendary in character by Plutarch's own time.

Many modern historians treat the Lycurgus tradition not as an accurate historical account of a single lawgiver's life and reforms, but as what specialists sometimes call a foundation myth, a later Spartan and pan Hellenic explanatory narrative that attributed a complex set of institutions, which in reality most likely developed gradually over an extended period through the accumulated decisions of many generations of Spartan citizens, kings, and officials, to a single authoritative founding figure and a single decisive constitutional moment. This pattern, attributing gradual institutional development to a single celebrated lawgiver, recurs elsewhere in Greek tradition, and students should recognize it as a broader feature of how many ancient societies explained the origins of their own laws and institutions, rather than as a peculiarity unique to Sparta.

This evidentiary problem connects to a wider phenomenon historians call the Spartan mirage, a term describing the ways in which surviving descriptions of Sparta reflect the particular interests, assumptions, and rhetorical purposes of the outside observers who produced most of the evidence, rather than providing a straightforward window onto Spartan reality as Spartans themselves would have understood it. Sparta generated relatively little of its own surviving written record compared to Athens, restricting outside access to independent Spartan documentation, while other Greeks, particularly Athenians, developed a lasting fascination with Sparta, sometimes admiring its perceived discipline, unity, and constitutional stability as an implicit critique of their own society's factional conflicts, and sometimes criticizing its treatment of the helots, its secrecy, or its rigid conformity. Later Roman and early modern readers added still further layers of idealization or criticism, often serving contemporary political purposes quite removed from ancient Spartan reality itself. Historians today read every surviving source about Sparta, including Herodotus, Thucydides, the Athenian writer Xenophon, who had direct personal experience of Sparta, the philosopher Aristotle, and above all Plutarch, with close attention to each author's date, sources, evident biases, and possible purposes, rather than treating any single account as a neutral factual record.

None of this means that Spartan institutions are unknowable or that this chapter's account of the dual kingship, Gerousia, ephorate, and Assembly rests on nothing but legend. Independent lines of evidence, including scattered inscriptions, the testimony of near contemporary or well informed observers such as Herodotus, Thucydides, and Xenophon, and the internal consistency of institutional descriptions across multiple ancient sources, give historians reasonable confidence in the broad outlines of how the Spartan constitution actually operated by the fifth century BCE, the period for which evidence is comparatively strongest. What remains genuinely uncertain is how, when, and through whom these institutions developed, and whether any single lawgiver, Lycurgus or otherwise, played the decisive founding role that later tradition assigned to him.

Section 10.5

Spartan Citizenship, Education, Common Messes, and Military Obligation

Full Spartan citizenship, restricted to Spartiates, carried a distinctive combination of political, economic, social, and religious obligations that together defined what later Greeks, and later readers generally, have regarded as a uniquely demanding form of civic membership. Spartiate status required, according to the traditions examined in this section, participation in a system of common messes called syssitia, communal dining groups in which adult male citizens ate together regularly, contributing an agreed portion of agricultural produce, generated from landholdings worked by helot labor, toward the shared meal. Failure to make one's required contribution, according to later tradition, could result in loss of full citizen status, a rule that, whatever its precise historical operation, illustrates the close connection ancient sources draw between economic capacity, communal participation, and political membership at Sparta.

Alongside the common messes, later tradition describes an extensive educational and training system for Spartan boys, generally referred to by modern scholars using the term agōgē, though this precise term for the system is attested mainly in sources of the Roman period rather than securely in earlier Classical texts, a point historians consider important for assessing how far the fully elaborated later description reflects earlier Archaic and Classical practice. According to the tradition as it survives, Spartan boys were removed from close family supervision at a young age to live and train communally, undergoing physical training, endurance exercises, and instruction intended to cultivate discipline, obedience, and military capability, continuing through adolescence and into early adulthood before full citizen status and its associated messing obligations began.

This chapter treats the traditional picture of the agōgē with appropriate caution rather than presenting it as an unchanging institution operating identically from the eighth century BCE onward. Much of the detailed description available to modern readers derives from later sources, particularly Xenophon's account written from a broadly admiring outside perspective in the fourth century BCE, and Plutarch's considerably later and more elaborate description. Historians reasonably infer that some form of communal upbringing and military training for Spartiate boys existed from a relatively early period, given its close connection to the broader structure of Spartan citizenship and military organization, but caution against assuming that every specific practice attributed to the system by later authors operated in precisely the same form throughout the centuries this chapter examines.

Military obligation formed an inseparable part of Spartiate citizenship. Spartan hoplites earned particular renown across the Greek world for their sustained training, their discipline in maintaining formation under pressure, and their reputation for steadiness in battle, a reputation that the events examined in Chapter 11's account of the Persian Wars would do a great deal to confirm and publicize. It would nonetheless be a significant oversimplification to reduce Spartiate citizenship to military service alone. Citizenship also entailed participation in the Assembly, eligibility for election to the Gerousia or the ephorate, membership in a syssition, land tenure sufficient to meet messing obligations, and full standing within Spartan religious and civic life. Military training was one essential and highly visible component of a broader civic status, not the whole of it.

Section 10.6

Helots, Perioikoi, and Spartan Women: The Foundations of Spartan Society

The distinctive Spartiate citizenship described in the preceding section rested materially upon the labor and subordination of populations who did not share in it. Understanding Spartan society requires careful attention to at least three additional groups: the helots, the perioikoi, and Spartan women, each of whom occupied a legally and socially distinct position within the wider Spartan state.

The helots, concentrated especially, though not exclusively, in conquered Messenia, formed the compelled agricultural labor force whose produce sustained the Spartiate citizen body through the messing contributions described above. Historians take considerable care to distinguish helotage from the privately owned chattel slavery more familiar from Athens and much of the rest of the Greek world, examined further in later chapters of this textbook. Helots were not, in general, privately purchased individuals bought and sold on an open market in the manner of chattel slaves; rather, they were attached collectively to the land and, through it, to the Spartan state and to individual Spartiate landholders in a status that ancient sources themselves recognized as unusual, sometimes describing helots as occupying a position between chattel slavery and full freedom. Ancient testimony, particularly from Thucydides in his account of events during the fifth century BCE, describes periodic helot unrest and even organized revolt, along with Spartan measures, including the ephors' annual ritual declaration of war against the helots noted in Section 10.3, that suggest a persistent and serious concern among Spartiate citizens about maintaining control over a population that considerably outnumbered them. Historians debate the precise numerical ratio, given the limitations of ancient demographic evidence, but broadly agree that helots substantially outnumbered Spartiates throughout the period this chapter examines.

The perioikoi, whose name literally describes those who dwell around Sparta, occupied a markedly different position from the helots. Perioikoi were personally free inhabitants of towns and communities within the wider Spartan state, particularly in Laconia, who managed their own local affairs and engaged in trade, craft production, and commerce, activities from which Spartiate citizens were, according to tradition, largely excluded or discouraged. Perioikoi lacked Spartan political citizenship and had no voice in the Spartan Assembly or eligibility for Spartan office, but they served in the Spartan army, sometimes in substantial numbers, and their economic activity supplied goods and services, including manufactured items such as weapons and other equipment, that the Spartiate economy, oriented heavily toward land based produce and military training, did not itself generate in comparable quantity. The perioikoi therefore occupied a status genuinely distinct from both the enfranchised Spartiates and the coerced helots, a reminder that Spartan society cannot be adequately described using only two categories of citizen and slave.

Spartan women, too, occupied a position that differed in important and well documented ways from common assumptions about women's roles elsewhere in the Archaic and Classical Greek world, without amounting to anything resembling the formal political rights exercised by male Spartiates. Spartan women did not vote, hold political office, or participate in the Assembly, Gerousia, or ephorate. Ancient outside observers, however, particularly Xenophon and Aristotle, both writing from perspectives external to Sparta itself, remarked with evident surprise, sometimes admiring and sometimes critical, on several features of Spartan women's lives that appeared unusual by comparison with practices they associated with Athens and other poleis: Spartan girls, according to these accounts, received physical training and exercise considered appropriate preparation for eventually bearing strong children for the state, Spartan women could own and control property, including land, to a degree that struck outside observers as notable, and Spartan women, freed in part from certain domestic labor performed by helots, moved with comparatively greater freedom in public than the more secluded pattern often associated, at least in later idealized description, with respectable women's lives at Classical Athens.

This chapter cautions against two equally mistaken conclusions that a careless reading of this evidence might invite. It would be inaccurate to describe Spartan women as enjoying anything resembling modern conceptions of political or social equality; they remained excluded from formal political power, and much of the ancient testimony about their lives comes from male outside observers writing for their own didactic, comparative, or even satirical purposes, requiring the same critical caution historians apply to outside descriptions of Sparta generally. It would be equally inaccurate, however, to assume that all Greek women, at Athens or elsewhere, shared a single, uniformly secluded experience simply because later idealized Athenian sources sometimes describe respectable women's seclusion as a social norm. Women's lives varied considerably across the Greek world, by social class as well as by polis, a theme this textbook will revisit in later chapters examining Greek religion, society, and daily life in Chapter 13.

Section 10.7

Athens Before Democracy: Aristocracy, Draco, and Solon

Athenian political life through much of the seventh century BCE was dominated by a narrow aristocracy. Annual officials called archons, drawn from aristocratic families, administered major civic, military, and religious functions, while the council known as the Areopagus, composed of former archons serving for life, exercised substantial judicial and advisory authority. Ordinary Athenians, including a growing population of small farmers burdened by debt, had little formal voice in this system and, according to later Athenian tradition, faced a legal environment in which unwritten aristocratic custom, interpreted and applied by aristocratic officials, left considerable room for arbitrary or self interested judgment.

Later Athenian tradition credits an official named Draco with publishing Athens's first written laws, conventionally dated to around 621/0 BCE. Of this legislation, Draco's homicide law is by far the most securely attested, known to modern historians through a later reinscription set up in 409/8 BCE, nearly two centuries after Draco's own time, when Athenians undertook a broader project of republishing and revising their laws. Whether Draco issued a more comprehensive body of legislation covering matters beyond homicide, and if so how extensive it was, remains less certain, and this chapter avoids claiming more precision about the scope of a Draconian code than the surviving evidence supports. Later Greek tradition remembered Draco's legislation chiefly for the severity of its prescribed penalties, a reputation preserved in the modern English adjective draconian, and most of whatever wider legislation existed was superseded by Solon's later reforms, with the significant exception of the homicide law, which continued to inform Athenian law well into the Classical period. The larger historical significance of Draco's laws lies less in their specific penalties than in the underlying principle they embodied: that Athenian law should exist in a public, written, and therefore at least potentially more consistent and accountable form, rather than resting solely on unwritten aristocratic custom.

The more far reaching reforms associated with the statesman and poet Solon followed roughly three decades later. Solon, traditionally dated to an archonship in 594/3 BCE, though some details of the chronology remain debated among specialists, was granted extraordinary authority to address a severe crisis affecting Athenian society, a crisis rooted substantially in debt. According to Athenian tradition, many small farmers had fallen into debt to wealthier landholders, in some cases pledging their own persons or family members as security, and a portion of the population had fallen into a condition of debt bondage or, in the most severe cases, had been sold into slavery abroad for failure to satisfy their obligations.

Solon's response, remembered by later tradition under the term seisachtheia, meaning the shaking off of burdens, addressed this crisis directly. Solon canceled outstanding debts secured against debtors' persons, addressed the condition of Athenians known as hektemoroi, a term later tradition associates with a population working land under some form of obligation to more powerful landholders, and prohibited the future use of personal freedom, one's own body, as security for loans. Historians debate the precise scope of this reform with considerable care. One influential line of scholarly interpretation distinguishes between Solon's clear prohibition of future enslavement for debt, well attested and central to the seisachtheia as later Athenians themselves understood it, and the more complicated question of whether every form of dependent agricultural labor and land tenure obligation was thereby abolished outright. It is therefore safer to say that Solon prohibited future enslavement for debt and dismantled important forms of personal dependency associated with the crisis, while the precise status of the hektemoroi and the full scope of the seisachtheia remain debated among historians rather than settled in every particular.

Solon also undertook political reorganization, traditionally described as establishing four property based classes, determining eligibility for particular offices and military obligations according to assessed wealth rather than birth alone, a change that, at least in principle, opened certain offices to wealthy non aristocrats while still excluding the poorest Athenians from the highest offices. He is also credited with creating or strengthening a council, sometimes described in later tradition as a Council of Four Hundred, drawn from the four traditional Athenian tribes, and with establishing or expanding a citizen right of appeal to a popular court, a measure later Athenians remembered as an important check on official power. Historians note, however, that the precise institutional details of Solon's reforms, including the exact character of any council he may have created, rest on later and sometimes inconsistent tradition, particularly the account preserved in the Aristotelian Constitution of the Athenians, composed roughly two and a half centuries after Solon's own lifetime, and some elements of the traditional property class system itself have been questioned by modern scholars examining the underlying evidence.

What can be said with confidence is this: Solon's reforms addressed a genuine and severe social crisis, expanded certain protections and opportunities for non aristocratic Athenians, and established precedents, particularly regarding written law, appeal, and property based rather than purely birth based qualification for office, that later Athenian democracy would build upon. What Solon's reforms did not do, and what later Athenian democratic tradition itself did not generally claim they did, was create anything resembling the participatory democracy of the fifth century examined in later chapters of this textbook. Solon did not abolish the Areopagus, did not open all offices to all citizens regardless of wealth, and did not resolve Athens's underlying political tensions, which continued to produce serious conflict in the decades following his reforms, conflict that would eventually open the door to the tyranny examined in the following section.

Section 10.8

Peisistratus, the Peisistratids, and Athenian Tyranny

Solon's reforms did not end aristocratic factional competition at Athens, and the decades following his archonship saw continued rivalry among aristocratic leaders and their followers, organized, according to later tradition, around regional and factional groupings within Attica. Into this unsettled environment stepped Peisistratus, an aristocrat with a distinguished military record who, applying the general pattern of Archaic tyranny described in Chapter 9, sought to convert popular support and personal prestige into a seizure of power outside the normal constitutional channels controlled by his aristocratic rivals.

Peisistratus's path to durable power was neither quick nor straightforward. According to the account preserved by Herodotus and later elaborated by the Aristotelian Constitution of the Athenians, Peisistratus made at least three separate attempts to establish himself as tyrant over roughly a decade and a half, beginning around 561 BCE, using tactics that included staging an apparent assassination attempt against himself to justify requesting an armed bodyguard, forming and later abandoning a political marriage alliance with a rival aristocratic faction, and, according to Herodotus's memorably skeptical account, staging a dramatic public procession in which a tall woman dressed as the goddess Athena rode into the city beside him, apparently intended to persuade at least some Athenians that the goddess herself endorsed his return to power. Historians treat some of these colorful details, particularly the Athena procession, with appropriate caution regarding their precise historicity, while recognizing that Herodotus's account, whatever its embellishments, reflects a genuine memory of Peisistratus's opportunistic and repeatedly interrupted path to power.

Having finally secured a durable tyranny by around 546 BCE, Peisistratus governed Athens, according to the consistent testimony of ancient sources, largely through existing constitutional institutions rather than by abolishing them outright. Archons continued to be appointed, the Areopagus continued to function, and Solon's laws generally remained in force, with Peisistratus and, later, his sons ensuring that friendly or compliant individuals held the offices that mattered most to maintaining their control. This pattern, ruling through rather than against existing institutions, is characteristic of Archaic Greek tyranny more broadly, as Chapter 9 explained, and helps account for why later Athenian democratic tradition, while ultimately hostile to tyranny as a form of government, could nonetheless remember aspects of Peisistratus's own rule, particularly his support for religious festivals, public building projects, and, according to some later tradition, agricultural credit for small farmers, in comparatively favorable terms, even as it condemned tyranny in principle.

Peisistratus died in 527 BCE. The surviving ancient tradition is not perfectly consistent about how authority then passed within the family, but the historian Thucydides is explicit that Hippias, the elder son, succeeded his father as the ruling tyrant, continuing many of Peisistratus's policies, while his brother Hipparchus remained a prominent and visible member of the regime without holding the principal authority himself. This arrangement ended abruptly in 514 BCE, when Hipparchus was assassinated by two Athenians, Harmodius and Aristogeiton, in circumstances that later Athenian democratic tradition would transform into a celebrated founding myth of resistance to tyranny, commemorating the two men as tyrannicides and heroes of liberty, even though the contemporary motive for the assassination, according to both Herodotus and the later Aristotelian account, appears to have been primarily personal rather than a considered political conspiracy against tyranny as such. Following his brother's assassination, Hippias, who had already been the effective ruler, continued in power alone and, according to ancient tradition, ruled increasingly harshly, until a combination of internal Athenian opposition, organized substantially around the aristocratic Alcmaeonid family from which Cleisthenes descended, and external Spartan intervention, led by the Spartan king Cleomenes, forced Hippias from Athens in 510 BCE, bringing Peisistratid rule to an end and opening the political contest between Isagoras and Cleisthenes examined in the following section.

Section 10.9

Cleisthenes and the Reorganization of Attica

The expulsion of Hippias in 510 BCE did not immediately produce a stable or broadly participatory political settlement. Two rival aristocratic leaders, Isagoras and Cleisthenes, competed for dominance in the resulting political vacuum. Isagoras, gaining an early advantage and enlisting Spartan military support under King Cleomenes, attempted to consolidate power through measures that included expelling a number of Athenian families, reportedly including Cleisthenes and his supporters, and dissolving the existing Council. This action, according to Herodotus's account, provoked a popular uprising among ordinary Athenians against Isagoras and his Spartan allies, who were besieged on the Athenian acropolis before eventually being permitted to withdraw. Cleisthenes and his supporters returned to Athens in the aftermath, and Cleisthenes, now enjoying substantial popular backing, proceeded to carry out a sweeping reorganization of Athenian political structure in 508 and 507 BCE.

The central mechanism of Cleisthenes' reform was a deliberate restructuring of the basic units of Athenian political identity. Cleisthenes organized the population of Attica into 139 or so local units called demes, villages and city neighborhoods that became the fundamental building blocks of citizenship: an Athenian's civic identity was now defined by membership in his deme, recorded and inherited through the deme register, rather than primarily through membership in one of the older aristocratic clan groupings that had structured political life previously. These demes were then grouped into thirty units called trittyes, ten drawn from the city region, ten from the coast, and ten from the inland countryside, and each of the ten new tribes was constructed by combining one trittys from each of these three zones. This arrangement, deliberately mixing demes from different and often distant parts of Attica within a single tribe, is widely interpreted by historians as helping to cut across the regional and factional power bases on which aristocratic leaders, including Peisistratus himself in earlier decades, had previously relied, though the precise weight of this intention relative to other possible purposes remains a matter of scholarly inference rather than direct statement in the ancient evidence.

Building upon this new tribal structure, Cleisthenes established a Council of Five Hundred, with each of the ten new tribes contributing fifty members selected, at least in later practice, by lot from among eligible citizens of that tribe, serving for a term of one year. This Council took over much of the deliberative preparatory function previously associated with earlier councils, reviewing business and preparing proposals for consideration by the citizen Assembly, whose membership, open to adult male citizens registered in the deme system, provided the basis for a considerably broader circle of formal civic participation than had existed under earlier Athenian arrangements, and a markedly broader one than the smaller and more socially exclusive Spartan citizen body examined earlier in this chapter.

Some ancient and modern accounts also associate Cleisthenes with the introduction, or at least the conceptual groundwork, of the practice later Athenians called ostracism, a procedure by which citizens could vote, using inscribed pottery fragments called ostraka, to exile a prominent individual for ten years without formal criminal charge. Later tradition and some modern interpretations connect the procedure with preventing renewed tyranny, although both its Cleisthenic origin and its original purpose remain debated among historians. Historians note that the earliest securely attested actual use of ostracism dates from 487 BCE, roughly two decades after Cleisthenes' reorganization, and some caution is warranted regarding how fully developed the practice was under Cleisthenes himself as opposed to being formalized and first employed somewhat later.

It is essential that students resist the temptation to describe Cleisthenes' reforms as having created Athenian democracy in its fully developed Classical form. The reorganization of 508/7 BCE established crucial structural foundations, broadened deme based citizenship, the ten tribe system, and the Council of Five Hundred, that later fifth century developments, particularly the reforms associated with the statesmen Ephialtes and Pericles examined in Chapter 12, would build upon substantially, including further limiting the powers of the Areopagus, introducing payment for jury and other civic service to enable participation by poorer citizens, and expanding the practical scope of Assembly deliberation. The system Cleisthenes established around 507 BCE, however consequential, was not yet the mature participatory democracy of Pericles' Athens, and this chapter's account accordingly stops at this earlier and more modest, though still historically decisive, stage of institutional development.

Section 10.10

Comparing Citizenship, Slavery, and Political Participation

Having examined Sparta and Athens separately, this final section draws the two systems together in direct comparison, organized around the categories the Authoritative Chapter Outline specifically assigns to this chapter: oligarchy, militarized citizenship, democracy, slavery, social structure, and political participation.

Regarding political structure, Sparta's arrangement is properly described as an oligarchy tempered by citizen participation rather than as a straightforward monarchy or a fully closed aristocratic regime. A relatively small body of full Spartiate citizens, likely numbering several thousand around the beginning of the fifth century BCE, though precise figures are difficult to establish with confidence and the citizen body later declined substantially, held exclusive rights to vote in the Assembly, hold office, and sit in the Gerousia, while two hereditary kings and an elected board of five ephors exercised substantial executive, judicial, and military authority. Athens, by the point Cleisthenes' reforms took effect around 507 BCE, had moved toward a considerably broader form of citizen participation, organized through the deme and tribal system, with a Council of Five Hundred and a citizen Assembly whose membership was drawn from a substantially larger and more broadly based citizen body than Sparta's narrow Spartiate elite; resident foreigners and enslaved men remained excluded from that citizen body entirely. It would nonetheless be premature, as Section 10.9 emphasized, to call the Athenian system of 507 BCE a mature democracy in the fuller sense that term would carry by the time of Pericles; it is more accurately described as the institutional foundation upon which fifth century Athenian democracy would later be built.

Regarding militarized citizenship, Sparta's system integrated military training and service more completely and more explicitly into the definition of citizenship itself than did the Athenian system examined in this chapter. Spartiate status was bound up closely with communal upbringing, training, messing obligations, and continuous military readiness, reflecting Sparta's particular historical formation through conquest and its consequent dependence on maintaining control over a large subordinated helot population. Athenian citizenship, while it certainly carried military obligations, with citizens wealthy enough to equip themselves generally serving as hoplites, though the precise relationship between the later zeugitai property class, hoplite service, and the original Solonian classification remains debated among historians, was not organized around a comparably comprehensive system of state directed communal upbringing and lifelong military integration, reflecting Athens's different economic base, oriented more heavily toward trade, craft production, and, increasingly, naval power in the century following the period covered here.

Regarding slavery and dependent labor, the two systems differed in legal form as well as in scale and character. Spartan helotage bound dependent cultivators to land and estates within Spartan territory and subjected them to both individual Spartiate exploitation and communal Spartan control, a status ancient sources themselves described as distinctive, occupying, in the words some ancient authors used, an intermediate position between full slavery and freedom, sustained by continuous Spartiate vigilance against the possibility of helot revolt. Athens, by contrast, relied substantially on privately owned chattel slavery, in which individual enslaved people, frequently obtained through trade or, at times, through warfare, were bought, sold, and owned by individual Athenian households and workshops, a system examined in greater depth in later chapters covering Classical Athenian society and economy. Both systems rested on coerced labor performed by people excluded from citizenship, but the legal mechanisms, historical origins, and lived social position of helots and chattel slaves differed in ways historians consider historically significant rather than a matter of terminology alone.

Regarding social structure more broadly, Sparta's population divided into three principal legal categories, Spartiates, perioikoi, and helots, each occupying a distinct and clearly bounded status. Athenian society, as it stood by 507 BCE, divided into citizens, organized by Solonian property class and, after Cleisthenes, by deme and tribe, a growing population of resident foreigners, some of whom historians associate with the later formal category of metics, whose precise legal definition and chronology of development remain debated, and enslaved people. Women in both societies lacked the formal political rights exercised by adult male citizens, but, as Section 10.6 explained, occupied socially and legally distinct positions that should not be collapsed into an undifferentiated category of powerlessness; Spartan women's documented property rights and greater public visibility differed from the pattern later idealized Athenian sources associate with respectable women's more secluded domestic role, a difference historians attribute to Sparta's distinctive economic and military organization rather than to any straightforward difference in underlying attitudes toward women as such.

Finally, regarding the balance and depth of surviving evidence, this chapter has emphasized throughout that historians know considerably more, and with considerably greater confidence, about Athenian institutions of this period than about Spartan ones. Athens generated a comparatively rich body of near contemporary and later documentation, including Solon's own surviving poetic fragments, while Sparta's institutional history survives mainly through later, external, and sometimes idealized or hostile testimony, epitomized by the problem of the Lycurgus tradition examined in Section 10.4. This asymmetry in the evidence should itself inform how confidently students, and historians, state conclusions about each society: firmer conclusions can generally be drawn about Athenian institutional development in this period than about the precise origins and early evolution of Sparta's distinctive constitutional order.

By around 500 BCE, then, Sparta and Athens stood as two of the most institutionally developed and, in different ways, most historically influential poleis in the Greek world, each having converted the common Archaic ingredients described in Chapter 9, aristocratic competition, hoplite military service, legal codification, tyranny, and civic reorganization, into distinctive and durable political orders. Neither order was complete or unchanging; both would continue to develop significantly over the following decades. Chapter 11 now turns to the crisis that would bring these two very different poleis into direct and sustained contact with one another and with the wider Mediterranean world: the expansion of the Achaemenid Persian Empire and the Greco Persian Wars that followed.

Historical Vocabulary
Helotage and Dependent Labor

Helotage describes the distinctive coerced labor status of the helots, a population attached collectively to land in Laconia and, especially, Messenia, and compelled to labor for the benefit of Spartiate citizens. Ancient authors themselves recognized helotage as an unusual category, distinct from the privately owned chattel slavery familiar from Athens and much of the rest of the Greek world, sometimes describing it as an intermediate condition between slavery and freedom. Historians today generally avoid describing helots simply as slaves without qualification, since doing so obscures important legal and social differences, including the collective, land bound character of helot status and the persistent possibility of organized helot resistance that shaped Spartan policy and institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Eunomia and Isonomia

Eunomia, meaning good order or good government, was a term Greek authors, including the Spartan poet Tyrtaeus, associated particularly with Sparta's constitutional stability, contrasting it favorably with the factional conflict, or stasis, that afflicted many other poleis. Isonomia, meaning equality under the law or equality of political rights, is a term modern historians and some ancient authors associate with the political changes Cleisthenes introduced at Athens, describing a principle of civic equality among citizens, especially in political membership and standing before communal institutions, without implying that all citizens yet possessed equal eligibility for every office, since inherited Solonian wealth qualifications for certain magistracies remained in place after 508/7 BCE. Neither term should be treated as a precise ancient technical label with a single fixed meaning across all periods and authors; both describe general political values that different Greek communities understood and applied somewhat differently.

Primary Source Window

Two Contrasting Voices: The Great Rhetra and the Poetry of Solon

Sources: (1) The Great Rhetra, an oracular constitutional pronouncement associated with Sparta, surviving only through quotation in Plutarch's Life of Lycurgus, composed in the late first or early second century CE. (2) Surviving fragments of the poetry of Solon, an Athenian statesman writing in his own voice around the early sixth century BCE, preserved through later quotation by ancient authors including Aristotle and Plutarch. · Type: A retrospective constitutional tradition attributed to a much earlier and uncertain period, compared with roughly contemporary political poetry composed by a named historical actor describing his own reforms.

Context: These two sources illustrate, with unusual clarity, the sharply different quality of evidence available for Sparta and Athens in this period. The Great Rhetra, whatever genuine early institutional memory it may preserve, reaches modern readers only through a source written many centuries after the events and institutions it describes, quoted by an author working from earlier writers whose own reliability cannot be independently verified. Solon's poetry, by contrast, though also transmitted only through later quotation, represents the words of a documented historical individual, writing at or near the time of the events and reforms he describes, defending and explaining his own actions in his own voice.

Paraphrase of the Great Rhetra, as preserved by Plutarch The pronouncement, attributed to the Delphic oracle and associated with Lycurgus, prescribes the establishment of a shrine to certain gods, the division of the people into tribes and other groupings, the establishment of a Gerousia of thirty including the kings, the periodic assembling of the people between two named landmarks for the purpose of deciding public business, and the principle that the people should have the final decision and power, though later tradition also preserves an addition, of disputed date, stating that if the people should choose crookedly, the kings and elders should have power to set the matter aside.
Paraphrase of Solon, fragment on his own reforms In verses that survive through later quotation, Solon describes removing the boundary markers, called horoi, that had marked land as pledged for debt, and declares that he brought back to Athens people who had been sold into slavery abroad, some of whom no longer even remembered Attic speech, while others still endured servitude at home in fear of their masters; he states that he freed these people through the exercise of the authority granted him, combining what he calls force and justice together, and claims to have written laws alike for the lowly and the powerful, fitting straight justice to each.

Analysis: The Great Rhetra's obscure, compressed phrasing and its survival only through a source written many centuries later require historians to treat it as evidence of later Spartan and pan Hellenic constitutional memory rather than as a verifiable transcript of a securely dated early Spartan enactment. Solon's poetry, though it too survives only in quoted fragments and must be read as self justifying political rhetoric rather than neutral reportage, offers a rarer opportunity in Archaic Greek history: a reform enacted by a named individual describing, in his own words and at or near the time of the events, what he believed he had accomplished and why.

Historical Debate

Did Lycurgus Create the Spartan System?

The question: Did a single historical lawgiver named Lycurgus design and establish the core institutions of the Spartan constitution, including the dual kingship's regulation, the Gerousia, the Great Rhetra, and the agōgē, in a single decisive act, as ancient tradition, especially Plutarch, claims, or did these institutions develop gradually over an extended period through the accumulated decisions of many generations, later attributed retrospectively to a single legendary founder?

Interpretation A — Lycurgus reflects a genuine, if imperfectly dated, historical reformer: Some historians argue that the persistence of the Lycurgus tradition across independent ancient sources, even though those sources disagree considerably on dates and details, suggests that a real reforming figure, whatever his precise dates, played a genuinely significant role in establishing or codifying key Spartan institutions, even if later tradition subsequently elaborated and idealized his story considerably.
Interpretation B — Lycurgus is primarily a later foundation myth: Other historians argue that the wide disagreement among ancient authors over Lycurgus's dates, the absence of any contemporary seventh or eighth century evidence for his activity, the legendary and sometimes supernatural elements attached to his story, including his consultation of the Delphic oracle, and the broader Greek pattern of attributing complex, gradually developed institutions to single celebrated lawgivers, together suggest that Lycurgus functions primarily as a later explanatory myth rather than as a reliably documented historical individual.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same limited body of ancient testimony, principally Herodotus's brief and cautious references, later Classical sources including Xenophon and Aristotle, and the much later and far more elaborate account preserved in Plutarch, but differ in how much historical weight to place on a tradition transmitted so unevenly and so far removed in time from the events it describes.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how confidently the origins of Spartan institutions can be dated and explained, and serves as a broader caution against accepting single origin narratives for complex institutions uncritically, a caution relevant well beyond the specific case of Sparta.

Student task: Identify what kind of evidence, if it existed, would help historians resolve this debate more confidently, and explain which interpretation the evidence discussed in Section 10.4 more strongly supports, while acknowledging what remains genuinely uncertain either way.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Under Two Systems

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Spartiate citizen: Full citizenship means a life bound closely to the common mess, the training ground, and the Assembly, sustained by land worked by helots whose numbers and occasional unrest make constant vigilance, not comfort, the price of Spartiate privilege.
From the perspective of a helot in Messenia: The land I work has supported my family for generations, yet it belongs, in the eyes of Spartan law, to a Spartiate citizen who never sets foot on it except to collect what is owed, and the Spartan state itself treats my community as a permanent danger to be watched rather than a population to be governed.
From the perspective of a small Athenian farmer after Solon's reforms: My person can no longer be pledged against a loan, and old debts secured against me have been lifted, a genuine relief after years of fear, but the wealthiest families still hold the offices that matter most, and it remains to be seen whether these changes will hold or whether the old rivalries will simply find some new outlet.
From the perspective of an Athenian citizen after Cleisthenes' reorganization: My deme, not my family's old regional loyalty, now defines my place among the citizens of Attica, and I sit alongside men from every part of the countryside in an Assembly that, whatever its limits, asks for my voice more directly than it did under the tyrants or even under Solon.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Archaic Citizenship to Two Distinct Political Orders

What Changed

  • Athenian political access broadened considerably between the aristocratic dominance of the seventh century and the deme based citizenship established by Cleisthenes
  • Athens moved from unwritten aristocratic custom, through Draco's written laws, to Solon's more comprehensive legal and economic reforms
  • Athenian political identity shifted from aristocratic clan and regional loyalty toward the deliberately constructed deme and tribal system
  • Sparta's institutions, though harder to date precisely, appear to have moved from an earlier, less formalized arrangement toward the more clearly defined combination of kings, Gerousia, ephors, and Assembly attested by the fifth century BCE

What Stayed the Same

  • Both societies continued to exclude women, and in Athens's case resident foreigners and enslaved people, from formal political participation throughout the period examined
  • Sparta's dependence on helot labor persisted as a constant structural feature and security concern throughout the period this chapter examines
  • Aristocratic families continued to compete for influence at Athens even after Solon's reforms, through the tyranny of Peisistratus, and into the era of Cleisthenes
  • Hoplite military service remained closely connected to citizenship and civic standing in both poleis throughout the period covered

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating either Sparta or Athens as a static, unchanging system, while also preventing the assumption that expanding political participation at Athens amounted to a complete transformation of the underlying social hierarchy.

Cause and Effect

Why Sparta and Athens Diverged

Long-term causes

Sparta's conquest based expansion into Laconia and Messenia created a large subordinated helot population, creating pressures that may have encouraged or reinforced a citizenship model organized around military readiness and internal cohesion; Attica's unification under Athens without comparable large scale internal conquest allowed a different trajectory of legal and political reform.

Short-term causes

At Athens, a specific and severe debt crisis prompted Solon's seisachtheia at a datable moment. At Sparta, conquest and the resulting dependence on helot labor created sustained structural pressure over a longer period, but the evidence does not allow historians to link the Great Rhetra confidently to any single Messenian revolt or crisis.

Effect

Sparta developed a small, militarized citizen body dependent on coerced agricultural labor and organized around communal messes and training; Athens developed a broader, though still exclusionary, citizen body organized increasingly around deme based political identity rather than birth or region alone.

This chapter's caution

Neither trajectory was predetermined by geography or economy alone; specific political choices, including the outcome of the Isagoras-Cleisthenes contest and the earlier acceptance of Solon's authority to reform Athenian law, shaped the final institutional outcomes in ways that could plausibly have developed differently.

Historical complexity: Some historians argue that Sparta's distinctive institutions developed substantially in response to the practical challenge of controlling the helot population, while others caution against reducing every Spartan institution to this single security driven explanation, noting that similar structural pressures did not produce identical institutions in every conquest based Greek community.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life Within Two Political Orders

Political institutions shaped daily life in both poleis in ways that extended well beyond the Assembly and the council chamber. A Spartiate man's day, according to the traditions examined in this chapter, was organized substantially around communal activity: shared meals in the syssition, physical training, and, for younger men, continued participation in the educational system associated with the agōgē, leaving comparatively little space for the private household economic management that occupied much of daily life for citizens in many other poleis. A helot family in Messenia, by contrast, lived under the constant, if variably enforced, oversight of Spartiate landholders and, according to ancient testimony, occasionally harsh measures intended to prevent organized resistance, while still maintaining its own agricultural labor, family structure, and, evidence suggests, elements of a distinct communal and religious life despite its subordinate legal status.

At Athens, the reforms examined in this chapter reshaped ordinary civic experience as well. A small farmer freed from the threat of debt bondage by Solon's seisachtheia could work his land with a different sense of security than his father or grandfather might have known, while a citizen registered in one of Cleisthenes' new demes after 508/7 BCE found his political identity, and his practical path to occasional service on the new Council of Five Hundred, tied to a local community structure that, in many cases, cut across older ties of extended family and aristocratic patronage. These ordinary experiences of security, obligation, and belonging, however unevenly distributed and however incompletely documented for the poorest Athenians and for women in both poleis, are as much a part of this chapter's history as the formal institutional structures described in its main sections.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has compared the political and social orders of two Archaic Greek poleis, Sparta and Athens, that emerged from a shared world of aristocratic competition, hoplite warfare, and civic experimentation described in Chapter 9, yet developed markedly different institutions for organizing citizenship, political authority, military obligation, and social hierarchy.

Sparta's distinctive order developed through conquest, first within Laconia and later in Messenia, producing a small citizen body of Spartiates sustained by the compelled agricultural labor of a much larger helot population, and governed through a constitutional arrangement combining hereditary dual kingship, an aristocratic Gerousia, five annually elected ephors, and a citizen Assembly with more limited deliberative initiative than its later Athenian counterpart. The traditional attribution of this entire system to a single lawgiver, Lycurgus, and to a single founding document, the Great Rhetra, cannot be verified with confidence, and this chapter has treated that uncertainty as an essential part of understanding Spartan history rather than a minor caveat to be set aside.

Spartan society depended on three legally and socially distinct populations: Spartiates, who alone possessed full citizenship; perioikoi, free but politically subordinate communities engaged in trade and military service; and helots, a coerced agricultural labor population whose subordination and periodic resistance shaped much of Spartan civic life and institutional development. Spartan women, while excluded from formal political power, occupied a social and economic position that outside observers considered notable by comparison with practices associated with other poleis, without amounting to political equality.

Athens, meanwhile, moved through a sequence of aristocratic dominance, Draco's written laws, Solon's more far reaching legal and economic reforms including the seisachtheia, a period of tyranny under Peisistratus and his sons, and, finally, Cleisthenes' reorganization of Attica into demes and tribes following the expulsion of Hippias in 510 BCE. Each stage addressed genuine social and political tensions without fully resolving them, and this chapter has been careful to distinguish Solon's significant but partial reforms, and Cleisthenes' foundational but not yet fully democratic reorganization, from the mature participatory democracy that would develop at Athens only in the following century.

The direct comparison offered in Section 10.10 emphasized that both systems rested on exclusion and on labor performed by populations without political voice, even as they differed substantially in the scale of citizen participation, the legal mechanisms of dependent labor, and the character of surviving evidence. Sparta's oligarchy, tempered by citizen participation and organized around militarized communal citizenship, and Athens's broadening, though still exclusionary, citizen body, organized increasingly around deme based political identity, together illustrate the genuine range of political possibility within the shared Archaic Greek civic world, without either polis representing the whole of that world.

The Historical Debate feature on Lycurgus illustrated a problem of evidence that recurs throughout the study of early Sparta: the difficulty of separating genuine early institutional development from later retrospective tradition. The comparison of the Great Rhetra and Solon's own poetry in the Primary Source Window demonstrated, concretely, why historians can speak with greater confidence about early sixth century Athens than about early Sparta.

These themes, comparative political structure, the legal and social distinction between different forms of dependent labor, the caution required when evidence is retrospective or externally produced, and the persistent exclusion of women, foreigners, and enslaved or subordinated populations from formal political power, will recur throughout the remainder of Part II. Chapter 11 now turns to the Persian Wars, in which both Sparta and Athens, still bearing the distinctive institutional characters this chapter has described, faced a common external threat from the Achaemenid Persian Empire.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Polis
An independent Greek city-state, the basic political unit of Archaic and Classical Greece, examined generally in Chapter 9 and here through the specific cases of Sparta and Athens.
Oligarchy
Rule by a relatively small, privileged group; a term applied to Sparta's political order, which restricted full citizenship, the Assembly, and eligibility for office to the Spartiate minority.
Spartiate
A full Spartan citizen, entitled to Assembly participation and messing rights, supported by landholdings worked by helot labor, and eligible for the Gerousia and ephorate.
Perioikoi
Free inhabitants of communities within the Spartan state who lacked Spartan political citizenship but engaged in trade, craft, and military service.
Helot
A member of the subordinated population, concentrated in Messenia and parts of Laconia, compelled to perform agricultural labor for Spartiate landholders under a distinctive coercive status.
Helotage
The distinctive Spartan system of coerced, land bound labor, distinguished by historians from privately owned chattel slavery.
Gerousia
The Spartan council of elders, composed of the two kings and twenty eight citizens over sixty years of age elected for life, playing a major role alongside the ephors in deliberation and in preparing business for the Assembly.
Ephor
One of five Spartan officials elected annually, holding significant judicial, financial, and oversight powers, including authority to call the kings to account.
Great Rhetra
A traditional oracular pronouncement associated with Sparta's constitutional order and the lawgiver Lycurgus, surviving only through a much later quotation in Plutarch.
Agōgē
The traditional term, attested mainly in later sources, for the Spartan communal upbringing and training system for citizen boys.
Syssitia
The Spartan common messes, communal dining groups whose membership and contribution requirements were closely tied to full Spartiate citizenship.
Draco
Athenian lawgiver credited by later tradition with Athens's first written laws around 621/0 BCE; only his homicide law is securely attested, while the scope of any wider legislation remains uncertain.
Archon
One of the annual chief officials of Athens, originally drawn exclusively from aristocratic families, whose powers were gradually redefined through the reforms examined in this chapter.
Areopagus
The Athenian council of former archons, holding substantial judicial and advisory authority throughout the period examined in this chapter.
Seisachtheia
Solon's cancellation of debts secured against debtors' persons and his prohibition of future enslavement for debt, meaning literally the shaking off of burdens.
Debt bondage
A condition in which a person's labor, or a pledge of personal freedom, secures repayment of a debt; addressed and substantially restricted by Solon's reforms at Athens.
Tyranny
In the Archaic Greek sense, rule by an individual who seized power outside traditional constitutional or hereditary channels through elite political competition, sometimes drawing wider popular backing, as exemplified by Peisistratus.
Deme
A local Athenian village or city neighborhood that became, under Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/7 BCE, the basic unit of Athenian citizenship and political identity.
Trittys (pl. trittyes)
One of thirty units, ten each from the city, coastal, and inland regions of Attica, that Cleisthenes combined, one from each region, to form each of his ten new tribes.
Hektemoroi
A population of Athenians, referenced in the tradition surrounding Solon's reforms, working land under some form of obligation to more powerful landholders; their precise status and the full effect of Solon's measures on their condition remain debated.
Isonomia
Equality under the law or equality of political rights, a term associated with the political changes Cleisthenes introduced at Athens.
Eunomia
Good order or good government, a term Greek authors associated particularly with Sparta's constitutional stability.
Synoikismos
The traditional process by which separate communities of Attica were brought together into a single political unit centered on Athens.
Council of Five Hundred
The Athenian council established by Cleisthenes, with fifty members drawn from each of the ten new tribes, responsible for preparing business for the Assembly.
Spartan mirage
A term historians use to describe the ways surviving accounts of Sparta reflect the interests and purposes of outside observers, particularly later ones, rather than providing direct, unmediated access to Spartan reality.
Citizenship
Formal membership in a polis carrying specific political, legal, military, and social rights and obligations, defined very differently at Sparta and at Athens across the period examined in this chapter.
Chattel slavery
A system of privately owned, individually bought and sold enslaved people, characteristic of Athens and much of the wider Greek world, and historians distinguish it from Spartan helotage.
Ostracism
A later Athenian procedure allowing citizens to vote to exile a prominent individual for ten years without formal charge, sometimes associated with Cleisthenes though first securely attested in actual use somewhat later.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that a shared Archaic Greek political culture, described in general terms in Chapter 9, could produce two markedly different political orders when worked out in the specific historical circumstances of Sparta and Athens. Sparta's path through conquest produced a small, militarized citizen body sustained by coerced helot labor and organized through a distinctive balance of kingship, council, elected oversight, and assembly. Athens's path through legal codification, economic reform, tyranny, and deliberate political reorganization produced a broader, though still exclusionary, citizen body increasingly defined by geography rather than birth alone.

Neither path was inevitable, and neither should be mistaken for the whole of Greek political experience. This chapter has insisted throughout on a disciplined comparison, one that resists the temptation to reduce Sparta to simple tyranny and Athens to simple freedom, and one that keeps in view the populations, helots, perioikoi, metics, enslaved people, and women in both societies, whose labor and subordination made citizen participation possible for those who held it.

This chapter has also modeled a habit of mind essential to the rest of this textbook: reading evidence critically according to its date, its authorship, and its likely purpose, rather than accepting every ancient claim at face value. The problem of Lycurgus and the Great Rhetra, set against the comparatively firmer ground of Solon's own poetry, illustrates why historians of the ancient world must constantly ask not only what a source says, but when it was written, by whom, and why.

Chapter 11 now turns to the Persian Wars, a crisis that would test both the Spartan and Athenian systems described in this chapter under the pressure of a common external threat, and that would begin to reshape the relationship between these two very differently organized poleis in ways that would dominate much of the remainder of Greek history examined in this Part.
