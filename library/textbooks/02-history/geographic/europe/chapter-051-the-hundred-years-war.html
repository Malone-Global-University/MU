The Hundred Years' War

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 17 July 1453, outside the Gascon town of Castillon, an English army led by John Talbot, earl of Shrewsbury, attacked a fortified French artillery camp and was destroyed. Within months Bordeaux surrendered. Yet no treaty ended the conflict, and English kings went on styling themselves kings of France for another three and a half centuries. The war's ending was as untidy as its beginning.

The conflict that later historians call the Hundred Years' War did not begin with a declaration, did not run continuously, and did not last exactly a hundred years. It consisted of campaigns, sieges, raids, naval actions, truces, and civil wars between 1337 and 1453, in which the kings of England and France, their allies, their dependents, and their rivals fought over lordship in Gascony, the French crown, Flanders, Brittany, Normandy, and the sea. The name itself is a modern convention, and a useful one only if it is remembered to be a convention.

At its center was a problem that medieval kingship had created for itself. The kings of England were also, through the duchy of Aquitaine, vassals of the kings of France. When a king owed homage to another king for lands he ruled as sovereign in all but name, every dispute over justice, appeals, or succession threatened to become a dispute over sovereignty. In 1328 the direct male line of the Capetian kings of France ended, and Edward III of England, whose mother was a Capetian princess, had a claim that he would eventually press. But the claim was an instrument as much as a cause, and the war grew out of rivalries over Gascony, Flanders, Scotland, and trade as well.

The war transformed the societies that fought it. Armies changed in composition and organization, as the English crown contracted with captains to supply paid retinues and French kings struggled to replace feudal levies with disciplined companies. Taxation expanded, and with it the bargaining between rulers and assemblies. Soldiers lived off the countryside, villagers sheltered in fortified churches or walled towns, and entire regions were laid waste. Armed bands, unemployed in times of truce, terrorized provinces, while ransoms circulated vast sums among nobles and captains.

The war also shaped political identity. Kings, chroniclers, preachers, and poets described enemies in ways that sharpened collective loyalties, and language, saints, and symbols were mobilized. Yet historians now warn against reading modern nationalism back into the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Subjects remained loyal to dynasties, lords, cities, and regions, and the Burgundian and Armagnac factions of France, or the Gascons who were attached to English rule, show how complicated allegiance could be.

The evidence is rich and uneven. Chroniclers such as Jean Froissart, Jean Le Bel, Jean de Venette, and Thomas Walsingham wrote vivid narratives with their own loyalties and literary aims. Royal letters, treaties, indentures, muster rolls, exchequer and tax accounts, parliamentary and Estates records, municipal registers, and letters of remission, French royal pardons that record how ordinary people described violent incidents, allow historians to reconstruct recruitment, finance, and local experience. The trial records of Joan of Arc, among the best documented of any medieval person, add a different kind of testimony.

Within Part VIII, Chapter 51 follows the demographic shock of Chapter 50 with the political and military crisis of the same century. The plague did not stop the war and did not directly cause its military or fiscal developments, but the two histories overlapped, and the strains of both shaped the possibilities open to rulers. Chapter 52 examines the labor, wages, and revolts that accompanied these pressures, Chapter 53 the crisis of the church, Chapter 54 Burgundy and other peripheries in a wider European setting, and Chapter 55 the gunpowder and print revolutions around 1500.

Within Volume II, the chapter shows medieval monarchies discovering both the power and the limits of their resources. By 1453 the French crown had expelled the English from nearly all of France and had acquired stronger fiscal and military instruments, while the English crown had lost its continental empire. But neither outcome was inevitable, and neither amounted to the emergence of a modern nation-state, a theme developed in Chapter 74 on the early modern state.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Hundred Years' War should be understood as a series of conflicts and truces and not a single continuous war.
  2. Analyze the causes of the war, including the French succession, Aquitaine, Flanders, Scotland, and economic and maritime rivalries, without reducing it to a dynastic claim alone.
  3. Trace the principal phases of the war, from the English victories of the 1340s and 1350s through French recovery under Charles V, civil war under Charles VI, Henry V's conquests, and the French reconquest.
  4. Describe changes in recruitment, tactics, fortification, and artillery, and assess the limits of claims that the longbow or gunpowder alone decided the war.
  5. Evaluate the effects of raiding, siege, occupation, and mercenary violence on civilians and regions.
  6. Explain how English and French rulers financed war through parliamentary subsidies, customs, loans, aides, salt taxes, and tailles, and how bargaining with assemblies shaped those systems.
  7. Assess the relationship between prolonged war and state formation without treating it as a smooth path toward the modern state.
  8. Interpret the Treaty of Troyes and the role of Joan of Arc as problems of legitimacy and political imagination.
  9. Evaluate competing interpretations of how the war affected political identity, and use the concept of nationalism with caution.
  10. Connect the war to the Black Death, the papacy, Iberia, Flanders, Burgundy, and other wider European contexts.

Historical Context

Chapter 33 traced the formation of the kingdoms of England, France, and the German kingdom, and Chapter 45 examined the growth of medieval monarchy in England after the Norman Conquest, including royal justice, taxation, and administration. Those chapters established that by 1300 both realms had developed institutions through which kings could command resources: courts, exchequers, chanceries, and assemblies of counsel. They also established that the English kings, as descendants of William the Conqueror and the Angevin counts, held lands on both sides of the Channel, a legacy that left a long and unstable relationship with the French crown.

The French monarchy of the early fourteenth century was among the richest and most prestigious in Europe, but it governed a realm of great regional diversity. Large principalities such as Flanders, Brittany, Burgundy, and Aquitaine retained their own customs, assemblies, and elites, and royal authority was exercised through a mixture of direct administration and negotiated lordship. The English monarchy, smaller and more compact, had a more centralized system of law and a parliament that was becoming a regular forum for taxation and petition, though the crown's ability to command resources still depended on cooperation from magnates and gentry.

Economic ties linked the two realms with Flanders and the wider North Sea world. English wool, the kingdom's principal export, supplied the cloth industry of Flemish towns such as Ghent, Bruges, and Ypres, which, as Chapter 50 noted, depended on imported raw materials and imported grain. Gascony exported wine from Bordeaux to England in large quantities, and the Gascon elites valued their trading privileges and their autonomy under the English crown. Control of these exchanges mattered as much as titles to the strategists of both courts.

The war began just before the Black Death, which Chapter 50 examined, and was shaped by the demographic and fiscal conditions of the age. Population in many regions was still large in 1337, though weakened in the north by the Great Famine of 1315 to 1317, and the plague's arrival in 1347 and 1348 interrupted campaigns without ending the conflict. The war's course was also shaped by the papacy at Avignon, which attempted to mediate, by the Holy Roman Empire and the Low Countries, whose rulers were courted as allies, and by the Iberian kingdoms, which were drawn into the conflict and which later provided naval support to France.

Two cautions guide the chapter. First, neither England nor France was a modern centralized state or national community. Kings relied on lords, towns, churches, and assemblies, and subjects' loyalties were layered. Second, the war's outcome should not be read backward from the later histories of England and France. The French victory was the result of particular events, mistakes, and circumstances, not an inevitable consequence of France's larger population and resources.

Section 51.1

What Was the Hundred Years' War?

The label "Hundred Years' War" was devised by later historians, and became established in the nineteenth century. Contemporaries did not know they were living through a single war of 116 years. They experienced campaigns, truces that sometimes lasted years, civil conflicts, local feuds, and diplomatic negotiations, and they used a variety of names for the struggles of their time. The conventional dates of 1337 and 1453 mark, respectively, Philip VI's confiscation of Gascony and the French victory at Castillon and surrender of Bordeaux. Neither date marks a clean beginning or end.

Historians often divide the war into three broad phases, labeled for the dynasty or ruler who dominated them. The first, sometimes called the Edwardian war, runs from 1337 to the Treaty of Brétigny in 1360 and is marked by English victories at Sluys, Crécy, and Poitiers. The second, the Caroline war, runs from the resumption of fighting in 1369 to the long truces of the late 1380s and 1390s and is marked by the French recovery under Charles V. The third, the Lancastrian war, begins with Henry V's invasion in 1415 and ends in 1453. Between these phases lay long truces and a constantly shifting set of local conflicts, such as the Breton succession war, the Castilian civil war, and the struggles between French princes.

Treating the conflict as a whole is nevertheless useful, because the same issues, the status of Gascony, the English claim to the French crown, French sovereignty, and the power of the princes, recurred across generations, and because the fiscal, military, and political institutions developed in one phase carried into the next. But the phrase should not suggest continuous fighting. Many years passed without a major campaign, and in some years the principal violence was committed by companies of soldiers who were not under the control of either king.

The evidence is correspondingly varied. Narrative sources, English, French, Burgundian, Flemish, and Castilian, offer sharply different perspectives. Administrative records, including English indentures, muster rolls, and exchequer accounts, and French royal ordinances, tax records, and municipal registers, allow historians to measure the scale of recruitment and finance. Treaties and diplomatic correspondence reveal negotiating positions. The letters of remission preserved in French archives, in which people seeking royal pardon described the circumstances of violent acts, provide unusually direct glimpses of soldiers and civilians. Each class of source must be read for its purposes and its silences.

Section 51.2

Dynastic Claims, Aquitaine, and the Anglo-French Monarchy Problem

The immediate trigger of the war's origins was the French succession. Between 1314 and 1328, the Capetian dynasty, which had ruled France since 987, lost its direct male line in rapid succession. Louis X died in 1316 leaving a daughter and a posthumous son who lived only a few days; his brother became Philip V, who died in 1322 leaving only daughters; a third brother, Charles IV, died in 1328, also without a surviving son. Each time, the magnates and counselors who decided the succession favored the nearest male relative and excluded women from inheriting the throne. In 1328 they chose Philip of Valois, a nephew of Philip IV and cousin of the late king, as Philip VI.

Edward III of England, then fifteen, was the late king's nephew through his mother, Isabella, a sister of Charles IV. Whether a woman could transmit a claim to the throne that she could not hold herself was a question for which no settled rule existed, and the decision of 1328 was in part a political settlement that excluded an English king from the French throne. Edward at first accepted it and, in 1329 and 1331, did homage to Philip VI for Aquitaine. The legal arguments that supported the Valois succession, including the so-called Salic law, were developed more fully later, as the war made the question urgent.

Behind the succession lay the structural problem of Gascony. The English kings held the duchy of Aquitaine, in practice largely Gascony around Bordeaux and Bayonne, as vassals of the French crown, a relationship confirmed in the Treaty of Paris of 1259. The French crown claimed the right to hear appeals from Gascon courts, to confiscate the duchy for disobedience, and to intervene in the duchy's affairs, and each such claim threatened English sovereignty. The Gascons themselves, who prized their privileges and their wine trade with England, often preferred a distant English duke to a nearby French king. A short war in 1324, the War of Saint-Sardos, had already shown how local incidents could escalate into confiscation and military action.

Other rivalries intensified the confrontation. Flanders, whose count was a French vassal and whose towns depended on English wool, was divided between a pro-French count and pro-English townsmen. Scotland, where Edward III supported claimants against the young David II, was bound to France by the Auld Alliance, and in 1334 David and his court took refuge in France. Maritime rivalry in the Channel, with piracy and privateering by Norman, Breton, and English sailors, produced a steady series of incidents and French preparations for coastal raids. Robert of Artois, a French magnate exiled in 1332 and welcomed in England, encouraged Edward to press his claim. The dynastic claim mattered, but it was woven into a dense web of lordship, commerce, and political grievance.

Historical Vocabulary
Salic Law

The Salic law (Lex Salica) was a Frankish legal code compiled beginning in the early sixth century. A provision concerning the inheritance of "Salic land" was only later adapted into a doctrine governing royal succession. The exclusion of women from the French crown was already political practice in 1316 and 1328, before that textual argument was developed. The chronicler and cleric Richard Lescot explicitly connected the succession to Salic law in 1358, and Jean de Montreuil used it against the English claim in the early fifteenth century, around 1408 to 1413. It became a central element of French royal ideology, but historians treat it as a legitimizing argument developed to justify a succession already made, not as a rule that determined the choice of 1328.

Section 51.3

From Feudal Dispute to Open War, 1337–1340

In May 1337, Philip VI declared the duchy of Aquitaine confiscated, citing Edward III's alleged breaches of his obligations as a vassal, including his sheltering of Robert of Artois. This was a standard legal move in feudal relations, but one with grave consequences when directed at a king. Edward responded by preparing for war, and in the following years he assembled an alliance of princes in the Low Countries and the empire, including the counts of Hainaut and Guelders and the duke of Brabant, paying them large subsidies and, in 1338, securing the support of the emperor Louis IV of Bavaria, who named him imperial vicar.

The cost was immense. Edward financed his alliances and campaigns through heavy borrowing from Italian bankers, notably the Bardi and Peruzzi, and from English merchants, and through an extraordinary arrangement to purchase and export wool for the crown's benefit. Parliament granted taxes, but with growing conditions, and the strain on the English economy and on the king's relations with his ministers was severe. In Flanders, a trade embargo on English wool in 1336 and the king's economic pressure helped bring the cloth towns, under the leadership of Jacob van Artevelde of Ghent, into alliance with England, which Flemish urban leaders saw as protecting their trade against their count.

In January 1340, at Ghent, Edward publicly assumed the title king of France and quartered the French royal arms with his own. The step was in part diplomatic, since it allowed the Flemish, who owed fealty to the French crown, to swear allegiance to Edward as their rightful overlord without breaking their oath. It also marked the beginning of a claim that English kings would maintain for centuries. On 24 June 1340, at Sluys, an English fleet destroyed much of the French fleet assembled to oppose an invasion, giving England command of the Channel for several years and protecting England from a major French landing. Philip's naval power, which had burned coastal towns such as Southampton in 1338, was broken for the time being.

The land war was less successful. Edward's siege of Tournai in 1340 failed, his finances collapsed, and a truce was agreed at Esplechin that autumn. In the following years, the war centered on Brittany, where a disputed succession from 1341 saw the French crown back Charles of Blois and the English back John de Montfort, and on Gascony and Scotland. This pattern, a dynastic and territorial dispute fought through local allies and sporadic campaigns, was characteristic of the war's early years. The crucial English victory came not through a conquest but through a landing in Normandy in 1346.

Cause and Effect

The Outbreak of War, 1337

Long-term causes

The feudal subordination of English kings to French kings for Gascony; ambiguity about sovereignty and appeals; commercial ties among England, Flanders, and Gascony; the Anglo-Scottish and Franco-Scottish alliances; maritime rivalry.

Short-term causes

The failure of the Capetian male line and the Valois succession of 1328; disputes over Gascony and homage; the exile of Robert of Artois in England; Philip VI's confiscation of Gascony in 1337; Flemish and Scottish tensions.

Event or transformation

The transformation of a feudal and territorial dispute into open warfare, culminating in Edward III's assumption of the French royal title in 1340.

Immediate effects

Heavy English borrowing and taxation; alliances in the Low Countries and empire; the naval victory at Sluys; the Breton succession war; naval raids on southern England.

Long-term consequences

A recurrent conflict over more than a century; major changes in military organization, taxation, and political identity; ultimately the loss of English lands in France.

Historical complexity

Edward III's claim to the French throne did not by itself cause the war. It was one instrument in a struggle over lordship, trade, and security in which his advisers and opponents each pursued varied aims.
Section 51.4

Crécy, Calais, and the English Invasions, 1346–1347

In July 1346, Edward III landed on the Cotentin peninsula in Normandy with an army that had been recruited by contract and paid wages. Having landed in Normandy, he marched through the province, sacked Caen, and moved up the Seine toward Paris, with the aim, historians infer, of drawing the French army into a fight on favorable terms and damaging Philip VI's prestige and revenues. Pursued by a large French army, he crossed the Somme at a ford and took up a defensive position at Crécy in Ponthieu.

On 26 August 1346, the French attacked. Edward's army fought mostly dismounted, with the men-at-arms in the center and archers armed with the longbow on the flanks. The French force included Genoese crossbowmen, who were quickly overwhelmed by arrow fire, and a mounted nobility whose repeated charges, uphill and in some disorder, were broken by the English line. Many French nobles were killed, including the blind king John of Bohemia, an ally of Philip. The sixteen-year-old Edward of Woodstock, the Black Prince, commanded part of the line.

Crécy mattered, but it must be interpreted carefully. It did not show that cavalry was obsolete or that the longbow rendered knights useless. The English men-at-arms were themselves knights and gentry who fought on foot, and mounted men continued to be essential for scouting, pursuit, and raiding. The English victory resulted from a combination of terrain, a defensive posture, discipline, the longbow's effectiveness against horses and lightly armored men at range, and French disorder. The French response was not to abandon armored cavalry but to adapt, a process examined in later sections.

After the battle, Edward laid siege to Calais, which fell in August 1347 after a long siege and the failure of a French relief attempt. The English expelled much of the population and settled English colonists, making Calais a permanent bridgehead and, for the English crown, a wool staple and garrison town that it held until 1558. The later story of the burghers of Calais offering themselves for execution to save the town derives from the chronicler Jean Froissart, and its details should be treated as literary. In October 1346, while Philip was occupied, the Scots under David II invaded northern England and were defeated at Neville's Cross, where David was captured. By late 1347, the English held the initiative, and the war had turned decisively, if not conclusively, in Edward's favor.

Section 51.5

War, Truce, and the Black Death, 1347–1355

A truce was agreed in September 1347, and, as Chapter 50 describes, plague reached England and France in 1348. Large-scale campaigning ended, but local warfare did not. Brittany remained a theater, the garrisons around Calais and in Gascony raided, and naval clashes continued: in August 1350 off Winchelsea, an English fleet defeated a Castilian squadron, a reminder that Iberian powers were becoming participants. The papal court at Avignon sponsored negotiations, though without lasting success.

The plague touched the courts directly. Edward's daughter Joan died of plague at Bordeaux in 1348 on her way to marry the heir of Castile, and Philip VI's queen, Joan of Burgundy, died in 1349. Philip VI died in August 1350 and was succeeded by his son John II, a king of conventional chivalric ambitions who founded the Order of the Star. The demographic effects of the plague complicated recruitment and revenue in both realms, and the fiscal and labor problems that followed, examined in Chapter 52, affected governments' ability to pay for war. But the plague did not directly cause the later military or fiscal systems of either side. Those developed out of the pressures of war itself.

Diplomacy in these years was frequent but unstable. Truces were extended, but they were violated by soldiers, by local feuds, and by competing claims. In France, John II's government faced fiscal difficulties, resorted to manipulation of the coinage, and was challenged by Charles of Navarre, a grandson of Louis X with his own claim to the throne and extensive lands in Normandy, whose alternating alliances with English and French kings would trouble France for decades. Negotiations at Guînes and Avignon in 1354 produced a draft settlement, but it collapsed. By 1355, both sides had returned to open war.

Section 51.6

The Black Prince, Chevauchées, and Poitiers, 1355–1356

In October 1355, Edward of Woodstock led a large mounted raid from Bordeaux through Languedoc as far as Narbonne, burning towns and villages, destroying crops, and seizing goods. The expedition avoided fortified cities that could resist, and its purpose was economic and political: to deprive the French crown of revenue, to show that the king could not protect his subjects, and to enrich the English army. Contemporary writers, including English chroniclers, described great destruction. The raid damaged the region's economy and drew resentment toward the French crown, which could neither prevent nor avenge it.

In 1356, the Black Prince launched a second raid northward from Gascony toward the Loire, while other English forces operated in Normandy. John II assembled a large army and pursued him. On 19 September 1356, near Poitiers, the English took a strong defensive position behind hedges and ditches. The French attack, partly dismounted, was delivered in successive divisions, and was defeated. King John, who fought on foot, was captured with his youngest son and many nobles.

The capture of a king was a catastrophe unlike any since the capture of Louis IX in 1250. It meant that the realm had to pay a ransom, that royal authority was suspended or exercised by a regency, and that the English possessed a bargaining asset of extraordinary value. Poitiers also spurred a crisis of confidence in the French nobility, who were blamed by contemporaries for the defeat, and prepared the political disorder examined in the next section.

Historical Vocabulary
Chevauchée

A chevauchée (from the French for a mounted ride) was a large-scale raid by a mounted army through enemy territory, intended to destroy resources, collect plunder and ransom, undermine the legitimacy of the enemy ruler, and provoke battle on favorable terms. English commanders employed it frequently in France in the 1340s to 1370s. A chevauchée was not random destruction, since it targeted resources that supported the enemy crown, and it relied on mobility and speed to avoid large-scale sieges. It is a central practice for understanding the war's effect on civilians, discussed in Section 51.10.

Section 51.7

French Political Crisis and the Treaty of Brétigny, 1356–1360

With the king captive, authority passed to his eldest son, Charles, the eighteen-year-old Dauphin, who was named regent. He faced an empty treasury, an unruly nobility, and a demand for reform. The Estates-General, summoned in October 1356 and again in early 1357, used the crisis to press for control over taxation, for the dismissal of unpopular royal officials, and for a reformed council. The urban leadership of Paris, particularly Étienne Marcel, provost of the merchants, played a prominent part, and the Great Ordinance of March 1357 sought to place royal finance under the supervision of the Estates.

The reform movement did not hold together. Disputes arose between the Estates and the Dauphin, between Paris and the provinces, and among nobles, townsmen, and clergy. Charles of Navarre, released from prison in late 1357, became a rival focus of loyalty. In February 1358, Parisian crowds killed two of the Dauphin's marshals in his presence. The Dauphin left Paris and began to gather forces, while Marcel allied with Navarre and, for a time, with the rural rebels of the Jacquerie, a peasant uprising in the Beauvaisis and nearby regions in the summer of 1358. That revolt, and the broader social tensions it expressed, are examined in Chapter 52. Marcel was killed in Paris on 31 July 1358, and the Dauphin re-entered the city in August.

Meanwhile, Edward III sought to turn his captive to advantage. A treaty negotiated in London in 1359 demanded enormous territorial concessions and a huge ransom, and the Estates rejected it. Edward then invaded France in the winter of 1359 to 1360 in an attempt to be crowned at Reims. He failed to capture Reims or Paris, and the campaign, hampered by bad weather and the Dauphin's policy of avoiding battle, did not bring a decisive result. The two sides reached agreement at Brétigny in May 1360, ratified at Calais in October.

The treaty gave Edward full sovereignty, without homage, over a much enlarged Aquitaine, together with Calais and Ponthieu, and set John II's ransom at three million gold écus. In return, Edward was to renounce his claim to the French crown. The exchange of renunciations was repeatedly deferred and never fully completed, which left room for the later resumption of the war. John was released in 1360, but when one of his hostages escaped he returned voluntarily to captivity in England and died there in 1364. To raise the ransom the French crown introduced permanent indirect taxes, which began a significant development in French fiscal practice, discussed in Section 51.12. Edward's son, the Black Prince, was installed as prince of Aquitaine in 1362, but the new principality included lords with little attachment to English rule.

Section 51.8

Charles V, Du Guesclin, and the French Recovery, 1360–1380

Charles V, who became king in 1364, was physically frail and temperamentally cautious, and he proved an effective ruler. In his first year, his commander Bertrand du Guesclin, a Breton knight of modest origins, won the battle of Cocherel against forces of Charles of Navarre, though the Breton succession war ended in defeat at Auray, where du Guesclin was captured, and the Treaty of Guérande in 1365 recognized John de Montfort as duke. The next major test was in Iberia, where Du Guesclin led companies of soldiers into Castile to support Henry of Trastámara against Pedro I, a venture that removed disorderly bands from France. In 1367 the Black Prince, supporting Pedro, defeated Henry and du Guesclin at Nájera, but the expedition left him with debts and illness.

To pay those debts, the Black Prince imposed a hearth tax in Aquitaine, and several Gascon lords appealed to Charles V as their sovereign. Charles accepted the appeal in 1368 and 1369, declared the English possession of Aquitaine forfeit, and prepared for war. Edward III responded in June 1369 by resuming the title king of France. The French monarch had used the legal ambiguities of Brétigny, and the unfulfilled renunciations, to reopen the conflict at a moment of his choosing.

The French strategy of the 1370s is often described as avoiding pitched battles, but it was more active than that suggests. Charles appointed du Guesclin constable in 1370 and developed a policy of harassing English columns while refusing a general engagement. English chevauchées, such as Robert Knolles's in 1370 and John of Gaunt's through France in 1373, inflicted damage but achieved little. French forces captured fortresses, negotiated surrenders, and exploited local allegiances, regaining Poitou, Saintonge, and much of Aquitaine by the mid-1370s. At sea, a Franco-Castilian fleet destroyed an English squadron off La Rochelle in 1372, and French and Castilian raiders attacked the English south coast in 1377. The Black Prince, chronically ill after the Castilian campaign of 1367 and increasingly unable to take an active military role, returned to England in 1371 and died in 1376, and Edward III followed in 1377.

By Charles V's death in 1380 the English held, in France, little more than Calais, Bordeaux, Bayonne, and a coastal strip, although they were not finally expelled. Truces were negotiated from the late 1380s, and in 1396 Richard II of England married Charles VI's daughter Isabella in a settlement that included a long truce. The broader easing of Anglo-French warfare and the peace negotiations of the 1390s helped make it possible for French and Burgundian nobles to direct military energies elsewhere. In 1396 a major contingent joined the crusade that ended disastrously at Nicopolis, where a Christian army was defeated by the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I, an episode linking the war to the wider history of crusading (Chapter 47) and Ottoman expansion in southeastern Europe. Richard II was deposed in 1399 by Henry of Lancaster, who became Henry IV, and the war entered a long period of uneasy peace, though not of concord.

Section 51.9

Armies and the Changing Practice of War

The war's armies differed from the feudal hosts of earlier centuries. English kings increasingly relied on paid, contracted forces. A noble or captain would sign an indenture with the crown, agreeing to supply a stated number of men-at-arms and archers for a fixed period at agreed rates, and in turn recruit his own retinue, often from his own tenants, neighbors, and dependents. This system, together with commissions to array archers from the shires, allowed the crown to raise large armies quickly and to pay them from taxation. The English armies were small by comparison with the French, but experienced and disciplined, and they combined armored men-at-arms, who usually fought on foot, with archers, many of whom were mounted for mobility and fought on foot.

French armies traditionally relied on the feudal summons, the arrière-ban, and on the heavily armored cavalry of the nobility, supplemented by crossbowmen, often Genoese mercenaries, and urban militias. The defeats of Crécy and Poitiers prompted adaptation. Charles V relied on paid men-at-arms, garrisoned strongpoints, and avoided battle unless conditions favored his forces. The nobility remained central to French armies but its tactics changed, and men-at-arms increasingly dismounted to fight. Plate armor gradually replaced mail, providing better protection against arrows, though at greater cost.

Sieges dominated much of the fighting, since fortified towns and castles were the keys to territory. Towns built or repaired walls, and fortifications were adapted to the new weapons, with gunports and lower, thicker walls. Gunpowder artillery appeared in the early decades of the war, and by the late fourteenth century cannon were used in sieges, though they were unreliable and expensive. Henry V made extensive use of siege guns, and Charles VII's artillery, directed by the brothers Jean and Gaspard Bureau, proved decisive in the reconquest of Normandy and Gascony. Gunpowder's effect was therefore cumulative, and its fuller consequences for fortification and state power are examined in Chapter 55. It did not by itself win the war.

Logistics, shipping, and horses were as important as weapons. Armies had to be fed and moved, and English expeditions required the requisition of ships, and horses for transport and mounts. Chevauchées relied on foraging and plunder because supplying large mounted forces from home was impractical. Naval warfare, from Sluys and Winchelsea to La Rochelle, was carried out largely by requisitioned merchant ships, supplemented by galleys. Each side also employed foreign contingents: Genoese and Castilian ships, Scottish and Welsh soldiers, Bohemian, German, and Gascon captains. The historical significance of the longbow lies in its combination with disciplined infantry, defensive tactics, and favorable ground, not in any technological monopoly.

Historical Vocabulary
Indenture

An indenture was a written contract, so called because the document was copied on a single sheet and cut in a wavy or toothed line (an "indent") so that the matching halves could be authenticated. In the Hundred Years' War, indentures between the crown and captains specified the number and type of troops, rates of pay, the length of service, and the division of spoils and ransoms. The system, widely used by English kings from the fourteenth century, gave the crown flexibility and allowed it to raise armies without relying on feudal service, but it also made royal armies dependent on the loyalty and financial reliability of the contracting captains.

Section 51.10

Chevauchée, Siege, Occupation, and Civilian Experience

For most inhabitants of France, the war meant not battles but the presence of armies. A chevauchée could burn villages, seize cattle and grain, destroy mills and vineyards, and take prisoners for ransom, and its effects persisted long after the army passed. In regions of repeated raiding, such as Languedoc, Normandy, and the Loire valley, peasants lost harvests and tools, and some abandoned their land. Many communities negotiated with captains for protection in exchange for payments, known in parts of southern France as appatis, a system that sometimes continued for years and that turned local lords and soldiers into collectors of tribute.

People responded in varied ways. Villagers fortified churches and built refuges, hid food and animals, or took shelter in walled towns. Towns, in turn, repaired and extended their walls, organized militias, and imposed taxes for their defense, burdens that fell heavily on inhabitants. Rural refugees crowded into towns, and in some regions large areas were depopulated. English-held areas saw systematic occupation: after the conquest of Normandy in 1417 to 1419, Henry V granted lands to English followers, required oaths of allegiance, and established an administration in which Norman officials served the English crown. Some Normans fled, others adapted, and many continued to work under whichever authority controlled their region.

England was not immune. French and Castilian raids in the 1330s, 1370s, and later burned parts of Southampton, Rye, Hastings, and other coastal places, and fear of invasion led to coastal defense, beacons, and levies. Civilians in England bore the cost of war through taxation, requisitions of food and shipping, and recruitment. Women ran estates and sometimes held castles in the absence of men, managed ransom negotiations, and, in Brittany and elsewhere, took part in the defense of towns. The war's burdens, and the violence between campaigns, are examined further in the next sections.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with War in a Norman or Gascon Village

A village near a main route might see soldiers every season: garrison troops requisitioning wagons, passing columns buying or seizing supplies, and routiers demanding payment for protection. Households chose between flight and endurance. Some hid grain in pits and drove cattle into woods, others took refuge in a fortified church or a nearby town. Seed corn, plow animals, and tools were vulnerable, and a single raid could put a household in debt for years. Records of letters of remission show peasants, artisans, and soldiers explaining acts of violence, theft, or killing, and they reveal how ordinary people lived in a world where armed men were a constant presence.

Markets and trade continued, though with risk. Merchants sought safe conducts, towns negotiated with captains, and rents and tithes went uncollected in devastated regions. Landlords reduced rents or lost tenants, and local communities sometimes won concessions in return for rebuilding. Women sold goods, ran households, and maintained fields when men were away or killed, and widows of soldiers and captains sometimes inherited ransom claims and properties. Religious institutions sheltered refugees, paid taxes and ransoms, and prayed for peace, and their records describe both destruction and continuity.

Section 51.11

Companies, Mercenaries, and Violence between Campaigns

When truces or treaties ended campaigns, soldiers lost their pay but not their weapons. After the Treaty of Brétigny in 1360, thousands of men, English, Gascon, Breton, German, and others, formed Free Companies, bands under experienced captains that seized castles and towns and lived by plunder and extortion. Contemporaries called them routiers, from a word for bands, and the so-called Great Companies ravaged Burgundy, Champagne, and the Rhône valley. In 1360, companies threatened the papal court at Avignon, and in April 1362 a company defeated a royal army at Brignais, a notable reminder that such bands could be formidable.

Rulers sought to remove them. Charles V's decision to send du Guesclin and the companies to Castile in 1365 was in part an attempt to relocate the problem, and similar bands found employment in Italy, where English captains such as John Hawkwood led mercenary companies in the service of Italian cities. These soldiers were not simply bandits, since they often served rulers under contract, but their activities overlapped with banditry, and the line between legitimate war and plunder was blurred. Many captains were noblemen, and their activities fed a ransom economy in which captured knights and rich townsmen were held for payment.

The problem recurred in the 1430s and 1440s, when unpaid soldiers, called écorcheurs or "flayers" because they stripped their victims, preyed on the French countryside after the Treaty of Arras in 1435 left many companies unemployed. Charles VII's military reforms of the 1440s, discussed in Section 51.17, were partly a response, seeking to bring soldiers under royal pay and discipline. The experience shows that war created a labor force of professional soldiers whose livelihood depended on conflict, and whose existence affected the options of rulers in both truce and war.

Section 51.12

Paying for War: Taxation, Credit, Parliament, and Administration

War was expensive, and financing it reshaped relations between rulers and their subjects. In England, the crown relied on a combination of revenues. Parliament granted lay subsidies, taxes on movable goods, at rates and times negotiated with the Commons, who increasingly used the occasion to present grievances, and customs duties on wool, which the crown negotiated with merchants and later with Parliament. The crown also borrowed from Italian bankers and from English merchants, though the failure of Florentine houses in the 1340s, noted in Chapter 50, made credit more difficult, and collected ransoms from captured enemies. By the 1370s, as costs rose and campaigns failed, discontent grew, and Parliament's 1376 session, the Good Parliament, attacked royal ministers. The poll taxes of the late 1370s, which contributed to the revolt of 1381, are treated in Chapter 52.

France built a different system. Kings summoned the Estates-General to grant taxes in emergencies, but also developed ways to tax without general consent. The aides, indirect taxes on sales, the gabelle, a tax on salt, and the fouage, a hearth tax, were introduced in the 1340s and extended as emergencies arose. The ransom of John II in 1360 led to the creation of permanent aides and a more developed financial administration, with officials called élus, general treasurers, and special courts. Charles V strengthened this machinery, though on his deathbed in 1380 he abolished the fouage. Many subjects interpreted the gesture as a broader abolition of the wartime tax regime, and attempts by Charles VI's regency to restore aides and other revenues contributed to the urban unrest of the early 1380s.

Regional assemblies mattered. In Languedoc, Normandy, and other provinces, local estates negotiated taxation with royal officers, and these negotiations gave provinces influence over the terms of royal revenue. Under Charles VII, a royal ordinance of 1439 asserted stronger royal control over recruitment, and the Estates consented to taxation for military reform. The crown subsequently continued to levy the taille, a direct tax, and it became effectively permanent during the 1440s, funding the new standing companies. Coinage manipulation, forced loans, and the sale of offices provided additional revenue. The combination of permanent taxation and a paid army significantly increased the capacity of the French crown, though it did so through negotiation, resistance, and compromise, and at the expense of provincial and corporate privileges.

These developments should not be read as a straightforward march toward centralization. English taxation depended on parliamentary consent and the cooperation of local elites. French taxation was uneven across provinces and often resisted. Princes with their own territories, such as the dukes of Burgundy, Brittany, and Anjou, competed for resources and influence, and in the early fifteenth century they diverted taxes to their own purposes. The war enlarged state capacity, but unevenly, and the institutions that emerged were products of bargaining among kings, assemblies, officials, and local communities.

Section 51.13

Royal Authority, Representation, and State Formation

The war affected the institutions of both monarchies, but in different ways, and not always in the direction of greater royal power. In England, the need for regular taxation made Parliament a more frequent and influential body. The Commons, representing knights of the shire and burgesses, developed the practice of linking grants of taxation to the redress of grievances, and by the later fourteenth century the Commons could initiate legislation by petition and impeach royal ministers. The crown, for its part, expanded the machinery of administration, in the Exchequer, Chancery, and Privy Seal, and relied on local gentry as justices of the peace and commissioners to carry out royal orders. War strengthened the working partnership between the crown and the landed elite, and it also exposed the king to criticism when campaigns failed.

In France, the situation was more complex. The crown possessed greater resources, but its authority was diffused among powerful princes, many of them royal kin who held great territories, called apanages, granted to cadet branches of the royal house. The Parlement of Paris and the Chambre des comptes provided continuity of justice and finance. The Estates-General, in contrast to the English Parliament, met irregularly, and the French crown learned to avoid it by negotiating with provincial estates and by establishing permanent taxes. The crisis after Poitiers showed the strength of demands for reform and the limits of the Estates' capacity to enforce them against a determined regent.

Legitimacy mattered intensely. French kings drew on sacral kingship, the anointing at Reims, the cult of Saint Denis, the royal banner called the oriflamme, and the Salic law, and they promoted an image of the king as defender of the realm. English kings asserted descent and conquest, and Henry V emphasized his divinely approved cause. The Lancastrian dynasty, which took power through deposition in 1399, had a particular need for legitimacy, which its continental war helped to provide until the war went wrong. Law, record keeping, and the growth of professional officials, notaries, and lawyers also developed in these years, in the service of both crowns.

Historians therefore caution against seeing the war as simply creating the modern state. Prolonged conflict encouraged the growth of taxation, administration, and military organization, but it also strengthened princes, assemblies, and local elites, produced revolts, and left lasting problems of debt and legitimacy. Some features that became characteristic of early modern states, permanent taxes, standing companies, and an administrative apparatus, were established in France in the 1430s to 1450s, but they were the product of particular circumstances, and their development is examined further in Chapter 74.

Section 51.14

Charles VI, Civil War, and the Burgundian-Anglo-French Crisis

Charles VI became king in 1380 at the age of eleven, and in the following years the realm was governed by his uncles, notably Philip the Bold, duke of Burgundy, and John, duke of Berry. The king assumed personal rule in 1388, but in August 1392 he suffered the first of a series of attacks of mental illness, which recurred for the rest of his life. The attacks removed the king from effective government for long periods, and the question of who should control the royal council, the royal revenues, and the king's family became the central political issue. The queen, Isabeau of Bavaria, the king's brother Louis of Orléans, and the dukes of Burgundy competed for influence. In 1394, in a measure unrelated to the war but significant for the history of Jewish communities, Charles ordered the expulsion of Jews from the kingdom.

Philip the Bold died in 1404 and was succeeded by John the Fearless, who disputed control of the government with Louis of Orléans. In November 1407, Louis was assassinated in Paris by agents of John, who publicly acknowledged responsibility and had his act defended in a theological justification of tyrannicide. Louis's heirs and allies, led by Bernard VII, count of Armagnac, formed a party that took his name, and the Armagnacs and Burgundians fought a civil war that divided the realm. Paris, where artisans and guilds supported Burgundy, experienced violent upheaval in 1413 in the Cabochien revolt, which forced the adoption of a reform ordinance that was soon abandoned.

Both factions sought English support, and the English crown, now under Henry IV and then Henry V, played them against one another. The English had not abandoned their claim, and the French civil war meant that the crown that Henry V invaded in 1415 was a realm without a united government. Burgundy appears in this chapter only to the extent needed to understand the war: the dukes' wider position as rulers of the Low Countries and a major northern European power is treated in Chapter 54. For the Hundred Years' War, the key fact is that a quarrel among French princes about control of an incapacitated king created an opening for English intervention, and that its resolution depended on the shifting alignments of Burgundy.

Section 51.15

Henry V, Agincourt, Normandy, and the Treaty of Troyes

Henry V, who became king in 1413, pursued the English claim with energy. After negotiations with the French court failed to produce satisfactory terms, he landed near Harfleur in August 1415 with a contracted army and besieged the town, which surrendered in September after a siege that cost him many men to dysentery. Rather than return directly to England, he marched toward Calais through Picardy. On 25 October 1415, near Agincourt, his tired and outnumbered army met a larger French force. The ground, narrow and muddy between woods, hampered the French cavalry and men-at-arms, who advanced in crowded ranks against English archers protected by stakes. The French suffered heavy losses, and many nobles were killed or captured, including Charles, duke of Orléans, who remained a prisoner in England for twenty-five years. Henry ordered the killing of a number of prisoners during the battle, an act that contemporaries and later historians have debated.

Agincourt was a great victory, but it did not by itself conquer France. It was the beginning of a systematic campaign. From 1417, Henry invaded Normandy with a large army, took Caen, and besieged and captured fortified towns one by one, using artillery and, at Rouen, a long blockade from the summer of 1418 to January 1419. The Burgundian faction, which had been partly neutral, was drawn into negotiations, and in September 1419, John the Fearless was murdered at Montereau during a meeting with the Dauphin Charles, an act that pushed the Burgundians toward alliance with England. His son, Philip the Good, joined Henry.

The result was the Treaty of Troyes, concluded in May 1420 by Henry, Charles VI, and Philip the Good. It recognized Henry as regent of France and heir to the crown, disinherited the Dauphin, and provided for Henry's marriage to Charles VI's daughter Catherine of Valois, which took place in June. The treaty envisaged a dual monarchy in which the crowns of England and France would be united in one person but the laws and institutions of each kingdom would remain distinct. Henry died in August 1422, shortly before Charles VI, and the infant Henry VI inherited both claims, with his uncle John, duke of Bedford, as regent in France. At Verneuil in 1424, Bedford defeated a Franco-Scottish army, and English-Burgundian rule extended over Normandy, Paris, and much of the north.

Multiple Perspectives

The Treaty of Troyes: Differing Views

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns found in many sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and these groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

From the perspective of the Burgundian party and many Parisians: After the murder of John the Fearless, the treaty offered the prospect of peace, revenge on the Dauphin's party, and stable government. Many Paris officials, university masters, and merchants supported it as the best available settlement, even if it meant accepting an English king.
From the perspective of the Dauphin Charles and his Armagnac supporters: The treaty was an illegal disinheritance and a betrayal by a mad king under foreign and faction pressure. Charles continued to claim the throne and to govern territories south of the Loire, and his party denied the validity of the treaty by appeal to the fundamental laws of the realm.
From the perspective of an English soldier-settler or official in Normandy: The conquest offered land, office, and employment, and the treaty seemed to confirm the lawfulness of Henry's cause. English garrisons and colonists in Normandy depended on the survival of the settlement, and had a stake in its defense.
From the perspective of a Norman villager or townsman: Allegiance was often a matter of survival. Oaths were sworn to the authority in control, taxes were paid to whoever collected them, and loyalty to a dynasty or cause might count for less than protection against raiders and requisitioning.

Section 51.16

Joan of Arc, Orléans, Reims, and the Crisis of Legitimacy

By 1428, English and Burgundian forces controlled most of northern France, and the Dauphin, who styled himself king Charles VII after his father's death in 1422 but had not been crowned, ruled from the Loire. The English began the siege of Orléans in October 1428, aiming to open the way to the south. The city, defended by the count of Dunois and the local garrison and citizens, held out through the winter. In early 1429, a young woman from the village of Domrémy in Lorraine, Joan, who said she had been commanded by saints' voices to relieve Orléans and have the Dauphin crowned, came to the court at Chinon. Church theologians examined her at Poitiers and found nothing contrary to the faith, and she was given a place with the army.

Joan reached Orléans in late April 1429, and on 8 May the siege was lifted. The relief resulted from the work of experienced captains, the arrival of reinforcements and supplies, and the effect of her presence on the morale of the defenders and the English. In the following weeks, French forces captured towns along the Loire and defeated an English force at Patay in June. Charles was escorted through Burgundian-held territory to Reims, where he was anointed and crowned on 17 July 1429, with Joan at his side. The coronation mattered, since the anointing at Reims was central to French royal legitimacy, and it countered the claim that Charles was the Dauphin of a disinherited line.

Joan's later career was less successful. A failed attack on Paris in September 1429 weakened her position, and in May 1430 she was captured at Compiègne by Burgundian forces, who sold her to the English. She was tried at Rouen by an ecclesiastical court headed by Pierre Cauchon, bishop of Beauvais, a supporter of the English cause, on charges that included heresy and wearing men's clothing. Condemned as a relapsed heretic, she was burned on 30 May 1431. Charles VII did not intervene. In 1455 to 1456, a church inquiry examined the trial and annulled the verdict, and she was canonized in 1920. Her trial and rehabilitation records are among the fullest sources for any medieval woman.

Historians avoid two extremes. Joan did not single-handedly win the war, and French recovery depended on military reform, Burgundian diplomacy, and English difficulties. But she was not incidental. Her appearance, in a crisis of morale and legitimacy, provided a religious and symbolic focus for the French cause, helped to make possible the coronation, and gave writers, such as Christine de Pizan in a poem of July 1429, a powerful image. Henry VI was crowned king of France at Paris in December 1431, an attempt to answer the legitimacy advantage Charles VII had gained at Reims. Joan's execution did not reverse that advantage, although her later memory would greatly magnify her symbolic importance.

Section 51.17

Charles VII, Military Reform, and the French Reconquest, 1430s–1453

The decisive diplomatic event was the Congress and Treaty of Arras in September 1435, in which Philip the Good of Burgundy abandoned the English alliance and recognized Charles VII as king, in return for concessions and the dismissal of responsibility for the murder of his father. The English regent Bedford died the same month, and Paris returned to Charles VII in April 1436. The English crown, under the young and ineffective Henry VI, was increasingly divided and short of money, and its hold on Normandy depended on garrisons that were costly to maintain.

Charles VII used the following years to reorganize his military and finances. The ordinance of 1439 asserted royal control over recruitment, and the taille, continued thereafter, became effectively permanent during the 1440s. A truce at Tours in 1444 allowed him to disband or discipline the roving companies, and in 1445 he established the compagnies d'ordonnance, a permanent royal military establishment of paid, regularly inspected companies organized around men-at-arms and their supporting lances. In 1448 he introduced the francs-archers, local levies of archers drawn from parishes. The financier Jacques Coeur and others supplied credit. The brothers Jean and Gaspard Bureau created a professional artillery train. These measures did not create a modern army, but they gave the French crown a core of permanent forces and a means of paying for them.

In March 1449 François de Surienne, called l'Aragonais, a mercenary captain in English service, seized Fougères in Brittany while the truce was in force. Charles VII treated the seizure, and the English failure to restore the town and make satisfactory reparation, as a breach of the truce and a justification for reopening the war. Within a year, Rouen and much of Normandy fell, and on 15 April 1450 a French army defeated an English relief force at Formigny, and Caen and Cherbourg surrendered by August. In 1451 French armies took Bordeaux and Bayonne. When the Gascons, who had long ties to England, welcomed an English expedition under John Talbot in 1452, the French returned. At Castillon, on 17 July 1453, Talbot attacked a fortified camp defended by French cannon and was killed, and the English army was routed. Bordeaux surrendered on 19 October 1453.

The French reconquest was not inevitable. It followed from Burgundy's defection, from the weakness of Henry VI's government, from the French crown's new fiscal and military capacity, and from effective use of artillery against English garrisons and field forces. It did not result from gunpowder alone, nor was English defeat in Normandy and Gascony a sign that the war had been lost from the beginning. As late as 1429, the English held most of the north. Castillon is nonetheless treated as the effective military end, though no peace followed: English kings continued to claim the French crown, and a truce in 1475 at Picquigny between Edward IV and Louis XI ended an invasion without a formal peace.

Section 51.18

War, Political Identity, and the Meaning of 1453

The war sharpened collective identities, but not uniformly. English kings and writers increasingly cast the conflict as a national struggle, promoted Saint George, whose feast Henry V raised in status, and used English in official correspondence, as Henry V did in letters from the late 1410s, so that English gradually gained standing as a language of administration alongside French and Latin. Parliament voiced hostility to foreigners and to French religious houses in England. In France, writers such as Alain Chartier and Christine de Pizan lamented the realm's divisions and appealed to loyalty to the king and the French people, and royal symbolism, Saint Denis, and the idea of the kingdom as a sacred community, gained prominence.

Yet loyalty remained layered. Many Gascons identified with the English crown, which protected their trade and privileges, and resisted French rule in 1451 and 1452. Burgundians and Armagnacs fought as Frenchmen, and Burgundian writers presented their duke as a defender of the realm. Normans, Bretons, Flemings, and Parisians weighed local interests, lordship, and security alongside royal allegiance. "French" and "English" were meaningful labels in propaganda and, by the fifteenth century, in popular speech, but they did not yet amount to the mass nationalism of the nineteenth century, a distinction discussed in the Historical Debate below.

The war changed monarchy, warfare, taxation, and territorial control. The English crown lost its continental empire except for Calais, shifted attention toward domestic politics, and entered a period of dynastic conflict. The French crown recovered its principal lands, acquired permanent taxes and a standing military core, and moved toward a stronger monarchy, though it still contended with princes such as the dukes of Burgundy and Brittany. Armies were more professional, artillery more important, and fortification more elaborate. Large areas of France had been devastated, and recovery from war damage and plague took generations. What did not change was the continued importance of lordship, the church, local privilege, and negotiation. The war did not produce a modern France or England, and the outcomes of 1453 were not predetermined by earlier events.

Historical Debate

Did the Hundred Years' War Create National Identity?

The question: Did the war give rise to a durable sense of English and French nationhood, or did loyalties remain primarily dynastic, regional, and local?

Interpretation A: The war accelerated national consciousness. Some historians argue that wartime propaganda, taxation that touched ordinary people, hostility toward the enemy, the growing use of vernacular languages, and symbols such as Saint George and Joan of Arc fostered a sense of belonging to a political community defined against an enemy. On this view, the war was a formative stage in the history of national identities.
Interpretation B: Loyalties remained dynastic, regional, and local. Other historians emphasize that most people's allegiance was to lords, cities, regions, and dynasties, that Gascons, Burgundians, and others shifted sides when their interests dictated, and that the language of "nation" in sources often meant a regional or corporate group. They caution that reading later nationalism into this period mistakes a political language of kingship and community for mass national feeling.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on chronicles, political poetry, parliamentary petitions, royal letters, and the behavior of towns and elites. They differ in how far they treat elite and propaganda sources as representative of broader populations, and in how they define a nation.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians understand the origins of European national identities and warns against treating later national histories as the natural endpoint of medieval conflicts.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Monarchy, War, and Taxation, 1337 to 1453

What Changed

  • Armies moved from feudal levies toward contracted and paid forces, and in France toward permanent companies
  • Taxation became more regular, with permanent aides, gabelle, and taille in France and parliamentary subsidies in England
  • Artillery and fortification altered siege warfare
  • The English crown lost nearly all its continental territory, and the French crown recovered it

What Stayed the Same

  • Kingship remained dynastic and sacral, dependent on legitimacy and counsel
  • Lordship, local privilege, and negotiation with assemblies continued to shape government
  • Agriculture and local markets remained the basis of most people's lives
  • English kings kept the title king of France

Who experienced change most directly? Villagers and townspeople in zones of campaign and occupation, taxpayers in France and England, and soldiers whose livelihoods depended on war experienced the most direct change; nobles, officials, and rulers experienced both new opportunities and new constraints. Why the pattern matters: It shows that war enlarged state capacity unevenly and at heavy social cost.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Hundred Years' War as a series of conflicts between 1337 and 1453, linked by recurring issues and by institutions that carried over from one phase to the next. The label is a modern convention. Contemporaries experienced campaigns, truces, civil wars, and local conflicts, and the conventional dates mark the confiscation of Gascony and the fall of Bordeaux, not a clean beginning and end.

The war arose from a dense combination of causes. The feudal subordination of the kings of England to the kings of France for Gascony created persistent disputes over sovereignty and appeals. The failure of the Capetian male line in 1328 gave Edward III a claim to the French throne, which he asserted in 1340. Commercial ties with Flanders, the Franco-Scottish alliance, and maritime rivalry added to the tension. The dynastic claim was an instrument as much as a cause.

The first phase brought English successes at Sluys, Crécy, and Poitiers and the capture of Calais, and then the capture of John II of France. English victory resulted from tactical discipline, defensive positions, the combination of dismounted men-at-arms and archers, and French disorder, and not from any simple replacement of cavalry by infantry. Poitiers produced a political crisis in France, with the Estates-General, Étienne Marcel, and the Jacquerie, and it led to the Treaty of Brétigny, whose incomplete renunciations left the dispute open.

Charles V and Bertrand du Guesclin reversed the English advantage in the 1370s through a combination of avoiding pitched battle, besieging strongpoints, exploiting appeals from Gascon lords, and fiscal consolidation. The long truces that followed did not end the underlying issues, and the incapacity of Charles VI opened a struggle among French princes. The assassinations of Louis of Orléans and John the Fearless produced civil war between Armagnacs and Burgundians and created the opening for Henry V.

Henry V's victory at Agincourt and conquest of Normandy, followed by the Treaty of Troyes, created a dual monarchy that disinherited the Dauphin. Its viability depended on the Burgundian alliance and military control of the north. The appearance of Joan of Arc and the coronation of Charles VII at Reims revived French legitimacy, though her career and execution show the force of political and religious conflict, and French recovery depended on far more than any single individual.

The war transformed armies and finance. English kings relied on indentures and parliamentary subsidies, and French kings developed permanent taxes, including aides, gabelle, and taille, and, under Charles VII, standing companies and an artillery train. Gunpowder artillery contributed to the reconquest of Normandy and Gascony, but its significance was cumulative and came alongside political and fiscal change. Prolonged war enlarged state capacity unevenly, through bargaining with assemblies and local communities, and also strengthened princes and provoked resistance.

Civilians bore much of the cost. Chevauchées, sieges, occupation, requisitioning, and the violence of Free Companies and écorcheurs destroyed crops and settlements, forced migration, and created systems of tribute and ransom. Civilians were not passive: they fortified churches, built walls, negotiated protection, and shifted allegiance for survival. Letters of remission and municipal records allow historians to recover some of this experience.

The war also shaped political identity. Propaganda, vernacular language, patron saints, and symbols such as Joan of Arc sharpened loyalties, but allegiance remained layered among dynasty, lord, city, and region, as the Gascons and Burgundians show. Historians debate how far the war created national identity, and the chapter has urged caution about reading modern nationalism into the fifteenth century.

The outcome of 1453 was contingent. The French recovery depended on Burgundy's defection at Arras, English political weakness, French reforms, and effective use of artillery. The war left France with a stronger crown and England without its continental empire except Calais, but it did not produce modern states or end the conflict by treaty, and English kings kept the French royal title. The consequences of the war's fiscal and military developments are taken up again in Chapter 74.

Key Terms

Hundred Years' War
The modern label for the Anglo-French conflicts of 1337 to 1453, comprising campaigns, truces, and civil wars and not a single continuous war.
Homage
The ceremony by which a vassal acknowledged obligations to a lord, here central to the English kings' relationship to the French crown for Gascony.
Sovereignty
Ultimate legal authority, the issue at stake in French claims to hear appeals and confiscate Aquitaine.
Salic law
A Frankish code later cited by French jurists to exclude women from transmitting the crown.
Gascony
The southwestern part of Aquitaine held by English kings, whose wine trade and privileges tied it to England.
Chevauchée
A large mounted raid designed to destroy resources, collect plunder, and undermine an enemy ruler's authority.
Men-at-arms
Heavily armored professional or noble soldiers who fought mounted or, increasingly, on foot.
Longbow
A tall bow used by English and Welsh archers, effective in volleys when combined with defensive positions and disciplined infantry.
Indenture
A written contract between the crown and a captain to supply paid troops for an agreed period.
Retinue
The following of men that a lord or captain brought to a campaign, often drawn from his tenants and dependents.
Arrière-ban
The French king's general summons of all men owing military service, by this period a limited and unreliable instrument.
Free Company
A band of soldiers led by a captain who lived by plunder, extortion, and contract between campaigns.
Routiers
A term for members of roving armed bands, especially in France after 1360.
Écorcheurs
"Flayers," the name given to unpaid soldiers who preyed on the French countryside in the 1430s and 1440s.
Ransom
A payment for the release of a captive, a major source of income for captains and a burden on prisoners and their families.
Appatis
Payments by communities to soldiers or captains in exchange for protection from plunder.
Lay subsidy
An English parliamentary tax on movable goods, granted for royal needs and often tied to petitions.
Aides
French taxes, usually indirect, levied on sales and goods; made permanent after the ransom of John II.
Gabelle
A royal tax on salt in France, an important source of revenue.
Fouage
A French hearth tax assessed on households.
Taille
A French direct tax, effectively permanent from the 1440s under Charles VII and used to finance the standing companies.
Estates-General
The French assembly of clergy, nobles, and townspeople, summoned irregularly for consent to taxes and counsel.
Apanage
A territory granted to a younger son or kinsman of a French king, the basis of princely power.
Armagnacs and Burgundians
The rival French factions of the early fifteenth century, led by the supporters of Louis of Orléans and the dukes of Burgundy.
Dual monarchy
The arrangement of the Treaty of Troyes uniting the English and French crowns in one person while preserving separate laws and institutions.
Letter of remission
A French royal pardon, whose text records a petitioner's account of a violent incident and is valuable for social history.
Compagnies d'ordonnance
The standing companies of paid men-at-arms and archers established by Charles VII in 1445.
Francs-archers
A French levy of archers drawn from parishes, introduced in 1448.
Truce
A temporary suspension of hostilities, used repeatedly in the war without ending its underlying issues.
Oriflamme
The sacred royal banner of the French kings, associated with the abbey of Saint-Denis.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a dispute rooted in feudal obligation and royal succession became a long struggle that changed armies, taxation, and the relationship between kings and their subjects. It also shows the limits of such change. War enlarged what rulers could demand, but it did so through bargaining, resistance, and compromise, and it strengthened princes and local elites as well as crowns.

The chapter links directly to its neighbors in Part VIII. The plague and famine of Chapter 50 shaped the demographic and fiscal context in which the war was fought, though they did not directly cause its developments. The labor, wages, and revolts of Chapter 52, the divisions of the church in Chapter 53, and the role of Burgundy and other powers in Chapter 54 complete a picture of a Europe under strain and in transformation.

For Volume II, the war illustrates how medieval monarchies reached the limits of their inherited institutions and began to construct new ones, from standing companies to permanent taxes. Those developments, together with the artillery and print revolutions of Chapter 55, prepare the way for the early modern states of Volume III, though nothing in the war made those states inevitable.

Finally, the chapter invites caution about memory. Joan of Arc, Agincourt, and the idea of a hundred-year struggle between England and France have been used by later generations to tell national stories. Historians' task is to recover the contingency, the divided loyalties, and the human costs that those stories can conceal. That habit of mind will serve students well in the chapters that follow.
