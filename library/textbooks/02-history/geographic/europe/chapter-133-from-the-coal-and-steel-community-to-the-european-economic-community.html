Chapter Opening

On May 9, 1950, in the Salon de l'Horloge of the French Foreign Ministry in Paris, Robert Schuman read a short statement proposing that French and German coal and steel production be placed under a common authority. It was a proposal, not a treaty, and few of those present could have known that it would become the starting point of the most durable institutional experiment in modern European history.

Five years earlier, much of Europe lay in ruins. Cities had been bombed, railways and bridges destroyed, industrial plants dismantled or exhausted, and millions of people displaced, imprisoned, or killed, as examined in Chapter 123. The same coal basins and steelworks of the Ruhr, Lorraine, the Saar, Belgium, and Luxembourg that had supplied the armaments of two world wars were now the physical foundation on which any European recovery would have to be rebuilt. Whoever controlled them, and on what terms, was a question of economics and of security at once.

This chapter studies how a group of western European governments answered that question not by occupation, punishment, or a restored balance of power, but by creating institutions in which they agreed to share authority over part of their economic life. It follows that decision from the Schuman Declaration through the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) established in 1951, the failed attempt to build a European Defence Community in 1954, the Treaties of Rome signed on March 25, 1957, and the construction of a customs union, an agricultural policy, and a body of Community law. It then traces the first enlargements of 1973, 1981, and 1986, the creation of the European Council and a directly elected European Parliament, and the Single European Act of 1986 and 1987, which bridges to Chapter 134.

The chapter advances a specific argument. European integration was neither the inevitable expression of a European unity that had always existed beneath the surface, nor merely a technical project of economic management. It resulted from deliberate choices made by particular governments under particular pressures: fear of renewed German power, the need for German recovery, dependence on American protection, and competing visions of what Europe should become. Progress was uneven. The ECSC succeeded where a European Defence Community failed. A common market was built, but only because France and Germany struck bargains over agriculture and overseas territories. Supranational institutions grew, but a French president forced a crisis in 1965 over how far their authority should extend. Britain stayed out, then was kept out, then joined.

Precision about names matters throughout. The European Union did not exist before the Maastricht Treaty entered into force in 1993, and the ECSC, the European Economic Community (EEC), and the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom) were three legally distinct Communities that gradually shared institutions and came to be called collectively the European Communities. These bodies must also be distinguished from the Council of Europe, from NATO, and from the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation, none of which was an organ of integration in the sense developed here. The chapter uses each term in the sense appropriate to its period, and students should resist reading the institutional shape of the twenty-first century back into 1950 or 1957.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and unusually official. Treaties, Council and Commission records, parliamentary debates, memoirs by Jean Monnet, Konrad Adenauer, and Paul-Henri Spaak, diplomatic papers, Court of Justice judgments, and economic statistics allow close reconstruction of decisions. Yet these sources largely record the viewpoint of ministers, officials, lawyers, and negotiators. Steelworkers, miners, farmers, consumers, and voters appear in them mainly as categories, and the memoirs of founders were written with an eye on how the story would be remembered. Part of the historian's task is to separate what happened from the tidier narrative of European unity that later generations constructed.

The chapter belongs to Part XX and to Volume VI. Chapter 132 closed Part XIX with the end of the Cold War; this chapter deliberately returns to 1945 because integration has a longer history than the revolutions of 1989, and because the story of the Communities runs alongside the Cold War division of Europe, not after it. Chapter 134 will take up the Maastricht Treaty, the European Union, and the euro, and Chapter 136 will address the integration of postcommunist Europe. The present chapter establishes what those later developments inherited, and what they did not.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why economic recovery, the German question, and security dependence on the United States made institutional cooperation plausible in western Europe after 1945.
  2. Analyze the strategic and economic significance of coal, steel, and the Ruhr for Franco-German relations.
  3. Distinguish among the Council of Europe, the OEEC, NATO, the ECSC, the EEC, Euratom, and the later European Union.
  4. Trace the path from the Schuman Declaration of May 9, 1950, through the Treaty of Paris of 1951, to the operation of the ECSC.
  5. Define supranationalism and assess how far the ECSC and EEC institutions embodied it.
  6. Evaluate why a European Defence Community failed in 1954 while economic integration revived at Messina in 1955.
  7. Describe the content of the Treaties of Rome, the institutional balance among Commission, Council, Assembly, and Court, and the doctrines of direct effect and primacy.
  8. Explain the development of the customs union and the Common Agricultural Policy, including their distributional consequences.
  9. Assess the Empty Chair Crisis and the Luxembourg Compromise as an episode in the tension between common decision-making and national sovereignty.
  10. Compare the motives of Britain, Ireland, Denmark, and Norway regarding membership, and explain why enlargement was contested and contingent.
  11. Interpret primary documents of integration with attention to purpose, audience, and limitation.
  12. Connect the developments of 1945 to 1987 to the Single European Act while avoiding the assumption that the European Union was the predetermined outcome.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did integration become politically plausible after 1945 when earlier plans for European cooperation, including those of the interwar years, had failed?
  2. How did the problem of German recovery shape the design of the first European institutions?
  3. In what senses were the early Communities supranational, and in what senses did national governments retain control?
  4. Why did the economic project advance while the proposed defense and political communities collapsed?
  5. How did agriculture, overseas territories, and domestic politics shape the bargains that made the Treaties of Rome possible?
  6. What roles did courts, lawyers, and officials play in extending the effects of integration beyond what treaty negotiators explicitly stated?
  7. Why was Britain absent at the founding, and what does its delayed membership reveal about competing models of postwar Europe?
  8. How should historians weigh the memoirs and public statements of the "founding fathers" against documentary evidence of how decisions were actually made?
  9. Did enlargement and institutional growth between 1973 and 1987 strengthen the Communities, expose their weaknesses, or both?

Historical Context

Ideas of European union long predated 1945. Medieval writers invoked Christendom as a community of rulers, early modern thinkers proposed leagues and perpetual peace, and the nineteenth century produced the Concert of Europe, customs unions such as the German Zollverein, and a variety of federalist and pacifist projects. In the interwar period, Count Richard Coudenhove-Kalergi's Pan-Europe movement and Aristide Briand's 1929 and 1930 proposals for a European federal bond attracted attention but foundered on the Great Depression, German revisionism, and the unwillingness of governments to surrender sovereignty. Those earlier ideas matter as an intellectual inheritance. They do not show that the Communities were bound to appear, and the institutions of the 1950s owed more to the immediate experience of war, occupation, and economic planning than to older blueprints.

The war itself supplied the decisive background. Nazi Germany had occupied most of the continent, exploited its industries, and carried out genocide, as examined in Part XVIII. After 1945 the continent's remaining great powers were not European: the United States and the Soviet Union dominated the new international order, and the division of Germany and of Europe, traced in Chapters 124 and 125, shaped every political calculation. Western European governments depended on American economic assistance under the Marshall Plan, examined in Chapter 126, and on American military commitment through NATO. Within that frame, France, Italy, the Benelux states, and the Federal Republic of Germany faced the task of reconstructing economies, welfare provision, and legitimacy in democratic form.

Postwar governments also inherited experience in economic planning and in international administration. French planning under Jean Monnet's 1946 modernization plan sought coal for French steel; wartime and postwar bodies allocated scarce resources across borders; and the Benelux states had formed a customs arrangement that came into operation in 1948. These experiences normalized the idea that economic questions could be managed by joint institutions, a precondition for the more ambitious step of 1950.

Students should also keep in mind the limits of the story told here. This chapter concerns western Europe, and in its first decades the Communities included only six of the continent's states. Eastern Europe, under Soviet domination, took no part, and several western and northern European states chose different paths, including through the European Free Trade Association. The Council of Europe, the OEEC, and NATO involved wider memberships than the Six. When historians speak of "European integration" in this period, they refer to one specific model of integration among several that were proposed, and the one that eventually proved most institutionally dynamic.

The historical record of these decisions is largely documentary. Treaty texts, which survive in authenticated official versions, show what governments formally agreed. Records of the Council, Commission, and Court show how institutions operated. National cabinet papers, parliamentary debates, and diplomatic correspondence show the pressures behind decisions. Memoirs, especially those of Monnet, Adenauer, Spaak, and de Gaulle, offer insight but also self-presentation. Statistical series on trade, production, and agricultural prices permit measurement, though they must be used with care because definitions and classifications changed. Historians have increasingly drawn on archives opened since the 1980s to test earlier accounts, including the founders' own, and the result has been a more complicated picture in which economic interest and national strategy appear alongside idealism.

From European Destruction to the Idea of Integration

Sections 133.1–133.4 · Principal period: 1945–1950
Section 133.1

Europe after 1945: Reconstruction, Security, and the Search for a New Order

The scale of material and human loss in 1945 shaped every postwar political calculation, and Chapter 123 examines it in detail. For the purposes of this chapter, three consequences deserve emphasis. First, industrial production across much of the continent was disrupted: transport networks were damaged, housing was destroyed, fuel and food were scarce, and trade among European economies had collapsed into a patchwork of bilateral arrangements, controls, and shortages. Second, population movements, forced labor, expulsions, and the return of prisoners and survivors created social pressures that governments struggled to manage. Third, the political order that had presided over the catastrophe, including aggressive nationalism, had been discredited, even if nationalism itself as a force in popular life had not disappeared.

Out of these circumstances emerged a conviction, held with varying intensity by different politicians, that economic recovery and the prevention of another war were linked. Industrial output required coal, and coal required cooperation across frontiers. Reconstruction in Germany, where occupying powers disagreed about reparations and the future of heavy industry, was inseparable from reconstruction elsewhere. Governments that had once treated trade as a tool of national strength increasingly saw barriers, quotas, and rivalry over key resources as obstacles to recovery.

The postwar international system also limited what European states could do alone. The United States and the Soviet Union possessed military and economic power on a scale no European state could match, and the emerging Cold War, examined in Chapters 124 and 125, forced western Europeans to choose between dependence and uncertainty. For France in particular, which had been defeated and occupied in 1940, security required both American protection and a means of ensuring that German power would not again threaten it. For Germany, the question was how to return to the community of nations after the crimes of Nazism. For Britain, a victor with a global empire and a "special relationship" with the United States, the answer to such questions seemed less urgent and took a different form.

Different motives, different projects

It would be a mistake to treat those who favored cooperation as a single movement. Some, such as Italian federalist Altiero Spinelli, whose Ventotene Manifesto had been written during wartime confinement, and many members of resistance and Christian Democratic circles, believed that the nation-state had proven incapable of preventing catastrophe and should be superseded by a European federation. Others, including many officials and economic ministers, were pragmatists who wanted efficient markets and secure supplies. Still others, particularly in French foreign policy, were primarily concerned with security and saw institutions as a means of containing German power. These aims overlapped without being identical, and the history of integration is partly a history of how they were reconciled in specific compromises.

Why, then, did integration become plausible after 1945 when interwar proposals had failed? Historians generally point to a convergence of conditions. The scale of the catastrophe had undermined confidence in traditional diplomacy. American policy, through the Marshall Plan, encouraged European cooperation as a condition of economic assistance. The Soviet threat, real or perceived, gave western governments a shared strategic interest. And a generation of politicians, including Christian Democrats in Germany, Italy, and France, had personal experience of fascism or occupation and were ready to accept new institutional forms. Chapters 125 and 126 supply details of the Cold War alliance structure and of Marshall aid; the point here is that these conditions created an opportunity, not a guarantee. The Schuman Plan was one of several possible responses, and its adoption depended on specific people at a specific moment.

Section 133.2

Franco-German Reconciliation and the Problem of Coal, Steel, and the Ruhr

The central problem facing postwar western Europe was how to prevent a renewal of German aggression while allowing German economic recovery. These aims pulled in opposite directions. A permanently weakened Germany would be a drag on European prosperity and a source of bitterness; a recovered Germany might again threaten its neighbors. French leaders in particular had lived through the invasions of 1870, 1914, and 1940, and they approached the question with a deep concern for security and a long memory of how German industrial strength had translated into military power.

The Ruhr and the logic of heavy industry

The Ruhr, a coal-rich industrial region in western Germany, had been the center of German steel and armaments production. The link between coal, steel, and military power was concrete: steel was the material of tanks, guns, ships, and shells, and coking coal was needed to make it. Control of these industries therefore seemed to French planners to be control over the capacity to wage war. France, for its part, lacked sufficient coking coal for its own steel industry, and French reconstruction plans depended on obtaining coal from the Ruhr and from the Saar. French security and French economic need thus pointed in the same direction, toward some kind of arrangement that guaranteed access to German coal and limited German industrial independence.

Between 1945 and 1949 the Allies experimented with different approaches. They dismantled some plants, limited production levels, and debated the political status of the Ruhr. In April 1949 the Ruhr Statute created an International Authority for the Ruhr, in which the occupying powers and the Benelux countries supervised the allocation of coal, coke, and steel. The Federal Republic of Germany, established in 1949 with Konrad Adenauer as its first chancellor, resented this control as discriminatory. At the Petersberg Agreement of November 1949, Adenauer secured a reduction in dismantling in exchange for German participation in the Authority, a sign of the limited leverage the new state possessed and of Adenauer's willingness to accept limits in exchange for gradual equality.

By 1950 this approach looked unsustainable. Occupation controls could not last indefinitely; the Cold War, and soon the Korean War, increased the importance of German industrial output; and the United States pressed for German recovery as a part of western defense. If France continued to rely on controls imposed from outside, it risked a resentful Germany and growing American impatience. The French problem was therefore to find a new framework that could preserve security and access to resources without relying on permanent coercion.

Adenauer and the choice for the West

Adenauer, a Rhineland Catholic and former mayor of Cologne, concluded that the Federal Republic's interest lay in firm integration with the West. A reliable western alignment would restore German sovereignty step by step, win trust, and protect the new state from Soviet pressure. This choice had domestic opponents, including Social Democrats who feared that it would deepen the division of Germany, and its long-term success was far from certain in 1950. Still, it provided French planners with a partner willing to accept shared institutions. The Saar question, over which France and Germany disagreed, remained a thorn: France had detached the Saar economically from Germany, and the dispute was only resolved after a 1955 referendum rejected a proposed European statute for the region and the territory returned to West Germany on January 1, 1957.

The significance of what followed lay in its departure from existing practice. The traditional means of dealing with a dangerous neighbor, whether occupation, punitive restriction, or alliance directed against it, had been tried after 1918 and judged a failure by most observers. A proposal to share authority over strategic industries, with Germany as a full participant rather than a controlled object, was a different kind of solution. It rested on the premise that interdependence could make aggression costly and discourage the suspicions that drove rivalry, and that the way to achieve interdependence was through shared institutions with their own powers.

Section 133.3

Competing Visions of European Cooperation: Federalism, Cooperation among States, and Atlanticism

Between 1945 and 1950, European cooperation took several institutional forms, and the proposals of these years show how many roads were open. The Congress of Europe, held at The Hague in May 1948, brought together hundreds of politicians, intellectuals, and activists from across western Europe, including Winston Churchill, who had called in 1946 for a "kind of United States of Europe" but who envisioned Britain as an associate rather than a member. The Congress yielded resolutions calling for economic and political union, a charter of human rights, and a European assembly, and it led to the creation of the European Movement. But participants split sharply. Federalists wanted a constituent assembly and real powers for common institutions. British and Scandinavian delegates favored cooperation among governments, with no transfer of sovereignty.

The Council of Europe

The compromise was the Council of Europe, established by the Treaty of London signed on May 5, 1949, by ten states and based in Strasbourg. It had a Committee of Ministers composed of national governments and a Consultative Assembly of national parliamentarians. Its purposes were broad, including the protection of democracy, the rule of law, and human rights, and it produced in 1950 the European Convention on Human Rights. But its decisions bound states only with their consent, and federalists were disappointed. The Council of Europe is not an organ of the European Communities, and its membership, which expanded over time to include many states beyond the Six, had no connection to the institutional path traced in this chapter. Confusion between it, the Council of Ministers, and the later European Council is common, and students should keep them apart.

Economic cooperation: OEEC and Benelux

The Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC), established in April 1948 by sixteen western European states, coordinated the distribution of Marshall Plan assistance. It encouraged member governments to liberalize trade and payments among themselves, and it accustomed national officials to working together on shared problems. But it operated by consensus and had no independent authority over members. It was thus a vehicle of intergovernmental cooperation and an important training ground, not a supranational body. Its later transformation into the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development in 1961 lies beyond this chapter.

The Benelux countries, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, offered a smaller-scale model of integration. Their governments in exile had agreed during the war to form a customs union, which began to function in 1948 and later developed into an economic union. Their experience demonstrated that neighboring states with differing economic structures could remove tariffs and coordinate policy, and Benelux officials, notably the Belgian Paul-Henri Spaak and the Dutch Johan Willem Beyen, would play a significant part in later initiatives. Their small size also gave them an interest in rule-bound cooperation as protection against domination by larger neighbors.

Atlantic security

Finally, European political cooperation must be distinguished from the Atlantic security structure. NATO, established by the North Atlantic Treaty of April 4, 1949, bound the United States, Canada, and several European states in a collective defense commitment against the Soviet Union, as discussed in Chapter 125. It was not an instrument of European unification, though it shaped the context in which integration advanced, because it offered western Europeans a security guarantee that made it easier to pool economic functions. Atlanticists, particularly in Britain and in parts of the Scandinavian and Dutch political classes, preferred to anchor European security to American power and viewed distinctive European institutions with caution. Others, including some French and German politicians, saw integration as a way to give Europe a stronger voice within the Atlantic partnership.

These various organizations differed in membership, purposes, and powers, and the differences shaped what came next. As of 1950, federalist hopes had been largely disappointed. The institutions that existed relied on national governments and could not compel action. If there were to be a more ambitious step, it would have to come from a different direction, from a limited, concrete proposal with real powers rather than a general constitutional design.

Section 133.4

Jean Monnet, Robert Schuman, and the Declaration of May 9, 1950

The Schuman Plan emerged in the spring of 1950 from the work of Jean Monnet, then head of the French planning commission. Monnet, a cognac merchant's son who had worked in international finance and wartime supply coordination, was neither a politician nor a diplomat by training. He believed that national rivalries could be overcome by creating institutions that obliged states to solve problems jointly, and he judged that the German question could not be left to occupation controls. With a small team, he drafted a proposal to place the whole of French and German coal and steel production under a common High Authority, within an organization open to other European countries.

Monnet took the plan to Robert Schuman, the French foreign minister, a Lorraine politician of Luxembourg birth who had been educated in the German system and had direct experience of the borderland between France and Germany. Schuman, a Christian Democrat who had already shown interest in Franco-German understanding, agreed to present it. Adenauer was informed before the public announcement and responded positively, seeing in it a chance for German equality. The proposal was then announced on May 9, 1950, at a press conference at the Quai d'Orsay, timed ahead of a meeting of western foreign ministers in London so as to present the Americans and British with a French initiative.

What the declaration said

The declaration was brief, and its wording was careful. It proposed that Franco-German coal and steel production be placed under a common High Authority within an organization open to other European countries. It stated that the pooling of these industries would lay common foundations for economic development as a first step toward a European federation, and that it would make war between France and Germany "materially impossible." It also acknowledged that Europe would not be made all at once but through concrete achievements creating solidarity. The statement thus joined practical economic coordination with a large political aspiration, though the details of how the High Authority would operate were left for negotiation.

Why coal and steel? The sectors were central to both economic recovery and war-making capacity, they were already subject to international controls and bound up in cartel traditions, and a joint arrangement could be presented as removing a source of rivalry rather than merely adding another. Their concentration in particular regions made them manageable as a pilot. And by choosing a limited sector with real institutional powers, Monnet and Schuman avoided the difficulties that had afflicted grander schemes.

Responses

The Federal Republic accepted at once. Italy, led by Alcide De Gasperi, whose government valued the political recognition and the prospect of access to resources, joined. The Benelux governments, though wary of French and German dominance and keen to protect their own industries and the role of small states, agreed to negotiate. Britain declined to take part in negotiations that began from acceptance of a supranational authority; Clement Attlee's Labour government, with nationalized coal and an interest in retaining control over its own economic policy, was unwilling to commit in advance. The six states that proceeded would form the ECSC. Negotiations opened in Paris in June 1950, and the outbreak of the Korean War the same month heightened the sense of strategic urgency.

Historical Vocabulary
Supranationalism

Supranationalism describes an arrangement in which states grant an institution independent authority to make decisions, or to propose and enforce measures, that bind them, even where individual governments might disagree. It differs from intergovernmentalism, in which cooperation depends on the continuing consent of each government, and from federalism, in which a single constitutional order divides authority between a central government and its component states.

The term gained currency in connection with the ECSC High Authority, whose members were required to act independently of national governments. It is useful but imprecise: no Community institution before 1987 combined all the features of a federal government, and states retained decisive influence through the Council. Students should treat early integration as mixed, with supranational and intergovernmental elements in tension.

Cause and Effect

Why the Schuman Plan Emerged after 1945

Long-term causes

Centuries of Franco-German rivalry culminating in three invasions in seventy years; the industrial concentration of coal and steel in border regions; older federalist and pacifist ideas and interwar experiments in cooperation.

Short-term causes

The failure of occupation controls on the Ruhr; the creation of the Federal Republic in 1949; American pressure for German recovery; French dependence on German coal; Cold War tension and, soon after, the Korean War.

Event and immediate effects

The declaration of May 9, 1950, and the opening of negotiations among six states, with Britain declining to join.

Long-term consequences

The ECSC and its institutions served as a template for the EEC and Euratom; Franco-German cooperation became a pillar of western European politics.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the plan. National interest (French access to coal and control over German power), the initiative of a small group of officials, a favorable American attitude, and German willingness to accept equality through participation all contributed. The plan was not inevitable: a different French government, a more resistant Germany, or a different American attitude could have produced a different outcome.

Section 133.5

The Treaty of Paris and the Creation of the European Coal and Steel Community

Negotiations among the six governments opened in Paris in June 1950 and lasted until the treaty was signed on April 18, 1951. Monnet led the French delegation and Walter Hallstein, a jurist and later the first president of the EEC Commission, headed the West German team. The founding members were France, the Federal Republic of Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. The talks were difficult. Dutch and Belgian negotiators feared domination by a High Authority that might be dominated by the larger states, and they pressed for a Council of Ministers that would represent national governments and could check the High Authority's actions. German industrialists and the Allied authorities disagreed about the deconcentration of the Ruhr combines, while French and Belgian producers worried about German competition. The result was a compromise containing both a supranational executive and mechanisms for national oversight.

The treaty established a common market for coal, iron ore, scrap, and steel. It prohibited import and export duties, quantitative restrictions, subsidies, and discriminatory practices among members within those sectors. It also contained competition rules, including controls on cartels and on concentrations that might give firms excessive market power, as well as provisions for the High Authority to intervene in prices and production in cases of crisis. It was concluded for fifty years. Its objectives were economic, namely expansion, employment, and a rising standard of living, but the preamble also expressed the political aim of replacing historic rivalries with a community of interest.

Ratification and implementation

Ratification occurred in 1951 and 1952 in national parliaments, usually with substantial majorities, though with opposition from French Communists and Gaullists on the one hand, who objected on grounds of sovereignty, and from German Social Democrats on the other, who feared that the arrangement would entrench the division of Germany and the dominance of heavy industry. The treaty entered into force on July 23, 1952. The common market for coal, iron ore, and scrap opened on February 10, 1953, and for steel on May 1, 1953, with special steels following in 1954. These dates are useful for students who wish to distinguish between the legal existence of the Community and the real functioning of its market.

What the ECSC was not

The distinction between the moment a treaty entered into force and the point at which a market began to function was especially clear in the ECSC. The Community's common market opened on February 10, 1953, for coal, iron ore, and scrap, and on May 1, 1953, for steel. These were staged administrative and commercial changes, not instant transformations in industrial geography. Prices, supply contracts, transport costs, quality specifications, and existing relations among producers continued to affect where buyers obtained their materials. The High Authority had to translate treaty prohibitions into usable rules while businesses adjusted to the disappearance of particular national protections. The different opening dates also show why historical chronologies should not collapse the Treaty of Paris, the first meetings of the institutions, and the practical commencement of the common market into a single founding event.

The practical measure of supranational power was whether decisions could constrain producers and governments when those parties preferred national arrangements. Rules against discriminatory transport charges and certain restrictive agreements challenged long-established commercial practices in coal and steel. Yet the new organization did not abolish national industrial ministries, national labor law, or the politics of allocating public investment. Firms remained embedded in domestic systems of ownership, finance, and bargaining. The resulting mixture of enforceable common obligations and persistent national institutions helps explain why the ECSC was innovative without being a European state. Its importance was as much the creation of a repeatable method for resolving disputes as the immediate efficiency gains obtained in its first years.

The ECSC should not be confused with a comprehensive customs union. It covered two sectors, coal and steel, and left the rest of the members' economies under national tariffs and rules. It did not create a common external tariff for all goods. Even within its sectors, the Community's powers were limited by the terms of the treaty, and its practical effect depended on the willingness of governments and firms to adapt. It was, as Monnet and Schuman had intended, a pilot: a narrow but real transfer of authority that might build trust and show what shared institutions could accomplish.

Section 133.6

Coal, Steel, Labor, and the Economic Operation of the ECSC

In practice, the ECSC operated through a mixture of market opening, regulation, and financial support. The High Authority collected a levy on coal and steel production, capped at a small percentage of the value of output, and used the revenue for administration, investment loans, research, and aid for workers. It enforced the prohibition of discriminatory pricing and transport rates, a technical but important matter, since railway tariffs that favored domestic producers could be as effective as customs duties. It monitored competition, authorized or blocked mergers, and examined the cartel-like arrangements that had long characterized heavy industry. And it encouraged investment by publishing forecasts of demand and production and by lending to firms for modernization.

The effects were uneven. Producers in the Ruhr and Lorraine benefited from access to wider markets, while high-cost coal mines, notably in Belgium, found it harder to compete. Belgium had been granted a transitional period and subsidies to adjust, and the persistent difficulties of its coal sector illustrate that integration produced winners and losers. Italian steelmakers, dependent on imported scrap and coking coal, gained from the removal of discriminatory pricing but faced the pressures of competition. French producers, who had hoped for secure coal supplies, obtained them, while German firms, which were subject to Allied deconcentration and then to the ECSC's own rules on concentration, found their room for maneuver constrained. The Netherlands and Luxembourg, whose economies were differently structured, had distinct interests, the former in its coal mines and trade outlets, the latter in its steel industry, which was among the most important in the Community in relation to the country's size.

The economic context mattered. In the 1950s, rapid growth and Korean War demand expanded markets for steel and coal, which eased adjustment. By the later 1950s and 1960s, competition from oil and imported coal began to undermine coal mining across the Community, and the High Authority faced demands to protect jobs. How far the ECSC contributed to growth, as opposed to simply accompanying it, remains debated. Economic historians generally regard the Community as helpful in reducing discriminatory practices and in providing a framework for dealing with restructuring, but they caution against attributing the western European boom primarily to it.

Social provisions

Financing provides a concrete example of how the new institutions operated. Rather than depend exclusively on national contributions decided anew each year, the High Authority could raise funds from levies on production and could borrow for authorized purposes. This capacity gave it an element of administrative independence uncommon in international organizations of the time. Loans could support investment in mines, steelworks, and related facilities, while separate social expenditure assisted adjustment. The distinction between investment and social assistance mattered: upgrading a plant might raise productivity but might also change the demand for labor. An authority concerned only with removing trade barriers would have had few instruments to address the consequences for displaced workers or communities dependent on a single industry.

Consider a mining town whose pit faced closure because its coal was expensive relative to supplies available elsewhere in the Community. For the mine operator, the new market intensified competitive pressure. For industrial customers, it could mean cheaper or more reliable fuel. For a worker whose family relied on the mine, it could mean uncertainty about retraining, housing, and the ability to move. The ECSC's readaptation programs did not guarantee employment or eliminate the political conflict surrounding closures, but they acknowledged that market integration had social costs. They also created an early precedent for later Community debates over whether economic policy required regional, labor, and social measures. This was one way the supposedly technical beginnings of integration could reach into ordinary life.

The treaty also included social provisions that set the ECSC apart from a purely market-opening arrangement. Workers displaced by restructuring or by the closure of mines and plants could receive "readaptation" assistance, including payments to support retraining and resettlement, and the High Authority funded the construction of workers' housing. Trade unions were represented in a Consultative Committee of producers, workers, consumers, and dealers, and German coal and steel workers had, in 1951, secured a distinctive system of co-determination in national law. These provisions were modest in scale, and they did not prevent hardship in declining regions. They did, however, establish the principle that the Community had a responsibility for the human consequences of economic adjustment, a principle that later shaped the European Social Fund and regional policy.

Everyday Life and Social History

Coal and Steel Workers in the New Community

For a miner in the Borinage in Belgium or in the Ruhr, the Treaty of Paris was not an abstract constitution. It was a set of decisions, made in Luxembourg, that could influence whether a pit stayed open, whether a steelworks expanded, or whether a worker would be offered retraining and housing elsewhere. Heavy industry organized entire communities around company housing, shift work, trade union branches, and a high risk of injury and disease. When mines closed, whole towns lost their main employer.

The ECSC's assistance schemes helped some workers find new jobs or relocate, and its housing program improved living conditions for thousands of families, though the scale was small relative to the need. Cross-border labor mobility, a theoretical objective, was limited in the early years by language, qualification recognition, and social security rules. The viewpoint of workers is hard to reconstruct from the Community's own records, which are largely administrative. Union archives, local newspapers, and oral history provide an important corrective. The important point is that integration affected sectors, regions, and generations differently, and that those who bore its adjustment costs were not always those who gained most from it.

Section 133.7

High Authority, Council, Assembly, and Court: A New Institutional Experiment

The ECSC's constitution reflected an effort to balance four different principles of authority. The High Authority, based in Luxembourg, was a nine-member executive whose members were appointed by agreement among the governments but were required to act independently of them. It could take decisions binding on firms, issue recommendations, and impose penalties, and it was the Community's principal supranational organ. The Special Council of Ministers, composed of representatives of national governments, was designed to harmonize the High Authority's actions with national economic policies and, in some matters, had to give its assent. It was the channel through which governments protected their interests. The Common Assembly, with seventy-eight members delegated from national parliaments, had the right to debate the High Authority's annual report and, by a vote of censure, to force the High Authority to resign. And the Court of Justice, with seven judges, reviewed the legality of High Authority and Council decisions at the request of member states or firms.

This structure expressed distinct views about legitimacy. A technocratic body with independence from governments could take decisions without paralysis, but needed oversight. A council of ministers gave national governments, especially smaller ones, a veto or voice. A parliamentary body brought democratic scrutiny, though it was not elected directly by European voters and had limited powers. A court ensured that the institutions acted within the treaty. The combination was novel, and it was not a copy of any national constitution.

Jean Monnet served as the first president of the High Authority from 1952 to 1955, and his approach, a combination of small expert teams, informal consultation with governments and industry, and insistence on the independence of the institution, shaped its character. He resigned in 1955, frustrated by the failure of political integration and by tensions with governments. The High Authority's actual authority was greatest in the early period; as economic conditions deteriorated and national governments became more assertive, the Council's role grew, and by the 1960s the ECSC was more constrained than its founders had hoped.

The ECSC also offered a useful test of accountability. The Consultative Committee brought together representatives of producers, workers, merchants, and consumers, enabling organized interests to advise the High Authority. Consultation could reveal problems that executive officials might not see, but it did not give these groups control of policy. Similarly, the Common Assembly had the power to scrutinize the High Authority and, under specified conditions, to force its collective resignation, yet it lacked the familiar authority of a national legislature to initiate and pass a comprehensive program of laws. National parliaments participated indirectly by supplying assembly members and ratifying the founding treaty, while ministers participated directly through the Special Council. Democratic legitimacy therefore rested on several incomplete channels rather than a single European electorate.

Disputes over the High Authority's decisions also gave the Court of Justice a practical role. Judicial review could determine whether an institution had exceeded its legal powers, failed to follow required procedures, or misapplied the treaty. That possibility mattered for smaller states and private enterprises as well as governments: common rules were more credible when the executive that implemented them was also subject to law. At the same time, neither judicial supervision nor consultation resolved the political question of who should set the Community's long-term priorities. This combination of expert administration, ministerial control, limited parliamentary supervision, and judicial remedies became a recurring pattern in later European integration.

The ECSC institutions should not be mistaken for the institutions of later Communities. The ECSC's High Authority held more direct powers than the later EEC Commission, which chiefly proposed and supervised. The Common Assembly was only a precursor of the European Parliament, and was not directly elected. The Special Council of Ministers had a more limited role than the later Council of the EEC. The ECSC thus provided a precedent and a vocabulary, but its institutional forms were not carried over unchanged, a point developed in Section 133.11.

Section 133.8

The Failure of the European Defence Community and the Limits of Political Integration

The ECSC's early promise might have led, in the thinking of its architects, to a progression from economic to military and political integration. The proposal for a European Defence Community (EDC) was the first major test of that expectation. It arose from the outbreak of the Korean War in June 1950, which intensified American demands that West Germany contribute to western defense. For France, the prospect of a rearmed German national army was alarming, only five years after liberation. In October 1950, French prime minister René Pleven proposed a European army, with integrated units under a common command and institutions, in which German soldiers would serve within a multinational framework rather than in a national army.

The six ECSC states signed the EDC Treaty in Paris in May 1952. The treaty provided for a European Defence Force with a supranational commissariat, a Council, an Assembly, and a Court, and it was accompanied by plans for a European Political Community that would broaden the common institutions. An ad hoc assembly drafted a statute for such a community in 1953. The prospects of a political union were therefore considerably more ambitious than those of the ECSC, and they involved matters closest to national sovereignty: armed forces and foreign policy.

Ratification proved difficult in France. French public opinion and politicians were divided. Gaullists objected that a European army would dissolve French sovereignty and the French army, Communists rejected it as part of an anti-Soviet bloc, and some Socialists and Radicals feared German rearmament or the loss of French independence, while Christian Democrats and many centrists supported it. Events altered the context. The death of Stalin in March 1953, the end of the Korean War in July 1953, and the defeat at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954 changed perceptions of the threat and strained French resources and political confidence. On August 30, 1954, the French National Assembly, by a procedural vote of roughly 319 to 264, refused to proceed with ratification, effectively killing the treaty. The European Political Community, which had depended on it, collapsed along with it.

The consequences

The failure did not leave West Germany unarmed or outside western defense. Within weeks, the Paris Agreements of October 1954 transformed the 1948 Brussels Treaty into the Western European Union (WEU), which included Germany and Italy, and arranged for the Federal Republic's accession to NATO, which occurred in May 1955. The Federal Republic regained sovereignty in May 1955 with the end of the occupation regime. Defense integration thus proceeded within an Atlantic and intergovernmental rather than a European supranational framework. The episode demonstrated that the transfer of authority over armed forces and foreign policy was far more contested than the pooling of coal and steel, and that French domestic politics could block initiatives that French governments themselves had launched.

Why did economic integration remain viable when military and political integration failed? Part of the answer is that economic integration offered tangible benefits and operated incrementally, while the EDC demanded an immediate and visible surrender of core symbols of statehood. Another part is that economic cooperation could be justified by national interest across many governments, and that it did not require the same level of public consent. The collapse of the EDC also had a lasting effect on the method of integration: it discouraged grand constitutional schemes and strengthened the preference of Monnet and others for sectoral, economic steps.

Historical Debate

Why Did Political and Defense Integration Fail in 1954?

The question: Was the rejection of the EDC the product of contingent French politics and changed international circumstances, or did it reveal a structural limit on integration in defense and political affairs?

Interpretation A: Contingency and French politics. Some historians emphasize the circumstances of 1954: the unstable Fourth Republic, the strain of the Indochina war and defeat, the death of Stalin, the lack of strong leadership behind ratification, and shifting coalitions in the Assembly. On this view, a different set of events or a different government might have secured passage.
Interpretation B: Structural limits. Other scholars argue that the failure reflected a deeper reality: states are most reluctant to transfer control over armed forces, which are closely bound up with national identity and security. Alan Milward, among others, argued that integration succeeded when it served the interests of national governments and failed when it did not, a view that treats the EDC as an overreach.

Evidence: Parliamentary debates, French cabinet records, American and British diplomatic correspondence, and public opinion surveys all bear on the question, as do the texts of the treaty and of the political community proposals. The two interpretations overlap, since contingent factors operated within structural constraints.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians view the potential of the early supranational project, and whether the later preference for economic integration was a considered choice or a response to defeat.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation, and explain whether the two can be combined into a single account.

Section 133.9

Messina, the Spaak Report, and the Revival of European Integration

The collapse of the EDC in August 1954 produced a sense of crisis among supporters of integration, but it did not end the project. Several governments concluded that the ECSC's economic approach should be broadened, while the political and military dimensions were set aside. Initiatives came from different directions. The Dutch foreign minister, Johan Willem Beyen, proposed a general common market, and the Benelux governments circulated a memorandum in 1955 calling for expanded economic integration, including in transport and energy. Jean Monnet, who had left the High Authority, pressed for integration in atomic energy and founded the Action Committee for the United States of Europe in 1955, which sought the support of political parties and trade unions across the Six.

The foreign ministers of the six ECSC states met at Messina in Sicily on June 1 and 2, 1955. Their resolution declared the intention to pursue further integration through the development of common institutions, the progressive merging of national economies, the creation of a common market, and the harmonization of social policies, and it set up a committee of governmental representatives and experts, chaired by the Belgian foreign minister Paul-Henri Spaak, to examine the options. Britain, invited to take part, sent an official representative to the early meetings but withdrew in November 1955, reflecting its skepticism about a customs union with a common external tariff.

The Spaak Report and the bargaining of 1956

The committee's report, delivered in April 1956, proposed two projects: a general common market, based on a customs union with a common external tariff and the progressive removal of barriers to the movement of goods, workers, services, and capital, and a separate community for atomic energy. The Six agreed in May 1956 at Venice to open negotiations on the basis of the report, and talks proceeded at the Château de Val Duchesse near Brussels through 1956 and into 1957.

The negotiations exposed the competing interests of the governments, and the final treaties reflect trades among them. France, with a protected, less competitive industrial sector and large agricultural and overseas interests, wanted guarantees: harmonization of social charges so that French firms were not disadvantaged, safeguards if market opening threatened particular industries, and the association of its overseas territories with the Community. It also wanted an atomic energy community. West Germany, with a more export-oriented industry, was divided: Economics Minister Ludwig Erhard favored wider free trade and doubted the protectionist implications of a customs union, while Chancellor Adenauer judged that political considerations, above all the consolidation of Franco-German partnership, justified accepting French demands. The Netherlands, a major exporter of agricultural goods, wanted agriculture included, while Italy wanted provisions for the movement of labor and for development aid to its poorer south. Belgium and Luxembourg pressed their own concerns about protecting their industries and transport routes.

International events influenced the final stage. The Suez crisis of October and November 1956, in which French and British forces were forced to halt by American and Soviet pressure, deepened French doubts about relying on Anglo-American partners and encouraged the French government of Guy Mollet to seek closer European cooperation. The Soviet suppression of the Hungarian uprising, discussed in Chapter 129, strengthened western European governments' sense of shared security interests. These events did not cause the treaties, but they supplied political momentum at a decisive moment, and the negotiations were completed within months.

Students should note that the history of these years is a series of stages rather than a single agreement. The Messina resolution of 1955, the Spaak Report of 1956, the Venice decision, the Val Duchesse negotiations, and the treaties of March 1957 were successive steps, each dependent on political decisions that might have gone otherwise. The move from sector-specific cooperation to a comprehensive economic community was a choice made in 1955 and 1956, not an automatic extension of the ECSC.

Section 133.10

The Treaties of Rome, March 25, 1957: Founding the EEC and Euratom

On March 25, 1957, representatives of the six governments signed two treaties in Rome, in the Palazzo dei Conservatori on the Capitoline Hill. One established the European Economic Community and the other the European Atomic Energy Community, known as Euratom. After ratification by national parliaments, both entered into force on January 1, 1958. Together they are commonly called the Treaties of Rome, though the term is often used loosely to refer to the EEC Treaty alone. Students must keep in mind that there were two treaties and two Communities.

The EEC Treaty

The EEC Treaty was a far more comprehensive instrument than the Treaty of Paris. Its preamble spoke of laying the foundations of an "ever closer union among the peoples of Europe," a phrase that would be much quoted later but whose meaning was left open. Its central objective was the establishment of a common market over a transitional period of twelve years, divided into three stages of four years each. This involved the progressive elimination of customs duties and quantitative restrictions on trade among the members, the establishment of a common customs tariff towards third countries, and the abolition of obstacles to the free movement of persons, services, and capital. The treaty also provided for common policies in agriculture and transport, rules on competition including restrictions on agreements and abuses of dominant position that distort trade, and the coordination of economic policies.

The treaty contained several features that reflect the compromises of 1956. It created a European Social Fund to support employment and worker mobility, and a European Investment Bank to finance development in less advanced regions. It included in Article 119 the principle of equal pay for men and women for equal work, a provision inserted largely at French insistence so that French firms, which already operated under such a rule, would not be disadvantaged. And in a part concerning overseas countries and territories, it associated the dependent territories of the member states, principally French and Belgian possessions in Africa, with the Community, with trade preferences and a development fund. This last provision shows that the founding of the EEC was entangled with European empire at the moment when decolonization was beginning, a theme examined in Chapter 128 and returned to in Section 133.14.

The treaty was concluded for an unlimited duration, in contrast to the fifty-year term of the ECSC. It was framework legislation: it set objectives, timetables, and institutional procedures, and left considerable detail to be worked out through later decisions. This feature gave the institutions significant room for development and made the treaty's implementation dependent on political agreement among governments.

Euratom

The Euratom Treaty had a narrower purpose: to promote cooperation in the peaceful development of nuclear energy. It provided for research, the dissemination of information, the creation of a common nuclear market, a supply agency for fissile materials, and safeguards to ensure that materials were not diverted from civilian uses. Its strengths were limited by the preference of France, which pursued its own nuclear weapons program, for national autonomy, and by the later fall in importance of nuclear energy as a political priority in several member states. Euratom remained legally distinct from the EEC, though it shared institutions with it, and it has had a less central place in the history of integration than the EEC.

Continuity and expansion

The Rome treaties represented a significant expansion beyond the ECSC. They covered most of the members' economies rather than two sectors. They established institutions on a model in which the Council of Ministers had stronger powers than the ECSC's Special Council, and the Commission, in contrast to the High Authority, was designed chiefly to propose and oversee rather than to decide. They envisaged the gradual replacement of unanimity by qualified majority voting in the Council on many matters. And they set timetables that created expectations, and political pressure, for the removal of barriers. At the same time, the transfer of authority remained partial, and the balance between common decision-making and national governments was left to be worked out.

Historical Vocabulary
Common Market

In the 1950s and 1960s, the common market was the usual name for the economic area the EEC Treaty set out to establish. It was understood to involve a customs union, the removal of barriers to the movement of goods, persons, services, and capital, and common rules on competition and some policies. A customs union removes tariffs among members and sets a common external tariff; a free-trade area removes internal tariffs but lets each member keep its own external tariff; and a fully integrated single market, as pursued from the mid-1980s, also tackles technical standards, regulations, and border formalities. The EEC of the 1960s achieved the first of these and made partial progress toward the second. The popular label "Common Market" was used in Britain and elsewhere for the Community as a whole, which risks suggesting that the EEC was purely commercial.

Section 133.11

The Commission, Council, Parliamentary Assembly, and Court of Justice

The EEC's institutions took shape in 1958. The Commission, based in Brussels, had nine members, two each from France, West Germany, and Italy and one each from Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, who were required to act in the Community's general interest and not on national instructions. Its principal functions were to propose legislation, to supervise implementation of the treaty, and to represent the Community in certain negotiations and administer its policies. It was therefore a guardian of the treaties and a source of initiative, but it was weaker than the ECSC High Authority in its power to take binding decisions alone. Its first president, Walter Hallstein, held office from 1958 to 1967 and combined a federalist vision with a legalistic, deliberate style.

The Council of Ministers was the central decision-making body, composed of ministers of the member governments, and was where national governments exercised influence. The treaty provided for decisions by unanimity in many areas during the first stages, with qualified majority voting to be used more widely in later stages. Votes in the Council were weighted: France, West Germany, and Italy had four votes each, Belgium and the Netherlands two, and Luxembourg one, with a qualified majority requiring twelve of seventeen. The Council of Ministers must not be confused with the European Council of heads of state or government, which did not exist until the 1970s (Section 133.18), nor with the Council of Europe.

The Parliamentary Assembly had 142 members delegated by national parliaments and was shared by the three Communities. Its powers were consultative and supervisory, including the right to dismiss the Commission by a vote of censure, but it did not make legislation. It renamed itself the European Parliament in 1962, though the treaties continued for some time to use the earlier term, and it was not directly elected until 1979. The Court of Justice, also shared by the three Communities, ensured that Community law was interpreted and applied, and its role proved far more consequential than many founders anticipated.

To understand how these institutions worked together, consider a proposed common rule affecting a product sold throughout the Community. The Commission could identify a barrier, study its economic effects, consult affected interests, and draft a proposal. Ministers sitting in the Council would negotiate its terms in light of national industry, employment, and budget concerns. The Assembly could scrutinize the proposal within the consultative powers provided by the treaties, and the Court might later be asked to interpret the rule or determine whether it had been lawfully adopted. The exact procedure differed by subject and period, especially where unanimity was required, so this example is a teaching model rather than a universal description of legislation. It nevertheless shows why the Commission was not simply a European cabinet and why the Council was more than an occasional diplomatic conference.

Institutional location also exposed the Community's multinational character. Political and administrative work occurred in Brussels, Luxembourg, and Strasbourg rather than in a single capital. Documents had to be prepared and interpreted in multiple languages, and representatives needed to reconcile different legal traditions and administrative habits. Translation was not a peripheral convenience: disagreement over a technical term in a regulation or judgment could alter how an obligation was understood and implemented. The legal and linguistic work required for a functioning common market helps explain why the Communities developed a permanent professional bureaucracy, even while elected national governments insisted that consequential political decisions should remain subject to their control.

Initially, the three Communities had separate executive bodies: the ECSC's High Authority, the EEC Commission, and the Euratom Commission, and separate councils. The Merger Treaty, signed in 1965 and in force from July 1, 1967, established a single Commission and a single Council serving all three. The Communities remained legally distinct, but their institutions were unified. This helps explain why historians speak of the "European Communities," in the plural, until the Maastricht Treaty.

Section 133.12

European Law, the Common Market, and the Expansion of Community Authority

The Court of Justice transformed the legal character of the Communities in the 1960s through a series of interpretive decisions that gave Community law a status that the treaty text did not explicitly spell out. The mechanism was the preliminary reference procedure, under which national courts could, and in some cases had to, ask the Court of Justice how Community law should be interpreted. This procedure turned national judges and private litigants into participants in the enforcement of Community law, and it created a channel for the Court to develop doctrine through concrete disputes.

Van Gend en Loos (1963)

In Van Gend en Loos, decided on February 5, 1963, a Dutch transport company challenged a customs duty imposed on a chemical product imported from West Germany. The company argued that the duty violated an article of the EEC Treaty that prohibited member states from introducing new customs duties or increasing existing ones. The Dutch court asked whether individuals could invoke that article directly. The Court of Justice answered that the Community constituted "a new legal order of international law," for whose benefit states had limited their sovereign rights, and that the subjects of this order included not only states but also individuals. Provisions that were clear, unconditional, and not dependent on further national measures could create rights that individuals might enforce before national courts. This principle came to be known as direct effect.

Costa v ENEL (1964)

In Costa v ENEL, decided on July 15, 1964, an Italian lawyer who opposed the nationalization of the electricity industry refused to pay a small bill and argued that the nationalization law conflicted with Community law. The Court held that the EEC Treaty had created its own legal system, which became an integral part of the legal systems of the member states and which their courts were bound to apply. A later national law could not be allowed to override rules that had been accepted on the basis of reciprocity, for otherwise the Community's legal basis would be called into question. This principle is known as the primacy of Community law.

These decisions mattered for practical reasons. If a firm could not rely on Community rules against a national customs authority, the common market would depend on the willingness of each government to implement its obligations. By allowing individuals to invoke the treaty before national courts, the Court created a decentralized method of enforcement. A trader who found that a state had introduced a prohibited duty could sue, and a national judge, supported by the Court of Justice, could disregard the offending measure. These rulings thus helped make the common market effective without requiring a large Community bureaucracy.

The preliminary reference procedure was especially consequential because it did not require a private claimant to persuade a national government to bring an international case. A business faced with an allegedly unlawful tariff could raise a Community-law argument in a domestic proceeding; the national judge could seek a definitive interpretation from Luxembourg and then apply that interpretation to the case. This linked ordinary courts to Community institutions and meant that many disputes over integration emerged from practical transactions rather than grand constitutional confrontations. It was not a general invitation for the Court of Justice to overrule every domestic decision. Jurisdiction depended on the treaties, the nature of the legal question, and the rules governing which provisions had enforceable effects.

The doctrine of primacy also generated a lasting constitutional tension. The Court reasoned that states could not establish a common legal system and then neutralize it whenever they passed later domestic legislation. National authorities, however, continued to understand their obligations through national constitutions and democratic mandates. Some constitutional courts accepted the operation of Community law while reserving questions concerning fundamental rights or the limits of transferred powers. Students should therefore distinguish the Court's asserted legal doctrine from the uneven history of its reception. The authority of Community law was constructed through continuing interaction between European and national courts, not declared once and universally accepted without debate.

The historical significance of the early cases should be distinguished from later developments. The Court's reasoning rested on the structure and purposes of the treaties and was not stated in the texts in so many words. Member state governments and national courts did not accept primacy in identical form or at the same pace, and constitutional courts in several states, including those of Germany and Italy, qualified their acceptance in later decades. Later EU law, including its principles of state liability and its relationship to fundamental rights, developed after the period covered here.

Section 133.13

The Customs Union, Trade Liberalization, and the Growth of the Common Market

The central economic achievement of the EEC's first decade was the customs union. The treaty had set a transitional period of twelve years, but the Six decided in 1960 and again in 1962 to accelerate the timetable, partly because early tariff cuts proceeded smoothly during years of strong growth, and partly because businesses and governments wanted certainty. On July 1, 1968, internal customs duties on industrial goods were abolished, and the Common External Tariff, calculated by reference to the average of the members' earlier tariffs, applied to imports from third countries. This was about eighteen months ahead of the original schedule.

It is important to distinguish what was achieved. The EEC became a customs union, not merely a free-trade area, because it adopted a common external tariff and a common commercial policy toward third countries, a change that required the Community to negotiate as a bloc in international trade talks, including the Kennedy Round of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade in the 1960s. Agriculture was handled separately through the Common Agricultural Policy (Section 133.14). And the customs union did not establish a fully integrated single market.

Goods, workers, services, and capital

The treaty named four freedoms of movement, but they advanced at very different rates. Trade in goods was liberalized most rapidly. The free movement of workers made progress in the 1960s through Community regulations that removed many restrictions and gave workers from member states rights to take employment and to social security coverage, and Italy, with high unemployment in its south, was a main source of migrants to Germany, France, and Belgium. Services and the right of establishment moved more slowly, hampered by differing national qualifications and regulations. Capital movements were liberalized only partially, since governments were reluctant to give up controls over exchange and credit. Practical implementation thus varied across sectors and periods, and the pace of legal change should not be mistaken for the pace of economic change.

Patterns of trade, investment, and specialization altered. Trade among the Six grew faster than their trade with the rest of the world, and firms in neighboring countries increasingly competed and cooperated across borders. Economists distinguish between trade creation, when integration shifts production to more efficient producers within the union, and trade diversion, when it shifts trade away from more efficient outside producers because of the common tariff. Both occurred, and measuring their balance is a matter of continuing scholarly discussion. The strong growth of the 1960s was also driven by factors independent of the EEC, such as postwar catch-up, high investment, and technological diffusion, which makes it difficult to isolate the Community's own contribution.

Eliminating internal customs duties did not remove other obstacles. Differing technical standards, health and safety rules, public procurement preferences, tax systems, and border formalities continued to separate national markets. Producers who wished to sell across borders still had to meet different national requirements, and customs posts continued to operate, partly to enforce indirect taxes and administrative controls. The completion of the customs union in 1968 thus marks the end of a chapter but not the achievement of a single market. This gap was a principal reason for the internal market program of the mid-1980s discussed in Section 133.20.

Section 133.14

The Common Agricultural Policy: Farmers, Consumers, and the Politics of Integration

Agriculture became one of the EEC's most important and contentious policy areas because it combined economic weight, political influence, and the weight of postwar memory. In the 1950s, farmers still formed a substantial share of the workforce in France, Italy, and parts of Germany, and every western European government protected agriculture with price supports, subsidies, and import controls. Memories of wartime hunger reinforced the aim of food security. A common market that excluded agriculture would have left a major sector under national regimes and would have benefited industrial exporters, especially West Germany, at the expense of France, whose farmers hoped to sell to German consumers. The French insistence on a common agricultural policy was thus a condition of the whole bargain.

The treaty set objectives, including increased productivity, a fair standard of living for farmers, stable markets, secure supplies, and reasonable prices for consumers, but left the details to be agreed. A conference at Stresa in July 1958 brought together representatives of governments, the Commission, and farm organizations, and Sicco Mansholt, the Dutch Commissioner for agriculture, played a central role in drafting proposals. After intense negotiations that culminated in a marathon session in December 1961 and January 1962, the Council adopted the first regulations in January 1962, establishing common market organizations for cereals, pork, eggs, poultry, fruit and vegetables, and wine, and creating a European Agricultural Guidance and Guarantee Fund. A common price level for cereals, a politically sensitive matter, was agreed in December 1964 and took effect in 1967.

How the CAP worked

In outline, the CAP rested on three principles: a single market for agricultural goods across the Community, Community preference over imports, and shared financing. For important products it set target and intervention prices. When market prices fell to the intervention level, Community agencies purchased produce to support them. Imports from outside were subject to variable levies that raised their prices to the Community level, and exports could be subsidized. This system protected producers' incomes and insulated the Community from fluctuations in world prices, and it also encouraged production because guaranteed prices were often higher than world prices.

The consequences were mixed and politically contested. The CAP provided stability to farmers and supported modernization, and productivity rose substantially. It also entailed costs. Consumers paid higher food prices than they would have in a more open market. The cost of support consumed a large share of the Community budget, which was financed, from 1970, partly by agricultural levies and customs duties as "own resources." Surpluses of products such as butter, milk, and wine, which became prominent in the 1970s and 1980s, were stored or exported with subsidies, damaging third-country producers and drawing public criticism. The benefits were also unevenly distributed: larger and more efficient farms received more, while smaller farms and poorer regions often gained less than the policy's rhetoric implied. In 1968 Mansholt proposed a plan for structural reform, aiming to reduce the farm population and increase farm size, which encountered fierce opposition from farm organizations and was only partly implemented.

The member states had different agricultural interests. France, with large grain, dairy, and meat sectors, expected to be a principal beneficiary. West Germany, with smaller and less efficient farms and a powerful farm lobby, accepted high support prices partly to protect its farmers and partly as a price for access to a wider industrial market. Italy was concerned with Mediterranean products such as fruit, vegetables, and wine, which initially received less favorable treatment than northern products. The Netherlands, a highly efficient exporter of dairy and horticultural products, supported a common market for agriculture. Belgium and Luxembourg were smaller players with their own interests.

Association with overseas territories

The EEC's external dimension must also be acknowledged. The treaty's association of the overseas territories of France, Belgium, and others, and then the Yaoundé Convention of 1963 with eighteen newly independent African states, gave those countries preferential access to the Community market and development aid. These arrangements reflected French and Belgian interests in sustaining relationships with former colonies, and they were criticized by some other countries as discriminatory. They show that the building of the European market was connected to the end of empire, a subject examined in Chapter 128, and that the Community was from the beginning a presence in the wider world.

The CAP was more than an administrative program. It was a political bargain that tied together French and German interests, absorbed the largest share of Community spending for decades, and shaped debates about budgets and enlargement. Students should avoid reading it as either an unambiguous achievement or a simple failure. It achieved certain goals, such as food security and income stability for farmers, while creating distortions and tensions that later reforms, discussed in Chapter 134 and beyond, would address.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farmers and Consumers in the Common Market

For a dairy farmer in Normandy, the CAP meant a guaranteed price and a market that extended to the cities of the Ruhr and the Netherlands. For a small farmer in southern Italy or in the hills of Bavaria, it meant a framework that sustained income but also encouraged consolidation, since larger farms could invest and expand. The 1960s and 1970s saw a rapid decline in the proportion of the workforce in agriculture, a trend present in all industrializing societies but accelerated by the opportunities of urban industry, and it was accompanied by rural depopulation and changes in village life.

For consumers, the effects were less visible. Food prices were affected by the support system, and households spent a significant share of their income on food in the 1950s and 1960s. In later decades, the public came to know the CAP mainly through its surpluses and costs. The farm lobby's organization, including national farm unions and a European coordinating committee, was a significant political actor. Ordinary rural and urban households appear in the Community's own documents mainly as statistics, so the historian must look to national press, farm organizations, and local studies for a fuller picture.

Section 133.15

Charles de Gaulle, the Empty Chair Crisis, and the Defense of National Sovereignty

Charles de Gaulle returned to power in France in 1958, in the crisis of the Algerian War, and became the first president of the Fifth Republic. He accepted the Treaties of Rome, and his government supported the Common Agricultural Policy, because the Community served French economic interests. But his view of Europe differed sharply from that of Hallstein, Monnet, and the federalists. De Gaulle envisioned a "Europe of states" in which national governments retained decisive authority, with France taking a leading role, and he distrusted supranational institutions as a threat to national independence. In 1961 and 1962 France supported the Fouchet Plans, which proposed a political union built on intergovernmental cooperation, but they failed in part because the Benelux governments feared French dominance and the weakening of the existing Communities.

The crisis of 1965

The confrontation came in 1965. The Commission under Hallstein submitted in March proposals to finance the Common Agricultural Policy from the Community's own resources, derived from agricultural levies and customs duties, and to give the European Parliament greater budgetary powers. The proposals linked financing, which France wanted for the CAP, to institutional reforms that would strengthen the Commission and Parliament. Under the treaty, the Council was to move to qualified majority voting on a number of matters from January 1, 1966, which would remove the national veto in those areas. De Gaulle objected to the linkage and the shift toward majority voting, and on June 30, 1965, negotiations collapsed. France then withdrew from participation in the Council and most Community business from July 1965, an episode known as the Empty Chair Crisis.

The crisis paralyzed the Community for about six months. The other five governments, which valued the common market and the CAP, did not agree to abandon their positions but wished to find a way out. De Gaulle was also weakened domestically when he failed to win the French presidential election of December 1965 on the first ballot, which encouraged a more conciliatory course. At extraordinary meetings in Luxembourg in January 1966, the Six reached a settlement that restored French participation, and the settlement has since been known as the Luxembourg Compromise.

The meaning of the compromise

The agreement was not a formal amendment of the treaty. It recorded that when very important interests of one or more partners were at stake in a decision that could be taken by majority, the members would try, within a reasonable time, to reach a solution that all could accept. France held that discussion should continue until unanimity was reached, while the other governments did not accept that position, and the final communiqué noted the disagreement. In practice, the compromise made it politically difficult to outvote a government that claimed a vital interest, and it discouraged use of majority voting on contentious matters for roughly two decades. The treaties' formal voting provisions remained unchanged, but the political practice diverged from them. The Commission's position was damaged, Hallstein's presidency ended in 1967, and the ambitions of federalists were set back.

Historians have debated the significance of these events. Some regard the crisis as a defeat for supranationalism and an affirmation of national sovereignty. Others note that the Community survived, that the CAP was financed, that the Court's doctrines of direct effect and primacy continued to develop, and that the Council's reluctance to vote did not stop the work of integration. The crisis thus illustrates a recurring tension between effective common decision-making and the defense of national sovereignty, a tension never fully resolved.

Historical Vocabulary
National Sovereignty

Sovereignty refers to the supreme authority of a state within its territory and its independence from external control. In the integration debates, "national sovereignty" was invoked by those who feared that Community institutions would take decisions over the heads of elected national governments. Advocates of integration argued that sovereignty could be pooled, that governments could exercise some powers more effectively together than separately, while critics replied that pooling meant losing control. The term is not self-defining, and its meaning shifted according to who used it: de Gaulle meant the freedom of the French government to decide, while others meant the authority of national parliaments or constitutions. Students should ask in each case what kind of authority was said to be at risk and who would exercise it.

Historical Debate

Supranationalism versus National Authority in the Early Communities

The question: Did early European integration erode national sovereignty, or did it strengthen the European nation-state by helping it to perform functions it could not perform alone?

Interpretation A: Pooling and transfer. Scholars in the tradition of neofunctionalism argued that integration in one sector created pressures for further integration, as institutions and economic interests developed a stake in extending common rules. On this view, the Court's doctrines, the Commission's initiatives, and the logic of the common market gradually shifted authority to the European level.
Interpretation B: The rescue of the nation-state. Other historians, notably Alan Milward, argue that governments built the Communities to secure national goals such as growth, welfare, and food security, and that national governments remained the decisive actors. On this view, the crisis of 1965 shows the limits of supranational ambition.

Evidence: Council and Commission records, cabinet papers, treaty provisions, voting practices, and judicial decisions inform both views. The evidence can be read in more than one way: the Luxembourg Compromise confirms national influence, yet the Court's doctrines and the growth of Community rules point to a gradual transfer of authority.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how one understands the later development of the Communities and the criticisms made of them, and prevents both the assumption that integration was unstoppable and the assumption that it changed nothing.

Section 133.16

Britain, EFTA, and the First Enlargement of the European Communities

Britain's absence from the founding of the Communities was a matter of choice and circumstance. Postwar Britain remained a global power with ties to the Commonwealth, a close relationship with the United States, and a political culture in which parliamentary sovereignty was a central principle. British governments of both parties were suspicious of supranational authority and preferred cooperation among governments. Commonwealth trade, which had been important to the British economy, seemed to be threatened by a continental customs union. And Britain's economic situation, with a strong coal industry and nationalized sectors, did not seem to require the kind of structure that the Six sought. At Messina, Britain's representative withdrew early, and the government concluded that a customs union with a common external tariff was incompatible with its interests.

EFTA

Britain's response was to propose a wider free-trade area for industrial goods within the OEEC, which would have removed tariffs among a broad group of countries without a common external tariff or common agricultural policy. Negotiations in 1957 and 1958 ended in November 1958 when France, under de Gaulle, refused to proceed. Britain then led the creation of the European Free Trade Association, established by the Stockholm Convention and operating from 1960, with Austria, Denmark, Norway, Portugal, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom as members. EFTA was an intergovernmental free-trade area with no common external tariff and no supranational institutions, and it demonstrated that there was a genuine alternative model of economic cooperation. But the EEC proved economically more dynamic and politically more attractive, and Britain's trade increasingly shifted toward Europe.

Applications and vetoes

In July 1961 the Macmillan government announced that Britain would apply for membership, and Ireland and Denmark applied soon afterward, with Norway following in 1962. Negotiations, led for Britain by Edward Heath, addressed Commonwealth trade, British agriculture, and EFTA partners. On January 14, 1963, de Gaulle announced at a press conference that Britain was not ready, and negotiations were broken off. His reasons included doubts about Britain's commitment to Europe, its Atlantic ties, and concerns for France's leadership of the Community. Britain applied again in 1967 under the Labour government of Harold Wilson, and de Gaulle vetoed that application in November 1967. After de Gaulle's resignation in April 1969, the summit at The Hague in December 1969, under his successor Georges Pompidou, agreed to the principles of completion, deepening, and enlargement, and negotiations opened in 1970 with Heath, now British prime minister, leading the British side.

The Treaty of Accession was signed on January 22, 1972, and Britain, Ireland, and Denmark joined on January 1, 1973, expanding the Communities from six to nine. Terms included transitional periods, arrangements for Commonwealth sugar and New Zealand dairy products, and a schedule for British contributions to the Community budget, which would prove contentious. In Britain, the membership question divided both major parties, and a referendum in June 1975, held after the Labour government's renegotiation of terms, produced a majority of roughly two to one for remaining in the Communities.

Norway and the limits of inevitability

The enlargement was not automatic. Norway negotiated and signed the accession agreement, but in a referendum on September 25, 1972, a majority of roughly 53 percent of voters rejected membership. Opponents emphasized concerns about fisheries, agriculture, and national control, and the vote exposed divisions between urban and rural areas and among regions. Norway thus remained outside, and the result illustrates that participation in the Communities was a matter of political choice. By contrast, Ireland's referendum of May 1972 produced a very large majority in favor, and Denmark's in October 1972 a clear but smaller majority.

Section 133.17

The European Communities amid the Economic and Political Crises of the 1970s

The institutions built during the postwar boom faced strain in the 1970s. Chapter 131 examines the collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary arrangements, the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979, and the combination of inflation and unemployment that followed. For the Communities, these developments mattered in specific ways. Exchange-rate instability disrupted the common agricultural prices, which were set in common units, and threatened the common market. Governments responded to recession with national measures, including subsidies and protective devices, that often undermined common rules. And differences in national economic performance and policy widened.

The Communities nevertheless continued to develop. In 1970 the Werner Report had set out a plan for economic and monetary union, which was frustrated by the turbulence of the early 1970s, but the attempt to limit exchange-rate fluctuations among members, the so-called "snake," prepared the ground for later efforts. Disputes over finances became prominent. The agricultural budget absorbed a large majority of Community spending, and countries that were net contributors without proportionate agricultural receipts, particularly Britain after 1973, objected that the system was unfair. These disputes culminated in the early 1980s in the struggle of the British government under Margaret Thatcher for a budget rebate, resolved at the Fontainebleau European Council in June 1984.

The British entry also contributed to the creation of the European Regional Development Fund in 1975, which was designed to channel resources to less prosperous regions, partly as compensation for the CAP's distribution of benefits and partly because membership increased regional disparities. The Fund was modest at first, but it marked the growing importance of policies intended to reduce disparities among member regions. In 1979 the European Monetary System was launched, with an exchange-rate mechanism and a unit of account, the ECU, designed to stabilize currencies. It must be distinguished from the later European Monetary Union and the euro, discussed in Chapter 134. Britain joined the system but did not participate in the exchange-rate mechanism.

The Community budget became an arena in which larger questions about integration were argued through apparently technical allocations. Agricultural expenditure was prominent because common price and market mechanisms created commitments that were difficult to adjust quickly. Britain, with a comparatively smaller agricultural sector and different patterns of food imports, increasingly questioned whether its contribution and benefits were fairly balanced. Regional policy offered one possible response to the wider issue of uneven gains. The European Regional Development Fund, created in 1975, supplied cofinancing for projects intended to improve conditions in disadvantaged regions. Its resources were initially modest, and governments often sought to align them with existing national development programs rather than surrender control over regional strategy.

Financial disputes demonstrated both the capacity and the limits of the institutional system. A common agricultural decision could impose costs in one national budget while generating benefits for producers elsewhere; a regional development program might respond to disparities without fully compensating those facing industrial decline. The Commission, Council, and Parliament approached budget decisions from different positions, while interest groups appealed to their own national representatives and to Community bodies. As membership expanded, the range of competing economic interests widened further. The significance of these arguments lies not only in the size of particular budget lines but in the growing recognition that a common market generated distributional questions that tariff reductions alone could not answer.

The period is often described as one of "Eurosclerosis," in which integration stagnated amid national protectionism. This is partly accurate, but the story is more complicated. Economic disruption could obstruct integration, as governments looked inward, and could also create arguments for stronger common institutions and policies, as firms and governments saw a larger market as a way to compete with the United States and Japan. The 1970s thus laid some foundations for the revival of the mid-1980s.

Section 133.18

Democratic Legitimacy, the European Council, and the First Direct Parliamentary Elections

As the Communities acquired more responsibilities, questions arose about who controlled them. Decisions were made largely by national ministers in the Council and by an appointed Commission, and the Parliamentary Assembly consisted of delegated national parliamentarians with limited powers. Critics, from several political perspectives, saw a democratic deficit, and supporters of integration saw the need to strengthen legitimacy.

The summit of heads of state and government at Paris in December 1974 decided to hold regular meetings of national leaders, which became the European Council. Its first meeting under that name took place in Dublin in March 1975. The European Council gave the national leaders a forum for settling major questions and setting general direction. It was not a legislative body and was not initially a formal institution of the treaties, which were amended later to recognize it. It must be distinguished from the Council of Ministers, in which ministers of the member governments adopt Community legislation, and from the Council of Europe.

The Parliament's powers also grew. Treaties of 1970 and 1975 gave it important powers over the Community budget, including, in the latter, the right to reject the budget as a whole, and a Court of Auditors was created to scrutinize Community finances. The treaties had long foreseen direct elections, and in 1976 the member states agreed on an act providing for them. The first elections to the European Parliament took place from June 7 to 10, 1979, with 410 members chosen by voters across nine states, and the Parliament elected Simone Veil, a French politician and survivor of Auschwitz, as its first president. Turnout was roughly three fifths of eligible voters, though it varied widely by country.

The introduction of direct elections did not mean that the Parliament immediately acquired the legislative authority of a national assembly. In the 1979 elections, voters chose representatives across nine member countries under varying national electoral arrangements. Some people interpreted the opportunity as a vote about Europe; others used it to reward or punish national parties. Political groups spanning member states helped organize the new Parliament, but national parties retained enormous influence over candidates and campaigns. Turnout and political enthusiasm differed substantially among countries, and the elected members inherited an institution whose treaty powers were still limited. This gap between electoral legitimacy and legislative capacity became a source of pressure for institutional reform.

Budgetary authority, though narrower than comprehensive lawmaking power, gave the Parliament a way to exercise influence. Disagreements over the distinction between compulsory and non-compulsory expenditure, and over the Parliament's ability to reject the budget as a whole, exposed competing interpretations of democratic control. The European Council meanwhile supplied political leadership from national executives; it could resolve conflicts left unsettled in ministerial negotiations, but it also strengthened the visibility of heads of government as the arbiters of integration. The result was not a simple transfer of power from states to a European parliament. It was a more complicated redistribution of influence among elected representatives, ministers, executive officials, and the courts.

Supporters regarded direct elections as a way to strengthen democratic legitimacy and to link the Communities to citizens, and many hoped the elected Parliament would press for deeper integration. Critics argued that the Parliament still lacked authority over legislation and that national campaigns were dominated by domestic issues, so that elections functioned as referendums on national governments. The Parliament used its new standing to press for reform, and in 1984 it adopted a draft treaty on European Union, inspired by Altiero Spinelli, which governments did not adopt but which influenced later debate. The questions of representation, accountability, and participation that arose in these years continue in the later history of the Union.

Section 133.19

Southern European Enlargement: Greece, Spain, Portugal, and the Twelve-Member Community

The overthrow of the dictatorships in Greece, Portugal, and Spain in the mid-1970s, examined in Chapter 131, transformed the political geography of integration. The Communities had made democratic government an implicit condition of membership, and the southern European governments saw membership as a means to consolidate democracy, to modernize their economies, and to anchor their countries in western Europe. For the existing members, enlargement was an opportunity to support new democracies and a commitment that was not easy to refuse, though it raised serious economic questions.

Greece, which had been associated with the Community since 1961, applied in June 1975, and negotiations led to the Treaty of Accession signed in 1979. It joined on January 1, 1981, as the tenth member. Spain and Portugal applied in 1977, and their negotiations proved long and difficult. Agricultural competition, especially in Mediterranean products such as fruit, vegetables, wine, and olive oil, concerned French and Italian farmers, and Spanish fisheries and fleet size were a major issue for northern fishing communities. Industry, regional development, and financial contributions required transitional arrangements. The treaties were signed in June 1985, and Spain and Portugal joined on January 1, 1986, bringing the membership to twelve.

Enlargement introduced substantial differences in economic development. The new southern members had lower average incomes, larger shares of the workforce in agriculture, and weaker industrial structures than the northern members. These disparities contributed to an expansion of regional and structural policies, including the Integrated Mediterranean Programmes launched in 1985, and to the later emphasis on "economic and social cohesion" in the Single European Act. Membership gave the new democracies access to markets, funding, and a framework of rules, but the relationship between membership, political stability, and economic development requires careful causal analysis: democratic consolidation in these states rested on domestic political choices and social change as well, and economic convergence was not automatic.

The timetable of accession was itself an important part of the bargaining. New members were not expected to adjust every tariff, farm arrangement, and regulatory practice overnight. Transitional provisions gave industries, farmers, and governments time to adapt, while existing members sought protection against sudden competition in sensitive sectors. These compromises could soften the immediate economic shock but also delayed the arrival of a uniform market. Negotiations over fisheries and Mediterranean agriculture were especially difficult because national circumstances diverged sharply. In a country with substantial agricultural employment, a policy designed around northern European production and prices could create pressures unlike those experienced in a heavily industrial economy.

For the new democracies, joining the Communities also had a political meaning that cannot be measured simply by gross domestic product. Membership signaled recognition that Greece, Spain, and Portugal belonged within the western European democratic order, and it made ongoing negotiation with other elected governments part of their ordinary political life. That did not make membership a substitute for constitutional safeguards or domestic civil society. Nor did it guarantee that poorer regions would quickly catch up with prosperous ones. Indeed, greater economic openness could initially intensify adjustment difficulties for less competitive firms. The twelve-member Community consequently inherited a larger responsibility to confront disparities while avoiding the assumption that political consolidation and economic convergence were the same historical process.

The southern enlargements thus served as both a political achievement and an institutional challenge. They showed that the Communities could attract and support new democracies, and they raised questions about decision-making with twelve members, about budgets, and about the balance between the wealthy north and the poorer south.

Section 133.20

From the Common Market toward the Single European Act: Historical Synthesis

By the early 1980s, the path from the Schuman Declaration to the twelve-member Communities was visible. Franco-German reconciliation had been anchored in the ECSC; the Treaties of Rome had created a broader economic community; the customs union had been completed; the Common Agricultural Policy had become a central, costly, and controversial policy; the Court of Justice had developed doctrines of direct effect and primacy; and the membership had expanded from six to twelve. The tension between supranational authority and national decision-making, expressed in the Empty Chair Crisis, persisted, now complicated by the larger number of members.

The limits of the common market

Yet the common market remained incomplete. Customs duties had disappeared, but national technical standards, regulations, public procurement practices, and border controls continued to fragment the market. Firms often found it easier to serve their home markets than to export to neighbors. Governments pursued different economic policies, and national subsidies and quotas proliferated during the recession. Unanimity in the Council, reinforced by the Luxembourg Compromise, made it hard to adopt legislation to harmonize rules. Business leaders, including those who joined the European Round Table of Industrialists, argued that fragmentation made European firms less competitive than their American and Japanese counterparts.

The internal market initiative and the Single European Act

The revival came in 1985. The Commission under its new president, Jacques Delors, adopted the completion of the internal market as its central program. A White Paper of June 1985, prepared by Commissioner Lord Cockfield, set out roughly three hundred legislative measures to remove physical, technical, and fiscal barriers and proposed a deadline of the end of 1992. At the Milan European Council in June 1985, the governments voted, over the opposition of Britain, Denmark, and Greece, to convene an intergovernmental conference on institutional reform. The result was the Single European Act, signed in Luxembourg and The Hague in February 1986 and entering into force on July 1, 1987.

The Act committed the Communities to establishing the internal market, defined as an area without internal frontiers in which goods, persons, services, and capital could move freely, by December 31, 1992. It extended qualified majority voting in the Council to most measures needed to complete the internal market, which reduced the practical effect of the Luxembourg Compromise in that field, and it introduced a cooperation procedure that increased the Parliament's role. It gave treaty recognition to the European Council and to foreign policy cooperation among the member states, and extended Community competence to areas such as the environment, research, and economic and social cohesion. These changes were significant but limited. The Single European Act did not create the European Union, monetary union, or European citizenship, and the more far-reaching transformation of the early 1990s is the subject of Chapter 134.

An accumulative and contested process

The Single European Act illustrates how an institution can be changed in response to obstacles revealed by its earlier achievements. By the mid-1980s the removal of customs duties was long complete, yet businesses and consumers still encountered separate national technical standards, procurement practices, administrative procedures, and restrictions on certain cross-border activities. Completing the internal market required decisions in areas where unanimity could make legislation slow or difficult. The Act extended qualified-majority voting in important internal-market fields and enlarged the Parliament's participation in specified legislative procedures. These institutional changes were designed to make an economic program practicable, rather than to establish a fully federal political constitution. They also reopened the familiar debate about whether efficiency and democratic accountability were advancing together.

The central conclusion of this chapter is that European integration was an accumulative and contested process. Its institutions developed through deliberate agreements, such as the Treaty of Paris and the Treaties of Rome, and through responses to problems that earlier agreements had left unresolved: the failure of the EDC led to Messina, the incomplete customs union to the internal market program, and the democratic deficit to direct elections. At every stage, governments pursued national interests, and outcomes depended on bargaining among them. The Single European Act prepared the ground for Maastricht, the Union, and the euro, but it did not make them inevitable.

European Institutions from 1950 to 1987

What changed?

  • Scope widened from two industrial sectors to most economic activity, with policies on agriculture, competition, regional development, and the environment
  • Membership grew from six to twelve states, and the Communities became a presence in international trade and relations with former colonies

  • Membership was limited to western and southern European states, with eastern Europe excluded by Cold War division

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how a group of western European governments, emerging from the devastation of the Second World War, created institutions that shared economic and legal authority across national frontiers. The origin of the project lay in a specific historical problem: how to prevent renewed German aggression while allowing German recovery. Coal and steel, the industrial basis of military power and economic reconstruction, became the sector in which that problem was addressed, and the Ruhr, Lorraine, and the Saar were its geographic focus.

Postwar conditions made new approaches plausible. The destruction of the war and the discrediting of aggressive nationalism, the dominance of the United States and the Soviet Union, the pressures of the Cold War, and American support for European cooperation created an opening. Between 1945 and 1950, several models of cooperation were tried, including the Council of Europe, the OEEC, the Benelux union, and the Atlantic alliance, and they differed in membership and powers. None had provided a means of integrating the German economy in a way acceptable to France.

The Schuman Declaration of May 9, 1950, drafted under Jean Monnet's direction and presented by Robert Schuman, proposed a common High Authority for coal and steel. It was a proposal, not a treaty. Adenauer's Germany, De Gasperi's Italy, and the Benelux states accepted, and Britain declined. The Treaty of Paris, signed on April 18, 1951 and in force from July 23, 1952, created the European Coal and Steel Community with a High Authority, a Council, an Assembly, and a Court. Its common market for coal and steel opened in 1953, and its operation produced uneven effects for producers, workers, and regions.

The ECSC was an institutional experiment with a distinct combination of supranational and intergovernmental elements. Its failure to extend into defense, in the case of the European Defence Community rejected by the French Assembly in August 1954, showed the limits of integration where core attributes of sovereignty were concerned. Historians disagree over how far the failure was contingent on French politics and how far structural. Either way, it redirected the project toward economic integration.

The Messina Conference of June 1955 and the Spaak Report of April 1956 relaunched integration, and negotiations among governments with differing interests produced the Treaties of Rome of March 25, 1957, which established the European Economic Community and Euratom. The EEC Treaty was a broad framework for a common market, with a Commission, Council, Parliamentary Assembly, and Court of Justice, and it included provisions on agriculture, competition, social policy, and the association of overseas territories. The three Communities remained legally separate, with institutions merged only in 1967.

The Court of Justice made the Communities more than an international organization. In Van Gend en Loos (1963) and Costa v ENEL (1964) it established that Community law could create rights for individuals and took precedence over conflicting national law. These doctrines were products of judicial interpretation, not explicit treaty text, and their acceptance by national courts was gradual and sometimes conditional.

The customs union was completed on July 1, 1968, ahead of schedule, and trade among the members grew faster than their trade with others. Yet removing tariffs did not create a single market, since technical and regulatory barriers remained. The Common Agricultural Policy, begun in 1962, was a political bargain central to the whole enterprise, offering income stability to farmers and food security, while generating high costs, surpluses, and distributional disputes. The Communities' association with overseas territories and former colonies shows that the building of the European market was linked to the end of empire.

The Empty Chair Crisis of 1965 and the Luxembourg Compromise of January 1966 revealed the persistence of national authority. De Gaulle's challenge to Hallstein's Commission and the prospect of majority voting ended in a political agreement that discouraged majority voting on vital interests for roughly two decades. Historians disagree over whether the episode was a defeat for supranationalism or a confirmation that governments, not institutions, controlled the pace of integration, and the chapter has suggested that elements of both are visible.

Britain's decision to stay out, its creation of EFTA, de Gaulle's two vetoes, and its eventual accession with Ireland and Denmark in 1973 showed that enlargement was negotiated and contested. Norway's referendum rejection in 1972 and the British referendum of 1975 reinforced the point. The 1970s tested the Communities with monetary and energy crises, yet they also saw the creation of the European Regional Development Fund, the European Council, the European Monetary System, and the first direct elections to the Parliament in June 1979, which sought to address questions of democratic legitimacy.

Greece joined in 1981, and Spain and Portugal in 1986, linking integration to the consolidation of new democracies and introducing substantial economic disparities among members. By the mid-1980s the Communities had twelve members, a body of law, and a market still fragmented by non-tariff barriers. The Commission's internal market initiative of 1985 and the Single European Act, signed in February 1986 and in force on July 1, 1987, set a program and adjusted decision-making procedures. They bridge to Chapter 134, but the chapter's central conclusion is that European integration was an accumulative and contested process, shaped by security concerns, national interests, legal innovation, and competing visions of Europe, and not an inevitable progress toward a single political union.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can be made out of the wreckage of another. The Communities were not a utopian plan realized, nor merely a technical device. They were instruments through which governments managed fear, ambition, and interdependence after 1945, and their design records the bargains that made them possible: a supranational executive tempered by a Council, a common market tied to a farm policy, a legal order built by judges as well as diplomats.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that runs through Volume VI. Integration advanced where it served identifiable national interests and stalled where it demanded the surrender of symbols of sovereignty, as the EDC and the Empty Chair Crisis show. Institutions grew through crisis and compromise as often as through design. These patterns help explain the debates of the later chapters, over Maastricht, the euro, enlargement, and the limits of integration, which inherited both the achievements and the unresolved tensions of the years before 1987.

The history of the Communities connects regions that other chapters treat separately. It joins the Ruhr and Lorraine, the farms of France and the Netherlands, the poorer south of Italy, the new democracies of Iberia and Greece, and the trading interests of Britain and Scandinavia, and it also links Europe to the colonial and postcolonial world through the association of overseas territories. It was at the same time a western European project, shaped by the Cold War division that kept half the continent outside.

For the argument of Volume VI, the chapter provides the institutional counterpart to the Cold War narrative: while the continent was divided, part of it built a framework of shared authority. Chapter 134 will show how that framework was transformed after 1986, and Chapter 136 how the end of Cold War division brought eastern Europe into relation with it. The enduring lesson is that this order was historically contingent, the outcome of choices made by particular people in particular circumstances, and that its future, like its past, depended on continuing negotiation.
