The Revolutions of 1820, 1830, and 1848

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 24 February 1848 a French king abdicated, a crowd entered the Chamber of Deputies, and a provisional government proclaimed a republic in Paris. Within weeks, rulers in Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Venice, and Budapest were making concessions they had refused for decades. Within eighteen months, almost every one of those concessions had been withdrawn, narrowed, or crushed by force.

The February Revolution in Paris began as a quarrel over the right to hold political banquets. For months, opponents of the July Monarchy of Louis-Philippe had used dinners as a way around the laws restricting political meetings, gathering to toast electoral reform and to hear speeches against a government that allowed only a small fraction of adult men to vote. When the government prohibited a large banquet planned for 22 February, crowds gathered in the streets. On the evening of 23 February, soldiers fired on demonstrators near the foreign ministry, killing dozens, and the bodies were carried through the city. Barricades rose across working-class and artisan districts. By the next day the monarchy had lost control of the capital and the king had left the Tuileries.

What followed shows the central problem of this chapter. The men who gathered at the Hôtel de Ville to form a government included moderate republican lawyers and journalists, a poet and orator, a socialist writer, and a mechanic who took part as a representative of the workers. They agreed that the monarchy was finished. They did not agree on what a republic was for. Was it a political form, with universal male suffrage, a free press, and an elected assembly? Or was it a social promise, in which the state would guarantee work and limit the power of employers and owners? Barricade fighters who stood together in February would face one another across barricades in June.

This chapter examines three waves of revolutionary challenge to the political order built after Napoleon's defeat in 1815: the constitutional and military revolts centered on the Mediterranean in 1820 and 1821, the upheavals of 1830 that overthrew a French king and created an independent Belgium, and the continental crisis of 1848 and 1849, the most widespread revolutionary movement in modern European history up to that point. Along the way it considers the Greek struggle against Ottoman rule, the Decembrist revolt in Russia, the Polish rising of 1830 and 1831, the Italian revolutions, the German National Assembly at Frankfurt, and the revolutions of the Habsburg monarchy, including the Hungarian war of 1848 and 1849.

The chapter is comparative rather than merely chronological. It asks why people in very different societies, with different grievances and different kinds of government, challenged established authority within a few weeks of one another, and why the outcomes diverged so sharply. A Spanish officer, a Brussels lawyer, a Warsaw cadet, a Parisian cabinetmaker, a Hungarian noble, a Viennese student, and a Wallachian landowner shared a language of constitutions, rights, and nations, but they did not share a program. Revolutionary coalitions were often unstable because the groups composing them wanted incompatible things. Governments, by contrast, often retained the one resource that decided the matter: armies that obeyed orders.

The evidence for these events is rich but partisan. Government decrees, police reports, parliamentary debates, newspapers, pamphlets, petitions, trial records, private letters, and memoirs survive in great quantity, and so do lithographs and caricatures. Each was produced by interested actors. A police report on a political club reflects what the police feared. A liberal memoir written in exile after defeat reflects the need to explain failure. This chapter treats such sources critically, and it distinguishes between contemporary documents and the later interpretations that historians and national traditions built upon them.

Within Part XV, the chapter turns from the ideas of Chapter 97 to their collision with governments, armies, and one another. Chapters 93 to 95 described the industrial and social transformations that created new classes and new grievances, and Chapter 96 examined the competing economic and socialist responses. Chapter 98 shows what happened when these forces entered politics. Its conclusion prepares the ground for Chapter 99, which examines the reform movements and constitutional changes that proceeded alongside and after revolution, and for Chapter 100, which examines the unification of Italy and Germany. Within Volume IV, it marks the point at which the political legacy of the French Revolution, discussed in Chapters 87 to 92, was tested in the generation after Napoleon.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Restoration settlement after 1815 failed to eliminate liberal, national, and republican political aspirations.
  2. Distinguish among revolution, rebellion, constitutional revolt, military pronunciamiento, and counterrevolution, and apply those categories to specific events.
  3. Compare the aims, participants, and outcomes of the revolutionary waves of 1820, 1830, and 1848.
  4. Analyze the role of constitutionalism, as a claim about limits on royal power, in the revolutions in Spain, Portugal, Naples, Piedmont, France, and Belgium.
  5. Assess how national movements in Greece, Belgium, Poland, Italy, Germany, and the Habsburg lands differed in their goals and in their dependence on the Great Powers.
  6. Trace the course of the French Second Republic from the February Revolution through the June Days to the election of Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte.
  7. Interpret the conflicts among liberals, democrats, nationalists, and social reformers in the German and Habsburg revolutions of 1848.
  8. Evaluate the importance of army loyalty and foreign intervention in explaining why governments recovered power.
  9. Identify the social groups that took part in revolutions, including workers, peasants, women, students, and soldiers, and explain how their aims differed.
  10. Interpret a source from the Frankfurt National Assembly and assess what revolutionary documents can and cannot establish about popular politics.

Historical Context

The revolutions of 1820 to 1848 occurred inside the political order established after the Napoleonic Wars. Chapter 92 examined the Congress of Vienna and the diplomatic settlement of 1814 and 1815; here it is enough to recall its principles. The victorious powers, Britain, Russia, Austria, and Prussia, with a restored Bourbon France soon admitted to their councils, sought to prevent a renewal of French expansion and of revolutionary upheaval. They restored many displaced dynasties, redrew territorial boundaries to create balanced states around France, and agreed to meet periodically to manage European affairs. The Quadruple Alliance, and the more ideologically colored Holy Alliance of Russia, Austria, and Prussia, expressed a shared conviction among conservative monarchies that rulers had a collective interest in suppressing revolt.

The settlement was not simply a return to 1789. Many of the states that emerged after 1815 retained features created during the revolutionary and Napoleonic years: centralized administration, codified law, the abolition of many legal privileges, and professional bureaucracies. France's Charter of 1814 granted a constitutional framework with a two-chamber legislature and a restricted electorate. Several German states in the south, including Bavaria, Baden, and Württemberg, adopted constitutions between 1818 and 1820. The Congress Kingdom of Poland, linked to Russia through its tsar, received a constitution in 1815. Yet other rulers, such as Ferdinand VII of Spain, rejected constitutions outright, and the Habsburg monarchy and Russia remained governed without representative bodies of the modern kind.

Those who wished to change the settlement had many reasons to do so. Liberals, whose ideas Chapter 97 examined, wanted written constitutions, representative assemblies, legal equality, and protection for property and speech. Nationalists wanted political communities organized around language, history, or claims of shared culture, though this could mean anything from cultural autonomy within an empire to a sovereign state. Democrats and republicans wanted broader suffrage, and often the end of monarchy. Social reformers and early socialists, whose arguments Chapter 96 traced, argued that political reforms without changes in work and property left most people's lives untouched. The industrial and demographic pressures described in Chapters 93 to 95, including population growth, urban crowding, and the dislocation of artisan crafts, sharpened these demands, particularly during harvest failures and economic downturns.

Governments responded with surveillance and cooperation. In the German Confederation, the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 imposed press censorship, supervised universities, and established an investigative commission against political associations. Austrian policy under Prince Metternich linked domestic policing to a European doctrine of intervention, developed at congresses in the early 1820s. Secret societies flourished in response, among them the Carbonari in Italy and France, student fraternities in the German states, and officers' conspiracies in several armies. These associations were rarely large, but they provided networks, rituals, and cadres, and they linked local grievances to a wider European idea of constitutional liberty.

Historians reconstruct these events from evidence of unusual variety. Revolutionary governments left decrees, parliamentary minutes, and proclamations. Their opponents left police reports, interrogations, and diplomatic correspondence, which often exaggerated conspiratorial coordination. Newspapers and pamphlets, expanding with cheaper printing, record public argument but also the biases of editors who were frequently prosecuted. Memoirs, such as Alexis de Tocqueville's reflections on 1848, supply vivid observation but were written after the events by participants with their own reputations to defend. Visual sources, such as lithographs and caricatures, show how events were imagined as well as how they appeared. The chapter uses all of these, noting in each case whose view the source represents and whose is missing.

Restoration Europe and the Revolutionary Wave of 1820–1825

Why did revolution return to Europe after 1815?

Section 98.1

The Restoration Order, Political Repression, and the Return of Revolution

The settlement of 1815 rested on a principle of dynastic legitimacy: the claim that established ruling families possessed rights that revolutionary governments and conquerors had violated and that peace required restoring. Louis XVIII returned to France, Ferdinand VII to Spain, Ferdinand I to Naples, and the House of Savoy to Piedmont. The idea was not simply sentimental. Governments that had spent two decades at war with France and its revolutionary example believed that stability depended on the cooperation of monarchies. The congresses held at Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818, Troppau in 1820, Laibach in 1821, and Verona in 1822 expressed that cooperation, and at Troppau the eastern powers articulated a doctrine by which states that had undergone revolutionary change could be forcibly returned to order by their neighbors. Britain, which accepted the territorial settlement but disliked a general right of intervention, distanced itself from this doctrine.

Restoration was nevertheless partial. Rulers returned to thrones, but they governed societies that had been remade. The Napoleonic administrative and legal systems, introduced across France, the Low Countries, the Rhineland, and Italy, had created property holders who held land bought from the church or nobility, officials who owed careers to talent rather than birth, and citizens accustomed to legal equality. Wartime mobilization had also fostered national feeling, especially in Germany, Spain, and Italy, where resistance to French domination had mixed with demands for political change. The restored rulers could attempt to repair the old order, as Ferdinand VII did in Spain by abolishing the Constitution of Cádiz in 1814, or compromise with the new one, as Louis XVIII did by granting the Charter. In neither case could they erase what had happened.

Repression was therefore a permanent feature of Restoration politics. After the murder of the writer and Russian agent August von Kotzebue by a student in 1819, the German Confederation adopted the Carlsbad Decrees, which censored the press, supervised universities, and dissolved the fraternities, the Burschenschaften, that had demanded national unity. Austria developed an extensive apparatus of police informants and postal surveillance. Naples, Piedmont, the Papal States, and the Spanish crown pursued former officials of the Napoleonic or constitutional governments. Such measures kept open opposition small, but they also pushed it underground, where it became conspiratorial, associated with secret societies, and dependent on officers and students who could be mobilized quickly.

Opposition drew on specific constituencies. Army officers had often served under Napoleon or in the wars against him, and many were placed on half pay, passed over for promotion, or dismissed after the peace. Lawyers, physicians, journalists, and minor officials had seen opportunities close as restored regimes distrusted men of the revolutionary era. Students formed communities of argument that were intensely politicized. In the cities, artisans and merchants experienced the economic distress that followed the end of wartime demand and the poor harvests of 1816 and 1817. None of these groups was unified, and many sought only reform of particular abuses. Together, however, they formed a diffuse constitutionalist opposition that responded to the same ideas and the same examples, ones that circulated through newspapers, correspondence, and exile networks.

This chapter treats revolution as a contested political process rather than as a single event. A revolution in the full sense involves a rapid change of government or regime through extra-legal action, mass participation beyond a conspiratorial elite, and an attempt to alter the basis of political legitimacy, and often social relations as well. Many of the movements studied here fell short of that standard in some respect, and their differing character matters to their outcomes. Historical Vocabulary
Revolution and Counterrevolution

Revolution in this chapter refers to a rapid, usually violent, extra-legal transformation of government that claims a new source of legitimacy, often popular sovereignty or a constitution, and typically involves broad participation. It is distinguished from a rebellion, an uprising against authority that need not aim to change the political system; from a constitutional revolt, in which participants seek to force a ruler to accept or restore a constitution; and from a pronunciamiento, the Spanish and Latin American term for a military declaration, in which officers announce a political demand and rely on troops and political support to secure it. These categories overlap. The Spanish events of 1820 began as a pronunciamiento and became a constitutional revolution.

Counterrevolution does not simply mean the restoration of what existed before. It includes organized resistance to revolutionary change, the use of force to reverse it, and the political settlement that follows. Counterrevolutionary governments often retained changes introduced by revolution, such as the abolition of feudal dues in the Habsburg lands after 1848, while removing the political rights with which those changes had been linked. Distinguishing revolution from counterrevolution helps students avoid assuming that defeat meant a return to the status quo.

Section 98.2

The Constitutional Revolutions in Spain and Portugal, 1820–1823

The first great revolutionary movement after Waterloo began in Spain, where the political conflicts of the Napoleonic period had never been settled. During the war against France, the Cádiz Cortes had adopted the Constitution of 1812, which affirmed national sovereignty, established a unicameral legislature elected through an indirect system of male voting, and limited royal authority. It was intended to apply across the Spanish monarchy, including its American territories, although the reach of these arrangements was contested. Separately from the constitutional text, the Cortes legislated against seigneurial jurisdictions and abolished the Inquisition by a decree promulgated on 22 February 1813; the Constitution itself retained Catholicism as the exclusive religion of the state. When Ferdinand VII returned in 1814, he annulled it, restored absolutism, and persecuted its supporters. By 1819 his government faced bankruptcy, the loss of most of its American colonies to independence movements, and growing hostility from officers excluded from preferment.

On 1 January 1820, Lieutenant Colonel Rafael del Riego declared for the Constitution of 1812 at the head of troops at Cabezas de San Juan in Andalusia. The soldiers had been gathered for an expedition to reconquer the Americas, a mission many had no wish to undertake. Riego's rising did not at first succeed militarily, but the declaration inspired garrisons and cities in Galicia and elsewhere, and in March the king, finding his army unreliable, swore to uphold the Constitution. The revolution thus began as a military initiative and won through the king's isolation rather than through mass insurrection, though urban crowds and liberal clubs soon became important.

The three years that followed, known as the Liberal Triennium, were politically turbulent. The constitutional Cortes renewed and advanced measures directed at religious establishments, entailed estates, and seigneurial rights, reopening disputes that had begun during the Cádiz period; the earlier Cádiz abolition of the Inquisition was also put into effect during the constitutional regime. These initiatives affected the position of the church and nobility. Liberals split between moderates, who wanted to stabilize the constitution and conciliate the king, and radicals, the exaltados, who pressed for more thorough change and relied on political clubs and the urban militia. Ferdinand sabotaged the government while appearing to cooperate. Armed royalist bands and clerical opposition spread in rural areas, particularly in Catalonia, Navarre, and the Basque provinces, and in 1822 a royalist regency was proclaimed at Urgel. The constitutional government never established firm authority outside the cities, and the loss of the American colonies continued.

At the Congress of Verona in 1822, France, with the support of the eastern powers and over British objections, secured authority to intervene. In April 1823 a French army of roughly one hundred thousand men, commanded by the Duke of Angoulême, crossed the Pyrenees. Resistance was less than in 1808, partly because the constitutional government was unpopular in much of the countryside, and the army took Cádiz, where the Cortes had taken the king, in the autumn. Ferdinand was restored as absolute monarch. Riego was captured, tried, and executed in November 1823, and the period known to Spanish liberals as the Ominous Decade followed, with purges and exile.

Portugal had a related but distinct experience. The royal court had moved to Brazil in 1807 to escape Napoleon, and Portugal itself was governed during João VI's absence by a regency under British military influence. Resentment over this arrangement, over the commercial opening of Brazil to foreign trade, and over political exclusion produced the Porto revolt of 24 August 1820, in which officers and civilians formed a junta and called a Cortes. The Cortes drafted a constitution, promulgated in 1822, which created a unicameral legislature and limited royal authority. João VI returned to Portugal in 1821 and accepted it, but the revolution came at a price: Brazil, left in the charge of his son Pedro, declared independence in 1822. In May 1823, a movement led by the king's younger son Miguel suspended the constitution. Portugal's constitutional struggle did not end there. In 1826, Pedro, now emperor of Brazil, issued a Constitutional Charter for Portugal, and a civil war between constitutional and absolutist forces lasted from 1828 until 1834.

The Spanish and Portuguese revolutions showed several patterns that recurred. Military officers could trigger revolutions but not necessarily govern. Constitutional change quickly alienated powerful institutions, notably the church, and created divisions among its supporters. External intervention could undo a revolution that internal opponents could not. Finally, the Spanish Constitution of 1812 became a model, studied and invoked in Naples, Piedmont, and Greece, and used as a banner by liberals across the Mediterranean.

Historical Vocabulary
Constitutional Revolution

A constitutional revolution, in the usage of this chapter, is a movement whose main aim is to establish or restore a written constitution that limits executive power and creates or empowers a representative body. It is not necessarily democratic. The Constitution of Cádiz was radical for its time but based on indirect suffrage, and many Restoration-era constitutions imposed property qualifications. Constitutionalism, the principle that government should operate within legal limits, could be combined with monarchy, as in the Belgian and French cases of 1830, or opposed to it, as in the republican movements of 1848.

The term also marks a difference between sources of legitimacy. A charter, such as the French Charter of 1814, was presented as a grant by the monarch, who thereby retained a theory of sovereignty above the law. A constitution adopted by an assembly, such as the Cádiz document, implied that sovereignty belonged to the nation. Revolutions over constitutions were therefore revolutions over where authority came from.

Section 98.3

Revolution in Naples and Piedmont: The Carbonari, Constitutionalism, and Austrian Intervention

The Spanish example reached Italy within months. In the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, ruled by Ferdinand I (Ferdinand IV of Naples before 1816), the restored Bourbon government had kept many officials and officers of the Napoleonic era, including those who served under Joachim Murat, but treated them with suspicion. The Carbonari, a secret society with local lodges and an initiatory ritual, had spread among army officers, professionals, and landowners. In early July 1820, cavalry officers at Nola, led by Michele Morelli and Giuseppe Silvati, declared for a constitution, and General Guglielmo Pepe, a veteran of the Napoleonic armies, joined them with troops. The king, facing the defection of his army, accepted the Spanish Constitution of 1812 on 13 July.

The experiment was unstable. The revolution had been made by officers and conspirators, but it needed wider support, and it divided on questions that the common adoption of the Spanish text had hidden. Moderates sought a workable constitutional monarchy, radicals pressed for a broader program, and landowners in the provinces were worried about disorder. Sicily presented a separate difficulty: Palermo rose in July 1820 demanding a separate parliament and constitutional autonomy for the island, which the Neapolitan government refused, and Neapolitan troops put down the Sicilian movement. The episode revealed that constitutional revolution did not resolve conflicts of territory and regional identity, and that revolutionaries could become repressors of other movements. The king meanwhile waited for outside help.

At the Congress of Troppau in late 1820, Austria, Russia, and Prussia concluded that revolutions threatening neighboring states could justify armed intervention, and Ferdinand was invited to Laibach, where he repudiated his oath to the constitution. In February 1821 an Austrian army of about fifty thousand men crossed the Po and moved south. Pepe's forces, poorly organized and short of loyal troops, were defeated near Rieti and Antrodoco in March, and Austrian forces entered Naples on 23 March. The constitutional regime ended after eight months, followed by arrests, executions, and flight into exile.

Piedmont's revolution came in the same spring. The kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, ruled by Victor Emmanuel I, had been restored in 1814 with a hostile attitude to Napoleonic institutions. Liberal officers and aristocrats, including Santorre di Santarosa, planned a revolution to secure a constitution and, in some versions, war against Austria to free Lombardy and create an Italian kingdom under the House of Savoy. Garrisons rose in March 1821. Victor Emmanuel abdicated in favor of his brother Charles Felix, who was absent, and the young prince Charles Albert, serving as regent, proclaimed the Spanish constitution before leaving. Charles Felix repudiated the revolution, called on Austrian help, and at Novara on 8 April royalist and Austrian forces defeated the constitutionalists. Santarosa went into exile and died in 1825 fighting in the Greek cause.

The Neapolitan and Piedmontese revolutions failed for reasons visible elsewhere: their base was narrow and heavily military, their leaders divided, the monarchs were able to turn the same officers' loyalty against them, and Austria could and would intervene. They nevertheless left a legacy. The experience persuaded some Italian patriots that secret societies, rooted in officer conspiracies and dependent on regional monarchs, were inadequate, and that a broader movement was necessary. Charles Albert, who had briefly proclaimed a constitution, would become king of Piedmont in 1831 and play a part in 1848. These developments are examined in Chapter 100. Here, they are significant as evidence that a Mediterranean revolutionary wave had existed, with common ideas and personnel, and that the conservative powers had committed themselves to military enforcement of the status quo in Italy.

Section 98.4

Greece, the Decembrists, and Divergent Paths of Revolutionary Change

The Greek uprising that began in 1821 took place in a different political setting. The Ottoman Empire, which Chapter 79 examined as a major European power, governed most Greek-speaking populations of the Balkans and the Aegean. Orthodox Christians had their own religious institutions under the ecumenical patriarch, and a Greek-speaking merchant and shipping class prospered in the Mediterranean and Black Sea trades, as did a Phanariote elite that supplied administrators, including rulers of the Danubian Principalities. Ideas of national revival, shaped by schools, printing, and diaspora merchant communities, interacted with admiration for ancient Greece among western Europeans. In 1814, Greeks in Odessa founded the Filiki Eteria, a secret society that aimed at an uprising.

The revolt began in two places in early 1821. In March, Alexander Ypsilantis, an officer in the Russian army and a leader of the Eteria, crossed into Moldavia, hoping for Russian support and a general rising. It failed, and Ottoman forces crushed the movement in the Principalities. The rising in the Peloponnese, beginning in late March, had greater success. Local notables, clergy, ship owners, and warlords, among them Theodoros Kolokotronis, took the countryside and besieged Ottoman strongholds. The fall of Tripolitsa in September 1821 was followed by the massacre of Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, one of a series of atrocities committed by both sides. The Ottoman government responded with the execution of the Patriarch Gregory V in April 1821, and with the devastation of Chios in 1822.

Greek leaders drafted constitutions and held assemblies, the first at Epidaurus in January 1822, but they could not establish a unified command. Regional factions, military chiefs, island shipowners, and political leaders fought one another, and civil wars in 1824 and 1825 nearly destroyed the movement. The Sultan's appeal to Mehmed Ali, ruler of Egypt, brought Ibrahim Pasha's disciplined army to the Peloponnese in 1825, and the fall of Missolonghi in April 1826 after a long siege seemed to end the revolution. What preserved it was an international change. Philhellenism, the movement of sympathy and fundraising across Europe and the United States that attracted volunteers and made Lord Byron, who died at Missolonghi in 1824, a symbol, turned public opinion against the Ottoman government. British, French, and Russian governments, each with its own interests in the eastern Mediterranean, agreed by the Treaty of London in 1827 to press for a settlement. At Navarino on 20 October 1827, a combined fleet destroyed the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet. The Russo-Turkish war of 1828 and 1829 and the Treaty of Adrianople then forced the Sultan to accept the outcome. The London Protocol of 3 February 1830 recognized Greek independence, and the Treaty of London of 1832 established the kingdom under Otto of Bavaria.

Greece demonstrates that a revolution could succeed even when its internal organization was weak, if it served the strategic interests of the Great Powers. It also shows the cost. The new state was small, poor, and governed by a foreign monarch chosen by the powers, and its first governor, Ioannis Kapodistrias, was assassinated in 1831 amid factional conflict. It also left questions of citizenship and territory unresolved, for most Greek-speaking people lived outside the new kingdom.

The Decembrist revolt in Russia was a very different event. Many of its participants were aristocratic officers who had served in the campaigns against Napoleon and had seen western Europe. They formed secret societies, the Northern Society in St. Petersburg and the Southern Society in Ukraine, with differing programs. Nikita Muravyov drafted a constitutional monarchy with a federal structure, while Pavel Pestel envisaged a centralized republic with radical social measures. The death of Alexander I in November 1825 and uncertainty over succession, since the next brother, Constantine, had secretly renounced the throne in favor of Nicholas, created an opening. On 26 December 1825 (14 December in the Julian calendar then used in Russia), about three thousand troops assembled in Senate Square in St. Petersburg, claiming to defend Constantine's rights and a constitution. Their nominal leader failed to appear, the crowd had no clear direction, and Nicholas I ordered artillery fire. A smaller revolt in the south by the Chernigov Regiment collapsed in January 1826. Five leaders, including Pestel, were hanged in July 1826 and over a hundred others were exiled to Siberia, where some were followed by their wives.

Comparing the two events shows why similar ideas produced such divergent results. The Greek revolt was a national and religious rising against a foreign imperial government, with mass participation, a long guerrilla war, and great-power interest. The Decembrists were an elite conspiracy within an autocracy that held the loyalty of most of its army, had no international sponsor, and had no popular base. The comparison also shows the importance of what a revolution challenged. The Ottoman government was weak and distracted, while the Russian state was strong, and in 1825 it was able to treat the Decembrists as a court problem rather than a political crisis. The Decembrist memory, however, would become an important part of Russian revolutionary tradition, a subject for the separate MGU history of Russia.

The Revolutions of 1830 and the Challenge to Restoration Europe

Why did the revolutions of 1830 produce different outcomes?

Section 98.5

The July Revolution in France and the Overthrow of Charles X

The second wave of revolution began in France, whose capital had set the example in 1789. The Bourbon Restoration under Louis XVIII had operated under the Charter of 1814, which created a Chamber of Peers and an elected Chamber of Deputies chosen by a tiny electorate of the wealthiest male taxpayers, perhaps a hundred thousand men. Louis's brother, who became Charles X in 1824, had a more ambitious conception of royal and religious authority. His government compensated nobles for property lost during the Revolution, introduced harsh penalties for sacrilege, and strengthened the church's influence in education. Liberal opposition, which included deputies, newspaper editors, and journalists such as Adolphe Thiers, argued that the government was undermining the constitutional spirit of the Charter.

Electoral politics in 1829 and 1830 brought the conflict to a crisis. Charles X appointed the Prince de Polignac, a staunch royalist, as head of the ministry in August 1829. The liberal majority in the Chamber of Deputies voted an address in March 1830 declaring that the ministry did not have the confidence of the nation. The king dissolved the Chamber, and new elections in June and July returned an even larger opposition majority. The government's hope that a military expedition to Algiers, which captured the city in early July 1830, would rally support failed. On 25 July, Charles X signed four ordinances that suspended freedom of the press, dissolved the newly elected Chamber, changed the electoral law to reduce the electorate sharply, and called new elections. The ordinances were published on 26 July.

Opposition journalists published a protest, and on 27 July crowds, including printers thrown out of work by press restrictions, students of the Paris schools, artisans, and former soldiers, filled the streets. Troops under Marshal Marmont moved to restore order but were stretched thin and found barricades rising across eastern and central districts. Some units refused to fire, and the National Guard, which Charles had disbanded in 1827 but whose members still had arms and organization, joined the insurgents. By 29 July, the Tuileries had fallen. These "Three Glorious Days" cost the lives of several hundred insurgents and fewer soldiers, with estimates varying. Charles X withdrew, abdicated on 2 August, and departed for exile.

The question was what came next. Republicans and many of those who fought on the barricades, including workers and students, wanted a republic, and some invoked the memory of 1792. But the leading liberal deputies and journalists, led in the chamber by figures such as the banker Jacques Laffitte and supported by the Marquis de Lafayette, a veteran of both the American and French Revolutions, feared republican radicalism and the possibility of war with the other powers. They offered the throne to Louis-Philippe, duke of Orléans, from a junior branch of the Bourbons with a family history of sympathy for liberal causes. Lafayette's embrace of the duke at the Hôtel de Ville, with a tricolor flag, helped legitimate the choice. Louis-Philippe was proclaimed lieutenant-general and then king of the French on 9 August, a title that stressed popular rather than dynastic legitimacy.

The revised Charter was no longer a royal grant but a compact. It removed Catholicism's status as the state religion, though it described it as the religion of most French people, restored the tricolor, abolished press censorship, lowered the age and property qualifications for voters, and gave the chambers a role in proposing laws. The electorate nonetheless remained small, roughly double its previous size but still a very small minority of adult men. The revolution thus removed a dynasty and its more aggressive clerical and royalist policies, and replaced them with a constitutional monarchy based on the propertied classes. It brought no democracy and did not address the social conflicts that the barricade fighters represented. Within a few years the regime was dealing with republican revolts and working-class uprisings, notably in Lyon in 1831 and 1834, and with press restrictions of its own.

The French revolution of 1830 was important beyond France. Because it succeeded rapidly in the most closely watched capital in Europe, it encouraged opponents of existing regimes elsewhere, and it demonstrated that the Restoration order, including the principle of dynastic legitimacy, could be overturned without the great powers intervening. The new regime disclaimed any right to export revolution and signaled a non-interventionist foreign policy, which was part of why the eastern powers tolerated it. That limitation had consequences for Belgium and Poland.

Section 98.6

Belgian Independence and the International Settlement of 1830–1839

The Congress of Vienna had united the former Austrian Netherlands, the Dutch Republic's successors, and the Prince-Bishopric of Liège under William I as the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, intending a strong buffer state north of France. The kingdom brought together Protestant Dutch and mainly Catholic southern provinces with different economies, traditions, and languages, including Dutch in Flanders, French in Wallonia and among elites, and German in some districts. The south was more industrialized, especially around Liège and Ghent, and many southern industrialists benefited from Dutch trade and colonial markets, while others sought protection for their industries.

Grievances accumulated. Southern Belgians were underrepresented in government offices and the army officer corps compared with their share of the population. William's language policy, which made Dutch the official language in Flanders, antagonized French-speaking elites, and his educational and religious policies, notably efforts to control Catholic seminaries, antagonized the Catholic clergy. Press prosecutions antagonized liberals. By the late 1820s, Catholics and liberals, who had been opponents, formed a Union of Oppositions around demands for freedom of the press, education, and worship and ministerial responsibility. Economic hardship in 1829 and 1830, with high bread prices and unemployment in industrial towns, added urgency. It is therefore misleading to reduce the Belgian revolution to Catholic versus Protestant conflict, since it depended on an alliance between Catholics and liberals and on economic, administrative, and linguistic grievances as well.

The immediate spark came in Brussels on 25 August 1830, after a performance of Auber's opera La Muette de Portici, whose plot concerned a revolt in Naples. Crowds attacked government property and newspaper offices, and unrest spread. After a period of negotiation, Dutch troops attempted to retake Brussels on 23 September, but they met determined resistance from volunteers, artisans, and former soldiers fighting in the streets, and they withdrew by 27 September. A provisional government was established in Brussels, a national congress was elected, and independence was declared on 4 October. The congress, elected on a restricted franchise and dominated by lawyers, landowners, and liberal Catholics, rejected the House of Orange, chose a monarchy rather than a republic, and adopted a constitution in February 1831, among the most liberal of its time, which guaranteed freedom of the press, association, education, and religion and established a bicameral parliament with a limited king.

International diplomacy shaped everything that followed. The Dutch king appealed to the great powers, and the eastern monarchies were hostile to a revolution that threatened the Vienna settlement. But Russia was soon absorbed by Poland, Austria by Italy, and Britain and France agreed to treat the matter through a conference at London. The conference accepted Belgian independence in principle in late 1830 and declared Belgium permanently neutral. The Belgian congress chose Leopold of Saxe-Coburg, a widower of a British royal and acceptable to Britain and France, who took the throne on 21 July 1831. When William I launched a short campaign in August 1831 that routed Belgian forces, a French army intervened and the Dutch withdrew. The final treaty took years to negotiate, since William refused to accept the terms until 1839, when the Treaty of London fixed the border, divided Luxembourg and Limburg between Belgium and the Netherlands, and confirmed Belgian neutrality.

Why did Belgium succeed when other revolutions of the period failed? Domestic organization mattered: a broad coalition of Catholics and liberals, a recognized provisional government, and a constitution that satisfied propertied elites. Military circumstances mattered, since the revolution was fought out in cities where the Dutch army could not easily win and the Dutch government lacked support from allies. Above all, the geopolitics mattered. An independent Belgium suited Britain, which wanted to keep the Channel coast out of French or great-power control, and France, which was happy to see the Dutch kingdom weakened. In other words, the success depended on international recognition at least as much as on domestic victory, a pattern also observed in the Greek case.

Section 98.7

The Polish November Uprising, Russian Power, and the Failure of National Revolution

The Kingdom of Poland, often called Congress Poland, was created in 1815 as a constitutional state linked to Russia by the person of the tsar, who was king of Poland. It had its own army, a Sejm, and a constitution that guaranteed certain liberties, and under its early years it saw economic and educational development. But the tsar's viceroy, the Grand Duke Constantine, commanded the Polish army, and the tsar's government increasingly curbed the constitution, censoring the press and suppressing student and officer secret societies. The Polish political nation, composed mainly of nobles and the educated, remembered the partitions of the eighteenth century, which Chapter 79 treats as part of the transformation of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, and many had served in Napoleon's armies. Poles of the former Commonwealth now lived under Russian, Prussian, and Austrian rule.

The rising began on the night of 29 November 1830 in Warsaw. Cadets from the military school, led by Piotr Wysocki, attacked the Belvedere Palace, residence of the viceroy, who escaped, and were joined by crowds and part of the garrison. The movement was at first an uprising of young officers and radicals, but Polish political leaders, most of them aristocrats and landowners, soon took charge. General Józef Chłopicki assumed dictatorial powers and tried to negotiate with the tsar. Nicholas I, however, insisted on submission. Radicals urged war, and in January 1831 the Sejm declared that the Romanov dynasty had forfeited the Polish throne.

The war was serious. A Russian army under Field Marshal Diebitsch entered Poland in February 1831 and fought the Poles at Grochów, where neither side gained a clear victory. Polish forces won some engagements, but they were outnumbered and suffered from divided command and disagreements among their leaders, who quarreled over strategy and over social policy. Many landowning leaders resisted measures that might have mobilized the peasantry, such as the abolition of serf obligations, because they did not wish to alienate their own class. Peasants, who had little to gain from a restored Polish state governed by nobles, generally did not rally. The Russians were also hampered by a cholera epidemic that killed Diebitsch in June, but his successor Paskevich took Warsaw on 8 September 1831, and the rising's last forces crossed the border into Prussia and Austria.

The international context was decisive. Public opinion across western Europe, especially in France, Britain, and the German states, sympathized with Poland, and committees raised funds. Governments did not intervene. France's new regime, the July Monarchy, did not wish to risk war with Russia and had its own insecurity. Britain was not prepared to fight for Poland, and Prussia and Austria, which ruled Polish territories, supported Russia's suppression. Sympathy was thus real but insufficient. Poland also lacked a strategic interest that could have attracted a powerful sponsor.

Repression was severe. The constitution of 1815 was replaced by the Organic Statute of 1832, which ended Polish autonomy, and the army was disbanded and its officers deported or conscripted into the Russian service. The universities were closed and many estates confiscated. Some ten thousand or more Poles, including political leaders, officers, and intellectuals, left in what became known as the Great Emigration, settling mostly in France but also in Britain, Belgium, and elsewhere. The emigration preserved Polish political and cultural life abroad, producing writers such as Adam Mickiewicz and political groups that would take part in later revolutions across Europe, including those of 1848. It was therefore both a defeat and a seed of future movements. The uprising's failure also shaped the thinking of Polish nationalists, who debated the role of peasants, the choice between conspiracy and mass mobilization, and the relationship with other European revolutions.

Section 98.8

Revolutionary Echoes in Italy, Germany, and Switzerland: Achievements and Limitations of 1830

The July Revolution's example reverberated in central Europe and Italy. In central Italy, in early 1831, conspirators in Modena, led by Ciro Menotti, and in Bologna and other cities of the Papal States, took advantage of a political opening and the hope, soon disappointed, of French support. The Duke of Modena, Francis IV, and Marie Louise of Parma were driven out, a provisional government in Bologna proclaimed a union of the insurgent provinces, and the revolution seemed to be spreading. But France declined to intervene, and an Austrian army, invited by the pope and rulers, moved south in March 1831 and swiftly dispersed the insurgents. Menotti was executed, and the Papal States were reoccupied. The failure had an important consequence. Giuseppe Mazzini, an exile from Genoa, concluded that the old conspiratorial methods of the Carbonari had failed and founded Young Italy in 1831, a movement that aimed to mobilize the people for a unified republic.

In the German Confederation, 1830 produced uprisings in several smaller states, including Brunswick, Hesse-Kassel, Saxony, and Hanover, where rulers granted constitutions or revised them. These movements involved the mobilization of townspeople, students, and sometimes peasants, and they usually produced modest reforms. The largest political demonstration was the Hambach Festival of May 1832 in the Bavarian Palatinate, which attracted some twenty to thirty thousand people, including students, professionals, artisans, and some foreigners. Speakers demanded freedom of the press, popular sovereignty, German unity, and sympathy for Poland, and participants flew black, red, and gold flags that later became associated with German nationalism. The reaction was swift. The Confederation's governments adopted new restrictions on assembly and the press, ordered prosecutions, and increased surveillance. An attempt by a group of radicals to seize the Frankfurt police guardhouse in April 1833 failed and reinforced the repression.

Switzerland followed a different path. The Swiss Confederation had been restored in 1815 as a loose association of cantons, with aristocratic or patrician governments in many of them. In 1830 and 1831, liberal movements, referred to as the Regeneration, led to the adoption of new constitutions in about a dozen cantons, including Zurich, Bern, Lucerne, and Aargau. These constitutions expanded representation, introduced freedom of the press, and established more equal citizenship. The changes took place largely without serious military conflict and within a political system where cantons had considerable autonomy. They created a progressive group of liberal cantons, and an increasingly polarized opposition from conservative and Catholic ones, a division that led to civil war in 1847.

Comparing the outcomes helps explain the wave. Where revolutions took place in states that could be shielded from great-power intervention, or where intervention suited some of the powers, as in Belgium and Switzerland, they produced lasting change. Where a revolution challenged the territorial or strategic interests of Russia or Austria, as in Poland and Italy, it was crushed. In France, a domestic revolt succeeded because the army did not hold, and foreign powers accepted a constitutional monarchy that did not threaten them. Constitutional reform in German states succeeded where rulers were willing to compromise and failed where the Confederation and Austria could apply pressure.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Comparing the Revolutionary Waves of 1820 and 1830

What Changed

  • The first wave began on the periphery, in Spain, Portugal, and southern Italy, and was led by military officers; the second began in Paris and spread through civilian crowds, journalists, and urban militias.
  • The 1820 wave produced no lasting new states, while that of 1830 created Belgium and consolidated Greek independence.
  • Great-power response became more differentiated: intervention in Spain, Naples, and Piedmont in 1821 to 1823 was followed by acceptance of France and Belgium in 1830 and 1831.
  • Opposition movements began to reconsider conspiratorial methods, with Mazzini's Young Italy and the Great Emigration showing a turn toward mass political organization.

What Stayed the Same

  • Constitutional government, press freedom, and representation remained central demands, along with national self-determination.
  • Revolutionary success continued to depend on army behavior and on the approval or tolerance of the powers.
  • Eastern monarchies continued to treat revolution as a threat and to rely on Austrian and Russian armies to suppress it where they could.

Who experienced change most directly? Belgians, the French propertied electorate, and Swiss liberals gained new constitutional systems; Poles and Italians experienced intensified repression and exile. Who experienced greater continuity? Most rural populations, whose lives were little altered by constitutional change, and workers in France, who gained no new rights from 1830. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the same ideas yielded divergent results according to military and diplomatic circumstance, a conclusion tested again in 1848.

Section 98.9

The European Crisis of 1845–1848: Economic Hardship, Political Opposition, and Revolutionary Contagion

The revolutions of 1848 grew from a combination of long-term pressures and a sudden crisis. Among the long-term pressures were population growth that outpaced employment in many regions, the decline of artisan crafts under competition from factory production, as with the Silesian weavers whose revolt in 1844 drew wide attention, and the rising cost of land and food. Politically, many states offered narrow representation. In France fewer than a quarter of a million men could vote. The Habsburg monarchy and most German states had weak or purely advisory assemblies, and censorship limited debate everywhere. Nationalist movements, encouraged by Romantic culture, historical scholarship, and language revival, had grown among Italians, Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, and others, though their goals differed widely.

The immediate crisis began with the failure of the potato crop, which struck from 1845 and was catastrophic in Ireland, where it caused a great famine, and severe in Flanders, the Rhineland, and parts of central Europe. Poor grain harvests in 1846 raised bread prices sharply. A downturn in industry and finance followed in 1847, driven by speculation in railway building, bank failures, and a credit squeeze, and it brought unemployment among textile and construction workers just as food was most expensive. The effects were felt across social groups: peasants and urban poor faced hunger, artisans and shopkeepers faced bankruptcy, and landowners and merchants faced losses. Political dissatisfaction became tied to material distress. Governments, perceived as indifferent or incompetent, lost credibility.

Political developments in the 1840s had already shown that old orders could be unsettled. The election of Pius IX in 1846 and his early reforms, including an amnesty for political prisoners, encouraged Italian liberals and nationalists. In Switzerland, the conflict between liberal and conservative cantons produced the Sonderbund War of November 1847. In 1846, a peasant uprising in Galicia, in which peasants attacked their noble landlords in the midst of a failed Polish nationalist plot, and the Austrian annexation of the Free City of Kraków, showed the volatility of social and national conflicts. In Prussia, the United Diet convened by Frederick William IV in 1847 refused to approve a loan without political concessions. In Hungary, the Diet of 1847 and 1848 saw reformers press for fundamental change. In France, opposition leaders organized a banquet campaign from July 1847, circumventing the ban on political assemblies and publicizing demands for electoral reform.

Revolutionary groups drew on different constituencies. Liberal constitutionalists, mainly propertied and educated, wanted representative government, civil rights, and in some places national unity. Republicans and democrats, who often drew support from artisans, shopkeepers, and professionals, demanded universal male suffrage and, in many cases, a republic. Nationalists sought autonomy or independence. Workers and social reformers wanted employment, wages, and protection from the effects of competition and, in some cases, new forms of property and production. The Communist Manifesto, published in London in February 1848, set out one revolutionary argument, but its readership at the time was small, and the events of 1848 owed little to it directly.

The chronology must be stated correctly. The first uprising took place not in Paris but in Palermo, Sicily, on 12 January 1848, when crowds rose against Bourbon rule after a period of agitation, demanding the restoration of the island's 1812 constitution. Within weeks the king of the Two Sicilies, Ferdinand II, granted a constitution for his mainland kingdom and later Piedmont, Tuscany, and the Papal States followed. These Italian events preceded Paris, but the February Revolution in France, because of France's prestige, the speed of the monarchy's collapse, and the proclamation of a republic, gave the movement continental momentum. News traveled quickly through newspapers, the telegraph in some regions, railways, and the exchange of letters. Within three weeks of Paris, movements in the German states, Vienna, and Hungary produced concessions.

Historians describe this spread as revolutionary contagion, a useful term if it is not misunderstood. Paris did not cause the revolutions elsewhere. The grievances were local, and in many places the pressure had been building for months. But a successful revolution in a major capital changed expectations, lowered the perceived risk of acting, and convinced rulers that they might not be able to rely on their troops. It also provided a model, the reform banquet, the barricade, the provisional government, and the demand for a constituent assembly, that could be adapted elsewhere. Section 98.10

The February Revolution in France and the Establishment of the Second Republic

By 1847, the July Monarchy had become identified with a policy of resisting reform. François Guizot, the dominant minister, defended the restricted franchise on the principle that wealth and capacity should determine political rights, and his government won elections in 1846 through patronage and a narrow electorate. A series of scandals involving corruption in high places, the economic downturn, and Guizot's refusal to extend the vote, even modestly, increased the opposition. The reform banquet campaign, supported by moderate liberals, dynastic opposition, and republicans, reached its peak in the winter of 1847 and 1848.

The crisis came when the government forbade a large banquet, planned in Paris for 22 February 1848. The opposition deputies at first decided to cancel, but students and workers appeared in the streets in any case. Crowds clashed with the municipal guard on 22 February, and on 23 February the National Guard, a bourgeois militia that was expected to support order, hesitated and some units shouted for reform. The king dismissed Guizot that day, hoping to calm the city. That evening, soldiers outside the foreign ministry on the Boulevard des Capucines fired into a crowd, killing around fifty people. Insurgents carried bodies through the streets on carts and called for revenge. Barricades, built in the eastern and central districts, spread through the night.

On 24 February, with the army demoralized and unable to hold the city, Louis-Philippe abdicated in favor of his grandson, the Count of Paris, and left. A crowd entered the Chamber of Deputies, where the regency was proposed but rejected, and a list of names for a provisional government was shouted, including the poet and deputy Alphonse de Lamartine, the radical deputy Alexandre Ledru-Rollin, and others. A parallel list proposed by the republican newspaper La Réforme included the socialist writer Louis Blanc and a worker, Albert, a mechanic. At the Hôtel de Ville, the groups merged, and on 24 and 25 February the republic was proclaimed politically. The elected Constituent Assembly would give the republic its formal parliamentary proclamation on 4 May 1848. The provisional government that resulted was led by the elderly Dupont de l'Eure, with Lamartine as foreign minister and its most effective orator, and it combined moderate republicans with democratic and socialist members.

The provisional government took a series of decisions of lasting significance. It abolished the death penalty for political offenses, ended press and association restrictions, and, through a decree of 5 March 1848, established direct universal male suffrage for the election of a Constituent Assembly, expanding the electorate from roughly a quarter of a million qualified men under the July Monarchy to more than eight million eligible men. It established the National Guard as open to all citizens. It also faced a symbolic confrontation: on 25 February, crowds demanded the adoption of the red flag as the republic's emblem, and Lamartine, in a speech that became famous, persuaded them to keep the tricolor, arguing that it represented the nation's history. The republic's foreign policy was also set out. Lamartine declared that France would not wage a war of conquest or interfere with other peoples' rights, calming Britain and the eastern powers.

The new republic embodied competing programs. For moderate republicans, including Lamartine, the republic was a political form, representative government based on universal suffrage, with civil liberties, and nothing more. For democratic republicans such as Ledru-Rollin, the republic required political democracy and an active state, and many supported measures like popular education and progressive taxation. For socialists, the republic must guarantee the right to work, and the political revolution was a first step to a social one. Workers, for their part, expected action on unemployment and wages. The distinction between political republicanism and demands for a transformation of employment and property relations lay at the center of the revolution's subsequent history.

The elections for a Constituent Assembly, held on 23 April 1848 on the new universal suffrage, delivered a surprise to radicals. Participation was high, but the voters, many of them peasants guided by local notables and priests, returned a large majority of moderate republicans and a substantial group of monarchists, with a small minority of radicals and socialists. The Parisian left, whose militants had expected the countryside to follow, felt betrayed. A demonstration on 15 May, in which crowds invaded the Assembly and attempted to proclaim a new government, was suppressed, and its leaders, including Blanqui, were arrested. The Assembly's majority was now determined to establish order. The new government's tax increase, the 45 centime surcharge on direct taxes imposed in March, antagonized the peasants, who blamed the republic. The stage was set for the clash between the political and social revolution.

Section 98.11

The June Days: Workers, the National Workshops, and the Division of the French Republic

Paris in the spring of 1848 had tens of thousands of unemployed men, as businesses closed during the political crisis, and the provisional government acted to provide relief. On 25 and 26 February, under pressure from crowds, it proclaimed the principle that government must guarantee work to all citizens. It set up two distinct institutions that are often confused. The first was the Luxembourg Commission, a body of workers' and employers' delegates meeting at the Luxembourg Palace under Louis Blanc and Albert to study labor questions. Blanc had argued for years, notably in his book on the organization of labor, for state-supported social workshops owned and directed by the workers themselves, a cooperative alternative to competitive capitalism. The Commission had no funds and no executive power, and the government never adopted his scheme.

The second, quite different institution was the National Workshops, organized from late February by the minister of public works, Alexandre Marie, and directed by a young engineer, Émile Thomas. They were not Blanc's workshops. They were a relief program that registered unemployed men and put them to work on earthmoving and similar projects, paying a modest daily wage, or a lower sum when no work was available. By May and June, about one hundred thousand men were enrolled. Their administration was loosely organized, many projects were unproductive, and they were hated by property owners as expensive, a burden on taxpayers, and a source of political danger. Many moderates and conservatives saw the workshops as the embodiment of the socialist threat, and linked them, wrongly, to Blanc. The confusion hurt Blanc and the left in the political debate.

After the May events, the Assembly's majority moved against the workshops. An executive commission replaced the provisional government, Blanc was excluded, the Luxembourg Commission was dissolved, and the Assembly voted to reduce the workshops. On 21 June the government decreed that unmarried workers aged eighteen to twenty-five would be enrolled in the army and others sent to work in the provinces, the beginning of dissolution. For many workers the decision was not merely a loss of income. It meant that the republic they had fought for was abandoning its promise of work.

On 22 and 23 June, crowds gathered and barricades rose again, mostly in the eastern and central working-class districts, including the Faubourg Saint-Antoine. The insurgents were not led by a single political organization, and they did not issue a coherent program, although some raised slogans of a democratic and social republic. The Assembly declared a state of siege, handing power to General Louis-Eugène Cavaignac, the war minister, who had served in Algeria. He used regular troops, the mobile guard, and the National Guard, including units brought in from the provinces. From 23 to 26 June, fighting was fierce, with artillery used against barricades. Archbishop Denis Affre of Paris was fatally wounded on 25 June trying to mediate. Casualties were heavy. Estimates of insurgent deaths run into the thousands, in addition to hundreds of soldiers and guardsmen killed, and about twelve thousand people were arrested. Military commissions, working through exceptional administrative procedures rather than ordinary criminal trials, ordered transportation for 4,276 accused persons. That number refers to decisions imposing the penalty, not people actually sent overseas: subsequent grants of clemency and changes in detention status left approximately 459 June insurgents ultimately transported to Algeria in 1850, with some later transfers to French Guiana.

The June Days changed French and European politics. They broke the alliance of the February Revolution between middle-class republicans and workers. For the propertied classes, they appeared to confirm that universal suffrage and a democratic republic could unleash social revolution, and property owners increasingly looked for order. For many workers, the republic that suppressed them was a betrayal. The Assembly extended working hours, restricted political clubs and the press, and in July banned women from political clubs. The constitution adopted in November 1848 established a republic with a single legislative assembly and a strong president elected directly by universal male suffrage, a design that would prove fateful.

In the presidential election of 10 December 1848, the voters, who included peasants disillusioned with the republic and tax increases, workers, and conservatives, chose Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte, the nephew of Napoleon I, by a decisive margin, roughly three quarters of votes cast, over Cavaignac and the republican and socialist candidates. The name Bonaparte stood for order, national glory, and social peace to many voters. His later seizure of power in December 1851 and the establishment of the Second Empire are examined in Chapter 99. For the present chapter, the June Days and the election show how the fear of social revolution led many of the French to turn from the republic they had just created.

Multiple Perspectives

Paris in June 1848: Four Perspectives on the Republic

A Parisian worker enrolled in the National Workshops: The republic had promised the right to work, and the workshops, however poorly organized, were the only visible sign of that promise. Being told to enlist or leave Paris looked like the abandonment of the February Revolution by the government it had created, and it threatened what little income remained to a family already in distress. The barricades were a defense of that promise as much as an attack on a government.
A moderate republican in the Assembly: The republic was a political achievement, secured by universal suffrage and civil liberties, and it was now menaced by disorder. The workshops were costly and badly managed, and the May invasion of the Assembly showed that the capital's crowds would not respect an elected majority. Restoring order was a condition of keeping the republic alive, even if the means were severe.
A property-owning liberal, such as a shopkeeper or landowner: Taxes had risen, trade was depressed, and the workshops seemed to be a subsidy to the idle, financed by those who worked. The language of the right to work sounded like an attack on property itself. The June fighting appeared to confirm fears that the capital might fall into the hands of a revolutionary mob, and to support a firm government to protect persons and property.
An advocate of social reform, such as a follower of Louis Blanc: Political equality without economic security was incomplete, and the workshops were a caricature of the cooperative organization of labor that the Luxembourg Commission had been asked to study and was never allowed to implement. The government's identification of Blanc with a program he had never been permitted to carry out was a distortion, and it shut the door on a peaceful path to social reform.

Historical note: These groups did not have equal power. The Assembly and its generals controlled the army and the state, and the insurgents did not. The purpose of the comparison is not to equate their claims but to show why the republic split along lines of class and property.

Section 98.12

The Italian Revolutions of 1848–1849: Sicily, Milan, Venice, Rome, and Piedmont

The Italian peninsula in 1848 was divided among a number of states, as Chapter 92 described: the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, the Papal States, the Grand Duchy of Tuscany, smaller duchies, and the Austrian-ruled provinces of Lombardy and Venetia. Italians could mean very different things by the idea of Italy. Some imagined a federation of existing states under the leadership of the pope, others a kingdom under the House of Savoy, and others, following Mazzini, a unified republic. Questions of Italian unity and of the removal of Austrian control were linked but distinct.

The year opened in Sicily. After the uprising in Palermo on 12 January 1848, the insurgents, led by Giuseppe La Masa and others, formed a provisional government, and within weeks the movement had taken most of the island. Ferdinand II granted a constitution to the mainland kingdom at the end of January. Sicilians demanded a separate state, and the Sicilian parliament declared the Bourbon dynasty deposed in April. In March, Charles Albert, king of Piedmont, granted a constitution, the Statuto, which became the basis for later Italian constitutional government, and the Grand Duke of Tuscany and the pope followed with their own concessions.

The decisive confrontation was with Austria. In Vienna, Metternich's fall on 13 March encouraged risings in the Austrian Italian provinces. In Milan, a popular insurrection known as the Five Days, 18 to 22 March, forced Marshal Radetzky and his Austrian garrison to withdraw to the fortresses of the Quadrilateral, the defensive line between Verona and Mantua. In Venice, a crowd released political prisoners, and on 22 March the lawyer Daniel Manin proclaimed the Republic of San Marco. Charles Albert, hesitating between fear of republicanism and hope of territorial gain, declared war on Austria on 23 March and sent troops into Lombardy. Volunteers from Tuscany, Naples, and the Papal States joined the campaign.

The war revealed the movement's divisions. Charles Albert wished to extend Piedmont's power, and was suspicious of republicans and volunteers, while Mazzini and Garibaldi, who had returned from exile in South America, wanted a popular war and a republic. In April, Pius IX declared in an allocution that he could not wage war on a Catholic power, a blow to hopes for a papal-led national movement. The Neapolitan king recalled his troops. Radetzky, reinforced, defeated the Piedmontese at Custoza on 24 and 25 July 1848, and an armistice followed in August, with Austria reoccupying Milan.

Revolutionary politics, however, continued in the Papal States, where discontent combined with the murder of the papal minister Pellegrino Rossi in November 1848. The pope fled to Gaeta in the Kingdom of Naples. In Rome, an elected Constituent Assembly proclaimed the Roman Republic in February 1849, governed by a triumvirate that included Mazzini. The republic abolished the temporal power of the papacy, introduced reforms in taxation, education, and church property, and tried to organize a defense. Charles Albert, under pressure from democrats at home, renewed the war against Austria in March 1849, only to be defeated decisively at Novara on 23 March. He abdicated in favor of his son, Victor Emmanuel II, who made peace with Austria but retained the Statuto.

The Roman Republic's defeat came from French intervention. Louis-Napoleon's government, wishing to appeal to Catholic opinion and prevent Austria from becoming the pope's sole protector, sent an expeditionary force under General Oudinot, which landed at Civitavecchia in April 1849. Garibaldi's volunteers repelled the first assault, but the French besieged Rome and entered on 3 July after weeks of bombardment. Garibaldi led a retreat across central Italy, with Austrian and other forces in pursuit, during which his wife Anita died. Venice held out under Manin through a siege marked by cholera and shortages until August 1849, when it surrendered.

The defeats were severe, but they did not erase the movement. Piedmont retained its constitution, and thus became a rallying point for Italian liberals. The failures of the Mazzinian and papal visions encouraged a shift toward a Piedmont-centered solution, as many patriots, including Manin, concluded that unity required a strong state with an army. Garibaldi and Mazzini remained symbols. The detailed account of how Cavour, Garibaldi, and diplomacy produced unification belongs to Chapter 100. For Chapter 98, the Italian revolutions show the intersection of constitutional demands, national war, republican experiment, and international intervention, with the Austrian and French armies deciding the outcome.

Section 98.13

The German Revolutions of 1848 and the Frankfurt National Assembly

The German Confederation, created in 1815, comprised some thirty-nine states, including the Austrian and Prussian lands in part, the medium-sized kingdoms of Bavaria, Saxony, Hanover, and Württemberg, and many small principalities and free cities. It had a Federal Diet at Frankfurt composed of envoys, not elected representatives. Before 1848, liberals and nationalists in the German states demanded constitutional government, press freedom, and some form of national unity, but they differed on what unity meant, and the existence of Austria, a great power with large non-German populations, complicated every proposal. Chapter 97 examined the ideas; the revolution now put them to the test.

In late February and early March 1848, news from Paris triggered mass meetings and petitions in the south-western German states, particularly Baden, where liberal leaders drew up lists of demands: freedom of the press, jury trials, citizen militias, and an elected German parliament. Rulers, fearful of unrest, made concessions and appointed liberal ministers, the so-called March ministries. In Prussia, the largest northern state, Frederick William IV hesitated. In Berlin, demonstrations in mid-March led to clashes between troops and civilians on 18 March, and the king, after promising reforms, saw the city rise in barricades. After a day of fighting in which several hundred civilians were killed, he ordered the army to withdraw. On 21 March he rode through Berlin wearing the black, red, and gold colors, an act that suggested he would support German unity, and he agreed to a Prussian constituent assembly.

The revolutionary demands combined several aims. They included civil rights, such as press freedom and equality before the law, constitutional government, popular representation, and national unification. They also included social demands, for peasants in the south and west sought the end of remaining feudal dues, and artisans sought protection from competition from industry, a demand that sat uneasily with liberal commitments to economic freedom. A self-appointed assembly of liberal and democratic leaders, the Pre-Parliament, met at Frankfurt in late March and early April and arranged elections for a National Assembly. Radicals, led by Friedrich Hecker and Gustav Struve, tried to start a republican uprising in Baden in April but were swiftly defeated by troops.

The Frankfurt National Assembly opened on 18 May 1848 in the Paulskirche. Its members, elected by a wide male franchise that varied by state, numbered several hundred and were largely educated professionals: lawyers, officials, professors, and teachers, with a sizeable group of landowners and few artisans or peasants. This composition earned it the later nickname of a "parliament of professors," although the label exaggerates. Heinrich von Gagern, a moderate liberal, became its president. The Assembly was organized in loose political groupings named after the taverns where they met, ranging from conservative to radical. In June, it elected the Austrian Archduke John as imperial regent and set up a provisional central authority, but this authority had no army, no tax system, and no administration of its own, and depended on the individual states.

The Assembly faced fundamental questions. What territory should the new Germany include? The debates centered on the Habsburg lands: if Austria joined, the new state would incorporate non-German populations, and Austria would dominate it; if Austria was excluded, Prussia would lead. Supporters of the first approach were later called grossdeutsch and those of the second kleindeutsch. The Assembly also had to decide the powers of the monarch, the relationship between the national parliament and the states, and the rights of citizens. A crisis in Schleswig-Holstein, where German nationalists backed the duchies against Danish claims, tested the Assembly's weakness. When Prussia made an armistice with Denmark at Malmö in September, the Assembly, lacking its own forces, had to accept it after a bitter vote, and the decision led to riots in Frankfurt in which two conservative deputies were killed. These events illustrate the dilemma described in the next sections: a parliament that claimed national sovereignty but depended on the armies of the states it sought to unite.

The Imperial Act concerning the Fundamental Rights of the German People, Frankfurt National Assembly, December 1848

Context: The Assembly debated fundamental rights in the summer and autumn of 1848 before taking up the structure of the state, a decision defended on the ground that rights defined a free citizenry but later criticized because it delayed constitutional decisions about power. Its authority was disputed: Austria did not accept it, several states refused to publish it, and the Federal Diet formally repealed it in 1851.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim extract) The act, in the editor's summary of its main provisions, stated that every German possessed national citizenship and equality before the law, that class privileges and noble titles as legal distinctions were to be abolished, and that personal freedom, freedom of movement, and the inviolability of the home were guaranteed. It established freedom of belief and conscience and ended the privileged position of any church in civil rights. It protected freedom of the press and abolished censorship, freedom of assembly and association, and freedom of teaching. It guaranteed property while providing for the abolition of remaining feudal burdens, and it required legal safeguards against arbitrary detention and punishment and provided for independent judicial authority. This paraphrase condenses a long legal text into the headings most relevant to this chapter. It should not be cited as the words of the Assembly, and students should consult the German primary-law text and publication chronology reproduced by the German Bundestag or a separately identified, verified scholarly translation for exact wording. The words above are not a translation of any one legal clause.

Analysis: The act shows what liberal revolutionaries meant by freedom: legal equality, civil liberty, religious liberty, a free press, and a property regime in which feudal dues were converted or abolished. It is a statement of intention by an elected assembly composed largely of educated men, and it says little about how the rights would be enforced. It does not give women political rights, nor does it indicate how the many peasants, journeymen, and artisans who participated experienced it. Its later fate is revealing, for the failure of the Assembly to compel the states to apply it demonstrates the gap between proclamation and power. Yet its language shaped later German constitutional ideas, including the rights catalogues of later constitutions.

Section 98.14

Vienna, Prague, and the Revolutions within the Austrian Empire

The Austrian Empire was a composite state of many nationalities: Germans, Czechs, Magyars, Poles, Ukrainians, Croats, Serbs, Slovenes, Romanians, Slovaks, Italians, and others, governed by the Habsburg dynasty through a bureaucracy centered on Vienna. Metternich's system relied on censorship, police, and the support of the nobility and church. Financial difficulties, an aging and mentally limited emperor, Ferdinand I, and a divided court weakened its capacity to respond to crisis.

The revolution in Vienna began on 13 March 1848, when students and workers demonstrated outside the Lower Austrian Diet, soldiers fired, and the crowd, joined by suburban workers, became insurgents. Court leaders, unable to rely on the army, dismissed Metternich, who fled to England. The emperor promised a constitution, abolished censorship, and allowed the formation of a National Guard and an academic legion of students. In April, a constitution was published that was criticized as too conservative, and on 15 May, new demonstrations compelled the government to promise an elected constituent assembly. The court fled to Innsbruck. A Reichstag convened in Vienna in July, and in September, it passed one of the revolution's most lasting measures, the abolition of the remaining feudal obligations of the peasantry, with compensation to landowners, after a motion by the deputy Hans Kudlich.

In the Bohemian lands, the revolution took a different shape. Czech leaders, including the historian František Palacký, petitioned for equal rights for the Czech language and autonomy for Bohemia within the empire, but they refused to send delegates to the Frankfurt Assembly, rejecting the idea that Bohemia was part of a German nation. Palacký argued that the Habsburg monarchy protected the small nations of central Europe from Russian and German domination. The Slavic Congress, held in Prague in June 1848, brought together delegates from Slavic peoples of the empire. Its members were divided over aims, though many sought a reorganized empire of equal nations. During the congress, a popular unrest in Prague led to street fighting, and the commander in Bohemia, Prince Alfred Windischgrätz, bombarded the city and put down the uprising by 17 June. The Slavic Congress dispersed.

Vienna's revolutionaries faced an army that was becoming more reliable. Radetzky's victory at Custoza in July showed that the monarchy could win in Italy, and Windischgrätz's actions in Prague showed that a general could suppress an urban revolt. In October, a mutiny of Viennese troops sent to fight Hungary and the murder of the war minister, Count Latour, led to a second revolution in Vienna, which the Reichstag's radical members supported. The court fled again, this time to Olmütz. Windischgrätz, joined by forces of the Croatian governor Josip Jelačić, besieged and bombarded Vienna and stormed it on 31 October. About two thousand people died, and executions followed, including that of Robert Blum, a delegate of the Frankfurt Assembly, in November, a killing that infuriated German democrats.

The result was that the Habsburg dynasty found a new leadership. Prince Felix zu Schwarzenberg became minister-president in November, and in December, Ferdinand abdicated in favor of his eighteen-year-old nephew, Franz Joseph, who was not bound by the promises made in March. The Reichstag was moved to Kremsier in Moravia, where its members drafted a federal constitution, but Schwarzenberg dissolved it in March 1849 and imposed a centralizing constitution that was never put into effect. The Habsburg revolutions show why nationalism could both challenge and sustain the empire: Czech and Slavic leaders sought to preserve a reformed monarchy, while German and Hungarian leaders pursued different national programs, and the court exploited these divisions.

Section 98.15

The Hungarian Revolution, Nationalities Conflict, and the Struggle for Imperial Survival

Hungary was a kingdom with its own constitution, diet, and noble political nation, joined to the Habsburg monarchy by the person of the ruler. Before 1848, a reform movement among nobles demanded constitutional change, the use of Hungarian rather than Latin or German in administration, and the end of noble tax privileges and serf obligations. Its leading figures included Count István Széchenyi, who emphasized economic modernization, and the lawyer and journalist Lajos Kossuth, an orator who campaigned for national and constitutional demands. The Hungarian Diet of 1847 and 1848 was the setting for these arguments.

After news of the Paris revolution, Kossuth delivered a speech on 3 March 1848 demanding constitutional government for the whole monarchy. In Pest on 15 March, a crowd of students and writers, among them the poet Sándor Petőfi, demanded a list of reforms and freed political prisoners. The Diet, meeting at Pozsony (Pressburg, now Bratislava), passed a series of reforms known as the April Laws, which the king confirmed. They established a ministry responsible to a parliament in Pest under Count Lajos Batthyány, extended the franchise to a wider group of property holders and educated men, abolished serfdom and feudal dues with state compensation to landowners, equalized taxation, and guaranteed press freedom and other civil liberties. For the peasants, the abolition of serfdom was the revolution's most important result, and it persisted, in modified form, after the defeat.

The problem lay in what Hungarian leaders meant by the Hungarian nation. They understood it as a political community that included all inhabitants of the historic kingdom, with Hungarian as the language of government. But roughly half of the population of the kingdom were not Magyars. Croats, Serbs, Romanians, Slovaks, Ukrainians, and Germans had their own languages, churches, and political leaders, and many sought recognition of their own rights. Croatian leaders wanted the autonomy of the Triune Kingdom of Croatia-Slavonia-Dalmatia and rejected control by the Hungarian ministry; Serbs in the south demanded a separate voivodeship; Romanians in Transylvania gathered at Blaj in May 1848 to demand national rights and rejected a union of Transylvania with Hungary; Slovak leaders also issued demands. The Hungarian government, whose reforms had been designed for the whole kingdom, saw these demands as a threat to the integrity of the state and refused most of them.

The Habsburg court exploited these divisions. Josip Jelačić, appointed ban of Croatia in March 1848, became the champion of Croatian claims against Hungary and, increasingly, of the dynasty against the revolution. In the summer, fighting broke out in the south between Hungarians and Serbs. In September, Jelačić led his forces across the Drava into Hungary, and the court's repudiation of the April Laws and conflict with the Hungarian government escalated into war. Hungary's defense was organized by a Committee of National Defense under Kossuth, who raised a national army, the Honvéd, with officers drawn partly from experienced veterans of the Habsburg army. After a winter of imperial advance, in which Windischgrätz took Pest in January 1849, Hungarian forces under generals such as Artúr Görgei and Józef Bem won a series of victories in the spring of 1849, recovering most of the country. On 14 April, the Diet at Debrecen declared the Habsburg dynasty deposed and elected Kossuth governor-president.

The declaration ended any chance of compromise and encouraged the Habsburg government to seek help from Russia. Nicholas I, concerned that a Hungarian victory would encourage Polish revolt, sent an army of some two hundred thousand men under Field Marshal Paskevich into Hungary in June 1849, while Austrian forces under Julius Haynau attacked from the west. Hungarian forces were outnumbered and divided, and Kossuth resigned in favor of Görgei, who surrendered to the Russians at Világos on 13 August 1849. The Austrians executed thirteen Hungarian generals and Batthyány in October, and imprisoned others. Kossuth went into exile, and Hungary was placed under military administration.

The Hungarian revolution illustrates the entanglement of constitutional liberalism, nationalism, social reform, and imperial power. Hungarian leaders sought freedom from Vienna but did not extend equal freedom to other nationalities in their lands, which provided the dynasty with allies. The tension also shows that a revolution could be at once progressive in one direction and exclusionary in another. The long-term consequences were large: Habsburg centralized absolutism in the 1850s proved unsustainable, and the compromise of 1867 that created Austria-Hungary, examined in Chapter 106, was in part a response to what 1848 had revealed about Hungarian strength and the weakness of the monarchy.

Historical Vocabulary
The Nationalities Question

The nationalities question is a historians' term for the problem of how multi-ethnic and multilingual states, especially the Habsburg monarchy, the Ottoman Empire, and Russia, could reconcile the claims of different peoples to language rights, autonomy, or independence with the survival of the larger state. In 1848 the question took concrete form: Hungarian liberals defended a constitutional kingdom but resisted autonomy for Croats, Serbs, Romanians, and Slovaks; Czech leaders asked for rights inside a reformed Austria; German nationalists at Frankfurt debated whether Austria's non-German lands could belong to a German state.

The term is easily misused. It does not mean that every ethnic group wanted a separate nation-state, and many leaders in 1848 sought equality inside existing empires. Nor were national categories always fixed, since many people, especially peasants, identified by religion, locality, or social status before they identified by nation. The nationalities question, then, was as much a problem of competing political projects as of ethnic diversity.

Section 98.16

Frankfurt's Failure and the Restoration of Central European Authority, 1848–1849

By the autumn of 1848, the Frankfurt National Assembly was in a vulnerable position. Prussia had suppressed its own assembly in November, dismissing it from Berlin to Brandenburg and then dissolving it, and imposed a constitution in December by royal decree. Austria, under Schwarzenberg, had made clear that it would not permit the breakup of the monarchy to create a German state, and the Habsburg suppression of the Vienna revolution, including the execution of Blum, had weakened the democratic left. The Assembly had to decide how to proceed without the two largest German powers in agreement.

The Assembly finally adopted a constitution on 27 March 1849, and the next day it elected Frederick William IV of Prussia as emperor of the Germans. The constitution provided for a hereditary emperor, a bicameral parliament with a lower house elected by universal manhood suffrage, a federal structure, and the declaration of rights adopted in December. It was a liberal constitution, one that gave an emperor real powers while making ministers responsible to parliament. But it was a kleindeutsch solution in practice, since Austria had rejected incorporation of its lands, and it relied on Prussia. A delegation traveled to Berlin to offer the crown. On 3 April 1849, Frederick William refused. He objected to receiving a crown from an elected assembly rather than from the princes, because he held that it undermined divine-right monarchy, and he did not wish to antagonize Austria and Russia. His refusal removed the Assembly's central purpose.

The Assembly's remaining members, many of whom were liberals, withdrew, and the more radical rump moved to Stuttgart, where it was dispersed by Württemberg troops in June. The constitution's supporters then turned to popular uprisings for its defense. In May 1849, risings took place in Saxony, where Dresden's fighting was suppressed with Prussian help and included the composer Richard Wagner and the anarchist Mikhail Bakunin among its participants, and in the Rhineland and the Palatinate. In Baden, soldiers mutinied, the grand duke fled, and a revolutionary government attempted to defend the constitution. Prussian troops commanded by Prince William, the future Wilhelm I, put down the uprising, and the fortress of Rastatt fell on 23 July 1849. Dozens were executed, and thousands emigrated, including many who went to the United States.

Frederick William IV, meanwhile, sponsored an Erfurt Union of German states under Prussian leadership, but Austria, backed by Russia, forced Prussia to abandon the plan in the Punctation of Olmütz in November 1850, and the old German Confederation was restored in 1851. The Federal Diet also annulled the Frankfurt fundamental rights that year. The year 1848 thus ended with a restored Confederation, and a Prussia and Austria that remained rivals.

Why did the German and Habsburg revolutions fail? The answer involves several elements. The Assembly's authority was moral and legal but not military, and it depended on armies commanded by monarchs who had made concessions under pressure but kept the means of coercion. Its members debated for months while the conservative forces regrouped, a delay that later critics emphasized. Divisions among liberals, democrats, and nationalists were deep, and fears of social disorder led many liberals to prefer cooperation with monarchs to alliance with radicals. The nationalities problem made a German nation-state impossible to define without conflict. And foreign powers, notably Russia, were prepared to intervene in support of conservative regimes.

Counterrevolution did not return to the pre-1848 order. The Habsburg regime kept the abolition of feudal obligations, and it built a more centralized bureaucracy. Prussia retained a constitution, albeit one that gave the king extensive powers, and a three-class franchise weighted by taxation. Many German states kept some form of constitution and parliament. Thus the revolutions did not succeed, but the political landscape that followed differed from that of 1847, with new institutions, new political parties, and a public that had learned to organize. Chapter 100 examines how Bismarck and Prussian power later accomplished a national unification that did not come from a parliament.

Section 98.17

Revolutions Beyond the Major Capitals: Wallachia, Moldavia, Switzerland, and the European Periphery

Historians of 1848 have increasingly looked beyond Paris, Vienna, and Berlin to regions where revolutions occurred in different political and imperial settings. The Danubian Principalities, Moldavia and Wallachia, were under Ottoman suzerainty and, since the Treaty of Adrianople in 1829 and the Organic Regulations of the 1830s, under Russian protection, with princes appointed by the Ottoman sultan with Russian approval and governed by councils dominated by great landowners, the boyars. Their political elites had been educated in Paris and elsewhere, and a generation of young boyars and intellectuals formed secret societies and discussed constitutional and national reform. The Principalities, for the purposes of this chapter, are dated by the Julian calendar used locally unless otherwise stated.

In Moldavia, a petition for reform in Iași in April 1848 was quickly suppressed by Prince Mihail Sturdza, and many leaders fled. Wallachia was the center of the more significant movement. On 9 June (Julian calendar), or 21 June (Gregorian calendar), 1848, a revolutionary proclamation issued at Islaz demanded political and civil equality, an elected assembly, press freedom, the emancipation of the peasantry with land provision, the abolition of rank, and the end of Russian protection in favor of constitutional autonomy under Ottoman suzerainty. A provisional government took power in Bucharest, where leaders such as Nicolae Bălcescu and Ion Heliade Rădulescu tried to broaden support. The program on landownership was contested: peasants wanted land, while many boyars and moderate liberals wished to limit expropriation, and the government's commission on property failed to resolve the matter. The revolution also had to define its political community, including the status of Jews, Roma, and other groups, and its treatment of these questions was uneven and contested.

The movement could not survive its imperial environment. The Ottoman government, nervous about Russian intervention, at first took an ambiguous position, but Russia demanded suppression. Ottoman troops under Fuad Pasha entered Bucharest in September 1848, followed by Russian forces. The revolutionary leaders were arrested or exiled, and in 1849 a convention between the Ottoman and Russian governments, signed at Balta Liman, strengthened the authority of the two powers. The Wallachian revolution is not best seen as only an unsuccessful precursor of the Romanian unification of 1859 to 1866, as later national narratives have presented it. It was a movement shaped by the specific problems of an imperial borderland, including serfdom-like peasant obligations, boyar privilege, and the rivalry of Ottoman and Russian authority, and its leaders held varied conceptions of what political community they were building.

Switzerland offers a contrast. The conflict between liberal and radical cantons and the conservative Catholic cantons of the Sonderbund, which formed a separate defensive league in 1845, led to a brief civil war in November 1847. Federal forces under General Guillaume-Henri Dufour defeated the Sonderbund in about three weeks with relatively few casualties, partly because Dufour sought a swift, relatively restrained victory before outside powers could intervene. Austria and France had connections to the conservative cantons, but they did not intervene militarily during the November 1847 fighting; the wider revolutions of 1848 subsequently made intervention still less practicable. The victory allowed the Swiss to draft a federal constitution in 1848, which created a federal state with a bicameral parliament and a federal executive, the Federal Council, while retaining cantonal authority. Switzerland thus achieved a durable constitutional settlement, as a victory of a liberal side within a small state that had benefited from the weakness of outside powers at a critical moment.

Britain provides a different comparison. The Chartist movement, which demanded universal manhood suffrage, secret ballots, and other political reforms through a People's Charter, mobilized large numbers in the 1830s and 1840s. In 1848, Chartists planned a mass meeting at Kennington Common in London on 10 April and a procession to deliver a petition to Parliament. The government, led by Lord John Russell and with the Duke of Wellington planning defense, mobilized special constables and troops, and the meeting was peaceful. The petition was presented, but its signatures were found to include false ones, and Parliament rejected it. Chartism declined thereafter. In Ireland, a small Young Ireland insurrection in July 1848 failed against a background of famine. Britain did not experience revolution in 1848, a fact explained by historians in several ways: its existing parliamentary system, the strength of the state's response, the earlier reforms examined in Chapter 99, the disunity of the Chartists, and a more favorable economic position. The British case shows that political protest and revolutionary overthrow were different things.

Section 98.18

The People of Revolution: Workers, Peasants, Women, Students, Soldiers, and Political Elites

Revolutions are often narrated through leaders and assemblies, but they were made by large numbers of ordinary people whose motives and expectations varied. In the cities, artisans and workers made up much of the crowds that built barricades, particularly in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, and Milan. They were not typically factory workers in the later sense, since in most of Europe the industrial workforce was still small and craft production predominated. Skilled craftsmen in trades such as furniture-making, printing, tailoring, and building were often the most politically active. Their grievances included unemployment, competition from cheaper goods and unlicensed labor, the loss of guild protections where these had been abolished, and, in 1848, the high price of bread. Their political goals included universal suffrage, the right to organize, and in France and some German states, a state role in providing work.

Students and professionals supplied much of the revolutionary leadership and were prominent in Vienna, Berlin, and German university towns, where student organizations, such as the Viennese Academic Legion, provided armed units and political organization. Middle-class liberals wanted constitutional guarantees and feared disorder from below, as the June Days in Paris and the radical movements in Germany and Austria showed. Landowners and nobles took part in some revolutions, notably the Hungarian and Polish, where nobles led national movements, while in others they were among the targets. Soldiers were crucial but ambiguous: officers made the pronunciamientos of 1820 and joined the Decembrist and Polish risings, rank-and-file troops sometimes refused to fire on crowds, as in Paris in 1830, and in other cases armies, such as those of Radetzky and Windischgrätz, proved loyal and effective.

Peasants constituted most of Europe's population, and their goals were often distinct from those of urban revolutionaries. They wanted the abolition of feudal dues, labor services, and seigneurial jurisdiction, access to forests and pasture, and relief from taxes. In 1848, peasant pressure produced abolition of serfdom in Hungary, in the Austrian provinces, and in the German states where remnants persisted. Once these gains were secured, many peasants lost interest in further revolution and in some cases became supporters of order, a pattern visible in Austria and Prussia, and in France, where they voted for Louis-Napoleon. The French peasantry's resentment of the forty-five centime tax and of Parisian dominance aligned many with conservatives. Peasants could also be mobilized against revolutions, as in Galicia in 1846, and in Transylvania and Croatia, where national leaders and imperial officials presented the dynasty as protector against landlords.

Women participated in many forms but were excluded from voting and from representative institutions. In Paris, women founded newspapers and clubs, among them the Voix des femmes, edited by Eugénie Niboyet, and some, such as Jeanne Deroin, campaigned for women's political rights. They joined demonstrations, supported strikers, made ammunition and bandages, and in some cases fought at barricades. The French government prohibited women from attending political clubs in July 1848. In German lands, women such as Louise Otto-Peters published political journals and organized women's associations, and Mathilde Franziska Anneke took part in the Baden uprising. In Vienna, a democratic women's association was founded in the summer of 1848. They also worked in less visible capacities, raising funds, sheltering fugitives, and keeping households functioning during shortages. Their exclusion from formal politics, and the fact that many demands were not met, helped shape the later women's movements that Chapter 102 examines.

Religious and ethnic minorities also took part in the revolutions. Jews, for instance, joined revolutionary movements in Vienna, Pest, Berlin, and elsewhere, and pursued legal equality, which several states granted in 1848 and revoked afterward, while also encountering outbreaks of anti-Jewish violence in some places. Revolutionary governments often claimed to represent a people, but local communities did not always accept their authority. National movements spoke in the name of the people, yet actual participation varied widely among villages, towns, linguistic groups, and religious communities, as the Hungarian and Wallachian examples show. These variations are important evidence against treating revolutions as expressions of a single popular will.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Revolutionary Neighborhood: The Faubourg Saint-Antoine, Paris

The Faubourg Saint-Antoine, in the east of Paris, had long been an artisan district, known for its workshops, especially furniture-making, and associated with popular uprisings from 1789 onward. Its streets held small workshops, courtyards with families living above or beside their workplaces, and wineshops and cafés where news and politics were discussed. Men and women worked in trades connected to wood, metal, and textiles, and many depended on the seasonal rhythm of orders. Because craftsmen worked in small shops with mutual acquaintance, neighborhood and trade ties provided networks for mobilization.

In 1830 and again in February 1848, barricades in such districts helped overthrow regimes. In June 1848, the faubourg was one of the centers of resistance, and its streets saw some of the most intense fighting. Daily life in these months was shaped by shortages and unemployment, by newspapers and clubs which provided information and political education, by rumors that circulated quickly in crowded streets, and by the needs of families whose breadwinners were in the workshops, at the barricades, or in prison. Women and children contributed to the barricades by bringing materials and food and by tending the wounded. After the June Days, the neighborhood suffered arrests and deportations, and the memory of repression remained a part of local political culture. Sources for this history include police and judicial records, newspapers, memoirs, and later oral and visual traditions, and each reflects interests of those who created it. The authorities, for example, described insurgents in terms of criminality, while republican writers often emphasized heroism.

Section 98.19

Why Revolutions Succeeded or Failed: Military Power, Coalition Divisions, and International Intervention

A comparative look at the revolutions shows that outcomes depended on a few recurring factors. The first was the army. Revolutions succeeded quickly where the army wavered, as in Spain in 1820, in Paris in 1830, or in Paris, Vienna, and Berlin in March 1848. They failed where the army remained loyal and was reorganized, as in Austria in the second half of 1848, in Prussia after the autumn, and in Russia throughout. Even where armies refused to suppress a revolt at first, rulers often recovered, because the officer corps and administration remained intact. Revolutionary governments lacked comparable instruments: Frankfurt had no army, the Hungarian Honvéd had to be built from scratch, and the Italian volunteers were brave but poorly coordinated.

The second factor was the unity of the revolutionary coalition. The groups that joined to overthrow regimes usually differed about the next step. Liberals wanted constitutions with property qualifications and feared mass participation, democrats wanted wider suffrage, nationalists wanted state-building or autonomy, and socially oriented groups wanted economic changes. Once the initial aim was achieved, divisions emerged, whether in the Spanish Cortes between moderates and exaltados, in Paris in June 1848, or in Frankfurt among conservatives, liberals, and democrats. Rulers and conservative forces exploited these divisions, offering concessions to some groups, as with the Habsburg abolition of peasant obligations, in order to detach them.

The third factor was international intervention. France intervened in Spain in 1823 and Rome in 1849, Austria in Naples, Piedmont, and central Italy, Russia in Poland and Hungary, and Prussia in Baden and Saxony. Revolutions that did not require great-power approval, or that coincided with the interests of some of the powers, survived, as in Belgium, Greece, and Switzerland. Revolutions that challenged the territorial or ideological interests of Russia or Austria usually did not. The role of diplomacy was therefore decisive.

Other factors included finance, since revolutionary governments often lacked tax systems and credit, administrative capacity, as shown by the Hungarian government's remarkable ability to build a state and the Frankfurt Assembly's inability, and social composition, as with peasants who were satisfied by agrarian reform or who stayed aloof from urban politics. The speed of the events mattered too: initial concessions created an impression of victory that obscured the unreformed state apparatus.

Historical Debate

Why Did Most Revolutions Fail to Achieve Their Immediate Political Programs?

The question: Between 1820 and 1849, many revolutions achieved dramatic initial success and then collapsed. Was the failure primarily the result of divisions within the revolutionary movements, of the military and diplomatic strength of the established regimes, or of deeper social and economic conditions?

Interpretation A: Internal divisions and social conflict. Many historians, beginning with participants such as Marx and Engels in their contemporary writing, have emphasized class and national divisions. On this view, the middle classes feared the workers and peasants, who in turn did not share liberal priorities, and nationalities in multinational states pursued mutually incompatible goals, so the revolutionary coalitions fell apart before they could consolidate. The June Days in Paris and the conflict between Hungarians and their neighbors are central examples. Writers such as Lewis Namier, in a more critical vein, argued that the leadership lacked political realism, and Jonathan Sperber has stressed how the divisions between democrats and moderates, and between regions and social groups, shaped the outcomes.
Interpretation B: Military force and international power. Other historians emphasize that revolutionary movements were defeated by armies. Established governments retained control of the military, and when that control was threatened, they sought foreign help. The Russian intervention in Hungary, French action in Rome, and Austrian victories in Italy and Bohemia decided outcomes on the battlefield rather than through internal collapse. On this view, internal divisions mattered, but they were not necessarily decisive: unified revolutions would still have faced overwhelming force. Recent scholarship such as Christopher Clark's account of 1848 stresses the transnational dimension of both revolution and repression.
Interpretation C: Longer-term achievements. A third view cautions against defining failure only by immediate aims. The abolition of feudal obligations in central Europe, the Belgian and Swiss constitutions, the Piedmontese Statuto, and the experience of universal suffrage in France and Germany changed the political landscape, so that the revolutions may be considered partial successes that transformed expectations and institutions, even when revolutionary governments fell.

Evidence: The interpretations rest on different kinds of evidence: parliamentary debates, police reports, and memoirs for political divisions; military and diplomatic records for intervention; and electoral and legal records, including changes in land tenure and constitutions, for longer-term effects. The evidence does not force a choice among them, and most recent work combines elements of all three.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how we think of 1848. If divisions were decisive, the revolutions were avoidable failures of political judgment; if force was decisive, they were defeated by superior power; if long-term results matter, they were an incomplete but consequential transformation. The debate also bears on how later movements, including those studied in Chapters 111 and 132, thought about revolutionary strategy.

Section 98.20

Revolution, Counterrevolution, and the Transformation of European Politics

The revolutions of 1820, 1830, and 1848 shared a set of aims and a political vocabulary, but they occurred in different circumstances and had different participants. The Mediterranean revolutions of 1820 were largely military conspiracies pursuing constitutional change on the Spanish model. The revolutions of 1830 were more urban and broader, creating a constitutional monarchy in France and a new state in Belgium, and failing in Poland and Italy. The revolutions of 1848 were the largest, engaging peasants, workers, middle-class professionals, and national movements across the continent, and the sharpest in their conflicts over class and nationality. A feature that links them is the way governments used the same combination of military force, diplomatic cooperation, censorship, and selective compromise to restore authority.

The achievements were considerable even if partial. Constitutional government was established in Belgium, France under a limited franchise, Piedmont, and Switzerland, with legislatures in several German states and in Prussia. Greek and Belgian independence created new states. Feudal obligations were abolished in the Habsburg lands, with lasting effects on rural society. Press freedom, assembly rights, and civil equality were proclaimed and, although often curtailed, gave political movements new models. In France, universal manhood suffrage, introduced in 1848, remained a central feature of political life, though under the later empire it was managed by the state. Slavery was abolished in the French colonies in 1848, a measure linking colonial and metropolitan politics.

The limits were as important. Revolutionary solidarity broke down where class, nationality, religion, property, and institutions divided participants. Liberals feared radicals, nationalists clashed with other nationalists, and rural communities pursued different goals from urban ones. Governments exploited this: they gave peasants emancipation, offered constitutions to moderates, used one nationality against another, and relied on the army's discipline. The suppression of revolutions was followed by arrests, executions, exile, press controls, and the dissolution of political associations. In many states, however, rulers also recognized that they could not simply restore the old order. The Habsburg regime centralized administration and modernized law, Prussia retained a constitution and parliament, and Napoleon III's France combined authoritarian rule with plebiscites and economic development.

Revolutionary networks, constitutional traditions, and national aspirations survived. Exiles from Poland, Hungary, Italy, Germany, and France lived in London, Paris, Brussels, Switzerland, and the United States, forming communities that preserved political ideas and organized future movements. Kossuth toured Britain and the United States, Mazzini organized from London, and many German democrats of 1848 helped shape politics in the United States. In the longer run, the Piedmontese constitutional monarchy, the Prussian state, and the Habsburg compromise provided the institutional bases of later developments. Many of the leaders who had been liberal revolutionaries came to accept state power as the means of unification, a shift that Chapter 100 examines.

Finally, the revolutions changed the character of European politics. They demonstrated the power of mass mobilization and the difficulty of controlling it. They separated political from social revolution, since after the June Days the idea of a social republic became associated with a threat to property and order, and socialist movements, as Chapter 102 shows, would develop their own organizations. They contributed to the conservative view that stability required a balance of reform and authority, and they led governments to adopt reforms, including those covered in Chapter 99, in order to forestall future upheaval. The mid-century settlement was neither a restoration of 1815 nor a triumph of revolution.

Cause and Effect

The Revolutionary Crisis of 1848 and Its Outcomes

Long-term causes

Political exclusion under restricted franchises and censorship; the unresolved legacy of the French Revolution and Napoleonic reform; the growth of national consciousness; social dislocation from population growth, urbanization, and the decline of artisan crafts; peasant grievances over feudal dues.

Short-term causes

Harvest failures and the potato blight of 1845 to 1846; the commercial and financial crisis of 1847; rising unemployment and food prices; refusal of rulers to reform; the example of Palermo and Paris; the spread of political banquets and clubs.

Event and immediate effects

Revolutions in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Venice, and Pest in early 1848; concessions of constitutions, abolition of censorship, and in places of feudal dues; assemblies at Frankfurt, Paris, Vienna, and elsewhere.

Long-term consequences

Counterrevolution by 1849 and a period of repression; survival of agrarian reform and some constitutions; exile networks; Napoleon III's empire; new approaches to national unification; a reassessment of the relationship between social and political reform.

Historical complexity: Economic crisis alone does not explain the revolutions, because the same hardships affected places without revolution, such as Britain and Russia. Political exclusion alone does not either, for it had existed for decades. The outbreak required a convergence of economic distress, political opposition, loss of confidence by rulers, and an example that showed resistance was possible. The outcomes similarly depended on divisions among revolutionaries, the loyalty of armies, and the actions of foreign powers, not on any single cause.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined three waves of revolutionary challenge to the political order established after Napoleon's defeat. Its central argument is that between 1820 and 1848 European societies repeatedly contested the Restoration settlement, as constitutional liberals sought limits on monarchical power, national movements demanded autonomy or independence, republicans challenged hereditary rule, and workers and social reformers advanced competing visions of political and economic justice. The revolutions produced extraordinary political mobilization, but revolutionary coalitions were divided by class, nationality, religion, and conflicting ideas of sovereignty, while established governments recovered power through armies, intervention, compromise, and the fragmentation of their opponents.

The Restoration order rested on dynastic legitimacy, conservative cooperation, and repression, but it could not eliminate the effects of two decades of revolution and war, which had left new property relations, legal equality, administrative systems, and national feeling. Officers, students, professionals, and artisans formed secret societies and constitutionalist networks. The Spanish pronunciamiento of 1820 began the first wave, which spread to Portugal, Naples, and Piedmont and was ended by French and Austrian intervention in 1821 and 1823. The Greek revolt, which began in 1821, succeeded because of philhellenic sentiment and great-power interests, culminating in Navarino and the recognition of independence in 1830, while the Decembrist revolt of 1825 showed the failure of an elite conspiracy against a strong autocracy.

The revolutions of 1830 began with the overthrow of Charles X in the Three Glorious Days of July, a revolution in which crowds, journalists, and defecting soldiers produced a constitutional monarchy under Louis-Philippe with a limited electorate. The Belgian revolt created an independent constitutional monarchy, recognized by the powers because it suited their interests, though the Netherlands accepted the settlement only in 1839. The Polish November Uprising was defeated by Russia, and the Great Emigration carried Polish politics into western Europe. Italian risings were crushed by Austrian troops, while Swiss cantons and some German states adopted constitutional changes. A comparison of outcomes shows that success depended on both domestic organization and international circumstances.

The crisis of 1845 to 1848 combined economic distress with political exclusion. Harvest failures, the potato blight, and the financial crisis of 1847 intensified resentment of regimes that refused reform. The revolution began in Palermo on 12 January 1848, before the February Revolution in Paris, which overthrew Louis-Philippe and established the Second Republic. Universal manhood suffrage, press freedom, and the National Workshops marked the republic's early phase, but disagreement between moderates and advocates of a social republic, combined with financial difficulties and rural resentment, led to the June Days, in which the army under Cavaignac crushed a workers' uprising. The distinction between the National Workshops and Louis Blanc's proposals is essential for understanding this conflict. The election of Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte in December 1848 reflected the republic's loss of its working-class base and the appeal of order.

In Italy, the revolutions of 1848 and 1849 combined constitutional change with a war against Austria. Constitutions were granted in several states, Milan and Venice expelled Austrian forces, and Charles Albert of Piedmont went to war, but divisions among patriots, the pope's withdrawal, and Austrian military recovery at Custoza and Novara led to defeat. The Roman Republic, led by Mazzini and defended by Garibaldi, fell to French intervention in July 1849, and Venice surrendered in August. Italy's unification was not achieved, but Piedmont retained its constitution and the revolutionary experience shaped later strategy.

In central Europe, revolutions in Berlin and Vienna forced concessions, and the Frankfurt National Assembly attempted to write a constitution for a united Germany. It faced the problems of defining the nation's boundaries, reconciling Austria and Prussia, and acting without an army or administration. Habsburg forces under Windischgrätz and Jelačić suppressed uprisings in Prague and Vienna, and Frederick William IV's rejection of the imperial crown in 1849 ended Frankfurt's work. In Hungary, the April Laws abolished serfdom and created a responsible ministry, but Hungarian leaders' resistance to the claims of Croats, Serbs, Romanians, and Slovaks gave the dynasty allies, and Russian intervention decided the war in 1849. Counterrevolution restored authority, but the abolition of feudal obligations and the survival of some constitutions meant that it did not restore the pre-1848 order.

The comparative sections have shown that revolutions were made by diverse groups, including workers and artisans, students, professionals, peasants, women, soldiers, and nobles, whose aims differed. Peasants often gained what they sought, the abolition of feudal obligations, and then withdrew from revolutionary politics. Women took part in journalism, clubs, and barricades while being excluded from formal politics. Revolutions in peripheral regions, such as Wallachia, Moldavia, and Switzerland, followed their own imperial and domestic logics, and Britain's Chartist movement shows the difference between protest and revolution.

The analysis of outcomes identified several decisive factors: the loyalty and effectiveness of armies, the unity of revolutionary coalitions, and international intervention, along with finance, administration, and social composition. The Historical Debate showed that historians weigh internal divisions, military force, and long-term achievements differently, and that a careful assessment must distinguish immediate defeat from long-term failure.

The legacy of the revolutions was considerable. Constitutional government expanded, new states appeared, feudal obligations ended in much of central Europe, exile networks preserved political traditions, and governments learned to combine repression with reform. The separation of political from social revolution after the June Days influenced later socialist and conservative politics. These themes lead into Chapter 99, which examines reform and political change in Britain, France, and the European states, and Chapter 100, which examines Italian and German unification and the remaking of central Europe.

Key Terms

Restoration
The post-1815 return of many pre-revolutionary dynasties and the attempt to stabilize Europe through conservative cooperation, while retaining some changes from the revolutionary era.
Legitimacy
In Restoration diplomacy, the principle that established dynasties had rights that revolutions and conquest could not extinguish; in revolutions, the competing claim that authority derived from a constitution or the people.
Revolution
A rapid, extra-legal transformation of government, usually with mass participation and a new claim to legitimacy.
Counterrevolution
Organized resistance to revolutionary change and the use of force or politics to reverse it, often retaining some of the changes it opposed.
Pronunciamiento
A military declaration of a political demand, as in Riego's revolt in Spain in 1820.
Constitutionalism
The principle that government should be limited by a written or established legal framework, which could coexist with monarchy.
Charter
A constitutional document presented as a grant from a monarch, such as the French Charter of 1814, in contrast to one adopted by an assembly.
Constitution of Cádiz
The Spanish constitution of 1812, with a single elected chamber and limited monarchy, which served as a model for revolutionaries in 1820 and 1821.
Carbonari
Italian secret society with local lodges and initiation rituals, active in constitutional conspiracies, especially in southern Italy.
Troppau doctrine
The principle, advanced by the eastern powers in 1820, that states which underwent revolution could be forced back to order by armed intervention.
Philhellenism
European and American sympathy for the Greek cause, expressed in fundraising, volunteering, and publication, which helped shape great-power policy.
Filiki Eteria
The Greek secret society founded in 1814 at Odessa to prepare an uprising against Ottoman rule.
Decembrists
Russian officers who attempted a constitutional coup against Nicholas I's accession on 26 December 1825 (14 December Old Style).
July Monarchy
The regime of Louis-Philippe, king of the French, from 1830 to 1848, based on a restricted propertied electorate.
Great Emigration
The exile of thousands of Poles, especially to France, after the failed uprising of 1830 and 1831.
Young Italy
The movement founded by Giuseppe Mazzini in 1831 to mobilize Italians for a unified republic through education and insurrection.
Hambach Festival
A large political gathering in the Palatinate in May 1832 that demanded freedom, national unity, and popular sovereignty and led to renewed repression.
Revolutionary contagion
The spread of revolutionary action through example, communication, and shared conditions, without implying a single coordinating plot.
Barricade
A street obstruction built of paving stones, carts, and furniture, used in urban uprisings to block troops and symbolize popular resistance.
Universal manhood suffrage
The right of all adult men to vote, introduced in France in 1848, but excluding women.
National Workshops
The French government's public works program for the unemployed in 1848, distinct from Louis Blanc's proposed cooperative social workshops.
Social republic
The idea that a republic should guarantee work and social protection, not only political rights.
State of siege
An emergency regime that transfers authority to the military, used in Paris in June 1848.
Constituent assembly
An elected body charged with drafting a constitution, as in Paris, Frankfurt, Vienna, and Rome in 1848 and 1849.
Grossdeutsch and kleindeutsch
Competing visions of German unification, with or without Austria's German-speaking lands.
April Laws
The Hungarian reforms of 1848 that created a responsible ministry, abolished serfdom, and extended civil rights.
Nationalities question
The problem of reconciling the claims of different peoples with the survival of multinational states, especially the Habsburg monarchy.
Feudal obligations
Dues, labor services, and legal dependencies owed by peasants to landlords, abolished in much of central Europe in 1848.
Bonapartism
A political style combining a leader's claim to popular legitimacy, appeals to order and national glory, and authoritarian government, associated with Napoleon I and Louis-Napoleon.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the political ideas examined in Chapter 97 behaved when they met armies, courts, assemblies, and one another. Between 1820 and 1848, constitutionalism, nationalism, republicanism, and social reform became organized political forces, and the contest between them and the Restoration order shaped nineteenth-century Europe. The revolutions revealed which forms of change governments would accept, which they would crush, and which depended on the decisions of foreign powers.

The chapter also reveals recurring patterns. Revolutionary success depended on the loyalty of armies and on international circumstances at least as much as on popular mobilization. Coalitions that were united against a regime broke apart over what should replace it, and the separation of political from social revolution after the June Days influenced both conservative and socialist thought. Nationalism could support revolution or undermine it, especially in multinational states, a problem that would return in the Habsburg monarchy, in the Balkans, and in the settlements after the First World War examined in Chapter 113.

Later developments were shaped by the revolutions' aftermath. Chapter 99 turns to the reforms through which governments adapted, and Chapter 100 to the unifications of Italy and Germany, which were achieved not by revolutionary parliaments but by states, armies, and diplomacy. Exile communities, constitutional precedents, and the abolition of feudal obligations continued to influence politics, and the memory of 1848 informed later movements, including the mass politics examined in Chapter 102.

Within Volume IV, this chapter marks the point at which the legacy of the French Revolution, the ideologies of the early nineteenth century, and the social transformations of industrialization came together in open conflict. Its lesson is not that revolutions were pointless, nor that they were doomed, but that their outcomes depended on contingent circumstances: who controlled the army, who recognized the new government, and whether those who made the revolution could agree on what it was for. Understanding those contingencies is essential for reading the rest of the century, and for resisting the assumption that the later outcomes were inevitable.
