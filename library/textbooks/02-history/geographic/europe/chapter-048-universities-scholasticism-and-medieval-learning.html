Universities, Scholasticism, and Medieval Learning

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between the eleventh and the early fourteenth centuries, the schools of Latin Christendom turned a loose collection of teachers, texts, and students into permanent institutions with statutes, degrees, privileges, and a shared method of argument. They did so by drawing on Greek, Byzantine, Arabic, and Hebrew learning as well as on Latin inheritance, and the institutions they created proved far more durable than any of the kingdoms that sponsored them.

In the Italian city of Bologna, visitors can still see the tombs of thirteenth and fourteenth century professors of law. Several are carved with lecture scenes, showing a master seated in a high chair with rows of students bent over books before him. These monuments tell historians something that written records only partly convey: that by the later Middle Ages, advanced learning had become a recognized profession, one whose practitioners and their families thought it worthy of public memory. A century and a half earlier, no such image could have been carved, because the institution it depicts had not yet taken shape. Teaching existed in abundance, in monasteries, cathedral cloisters, and the households of individual masters, but the corporate community of scholars that medieval people eventually called a universitas was still in formation.

This chapter examines how that formation occurred and what it made possible. It begins with the older world of monastic and cathedral schools, shows how the expansion of towns, churches, courts, and law created a demand for trained men, and then follows the movement that placed unfamiliar bodies of knowledge, above all the works of Aristotle and the scientific and medical literature of the Islamic world, into the hands of Latin scholars. It next explains how teachers and students organized themselves at Bologna, Paris, Oxford, and elsewhere into corporations with legal standing, how those corporations structured degrees and curricula, and how the characteristic method of scholastic argument turned reading into questioning and questioning into disciplined debate.

The chapter then turns to the intellectual substance of the period: the absorption of Aristotle, the theological syntheses of figures such as Peter Lombard, Bonaventure, and Thomas Aquinas, the controversies and condemnations that tested the limits of inquiry, and the professional disciplines of law and medicine that gave universities much of their practical importance. It closes with the social world of learning, the students, masters, and mendicant friars who populated it, and with an assessment of medieval learning as a system linking Latin Europe to Byzantium and to the Islamic and Jewish worlds of the Mediterranean.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, medieval learning should not be treated as a waiting room for the Renaissance or the Scientific Revolution. Its scholars asked questions that mattered within their own frameworks, and they should be understood on those terms. Chapter 57 will examine Renaissance humanism and its distinctive attitude toward classical texts, and Chapter 81 will examine the Scientific Revolution, but neither follows automatically from the developments traced here. Second, the chapter focuses on institutions, methods, and exchange rather than on a catalogue of famous philosophers. Individual thinkers appear as participants in schools, faculties, and networks that shaped what they could ask and how they could answer.

Historians reconstruct this subject from an unusually rich body of evidence: thousands of surviving manuscripts, many carrying the marginal notes of readers; university and college statutes; papal and royal privileges; lecture notes and recorded disputations; library catalogues; student letters and formularies; the records of lawsuits between scholars and townspeople; and visual evidence such as the Bologna tombs and manuscript illuminations of classrooms. This evidence is overwhelmingly clerical and male in authorship and concentrates on those who reached the schools, so the chapter also asks who was absent from the lecture hall, including women, most laypeople, and Jews and Muslims, who were generally excluded from Christian university degrees yet contributed profoundly to the knowledge those degrees certified.

The chapter belongs to Part VII, whose theme is the demographic, commercial, religious, and institutional expansion of Europe between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. It follows Chapter 47's treatment of the crusading movement and precedes Chapter 49's broader portrait of medieval society and everyday life. Its place in Volume II is also significant. Having traced the formation of Latin Christendom, the Orthodox world, and the medieval states, the volume here examines one of the institutional creations of that expansion that endured beyond the Middle Ages, even as its curricula, methods, and social meaning were repeatedly challenged and transformed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what learning meant in the High Middle Ages and why the idea of an intellectually dormant "Dark Age" is inadequate.
  2. Trace the development from monastic and cathedral schools to the universities of the thirteenth century.
  3. Analyze how translation from Greek and Arabic into Latin, sometimes through Hebrew or vernacular intermediaries and often through multilingual collaboration, enlarged the body of knowledge available to Latin scholars.
  4. Compare the corporate organization of Bologna and Paris and explain why there was no single university model.
  5. Describe the faculties, degrees, texts, and teaching practices of the medieval university.
  6. Define scholasticism as a method of textual analysis and disputation rather than a fixed body of doctrine.
  7. Assess the opportunities and difficulties created by the reception of Aristotle and his Arabic and Greek commentators.
  8. Interpret the theological work of Peter Lombard, Bonaventure, and Thomas Aquinas as part of a broader debate about faith and reason.
  9. Evaluate the condemnations of 1270 and 1277 without reducing them to a conflict between religion and science.
  10. Connect the revival of Roman law, the growth of canon law, and the development of university medicine to the institutional needs of church, state, and town.
  11. Identify who participated in and who was excluded from university culture, including women, laypeople, and religious minorities.
  12. Contextualize medieval learning within a wider Mediterranean intellectual system that included Byzantium and the Islamic and Jewish worlds.

Historical Context

The educational institutions examined in this chapter grew out of the Latin Christian world that Part V traced from the post Roman kingdoms through the Carolingian empire. Learning in the early medieval West had been preserved and organized primarily by the church. Monasteries copied manuscripts and trained monks, bishops maintained clerical education, and rulers such as Charlemagne, examined in Chapter 31, sponsored reforms of schooling and script to secure a literate clergy and a workable administration. Late Roman education in grammar, rhetoric, and the liberal arts, together with the writings of Augustine, Boethius, Cassiodorus, and Isidore of Seville, supplied the intellectual capital on which this early medieval culture drew.

By the eleventh century that inheritance was being supplemented by a series of changes explored elsewhere in Part VII. Agricultural expansion and population growth, examined in Chapter 42, enlarged the number of people the land could support and freed some families to send sons to school. The commercial revival and urban growth traced in Chapter 43 created towns with markets for books, lodging, and teaching. The reform movement of Chapter 44 emphasized clerical discipline and law, and the monarchies and principalities of Chapters 45 and 46 expanded written administration and royal justice. Each of these developments required people who could read, write, calculate, argue, and interpret legal texts.

The world beyond Latin Christendom was equally important. In Byzantium, the Greek literary and philosophical tradition had never been interrupted, and Constantinople retained schools, libraries, and a vigorous tradition of commentary on ancient authors. In the Islamic world, translation movements of the eighth to tenth centuries had rendered Greek, Syriac, and other works into Arabic, and scholars writing in Arabic, among them Muslims, Christians, and Jews, had critiqued, systematized, and extended the sciences and philosophy they inherited. Contact between these worlds and Latin Europe intensified through Iberia, Sicily, southern Italy, the Crusader states examined in Chapter 47, and the Italian trading networks of the Mediterranean. The Great Schism of 1054 and its consequences, examined in Chapter 40, did not stop the movement of texts and scholars between Greek and Latin worlds, though it altered the institutional setting in which they met.

Students should also keep clear what this chapter does not cover. Everyday life for the great majority of medieval people, peasants, townspeople, and women, belongs to Chapter 49, and the Renaissance reuse of classical texts belongs to Part IX. The late medieval changes in universities, including the founding of new universities in central and northern Europe and the pressures of plague and schism, belong mostly to Part VIII, though this chapter occasionally looks forward to them. Here the focus remains on the formative period, roughly 1050 to 1320, when the principal institutions, methods, and bodies of knowledge of medieval higher learning took shape.

The evidence for this period differs from that of earlier chapters in Volume II. Instead of the sparse documentation of the early Middle Ages, historians have thousands of manuscripts, whose scribes, owners, and annotators can sometimes be identified, along with statutes, charters, and records of disputes. Modern editions of university documents, such as the long running collections of Parisian and Bolognese charters, make these sources accessible, but they must be read critically. Statutes describe how institutions wished to operate, not necessarily how they did, and lecture notes preserve what students recorded, not always what masters said. Historians also distinguish between contemporary testimony, such as the chroniclers and letter writers who described student life, and later university traditions that sometimes invented glorious foundation dates and legendary founders.

Section 48.1

What Was Medieval Learning?

To understand the institutions of the High Middle Ages, one must first ask what contemporaries meant by learning. The Latin word studium described both the activity of study and the place where it occurred, and the people who taught were called magistri, masters. Within a cathedral, the scholasticus was the official responsible for the school, and the term gave rise to the later word "scholastic." Learning was not conceived as a separate secular enterprise. It was bound to the church, whose clergy supplied nearly all teachers and students, and its purposes were largely religious, legal, and administrative: to understand scripture, to administer sacraments, to interpret law, and to manage the written business of bishoprics, monasteries, and courts.

Latin was the principal language of scholarship across Latin Christendom, and its role cannot be overstated. Because a student from Poland or Scotland and a master from Italy could meet in a classroom where all spoke and wrote the same learned language, scholarship acquired a transregional character that political boundaries did not match. Latin was, however, a learned language acquired through schooling and not a mother tongue for anyone. Vernacular languages, whether Romance, Germanic, Slavic, or Celtic, carried an enormous literature of their own, and Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, and Church Slavonic sustained other scholarly worlds. Medieval learning was therefore multilingual, but Latin was the language of the university.

Literacy in this period deserves careful handling. Historians generally agree that the proportion of the population able to read and write Latin remained small, concentrated among clergy and a growing number of lay administrators, merchants, and lawyers. Yet the period saw a notable expansion of what has been called pragmatic literacy, the use of written records in royal government, estate management, town administration, and commerce. Documents multiplied, and with them the need for people who could prepare and interpret them. Because this was a manuscript culture, in which every book was copied by hand on parchment, books were expensive and scarce, and access to them shaped who could study.

Older narratives sometimes depicted the centuries between antiquity and the Renaissance as a "Dark Age" of intellectual stagnation. Modern scholarship has decisively rejected that view as applied to the High Middle Ages. Although the early medieval West had lost access to much Greek learning and had fewer institutions of advanced study than the Roman Empire at its height, Carolingian and later scholars developed new schools, preserved and copied ancient texts, and produced original work in theology, grammar, music, computation, and law. By the twelfth century, the pace of change was rapid, and contemporaries themselves sensed that they lived in an age of new learning. Medieval knowledge cannot be understood simply as a waiting room for later developments, and the chapter treats it as a historical culture in its own right, with its own problems, authorities, and achievements.

Section 48.2

Monastic and Cathedral Schools before the Universities

The institutional inheritance on which the universities built was assembled from monasteries, cathedrals, and episcopal households. Monastic schools trained monks, primarily in reading, chant, scripture, and the writings of the church fathers, and they maintained libraries and scriptoria. Cathedral schools, attached to the seat of a bishop, trained the secular clergy who staffed parishes, chapters, and bishops' administrations. The Carolingian reforms of the late eighth and ninth centuries, including Charlemagne's Admonitio generalis of 789, had called for schools in cathedrals and monasteries and promoted the standardization of Latin and the copying of texts. The Third Lateran Council of 1179 and the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 later required cathedrals to provide for the teaching of clerics, though contemporary enforcement varied considerably.

During the eleventh and twelfth centuries, some cathedral schools became unusually prominent. Chartres, under bishops and teachers such as Fulbert in the early eleventh century, attracted students interested in the liberal arts and classical authors. Laon, associated with Anselm of Laon, became a center of biblical commentary. Paris, where teachers gathered on the Île de la Cité and later on the left bank of the Seine, drew students with its masters of dialectic and theology. The monastery of Bec in Normandy, home to Lanfranc and then Anselm, who later became archbishops of Canterbury, developed a notable tradition of rational theology. Anselm's famous formulation of theology as faith seeking understanding captured an intellectual ambition that later scholastic thinkers would pursue far more systematically.

Historians have been careful not to overstate institutional coherence. Older accounts often described a "School of Chartres" as a single institution that anticipated modern humanism, but several scholars argue that the evidence supports more modest claims: Chartres was one of several centers where masters taught, and the intellectual tendencies attributed to it were shared across northern France. Equally, it would be a mistake to suppose that monasteries "became" universities. Monastic communities pursued a contemplative life and retained their own educational mission, while the schools of towns developed under conditions of competition, student mobility, and fee paying instruction that monastic cloisters did not share. The changing educational landscape of the twelfth century supplied the teachers, texts, methods, and students from which universities emerged, but their emergence involved new institutional forms.

The cathedral and monastic schools also illustrate the diversity of regional experience. In Italy, urban lay culture and a continuing tradition of notarial and legal training gave schooling a more secular tone than in northern Europe. In England, monastic schools remained important after the Norman Conquest examined in Chapter 45. In Iberia, the presence of Muslim and Jewish communities and the frontier between Christian and Muslim rule created a distinctive environment for translation and exchange. In the Orthodox world, as Chapters 36 to 39 showed, a different tradition of Greek and Church Slavonic learning developed around the patriarchate, monasteries, and imperial court. The schools of Latin Christendom were thus one set among several, though they were the ones that produced the institution of the university.

Section 48.3

The Twelfth Century Expansion of Learning

The scholarly vitality of the twelfth century was so marked that the American historian Charles Homer Haskins gave it a famous name, "the Renaissance of the twelfth century," in a book published in 1927. Haskins drew attention to the revival of Roman law, the recovery of Greek and Arabic science, the growth of Latin poetry, and the flourishing of schools. Later historians have debated the label, since "Renaissance" implies a break with a supposedly stagnant past and since the twelfth century drew heavily on Carolingian and earlier precedents, but they have generally accepted Haskins' central observation that the period witnessed an extraordinary expansion in the scope and organization of learning.

This expansion was tied closely to the wider developments of the High Middle Ages. Growing towns, examined in Chapter 43, offered markets for services and concentrations of people who could support teachers. Church reform, traced in Chapter 44, produced a papacy that governed through law and written correspondence and thus required canon lawyers and administrators. Royal governments in England, France, Sicily, and the German lands, discussed in Chapters 45 and 46, expanded their chanceries, courts, and treasuries and recruited clerics and lawyers to staff them. Emperors, popes, and city communes all looked to learned men for arguments about authority, jurisdiction, and legitimacy. The demand for trained clerics, lawyers, administrators, teachers, and physicians increased in tandem with the supply of institutions able to produce them.

Intellectual life also became more mobile. Students traveled long distances to follow famous teachers, and teachers moved among towns. Manuscripts circulated widely, carried by merchants, pilgrims, clergy, and crusaders. The result was a more competitive and interconnected scholarly environment than earlier centuries had known. A master who attracted students could earn a living from their fees, and students who sought the best teachers contributed to the growth of centers like Paris and Bologna. Distinct schools of thought also emerged, such as the dialectical approach of Abelard, the monastic theology of Bernard of Clairvaux, who was deeply suspicious of Abelard's methods, and the Victorine school of the Abbey of St Victor in Paris, whose Hugh of St Victor wrote an influential guide to the arts and to reading. These rivalries show an intellectual world that was active, argumentative, and increasingly institutional.

The expansion should not be romanticized. Study was costly, and most of the population had no access to it. Controversies could be bitter, and condemned teachers might lose positions or freedom. Nevertheless, by about 1200, the conditions for a new kind of educational institution were in place: a pool of students and teachers concentrated in a few towns, a body of texts and methods around which study could be organized, powerful patrons who needed what the schools produced, and a growing sense among teachers and students that they constituted a distinct community with shared interests that required legal protection.

Section 48.4

Translation and the Enlarging World of Knowledge

One of the most consequential developments of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries was the translation into Latin of a large body of Greek and Arabic works. Before this movement, Latin scholars had direct access to only a portion of ancient Greek learning, notably a few logical works of Aristotle translated by Boethius in late antiquity, parts of Plato's Timaeus, and a limited number of medical and mathematical texts. After it, they had at their disposal Euclid's geometry, Ptolemy's astronomy, the major medical works of Galen and Avicenna, extensive writings on optics, algebra, and astronomy by Arabic authors, and the great bulk of Aristotle's natural philosophy, metaphysics, and ethics, together with Arabic and Greek commentaries.

The movement had several centers. In Iberia, Toledo, conquered by Castile in 1085, became the most famous. There, under the patronage of archbishops and others, teams of translators, often working across several languages, rendered Arabic works into Latin. Gerard of Cremona, an Italian who settled in Toledo and died in 1187, is associated with dozens of Arabic to Latin translations, including Ptolemy's Almagest and Avicenna's medical Canon. Translation at Toledo sometimes involved multilingual collaboration, although the surviving evidence does not identify every collaborator or working method. Dominicus Gundissalinus, working with Jewish and Muslim collaborators, translated and adapted philosophical texts, and Michael Scot translated Aristotle and the commentaries of Averroes in the early thirteenth century. In southern Italy and Sicily, where Greek, Arabic, and Latin cultures coexisted under Norman and then Hohenstaufen rule, translators worked from Greek and Arabic sources. In Italian trading cities such as Pisa and Venice, scholars like Burgundio of Pisa and James of Venice translated directly from Greek, drawing on contacts with Constantinople. Later in the thirteenth century, William of Moerbeke, a Dominican, produced Latin translations of Aristotle directly from Greek that were widely used by Thomas Aquinas.

It is essential to avoid the familiar and misleading formula that Muslims "preserved" Greek knowledge and handed it back to Europe. That account is inadequate in several respects. Latin scholars inherited some ancient texts directly. Byzantium maintained extensive Greek traditions of philosophy, medicine, mathematics, and law, and Byzantine commentators such as those whom Anna Komnene's circle encouraged in the twelfth century produced new commentaries on Aristotle. Arabic speaking scholars, whether Muslim, Christian, or Jewish, did far more than preserve. Their translation movement in Baghdad and elsewhere beginning in the eighth century rendered Greek texts into Arabic, and generations of scholars then criticized, corrected, and extended them. Al-Khwarizmi's writings helped create algebra, Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) reconceived optics, Avicenna constructed a comprehensive philosophical and medical system, and Averroes composed detailed commentaries on Aristotle while criticizing aspects of Avicenna's thought. Al-Ghazali's critique of philosophy profoundly shaped debate within Islamic thought, but Latin readers chiefly encountered him through a translation of his Doctrines of the Philosophers, which often caused them to regard "Algazel" as an expositor of Avicennian philosophy rather than as one of its major critics. Intellectual exchange in this period was creative transmission, not mere preservation.

Jewish scholars played a crucial and often underappreciated part. Because many lived in both Arabic and Latin or Hebrew speaking environments, they served as intermediaries and translators. Moses Maimonides, who wrote in Arabic in Egypt, produced a philosophical work, the Guide of the Perplexed, that was translated into Hebrew and then Latin and was read by Christian theologians, including Aquinas. Hebrew translations of Averroes and other Arabic authors circulated in Provence and Italy, and some Latin translations were made from Hebrew versions. Frederick II's court, and the Castilian court of Alfonso X later in the thirteenth century, drew Jewish, Muslim, and Christian scholars into collaborative projects, including astronomical tables.

The translation movement did not occur in a context of uniform tolerance. Christian rulers conquered Muslim territories in Iberia and Sicily and imposed new regimes, and scholars sometimes worked under shifting political pressures. Jewish communities in Latin Christendom faced growing restrictions and periodic persecution, as when the Talmud was put on trial and burned in Paris in the early 1240s. Yet interaction was never only conflict. Scholars cooperated across religious lines, patrons supported collective projects, and the resulting translations changed the Latin intellectual world irreversibly. Historians study the process from translators' prefaces, which sometimes name collaborators, from the manuscripts themselves, and from the changing contents of library catalogues, while recognizing that many collaborators, especially Jewish and Muslim assistants, went unnamed or unrecorded.

Section 48.5

Bologna and the University as a Corporation

The word universitas in the Middle Ages meant simply a community or corporation, any group of people legally recognized as a body. Applied to scholars, it referred to the association of masters, students, or both that gained legal status and privileges. The medieval university thus emerged gradually as a corporate community, not through a single act of foundation. Bologna's traditional founding date of 1088, celebrated in modern anniversaries, is a later convention adopted for commemorative purposes and is not supported by contemporary evidence. What can be shown is that by the early twelfth century legal teaching in Bologna attracted growing numbers of students, and that by the later twelfth and early thirteenth centuries these students had formed associations to protect themselves.

Bologna's fame rested on law. Irnerius and other early teachers of the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries lectured on the compilation of Roman law assembled under the emperor Justinian in the sixth century, and the study of canon law grew rapidly around the same time, as Section 48.12 explains. Because students came from across Europe to a city where they had no citizenship, they were vulnerable to local authorities and to reprisals when fellow countrymen defaulted on debts. They therefore formed associations, called universitates, often organized by regions or "nations," such as those of the ultramontani (from beyond the Alps) and citramontani (from within Italy). In the mid 1150s the emperor Frederick Barbarossa issued the privilege known from its opening word as Habita (scholars debate whether it dates to 1155 or took its surviving form around the Roncaglia meeting of 1158), which protected scholars traveling for study and allowed them to be tried by their masters or the bishop, not by local magistrates.

In Bologna the students themselves held considerable power. Student corporations elected rectors, and they negotiated with the city and the masters. Masters were paid fees by students, and they were also subject to rules governing attendance, lecturing, and punctuality. The commune of Bologna, which at times threatened scholars with the loss of their business if they left, eventually began to pay salaries to some professors. The balance of authority among students, masters, city, emperor, and pope shifted over time, but the Bolognese pattern, in which students organized themselves to hire and regulate teachers, differed fundamentally from the arrangement that developed at Paris.

Bologna's university also influenced the creation of others. Padua, founded in 1222 partly through a migration of scholars from Bologna, developed its own strength in law and, later, medicine and natural philosophy. Students and masters who migrated from one town to another could, by threatening to leave, extract concessions from authorities, a form of leverage that the secession was designed to create. The ability to migrate, with the universities' main resource, their people, being portable, helps explain why the medieval university gained such resilience despite its lack of buildings or endowments.

Historical Vocabulary
Universitas and Studium Generale

Universitas in medieval Latin meant a legally recognized corporate body, whether of merchants, craftsmen, townspeople, or scholars. A universitas of scholars was thus an association with privileges, officers, and statutes; the term did not imply that all fields of knowledge were taught, as the modern English word "university" suggests. Studium generale designated a school of general or wide attraction, drawing students from many regions, and by the thirteenth century the term came to imply recognition, especially by pope or emperor, that its degrees would be respected elsewhere. Bologna, Paris, and Oxford were the great early examples. These terms matter because they remind students that the medieval university was a form of corporate organization before it was a set of buildings or a curriculum.

Section 48.6

Paris, Oxford, and the Masters' University

At Paris, the pattern was reversed. There, the dominant organization was a corporation of masters, who controlled admission to the teaching profession, set curricula, and negotiated with bishop, king, and pope. Paris grew from cathedral school culture, and the masters' relationship with the cathedral chancellor, who granted the licentia docendi, the license to teach, remained a source of tension. Paris became the leading center for the arts and theology, and the combination of these faculties made it the most intellectually influential university of the period. By 1200 the king, Philip II, had granted privileges after a violent clash between students and townspeople, and in 1231 Pope Gregory IX's bull Parens scientiarum, sometimes described as the university's great charter, confirmed the masters' right to organize and regulate themselves after a dispute and strike in 1229 to 1231.

The Parisian university was organized in faculties of arts, theology, canon law, and medicine, with the arts faculty, by far the largest, divided into four "nations": French, Norman, Picard, and English (later called English-German). Its head, the rector, emerged from the arts faculty. Because the masters often held ecclesiastical positions and the university's members were clerics, they enjoyed ecclesiastical legal privileges, and disputes frequently involved the question of whether the bishop, the king, or the pope had jurisdiction. The university's capacity to suspend lectures as a collective weapon, as in 1229, proved a powerful tool.

Oxford developed somewhat later. Scholars were gathering at Oxford by the 1160s, and the town developed rapidly after 1167, when Henry II barred English students from attending Paris during his conflict with Thomas Becket. In 1209, after townspeople hanged two scholars following a killing, many masters and students left Oxford, and some settled at Cambridge, which thereby developed as a second English university. A papal legate later forced the town to make amends. In both places, residential colleges, such as Merton College at Oxford (founded 1264) and Peterhouse at Cambridge (1284), were founded for scholars, a form of endowment that would gradually shift the university's character from that of a free association of migrant scholars toward one rooted in permanent buildings and endowments. English common law developed outside the universities. By the later thirteenth century, would be common lawyers learned through the royal courts and associated professional instruction around Westminster, and the Inns of Court emerged as the principal collegiate centers of common law education only in the fourteenth century. Oxford and Cambridge instead taught Roman and canon law.

Other universities appeared throughout Latin Christendom in the thirteenth century. Montpellier became famous for medicine. Naples was established by Frederick II in 1224 as an imperial studium to train officials for his Sicilian kingdom. Salamanca and Coimbra developed on the Iberian peninsula, and Toulouse was founded in the context of the aftermath of the Albigensian Crusade. Their creators included kings, popes, and cities. This variety demonstrates that there was no single university model. Papal, royal, episcopal, municipal, student, and master authority varied substantially from place to place, and later foundations in central Europe, including those in Prague, Kraków, and Vienna, discussed in Part VIII, followed these precedents while adapting them to new political settings.

Cause and Effect

The Emergence of Universities

Long-term causes

The Carolingian and post Carolingian school tradition, the growth of towns and population, the expanding needs of church and royal administration, the study of Roman and canon law, and the flow of new texts from Greek and Arabic sources.

Short-term causes

The concentration of famous teachers and large numbers of students in a few towns; disputes with townspeople, bishops, and chancellors; and the need for collective legal protection for scholars who were strangers.

Event or transformation

Between roughly 1150 and 1250, groups of masters and students at Bologna, Paris, Oxford, and elsewhere acquired corporate status, statutes, and privileges recognized by popes, rulers, and cities.

Immediate effects

Standardized curricula and degrees, regulated teaching, protection for scholars, and a recognized path to careers in church, law, medicine, and government.

Long-term consequences: The university became one of the most durable European institutions, outlasting the political arrangements that created it, and it supplied the professional classes on which later states and churches relied.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the universities. Demand from institutions, the initiative of scholars, intellectual change, and political patronage all interacted, and they developed differently in different places.

Section 48.7

Faculties, Degrees, and the Medieval Curriculum

Medieval higher education was organized into faculties, each devoted to a field of study. The faculty of arts taught the liberal arts, the foundation of all further study. Above it stood the "higher" faculties of theology, canon and civil law, and medicine. Students normally began in arts, often in their early to mid teens, and only some proceeded to a higher faculty. The arts curriculum was traditionally organized around the seven liberal arts inherited from antiquity: the trivium of grammar, rhetoric, and logic or dialectic, and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. These categories shaped medieval ideas of learning, but they do not fully describe mature university study. By the thirteenth century, the arts curriculum at Paris and elsewhere centered heavily on logic and, increasingly, on Aristotle's works in natural philosophy, metaphysics, and ethics, which replaced much of the older grammar and rhetoric.

Degrees marked stages of progress. A student who completed the initial course of study became a bachelor and could begin teaching under supervision. The license, the licentia docendi, certified fitness to teach, and the ceremony of inception admitted a new master, or doctor, into the guild of teachers. The word magister and doctor were at first interchangeable, though in law and medicine "doctor" became customary. The study of theology was long, often a decade or more beyond arts, and its masters held great prestige. Because study was costly and prolonged, most students did not complete degrees, and many left after some years of arts, carrying their education into parish work, administration, or notarial practice.

Teaching took the form of lectures and disputations. In a lecture (lectio), a master read an authoritative text, such as a book of Aristotle, the Bible, the Sentences, or a legal code, and commented on it line by line, explaining difficulties and raising questions. Because books were expensive, many students relied on notes, and universities developed the pecia system, in which stationers rented out sections of approved exemplar texts for copying so that multiple students could copy simultaneously and the university could oversee textual accuracy. Examinations, which in the Middle Ages were largely oral, tested candidates' ability to answer questions and to participate in disputation.

Students should understand how this system actually worked, instead of memorizing a list of university names. Education was oral, communal, and text based. It depended on authoritative books, which were commented upon rather than replaced, and on a style of argument that valued clarity and rigor. It served practical ends as well as intellectual ones, for degrees in law, medicine, and theology led to careers, and the arts degree provided the common preparation. And it was embedded in a legal and social setting that gave scholars particular protections and obligations. The following sections examine the method that gave the curriculum its distinctive intellectual character.

Section 48.8

Scholasticism: Question, Argument, and Disputation

The term scholasticism, derived from the schools, is best understood primarily as a method of disciplined textual analysis and argument, not as a fixed doctrine. Scholastic thinkers disagreed on many fundamental questions, so no body of beliefs can be called "scholastic" without qualification. What they shared was a way of working: reading authoritative texts closely, identifying questions and apparent contradictions, setting out arguments on both sides, drawing distinctions to resolve them, and reaching a reasoned conclusion. The method applied to scripture, philosophy, law, and medicine alike, and it gave the diverse disciplines of the universities a shared intellectual style.

Its building blocks were the lecture, the question, and the disputation. In the lectio, the master read and glossed a text. Where the text raised a difficulty, the master posed a quaestio, a question, and considered arguments for and against. In the disputatio, a formal oral debate, a master or bachelor would defend a thesis against objections raised by others, and the presiding master would then offer a determination. Regular disputations were held within faculties, and the special "quodlibetal" disputations, held a few times a year, allowed any question to be put to a master. The written summas, such as Aquinas' Summa theologiae, preserve the form: each article poses a question, lists objections, cites a contrary authority, gives the author's response, and answers each objection in turn.

The method had earlier roots. Anselm and others had applied dialectical reasoning to theology, and Peter Abelard in the early twelfth century compiled a collection of conflicting authorities in Sic et Non ("Yes and No"), accompanied by a prologue explaining how to approach contradictions: by distinguishing senses of words, assessing the reliability of texts, and reasoning carefully. Canon lawyers like Gratian applied a similar approach to church law, reconciling discordant canons. Abelard's example is instructive, but scholasticism should not be reduced to Abelard, whose own career was marked by condemnation at Soissons in 1121 and at Sens in 1141 after opposition led by Bernard of Clairvaux. Scholastic method matured over generations, with many contributors.

Scholasticism also had limits that historians recognize. Its heavy reliance on authority meant that new ideas often had to be presented as interpretations of established texts. Its focus on the analysis of arguments could lead to elaborate technical distinctions that outsiders found sterile, a charge made by contemporaries and later humanists. Yet it also produced remarkable intellectual achievements in logic, semantics, metaphysics, and ethics, and it trained generations of students to formulate precise arguments and weigh evidence. The method organized disagreement, treating it as a productive part of inquiry, and that was perhaps its most lasting legacy.

Historical Vocabulary
Scholasticism

Scholasticism refers to the method and culture of learning developed in the medieval schools and universities. It is often misunderstood as a single philosophy, or as a synonym for hair splitting and dogmatism. In its historical context, it was a way of organizing study around authoritative texts, questions, disputations, and distinctions, used by thinkers who reached very different conclusions. Dominicans, Franciscans, secular masters, lawyers, and physicians all worked in scholastic ways. Understanding it as method explains how Aquinas and the Franciscan Bonaventure, or the Aristotelian arts masters and their theological critics, could argue vigorously with one another while sharing the same intellectual framework.

Section 48.9

Aristotle and the Transformation of the Curriculum

Aristotle's writings arrived in Latin Europe in stages. The earliest, the "old logic" (logica vetus), consisted of the Categories and On Interpretation, available through Boethius' translations since late antiquity. In the twelfth century, the "new logic" (logica nova), including the Prior Analytics, Posterior Analytics, Topics, and Sophistical Refutations, came into circulation. Then, between about 1150 and the later thirteenth century, translators rendered his works on natural philosophy, including the Physics, On the Heavens, and On the Soul, along with the Metaphysics and the Nicomachean Ethics, which Robert Grosseteste translated in full around the mid thirteenth century, and the Politics, translated by William of Moerbeke in the 1260s. Greek and Arabic commentaries accompanied many of these works.

Aristotle's importance lay in the comprehensiveness of his system. He offered an integrated account of logic, nature, the soul, being, ethics, and politics grounded in reasoning from observation and argument and not from revelation. For the arts masters, this was a wonderful resource, giving them a body of material for sophisticated teaching. By the middle of the thirteenth century the arts faculty at Paris required students to study a prescribed set of Aristotle's works. Aristotle thus transformed the curriculum, bringing natural philosophy, metaphysics, and ethics to the center of arts education and creating a demand for precise commentary.

At the same time, Aristotle created theological problems. Some of his positions, as interpreted by his commentators, seemed to conflict with Christian doctrine: the eternity of the world, which contradicted the doctrine of creation; the nature of the soul and its relationship to the body, which raised questions about personal immortality; and the apparent limits on divine action. Some of the commentaries, especially those of Averroes, intensified these difficulties. Theologians thus had to decide how to respond, and responses ranged from restriction to selective adoption to ambitious reconciliation.

The Arabic authors who mediated Aristotle should not be viewed as mere conduits. Ibn Sina (Avicenna) constructed an original metaphysics and psychology that deeply influenced Latin theologians, including Aquinas and Albert the Great, even when they disagreed with him. Ibn Rushd (Averroes), who worked in Córdoba and Marrakesh and was a judge as well as a philosopher, wrote detailed commentaries so respected in the Latin world that he was simply called "the Commentator." Maimonides, writing as a Jewish thinker, offered a model for reconciling philosophy and revealed religion. Latin scholars read these authors critically and adapted their ideas for their own purposes, a pattern of reception that continued the creative transmission described in Section 48.4.

Section 48.10

Theology: Faith, Reason, and the Scholastic Synthesis

Theology, the study of God and revelation, was regarded in the Middle Ages as the highest science, and its masters held the greatest prestige in the university. The monastic theology of Anselm, whose argument for God's existence in the Proslogion and whose analysis of the Incarnation in Cur Deus Homo demonstrated the power of rational argument within faith, supplied important background. In the twelfth century, masters such as Peter Lombard, who taught at Paris and became bishop there, collected the opinions of the church fathers on major theological topics in his Sentences, arranged in four books on God, creation, the Incarnation and virtues, and the sacraments and last things. The work became the standard theological textbook, and from the thirteenth century onward, bachelors in theology commonly lectured on the Sentences as a required stage before promotion to master, producing hundreds of commentaries. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 endorsed Lombard's teaching on the Trinity, which confirmed its authority.

The thirteenth century saw several theologians produce large systematic works that engaged Aristotle in different ways. Albert the Great, a Dominican who taught at Paris and Cologne, undertook a vast program of commenting on the whole range of Aristotle's writings. Bonaventure, a Franciscan who became minister general of his order, built a theology grounded in the Augustinian tradition and emphasized the soul's journey to God, regarding Aristotelian philosophy as useful but limited. Thomas Aquinas, a Dominican born in Italy around 1225, studied at Paris and Cologne, taught at Paris, and spent time in Italy. His Summa contra Gentiles and the Summa theologiae, left unfinished at his death in 1274, constructed a comprehensive account of God, creation, human nature, ethics, and the sacraments, using Aristotelian concepts while insisting on the distinct role of revelation.

The central problem all these theologians addressed was the relation between faith and reason. Aquinas held that reason and revelation could not ultimately contradict, because both came from God, and that natural reason could establish some truths about God, such as his existence, while others, such as the Trinity and the Incarnation, could be known only through revelation. This position, sometimes called natural theology combined with revealed theology, stood between those who feared philosophy's corrosive effect and those who gave it a more autonomous role. Theologians also addressed creation, the nature of the soul, sin and grace, the sacraments, and ethics, applying the method of question and distinction to each.

Aquinas deserves substantial attention, but he should not be presented as the inevitable culmination of medieval theology or as the sole representative of scholasticism. His contemporaries disagreed with him, and some of his teachings were controversial; aspects of his thought were the subject of the condemnations discussed in the next section. After his death, other thinkers, including the Franciscan Duns Scotus, developed different positions, and the fourteenth century saw further shifts, including the nominalist thinking associated with William of Ockham. Aquinas was canonized in 1323 and his authority grew over time, especially in the Dominican order and, much later, in the Catholic Church more broadly, an outcome examined in connection with the Catholic Reformation in Chapter 65. His eventual stature was a later development and not a feature of the thirteenth century debate.

Section 48.11

Debate, Condemnation, and the Limits of Inquiry

Medieval intellectual life was neither a regime of unrestricted inquiry nor a story of "the Church against science." The church authorities, theologians, university masters, and philosophical factions who clashed over Aristotle often overlapped: masters were clerics, bishops had been trained in the schools, and popes acted both as supervisors and as patrons. The disputes of the thirteenth century are therefore best understood as arguments within an intellectual community about how to interpret and use powerful new sources.

The first restrictions arose in Paris. The provincial synod of Sens in 1210 prohibited the teaching of Aristotle's natural philosophy at Paris (a measure distinct from the 1141 Council of Sens that condemned Abelard), and the statutes issued in 1215 by the papal legate Robert de Courçon, while allowing the study of Aristotle's logic and ethics, continued to restrict the natural books and the metaphysics. In 1231 Gregory IX instructed that the forbidden works should be examined and corrected, and in practice the restrictions faded as the arts faculty incorporated Aristotle, with the prescribed curriculum of 1255 requiring study of nearly all his known works. These early measures were responses to specific fears, partly about the connection of some interpretations with pantheistic or heterodox ideas, and they were uneven in enforcement.

The more famous condemnations came later. In 1270, Bishop Étienne Tempier of Paris condemned thirteen propositions, many concerning the unity of the intellect, the eternity of the world, and determinism. After Pope John XXI instructed Tempier to investigate reports of doctrinal error, Tempier issued, on 7 March 1277, a much larger condemnation of 219 propositions, which targeted ideas associated with some arts masters, such as Siger of Brabant, and also touched positions held by theologians, including some resembling teachings of Aquinas, who had died in 1274. Historians note that Tempier may already have been preparing action of his own. Robert Kilwardby, archbishop of Canterbury, issued a related but formally prohibitory list of thirty propositions at Oxford later that month, and his successor John Pecham later renewed the prohibitions. The 1277 list was a miscellany and has been difficult for historians to interpret as a coherent statement. In 1325, two years after Aquinas' canonization, the bishop of Paris clarified that the 1277 condemnation should not be understood to apply to Aquinas' teaching, without thereby endorsing all of his positions.

Behind these condemnations lay disputes between arts masters who claimed the freedom to follow Aristotle's reasoning wherever it led within philosophy and theologians who insisted that the higher science of theology had the right to judge philosophy. Historians have long debated whether a faction of "Latin Averroists" existed or whether the label was an invention of opponents, a question taken up in the vocabulary box below. They have also debated whether the notorious "double truth," the idea that something might be true in philosophy and false in theology, was actually held by anyone. Most scholars doubt that any medieval master advocated it as such, and think the charge reflected how opponents interpreted a more modest claim, that philosophy could examine what Aristotle taught without endorsing it as the truth of faith. The episode shows that students need a model of intellectual conflict richer than a binary of science versus religion.

Historical Vocabulary
Latin Averroism

Latin Averroism is a modern label, popularized in the nineteenth century, for a group of thirteenth century arts masters, notably Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia, who were thought to follow Averroes' interpretation of Aristotle in ways that conflicted with Christian doctrine. Current scholarship is divided over the usefulness of the term. Many historians now prefer "radical Aristotelians" or "heterodox Aristotelians," observing that these masters did not simply follow Averroes, that they disagreed with him on several points, and that the cohesion of a movement is partly an artifact of the condemnations. The term matters because it shows how labels created by opponents or by later historians can shape interpretation, and it reminds students to ask who coined a label and why.

Historical Debate

Did the Condemnation of 1277 Hinder or Stimulate Medieval Natural Philosophy?

The question: The condemnation of 219 propositions at Paris in 1277 restricted a range of claims drawn from Aristotelian philosophy. Historians disagree about what effect, if any, this had on the development of natural philosophy, and whether the episode should be read as a restriction of inquiry or as an unintended stimulus to new thinking.

Interpretation A — The condemnations stimulated new speculation: The French physicist and historian Pierre Duhem argued in the early twentieth century that the 1277 condemnation, by challenging Aristotelian necessity, opened the way to new ideas about nature and so represented a birth moment for modern science. More moderate versions of this view, advanced by later scholars, hold that since the condemnation insisted on God's absolute power, it encouraged thinkers to consider hypothetical alternatives to Aristotelian physics, such as the possibility of other worlds or of a vacuum, and so broadened the range of speculation.
Interpretation B — The effects were limited, local, or mixed: Other historians argue that the claim of a decisive stimulus is overstated. The condemnation was a local Parisian and Oxonian measure, its enforcement was uneven, and Aristotelian natural philosophy continued to dominate the curriculum. Speculation about alternatives to Aristotle already had precedents, and later scientific change had many causes unrelated to 1277. Some scholars add that the condemnation did chill certain lines of philosophical argument, at least temporarily, and that its significance is better understood as part of the ongoing negotiation over the relation of philosophy and theology.

Evidence: The debate rests on the text of the condemned propositions, the writings of fourteenth century natural philosophers who discuss hypothetical alternatives, the later history of Aristotelian teaching, and the problem of showing causal connection between a Parisian decree and later developments elsewhere.

Why it matters: If the condemnation had a decisive liberating effect, medieval theology may be seen as inadvertently fostering scientific thought; if it did not, the origins of modern science must be sought elsewhere. Either way, the debate cautions students against reading medieval controversies as steps toward modern outcomes, a point developed further in Chapter 81.

Section 48.12

Law: Roman Law, Canon Law, and Professional Learning

Law was the profession most closely tied to the growth of universities, and Bologna's fame rested on it. The compilation of Roman law issued under Justinian in the sixth century, the Corpus Iuris Civilis, had never been entirely forgotten in the West, but from about the late eleventh century the Digest and other parts of it were studied with new intensity. Irnerius and the generations of teachers called glossators wrote short explanatory notes (glosses) between the lines and in the margins of the text. In the early thirteenth century, Accursius compiled the standard gloss, the Glossa ordinaria, which became the accepted commentary. Later jurists, sometimes called commentators, adapted Roman law to contemporary needs, with Bartolus in the fourteenth century among the most influential.

Canon law, the law of the church, was transformed by Gratian's compilation, completed in the mid twelfth century under the title Concordia discordantium canonum ("Harmony of Discordant Canons") and later known as the Decretum. Gratian arranged canons from councils, popes, and church fathers, and set out reconciliations of those that seemed to conflict, using methods akin to Abelard's. Later popes issued decretals, which were gathered in collections, and in 1234 Gregory IX promulgated an authoritative compilation, compiled by the Dominican Raymond of Peñafort. Universities offered degrees in civil law, canon law, or both, and the combined degree in "both laws" (utroque iure) became prestigious. The intertwined body of Roman and canon law that Latin Europe developed is often called the ius commune, a common legal framework underlying local custom and statute.

The practical consequences were far reaching. Popes and bishops governed increasingly through law, and the papal court employed trained canonists. Monarchs relied on lawyers to formulate claims, draft charters, and staff courts, and the emperor Frederick Barbarossa consulted Bolognese jurists in the 1150s as he asserted imperial rights in northern Italy. Cities hired lawyers to defend their liberties. The universities thus supplied a professional class whose members were often of modest origins but whose training gave them influence and wealth. This connects intellectual history directly to the institutional growth examined in Chapters 44 to 46.

Law was not uniform across Europe. In England, the common law developed in the royal courts and was learned through practice and professional instruction outside the universities, while Roman and canon law flourished at Oxford and Cambridge, creating a distinctive division. In the German lands, Roman law gained ground more gradually, and its "reception" has been a long standing subject of debate. In southern Europe, the influence of Roman law was stronger. Contemporary critics also complained that law attracted students motivated by profit more than learning, a charge that appeared in sermons and satires and suggests the changing status of lawyers within society.

Section 48.13

Medicine, Natural Philosophy, and the Study of Nature

Medicine had been taught in southern Italy at Salerno from at least the eleventh century, and Salerno's practitioners and writings, including a body of texts later collected for teaching, formed a major base for medical learning. Constantine the African, a convert from North Africa associated with the monastery of Monte Cassino in the later eleventh century, translated numerous Arabic medical works into Latin, introducing Arabic medical theory to Latin readers. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, further translations, including Gerard of Cremona's version of Avicenna's Canon of Medicine, supplied a systematic framework that dominated medical teaching for centuries. Montpellier, where Jewish, Muslim, and Christian learning intersected, developed a major school, and medicine was taught at Bologna, Paris, and other universities.

University medicine was heavily textual and theoretical. It relied on the writings of Hippocrates, and especially Galen, along with those of Arabic authorities like Avicenna, and on the view that health depended on a balance of bodily humors and qualities. Students learned to diagnose, to recommend regimen, diet, and remedies, and to reason about causes. Surgery was often considered a manual craft separate from learned medicine, practiced by surgeons trained through apprenticeship, though some learned surgeons wrote texts of their own. Human dissection began to be practiced in a limited and ritualized way in Italian universities around the early fourteenth century, and its place in teaching grew gradually. The Black Death, discussed in Chapter 50, would test the explanations available to medical learning.

The study of nature extended beyond medicine. Natural philosophy, the discipline based largely on Aristotle's Physics and related works, investigated motion, matter, causation, and the cosmos. Scholars also pursued mathematical sciences. Astronomy built on Ptolemy and on Arabic tables and instruments, and works such as Sacrobosco's De sphaera became standard introductions. Optics drew heavily on Ibn al-Haytham. In England, Robert Grosseteste, a chancellor of Oxford and later bishop of Lincoln, wrote on light, the rainbow, and scientific method, and Roger Bacon, who studied and taught at Oxford and Paris, championed mathematics and experience in the study of nature, though his ideas were not widely adopted in his lifetime. Albert the Great combined commentary with observation of plants and animals.

The point is not that medieval scholars were merely awaiting the Scientific Revolution. They investigated nature within Aristotelian, medical, mathematical, and theological frameworks that set the questions they asked and the standards of explanation they accepted. Some of their work, such as analyses of motion and optics, was sophisticated and influential, while other parts reflect assumptions later abandoned. Historians now generally treat medieval natural philosophy as a legitimate subject, one that shaped what followed without determining it, and the relationship between it and the changes examined in Chapter 81 remains actively studied.

Section 48.14

Students, Masters, Mendicants, and the Social World of Learning

Who actually participated in university life? The great majority were young men who held, or aspired to, clerical status. Being a cleric, which required only the tonsure and minor orders and did not entail priesthood, gave a student legal protection and access to church careers. Students came from varied backgrounds, including the sons of nobles, townspeople, and some from modest rural families, though costs limited the numbers of the poor, who sometimes depended on patrons, scholarships, or begging. Many migrated considerable distances. Contemporary estimates of student numbers are unreliable, and modern figures for Paris in the thirteenth century range roughly from low thousands to several thousand, with great uncertainty.

Living arrangements changed over time. Early students rented lodgings in the town or lived in hostels. Charitable endowments established colleges, residential communities that offered lodging and sometimes teaching to a number of scholars: the Collège de Sorbonne at Paris, founded in the 1250s by Robert de Sorbon, and Merton at Oxford were among the earliest. Books were costly, and the stationers who supervised the pecia system, the parchment makers, and the copyists formed a trade that grew up around the university. Relations with townspeople were frequently tense, since scholars claimed legal privileges, drove up rents, and sometimes behaved riotously. Disputes could turn violent, as in Paris in 1200 and 1229 and Oxford in 1209.

The mendicant orders transformed the university's social and intellectual world. The Dominicans, approved in 1216, and the Franciscans, whose way of life was formalized in 1223, established houses at university towns and sent their ablest members to study. By the 1230s, each held chairs in theology at Paris. Their presence provoked conflict with secular masters, who resented the privileges of the friars and feared the competition of an organized order, and in the 1250s a bitter dispute raged in which the secular master William of Saint Amour attacked the mendicants. The friars, however, prevailed, and Aquinas, Bonaventure, and Albert the Great exemplify the weight of mendicant influence in theology.

Women were overwhelmingly excluded from the universities themselves, since the universities were male clerical corporations embedded in educational and professional systems from which women were largely excluded, of which the church's bar on women's ordination was one part. Nevertheless, women participated in intellectual culture in other ways. Heloise, abbess of the Paraclete, was learned in Latin and engaged in philosophical and theological correspondence. Hildegard of Bingen wrote on theology, medicine, and natural history, and convents such as Helfta in Saxony nurtured mystical and theological writing in the thirteenth century. Women served as patrons and readers, and some, as midwives and healers, practiced medicine outside the university framework. The attribution of medical writings associated with the name "Trotula" of Salerno remains debated among scholars. Religious minorities were also generally excluded from degrees, though Jewish physicians often trained within their own communities and Muslim and Jewish scholars contributed to the knowledge that universities taught. Chapter 49 will examine the wider social life of medieval Europe, of which this university world was only one small part.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Student's World: Lodging, Books, and Livelihood

A student in the thirteenth century faced practical problems that the grand institutions of the period tend to obscure. Lectures took place in rented halls or masters' own lodgings, with students sitting on benches or, in some traditions, on straw. Parchment was costly, and a student's most important possessions were often a small number of books or, more commonly, notes taken during lectures. Those who could not afford a copy might rent portions of a text from a stationer. Food, lodging, and fees consumed money, and letters from students to their families, preserved in model collections, regularly request funds, a pattern so common that historians treat it as a recurrent theme in the evidence.

Student culture was not solely serious. Contemporary sources record songs, drinking, gambling, brawls, and complaints from townspeople and church authorities about disorder. Collections of student and wandering clerics' verse circulated in manuscripts, though their authorship and context are difficult to establish. At the same time, the prospect of a career in church or law motivated sustained effort. For many young men, university study was a path to social mobility; for others, it was a brief experience before returning to local life. The student's world was therefore shaped by economic constraint, legal privilege, communal loyalty, and a combination of discipline and youthful exuberance.

Section 48.15

Medieval Learning as a European and Mediterranean Intellectual System

Taken together, the developments of this chapter created a distinctive intellectual system. Universities were durable corporations, protected by privileges and maintained by the continuous renewal of their members. Scholastic methods organized disagreement, giving scholars a disciplined way to debate difficult questions across disciplines. Translated knowledge transformed curricula, making Aristotle and the sciences of the Islamic world central to higher education. Law and medicine professionalized specialized learning, providing church, state, and town with trained experts. And intellectual exchange tied Latin Europe to Byzantium and to the Islamic and Jewish worlds in a Mediterranean system of ideas that no single religion or empire controlled.

That system also had limits. It was clerical, male, and Latinate. Access depended on resources and status, and the great majority of the population, including most women and peasants, were excluded. Its reliance on authority and its focus on textual commentary constrained certain kinds of inquiry, and its institutions could be used to enforce orthodoxy as well as to protect scholars. Relations with Jewish and Muslim scholars combined exploitation of their knowledge with hostility toward their communities. The achievements and limits belong together, and a balanced assessment must recognize both.

It would also be wrong to conclude that the universities inevitably produced the Renaissance or modern science. Humanist scholars of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, examined in Chapter 57, often criticized scholastic learning and developed different approaches to classical texts, even as many humanists were educated in universities. The relation between medieval and early modern learning was a combination of continuity and rupture, in which institutions like the universities persisted while their curricula, methods, and social roles changed. The later medieval developments of Part VIII, including the plague, the Great Schism discussed in Chapter 53, and the founding of new universities, further reshaped this world. In Volume II's larger argument, medieval learning appears as one of the lasting institutional creations of the High Middle Ages, and as an example of how Latin Christendom developed through contact with, not isolation from, the wider Mediterranean world.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Learning in Latin Europe, c. 1050 to c. 1300

What Changed?

  • Teaching shifted from monastic and cathedral schools toward corporate universities with statutes, degrees, and privileges
  • The available body of texts expanded dramatically through translation, especially of Aristotle, Arabic science, and medicine
  • Scholastic method of question and disputation became the shared style of advanced study
  • Law and medicine emerged as professional university disciplines supplying trained experts to church and state

What Remained Continuous?

  • The church remained the principal sponsor and framework of learning, and theology remained its highest discipline
  • Latin remained the language of scholarship, and learning remained based on manuscripts and authoritative texts
  • The liberal arts and the writings of the church fathers continued to provide intellectual foundations
  • Access remained limited to a small, mostly male and clerical minority

Who experienced change most directly? Masters and students in the university towns, along with the lawyers, physicians, and administrators they trained, experienced the greatest change. Who experienced greater continuity? Most of Europe's population, including peasants and the majority of townspeople, lived lives little touched by university learning.

Why the pattern matters: The mixture of innovation and continuity guards against two errors, treating the period as static before the Renaissance and treating its institutional creations as a break with the past.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the schools of Latin Christendom developed into universities and how those universities, with their characteristic methods, curricula, and professions, organized knowledge in the High Middle Ages. It began by rejecting the idea of an intellectually dormant "Dark Age" and by defining learning as it was understood in the period: Latin based, clerical, manuscript dependent, and tied to the needs of church, law, and administration.

The monastic and cathedral schools of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, drawing on Carolingian precedent, provided the institutional inheritance. Chartres, Laon, Paris, and Bec produced teachers and methods, though the notion of a single "School of Chartres" has been qualified by modern scholarship. The wider expansion of the twelfth century, driven by the growth of towns, church reform, and royal government, created a growing demand for trained clerics, lawyers, administrators, and physicians.

Translation from Greek and Arabic into Latin, sometimes through Hebrew or vernacular intermediaries and often through multilingual collaboration, enlarged the world of knowledge in ways that proved decisive. The movement drew on centers like Toledo and Sicily and on direct contact with Byzantium. It was not a simple matter of Muslims preserving Greek learning for Europe: Latin scholars retained some ancient texts, Byzantium sustained a Greek tradition, and Arabic speaking Muslim, Christian, and Jewish scholars criticized and extended what they inherited. Exchange was creative transmission.

Universities emerged gradually as corporations of scholars. Bologna, shaped by law and student organization, and Paris, shaped by arts and theology and master organization, represent two principal patterns, while Oxford, Cambridge, Montpellier, Naples, Salamanca, and others reveal further variety. Privileges from emperors, kings, popes, and cities protected scholars, and the faculties, degrees, and teaching practices of the university gave higher learning its structure.

Scholasticism, understood as a method of reading, questioning, and disputation and not as a fixed doctrine, gave the university disciplines their shared style. The reception of Aristotle transformed the arts curriculum and provoked theological problems that thinkers such as Albert the Great, Bonaventure, and Thomas Aquinas addressed in different ways. Aquinas' synthesis was neither the sole nor the inevitable culmination of medieval theology.

Intellectual conflict took institutional form in the Parisian restrictions on Aristotle, in the disputes between arts masters and theologians, and in the condemnations of 1270 and 1277. These episodes are best understood as arguments within an intellectual community about the relation of philosophy and theology, not as a conflict between the Church and science, and historians continue to debate their consequences, as the Historical Debate feature showed.

Law and medicine professionalized learning. The revival of Roman law, Gratian's systematization of canon law, and the growth of the ius commune connected learning to the governing institutions examined in Chapters 44 to 46. University medicine drew on Greek and Arabic sources to create a theoretical and textual discipline, while natural philosophy and the mathematical sciences investigated nature within established frameworks.

The social world of learning was shaped by clerical status, student migration, colleges, book production, tense relations with townspeople, and the presence of the mendicant orders. Women, most laypeople, and religious minorities were excluded from university degrees, though many contributed to intellectual culture in other settings. Medieval learning formed a European and Mediterranean intellectual system with real achievements and real limits, and its relation to the Renaissance and to modern science was one of continuity and rupture and not of simple progression.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 48 matters because it shows how a society built institutions to preserve, test, and extend knowledge. The medieval university was not simply a place where existing learning was repeated. It was a corporation with legal rights, a curriculum with agreed standards, and a method that treated disagreement as an engine of inquiry. Those features made it unusually adaptable, and they help explain why the institution survived the political and religious upheavals that destroyed many of the structures of medieval Europe.

The chapter also reveals how interconnected Europe's intellectual world was. The works that transformed Latin curricula came from Greek, Arabic, and Hebrew traditions and passed through the hands of Muslim, Jewish, Christian, and Byzantine scholars. Latin Christendom's learning cannot be understood as a self contained achievement, and the chapter therefore supports the larger argument of Volume II that medieval Europe was formed through contact across religious and linguistic boundaries as well as through the consolidation of Latin Christian institutions.

The developments traced here shaped what followed without determining it. Renaissance humanists, examined in Part IX, would criticize scholastic learning while relying on the universities and the manuscripts that medieval scholars had gathered. Protestant and Catholic reformers, discussed in Part X, would argue over the heritage of scholastic theology, and the continuing prestige of Aristotle would be challenged only gradually in the Scientific Revolution. The long persistence of the university's basic forms, degrees, faculties, and disputation, testifies to the strength of what medieval scholars created.

At the same time, the chapter has insisted on limits. Learning was shaped by clerical status, gender, wealth, and religion, and its institutions sometimes protected orthodoxy at the expense of inquiry. Chapter 49 now turns from this specialized world to the wider society of medieval Europe, the nobles, peasants, townspeople, women, children, and minorities, whose lives provide the setting in which the schools and universities existed and whose experience is essential to a full understanding of the High Middle Ages.
