Financial Crisis, Sovereign Debt, and Brexit

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 2007 and 2026, a crisis that began in the mortgage markets of the United States became a test of whether a continent that had pooled its currency, its markets, and much of its law could also pool the burdens of failure. The answer was partial, contested, and costly, and it changed European politics.

On the morning of Friday, September 14, 2007, long queues formed outside branches of Northern Rock, a mortgage lender based in Newcastle, England. The night before, the BBC had reported that the bank had asked the Bank of England for emergency support, and customers who had never imagined that a British bank could fail came to withdraw their savings. The scene was filmed, broadcast, and replayed around the world. It was the first run on a British bank in well over a century, and it revealed something that regulators, bankers, and governments had underestimated: modern banks financed by short-term borrowing in global markets could be undone by a loss of confidence with remarkable speed, whatever the apparent strength of their balance sheets.

Eight years later, on July 5, 2015, voters in Greece went to the polls in a referendum on whether to accept a package of proposals from the country's creditors. Banks had been closed for a week, cash withdrawals were limited by capital controls, and the question of whether Greece would remain in the euro was openly debated in European capitals. A little less than a year after that, on June 23, 2016, voters in the United Kingdom decided by 51.9 percent to 48.1 percent that their country should leave the European Union. These three episodes, a bank run, a currency crisis, and a withdrawal vote, are the principal subjects of this chapter. They are related, but they are not the same event, and a central responsibility of this chapter is to keep them distinct.

The global banking crisis of 2007 to 2009 began in the financial markets of the United States, but it exposed weaknesses in European banking systems that were partly home grown: reliance on wholesale funding, high leverage, large cross-border exposures, and property booms in Ireland, Spain, and the United Kingdom. The euro-area sovereign-debt crisis that followed, from late 2009 to roughly 2013, was a different problem. It arose from the design of monetary union, in which twenty years earlier governments had agreed to share a currency and a central bank while keeping responsibility for taxation, public spending, and, to a large degree, bank rescue. Brexit was different again. Britain had never joined the euro, and its long ambivalence toward European integration, examined in this chapter from the 1970s onward, had political roots that predated the financial crisis by decades. The crisis shaped the setting in which the referendum was held; it did not, by itself, produce the result.

This chapter therefore studies three connected but separate developments. It asks how a banking crisis became a sovereign-debt crisis in some countries and not in others; how European institutions, above all the European Central Bank, improvised and then formalized new instruments to prevent the collapse of the euro; why those responses, and the policies of fiscal adjustment attached to them, produced intense political conflict over democratic accountability and national sovereignty; and how, in Britain, a long-standing dispute about the terms of membership became a decision to leave, followed by a prolonged and legally complex separation that was still being renegotiated in November 2026.

The evidence is unusually abundant and unusually contested. Central banks, finance ministries, the European Commission, the International Monetary Fund, and the statistical office of the European Union, Eurostat, published large volumes of data, though many figures were later revised, sometimes substantially. Parliamentary debates, court judgments, treaty texts, and the certified results of elections and referendums provide a firm documentary base. Opinion polls, surveys, and memoirs by participants illuminate motives, but they also reflect the interests of those who produced them. Because the events are recent, historians are still working out which interpretations will endure, and students should read contemporary accounts, including some of the documents reproduced in this chapter, as evidence to be weighed rather than as settled verdicts.

Within Part XX, this chapter builds on the creation of the single currency and the Maastricht framework examined in Chapter 134, the post-Cold War enlargement examined in Chapter 136, and the debates about migration, identity, and populism examined in Chapter 137. It precedes Chapter 139, which turns to Russia's war against Ukraine, energy, and European security. Within Volume VI, it forms part of the argument that European integration after 1945 was neither inevitable nor irreversible: it could be deepened under pressure, it could be reshaped by improvisation, and, as the British case showed, it could be left.

Historical cutoff. This chapter carries its narrative to November 18, 2026. A second United Kingdom–European Union summit was scheduled for November 20, 2026, in Brussels. Because it had not taken place by the cutoff, the chapter treats reported draft agreements as negotiations in progress, not as concluded outcomes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how housing credit, securitization, leverage, and interconnected wholesale funding turned a United States mortgage downturn into a European banking crisis.
  2. Distinguish the global banking crisis of 2007–2009, the euro-area sovereign-debt crisis of 2009–2013, and the British decision to leave the European Union as separate but connected developments.
  3. Compare the economic experiences of Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Spain, Iceland, and selected eastern European economies during the recession.
  4. Analyze the design of monetary union, including the roles of the Stability and Growth Pact, the no-bailout clause, and the absence of a fiscal union, in making a sovereign-debt crisis possible.
  5. Trace the sequence of Greek, Irish, Portuguese, Spanish, and Cypriot assistance, distinguishing full sovereign programs from banking-sector support.
  6. Assess the actions of the European Central Bank, especially the July 2012 commitment and Outright Monetary Transactions, and explain why they changed the crisis.
  7. Evaluate competing arguments about austerity, growth, and debt sustainability using economic evidence and institutional context.
  8. Interpret the Greek crisis of 2015, distinguishing the referendum result from the agreement that followed it.
  9. Describe the banking union and the new instruments of European financial governance and identify their limits.
  10. Explain the tension among national democracy, creditor and debtor interests, and supranational economic governance, using several national perspectives.
  11. Interpret the 2016 referendum result by nation, region, and social group, without reducing Brexit to austerity or to a single motive.
  12. Connect the Withdrawal Agreement, the Irish border question, the Trade and Cooperation Agreement, and later UK–EU negotiations as successive stages of a single constitutional and economic separation.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the financial and political crises of the early twenty-first century threaten European integration, and how did they reshape the balance among economic interdependence, national democracy, and European sovereignty?
  2. Why did a global banking crisis produce sovereign-debt emergencies in some European countries but not in others?
  3. Which weaknesses in the design of the euro were exposed by the crisis, and which were produced by decisions made after it began?

  8. Why did implementing a referendum result prove far more complicated than winning it?

Historical Context

By the mid-2000s the European Union appeared to be at a high point of economic integration. The single market, completed in its main outlines in the early 1990s, covered goods, services, capital, and labor. The euro, introduced as an accounting currency in 1999 and as banknotes and coins in 2002, was shared by twelve states at its physical launch and by fifteen by 2008. The enlargement of 2004 and 2007, examined in Chapter 136, had brought into the Union a group of post-communist economies whose growth rates and capital inflows were high. Policymakers often described the previous decade as a period of stability, and many assumed that the architecture established at Maastricht in 1992, examined in Chapter 134, had been tested and had held.

Beneath that stability, imbalances were accumulating. Before 1999, investors had charged governments such as those of Greece, Italy, Spain, and Portugal considerably higher interest rates than they charged Germany, reflecting differences in inflation, exchange-rate risk, and perceived creditworthiness. After the euro was introduced, these differences narrowed dramatically, as markets treated euro-area government bonds as close substitutes. Cheap credit flowed into southern Europe and Ireland, financing housing booms in Ireland and Spain, consumption and public spending in Greece, and large current-account deficits in several economies. Germany, by contrast, ran large surpluses after a period of wage restraint in the early 2000s. Economists disagree about how much blame to assign to borrowers, to lenders, to the structure of the euro, and to the policies of individual governments, but there is wide agreement that capital flows within the euro area built up vulnerabilities that were not widely priced by markets until 2008 to 2010.

The United Kingdom stood in a different position. It was a member of the European Union but not of the euro or the Schengen area, and it retained its own currency, its own central bank, and a large financial sector in London that served European and global clients. Its relationship to integration had been marked from the outset by debate, as examined in section 138.15. British banks and households were deeply affected by the financial crisis, but British governments, free to set monetary policy and to let the pound depreciate, faced different constraints than euro-area governments did.

The evidence base for this chapter differs from that of earlier chapters. Statistical series from Eurostat, the European Commission's AMECO database, national statistical offices, the IMF, and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development allow comparison, but figures for GDP, deficits, and debt were frequently revised, and different institutions use different definitions. Central bank minutes and speeches, treaty articles, parliamentary records, constitutional court judgments, and memoranda of understanding provide documentary evidence for decisions. Public opinion surveys such as the Eurobarometer and post-referendum polling help historians study attitudes, though they measure self-reported views at particular moments. Memoirs by finance ministers, central bankers, and prime ministers offer detail but are shaped by hindsight and self-justification. Each kind of source answers some questions and not others.

Finally, students should note that terms in this chapter are easily confused. The European Union is the broader political and legal organization, with twenty-seven members after January 31, 2020. The euro area, or eurozone, comprises those members that have adopted the euro. The European Central Bank is the euro-area monetary authority; it is not the same as national central banks such as the Bank of England, which belongs to a country outside the euro, or the Deutsche Bundesbank. The International Monetary Fund is a global institution of which European states are members; it is not a European Union body. The chapter keeps these distinctions visible because the political arguments of the period often blurred them.

The Global Financial Crisis and the European Economy, 2007–2009

How a crisis born in globally interconnected financial markets developed differently across European economies.

Section 138.1

The Origins of the Global Financial Crisis

The crisis that struck Europe in 2007 and 2008 had underlying vulnerabilities and immediate triggers, and historians distinguish between them. The underlying vulnerabilities developed over roughly a decade. After the technology-stock collapse of 2000 to 2001, interest rates in the United States, the United Kingdom, and the euro area were low by historical standards, and large pools of savings, including those of oil exporters, Asian economies, and European banks and pension funds, sought safe and profitable assets. Housing credit expanded rapidly in the United States, where mortgage lending standards deteriorated, and the market for subprime mortgages, loans to borrowers with weak credit histories, grew to a very large scale. House prices rose in many countries, and lenders and borrowers alike assumed that they would continue to rise.

Two financial innovations transformed these loans into global risks. The first was securitization: banks and specialized lenders pooled mortgages and sold claims on the resulting income streams as bonds, often in layered structures such as collateralized debt obligations, in which senior tranches were rated very highly by credit-rating agencies. The "originate and distribute" model weakened the incentive of lenders to scrutinize borrowers, because the loans were quickly sold on. The second was leverage: banks, hedge funds, and off-balance-sheet vehicles financed holdings of such securities with large amounts of borrowed money, much of it short term. A small decline in asset values could therefore wipe out equity, and a loss of confidence could make rolling over short-term debt impossible. Risk-management models, calibrated to the calm years of the early 2000s, systematically underestimated the chance that many assets would fall in value together.

Europe was not a bystander in this system. European banks purchased large quantities of United States mortgage securities and, equally important, built dollar balance sheets of great size, financed by borrowing in dollar wholesale markets and through foreign-exchange swaps. Economists at the Bank for International Settlements, and later historians such as Adam Tooze, have emphasized that the transatlantic banking system was a single, integrated network, and that the largest European banks were among its most highly leveraged participants. Some had assets that were several times the annual economic output of their home countries, an arrangement that worked only so long as markets remained willing to lend. The supervisory framework, based on national authorities applying common international capital standards, did not match the cross-border scale of the banks it supervised.

Europe also had housing booms of its own. In Ireland, a long expansion in construction and mortgage lending, financed substantially by borrowing from abroad, transformed the banking sector. In Spain, regional savings banks and commercial banks financed an extraordinary volume of residential and commercial building, and the construction sector became a major employer. In the United Kingdom, house prices rose steeply and banks such as Northern Rock, Bradford & Bingley, and HBOS depended heavily on wholesale funding. In each case, the vulnerability was in part domestic, and the American subprime market acted as a trigger more than as a sole cause.

The immediate triggers came in 2007. United States house prices had peaked around 2006, and as defaults on subprime loans rose, the securities backed by them lost value and became difficult to price. On August 9, 2007, the French bank BNP Paribas suspended redemptions from three funds, stating that it could no longer value their holdings reliably. Interbank lending rates rose, and the ECB injected large amounts of overnight liquidity into the euro-area banking system that day. This episode is often taken as the beginning of the European phase of the crisis, because it showed that banks no longer trusted one another's balance sheets.

Historians therefore describe the crisis as having several layers: a long credit boom, financial structures that concentrated and hid risk, regulatory frameworks that failed to constrain leverage, and a triggering shock in United States mortgage markets that spread through an interconnected system. Disagreement remains over the weights to assign to each, over the role of global imbalances, and over whether the principal failure lay in regulation, in monetary policy, or in private risk-taking. These disagreements matter for the rest of the chapter, because they shaped what policymakers thought needed repair.

Primary Source Window

International Monetary Fund, Global Financial Stability Report, April 2008 (and October 2008 update)

Creator: IMF staff, Washington, D.C. · Date: April and October 2008 · Type: Semiannual official assessment of global financial risks, used by finance ministries and central banks

Context: The IMF's financial stability reports were published twice a year and were read by policymakers and markets. The April 2008 edition, issued after the rescue of the American investment bank Bear Stearns in March, argued that the turmoil was more than a liquidity problem and offered an estimate of potential losses on United States–originated credit assets. By October 2008, after Lehman Brothers, the report revised its assessment of losses sharply upward.

Editorial paraphrase (not quotation): the April 2008 IMF report estimated potential losses on US-originated credit assets of approximately $945 billion; the October 2008 edition raised the estimate to approximately $1.4 trillion as the crisis spread. Both were contemporary risk estimates, not verified final losses or amounts paid by governments.

Where to find it: IMF Global Financial Stability Report archive, imf.org/en/Publications/GFSR. Students should consult the exact chapters and loss tables in the original edition and verify figures against the published text.

Verified documentary texts: IMF, April 2008 Global Financial Stability Report · IMF, October 2008 Global Financial Stability Report

Analysis: The report is valuable because it was written while events were unfolding, and it shows what experts believed they knew at particular moments. Its sharp upward revision of loss estimates demonstrates how quickly assessments changed during the crisis. It is also an institutional document: the IMF drew on data supplied by member governments and major institutions, and its focus on global rather than European exposures meant that the particular weaknesses of euro-area bank balance sheets received less attention than they would later.

Section 138.2

The Banking Crisis Reaches Europe

The European crisis unfolded in two stages: a slow build-up between August 2007 and September 2008, followed by a sudden global panic. Northern Rock illustrates the first stage. The bank had grown rapidly by lending mortgages funded largely by wholesale borrowing and by securitizing its loans; deposits covered only a minority of its funding. When wholesale markets froze in August and September 2007, it could no longer refinance itself. On September 13, 2007, it was reported that the bank had sought emergency assistance from the Bank of England, and on September 14, depositors queued outside branches. Britain's deposit insurance at the time was partial and, to many savers, confusing. On September 17, Chancellor of the Exchequer Alistair Darling announced that the government would guarantee deposits, and the run subsided. Northern Rock was taken into public ownership in February 2008 after attempts to find a private buyer failed.

The more consequential stage began on September 15, 2008, when Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy after the United States government declined to rescue it. The consequences for Europe were immediate. Lehman had counterparties and affiliates around the world, including large operations in London. The American insurer AIG, which had sold insurance on many European banks' securities, was rescued by the United States government within days. A major money market fund in the United States "broke the buck," and short-term funding markets, on which many European banks depended, seized up. Interbank lending effectively stopped, as banks hoarded cash and refused to lend to one another for more than a few days. Spreads between interbank rates and expected policy rates widened to levels not seen before.

Over the following weeks, European governments faced a series of failures. In Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, the financial group Fortis was partly nationalized and split. In Germany, the property lender Hypo Real Estate required rescue. In Britain, Bradford & Bingley was nationalized and HBOS was absorbed by Lloyds TSB. In Iceland, the three largest banks, Glitnir, Landsbanki, and Kaupthing, which had grown to many times the size of the Icelandic economy, collapsed in early October 2008. Icelandic internet banks had attracted large deposits in Britain and the Netherlands, and when the banks failed, British authorities used anti-terrorism legislation to freeze Landsbanki's assets in the United Kingdom, an action that caused a diplomatic dispute. Iceland imposed capital controls and sought an IMF-supported program in late 2008.

The ECB and other central banks responded by supplying liquidity on a scale and on terms that would have been unthinkable a year earlier. In October 2008 the ECB began providing unlimited funds against collateral at a fixed rate, and central banks, including the ECB, the Bank of England, the Federal Reserve, and others, coordinated an interest-rate reduction on October 8. Governments simultaneously announced guarantees of bank debts and, in some cases, of deposits. These measures prevented a collapse of the payments system, but they did not immediately restore confidence.

Why were European financial institutions so vulnerable despite their different national contexts? Several factors recur. Many relied on short-term wholesale and dollar funding that could evaporate in a panic. Many were highly leveraged, with relatively thin capital cushions. Some held large portfolios of American securities; others held domestic property loans; and some, including those in Ireland and Spain, were exposed to both. Cross-border banking meant that failures in one country affected others: Dutch, Belgian, British, and Icelandic depositors, for example, found themselves entangled in each other's institutions. Finally, supervision was national, while the largest banks operated across borders, so that responsibility for rescuing an international bank was disputed. Differences among countries mattered, however: German Landesbanken, French universal banks, British retail and investment banks, and Spanish savings banks failed or survived for different reasons, and a single story of "European banks" obscures this variety.

Historical Vocabulary
Bank Run, Liquidity, and Solvency

A bank run occurs when many depositors or lenders try to withdraw funds at the same time, fearing that the institution cannot pay them. Banks lend long and borrow short, so even a sound bank cannot repay everyone at once. A liquidity problem means that an institution lacks cash or assets that can be quickly sold at fair prices to meet immediate obligations; a solvency problem means that the value of its assets is less than its liabilities, so that it is in effect bankrupt. Central banks traditionally act as lenders of last resort to solvent but illiquid banks, but in a panic it is hard to tell which problem a bank has, and the distinction became central to arguments in 2008 and again in the sovereign-debt crisis. In 2007 the run on Northern Rock was a run by depositors; in 2008 many runs were by institutional lenders in wholesale markets, who withdrew without queues or television cameras. Modern runs may therefore be invisible to the public while being just as destructive.

Section 138.3

Recession, Unemployment, and Economic Disruption across Europe

The financial panic of autumn 2008 was followed by the deepest recession in Europe since the Second World War. Because world trade contracted sharply in late 2008 and early 2009, export-oriented economies were hit quickly. The effects were uneven, and the differences foreshadowed later political divisions.

Germany's economy, heavily dependent on exports of machinery, vehicles, and capital goods, suffered one of the steepest falls in output among large economies, with GDP declining by roughly 5 to 6 percent in 2009. Yet unemployment rose only modestly. Employers and the government made extensive use of short-time work schemes, known as Kurzarbeit, in which the state subsidized reduced hours so that firms could retain skilled workers. When world demand recovered in 2010, German industry rebounded quickly. France experienced a smaller contraction, cushioned by its large public sector and automatic stabilizers such as unemployment benefits. The United Kingdom, with an economy dependent on financial services, housing, and consumer spending, experienced a deep recession of roughly 4 to 5 percent in 2009, a slow recovery, and a large rise in public borrowing.

In Ireland and Spain, the collapse of property booms was far more destructive than a trade shock. Construction had accounted for a very large share of employment and tax revenue, and when it ended, jobs vanished and tax receipts fell sharply. Irish output fell steeply, unemployment climbed from under five percent before the crisis to about fifteen percent at its peak around 2012, and the exposure of the banks to property loans threatened to overwhelm the state. In Spain, unemployment rose from about eight percent in 2007 to roughly one quarter of the labor force by 2013, with youth unemployment more than twice that level. Greece's recession began slightly later but proved the longest, as examined in later sections.

Iceland provides an extreme case. Its banks had expanded abroad on the basis of cheap borrowing, and when they failed, the currency collapsed, inflation rose, and the economy contracted sharply, but the Icelandic state, unlike Ireland's, allowed the large foreign-liability banks to fail and protected domestic depositors by transferring domestic operations to new institutions. Icelandic output recovered earlier than that of many euro-area crisis states, a contrast that has been much debated, with some analysts crediting currency depreciation and capital controls and others pointing out that Iceland's circumstances were exceptional.

Eastern Europe's experience also varied. In the Baltic states, rapid credit expansion and fixed exchange rates ended in deep recessions, with Latvia's output falling by about a fifth from peak to trough, and in Hungary, Latvia, and Romania, EU and IMF assistance was required beginning in late 2008. Many households in the region had borrowed in euros or Swiss francs, and currency depreciation raised the burden of these debts. Poland, with a large domestic market, a floating currency, and less foreign-currency borrowing, avoided a recession in 2009. These differences should caution students against treating "eastern Europe" as a single economic category, a point that connects to the discussion of post-communist transformation in Chapter 136.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households in the First Recession

For ordinary families, the crisis was experienced through jobs, housing, and credit. In Ireland and Spain, hundreds of thousands of households had taken out mortgages at the peak of the boom and now owed more than their homes were worth, a condition known as negative equity. Ireland experienced widespread mortgage arrears; Spain, where mortgage law allowed lenders to pursue borrowers for the remainder of a debt after repossession, saw large numbers of evictions and a public campaign for reform of the rules. In Britain, interest-rate cuts protected many mortgage-holders with variable-rate loans, but unemployment and falling real wages strained household budgets, and the use of food banks later became a visible sign of hardship.

In Germany, short-time working meant that many industrial workers kept their jobs with reduced hours and wages, a very different experience from the mass layoffs in Spain. Young people entering the labor market in 2008 and 2009 faced particular difficulties. Temporary and part-time contracts were widespread in southern Europe, so that the burden of job loss fell disproportionately on younger workers and migrants, while older workers with permanent contracts were often protected. Migrants, who were concentrated in construction and services, were hit particularly hard in Spain and Ireland, and some returned to their countries of origin, while others moved on to other destinations. The social impact of the crisis was therefore shaped by existing labor-market institutions, housing systems, and welfare provisions, as much as by the size of the national recession.

Section 138.4

Bank Rescues, Stimulus, and the First European Responses

Governments responded to the autumn 2008 panic by committing public resources to banks on an unprecedented scale. The pattern was broadly similar, but the details differed in ways that proved consequential.

In the United Kingdom, the government announced a comprehensive package on October 8, 2008. It included recapitalization of banks through the purchase of shares, a guarantee of new bank debt, and expanded liquidity support from the Bank of England. The Royal Bank of Scotland, which had acquired part of the Dutch bank ABN AMRO in 2007 at the peak of the boom, required very large injections and ended up largely in public ownership, while Lloyds Banking Group, formed from the takeover of HBOS, also received capital. Northern Rock and Bradford & Bingley remained nationalized. In 2009 the Bank of England began quantitative easing, creating money to buy government bonds, and the government cut value added tax temporarily as fiscal stimulus.

In Ireland, the government announced on September 30, 2008, a guarantee of the liabilities of six domestic banks, covering deposits and most bonds, an enormous commitment relative to the size of the Irish economy. The guarantee was intended to calm markets but it tied the state's fate to that of its banks. In 2009 the government created the National Asset Management Agency to purchase property-related loans from the banks, and Anglo Irish Bank was nationalized in January 2009. The costs of the banking collapse eventually weighed heavily on public finances and pushed Ireland toward a formal program, as discussed in section 138.7.

In Germany, the government announced guarantees of savings deposits in early October 2008 and then established a financial-market stabilization fund with authority to provide capital and guarantees on a very large scale, with a fund of several hundred billion euros. Germany also relied on short-time work subsidies and a car-scrappage program to support industry. France supported banks through guarantees and capital injections, and also stimulus. At the level of the European Union, a European Economic Recovery Plan of November 2008 coordinated national and Union stimulus equal to about one and a half percent of EU GDP, though most of the money came from national budgets. The Commission relaxed its state-aid rules, allowing governments to rescue banks under temporary conditions designed to limit distortions of competition.

The ECB cut its main interest rate in stages to one percent by May 2009, provided liquidity on unlimited terms, extended the range of collateral it accepted, and began purchasing covered bonds. Because the ECB had no mandate to finance governments directly and the euro area had no joint treasury, the weight of fiscal response fell on member states. The Group of Twenty, meeting in London in April 2009, increased the resources of the IMF and committed to coordinated action.

By late 2009 and 2010, the focus of European policy shifted from rescuing banks and stimulating demand to controlling public deficits. Deficits had widened because of recession, falling tax revenues, stimulus, and bank rescues, and public debt had risen sharply in several countries. At the Toronto summit in June 2010, the major economies pledged to reduce deficits, and in Britain the new coalition government of Conservatives and Liberal Democrats announced a program of fiscal consolidation in its emergency budget of June 2010. In the euro area, the Commission began excessive-deficit procedures against numerous member states. This shift from rescue to consolidation, occurring while many economies remained fragile, shaped the sovereign-debt crisis and the arguments over austerity examined in Division III.

The distinction between the types of bank support is important. In Britain, the state acquired large ownership stakes in banks, in Ireland, it guaranteed and then absorbed bank debts that dwarfed its revenues, and in Germany, it supported banks through a stabilization fund while the economy recovered quickly. These choices affected the extent to which private banking losses became public debt, and thus how vulnerable each government would be when bond markets turned against it.

The Eurozone Sovereign-Debt Crisis, 2009–2013

Section 138.5

The Euro's Institutional Design and the Problem of Sovereign Debt

As Chapter 134 explained, the Maastricht Treaty of 1992 created a framework in which participating states would adopt a single currency, managed by an independent European Central Bank with the primary objective of price stability. Monetary policy, meaning the setting of short-term interest rates and the supply of money, passed to the euro-area level. Fiscal policy, meaning taxation and public spending, remained with national governments, as did responsibility for banking supervision and for the resolution of failing banks. This division of labor was a deliberate political compromise. Some governments, notably Germany's, would accept a shared currency only if it was protected against inflationary financing of deficits; others, notably France's, wanted monetary union as a step toward political union. The result was a currency union without a fiscal union.

To protect against irresponsible borrowing, the treaty contained several safeguards. It prohibited the ECB and national central banks from lending directly to governments, so-called monetary financing. It contained a "no-bailout" provision stating that neither the Union nor a member state would be liable for the commitments of another member state's government. It set reference values of three percent of GDP for annual deficits and sixty percent for public debt, and established an excessive-deficit procedure. In 1997 the member states agreed on the Stability and Growth Pact, which elaborated rules and sanctions. Yet the Pact was weakly enforced: France and Germany exceeded the deficit limit in the early 2000s and, in 2003, the Council declined to apply sanctions, after which the rules were softened in 2005. Greece was later found to have reported misleading deficit data in the years around its entry to the euro, and the Commission lacked the power to audit national statistics until the system was reformed.

The design assumed that markets would discipline governments, and that the combination of rules and the no-bailout clause would deter excessive borrowing. In practice, the early years of the euro saw the opposite. As noted in the Historical Context, bond yields converged, so that governments with higher debts and weaker economies could borrow almost as cheaply as Germany. Markets appeared to assume that euro membership implied a form of implicit support, or at least that default was very unlikely. Once the crisis started, the assumption was reversed, and yields on the bonds of vulnerable states rose sharply, a process that could become self-reinforcing. If investors feared default, they demanded higher yields; higher yields raised the government's borrowing costs and made default more likely.

A government that borrows in its own currency and controls its central bank is in a different position from one that borrows in a currency it does not control. The former can in principle ensure that its central bank buys its bonds in a crisis; the latter, as euro-area members were, can face a funding squeeze that its own policies cannot relieve. Economists such as Paul De Grauwe argued that this made euro-area governments vulnerable to self-fulfilling crises of confidence in a way that the governments of the United Kingdom or the United States, with similar or higher debts, were not. Others emphasized that the main reasons for the crises were differences in fiscal and economic fundamentals, so that the problem lay in policy more than in design. The relative weights remain debated, and the evidence from the crisis supports elements of both views.

Other features of monetary union added to the vulnerability. Member states could no longer devalue their currencies to regain competitiveness after a boom had pushed up wages and prices; adjustment had to come through reductions in wages and prices, through emigration, or through transfers, none of which was easy. The euro area had no substantial central budget that could transfer funds to regions in trouble, as the budgets of federal states such as the United States and Germany do. Labor mobility among euro-area countries was limited by language and social ties. Banking supervision and resolution remained national, even though the banks were closely linked across borders, and the ECB could not act as a lender of last resort to governments. The next three divisions of this chapter examine how these gaps were exposed and partially filled.

Section 138.6

Greece and the European Sovereign-Debt Crisis

Greece was the first euro-area country to lose access to bond markets and the one whose crisis lasted longest. Its vulnerabilities were substantial and in part specific to Greece. Successive governments had run large deficits, financed by borrowing, since the 1980s. Public debt had exceeded one hundred percent of GDP for years before the crisis. The tax system raised less than its statutory rates implied, because of widespread evasion and an extensive informal economy. Clientelist politics, in which parties used public employment and benefits to reward supporters, shaped the public sector and the pension system. The Greek economy had a large current-account deficit, financed by capital inflows after euro entry in 2001, and its statistical record was unreliable, as Eurostat had noted on several occasions.

The immediate trigger came after the October 2009 election in which George Papandreou's PASOK defeated New Democracy. The new government announced that the previous government's estimate of the 2009 deficit, about six percent of GDP, was far too low, and the figure was later revised to approximately 15 percent after Eurostat audits. Rating agencies downgraded Greek bonds in December 2009. Investors, who had treated Greek bonds as nearly equivalent to German ones a few years earlier, began to demand much higher yields. By spring 2010 the Greek government faced yields that made borrowing for ordinary needs prohibitively expensive, and large debt repayments were falling due in April and May.

Greece's difficulties interacted with its banking system. Greek banks held large quantities of Greek government bonds, so that the fall in bond values weakened their balance sheets; a weak sovereign could not credibly guarantee its banks; and as depositors withdrew funds, the banks became dependent on emergency liquidity from the Eurosystem. This feedback loop between sovereign and bank solvency, sometimes called the doom loop, appeared first in Greece and later in Ireland, Spain, Cyprus, and Italy, in different forms. In Ireland the causation ran from banks to the sovereign; in Greece it ran initially from the sovereign to the banks.

It is important to distinguish Greek-specific weaknesses from general euro-area problems. Greece's fiscal record and statistical problems were without close parallel among the large euro-area economies. Ireland and Spain, by contrast, had run budget surpluses or small deficits and had low public debt before the crisis; their trouble came from private-sector credit booms and bank failures. Italy had a very high public debt, about 100 percent of GDP, but moderate deficits and a large domestic savings base. What the countries shared was that all had borrowed heavily from abroad, that all faced sharp reversals of capital flows, and that none could rely on its own central bank. A purely moralizing interpretation that blamed "profligate southern governments" therefore fits Greece better than the others, and an interpretation that blamed only the euro's design underestimates the specifically Greek problems. Most scholars combine the two.

Historical Vocabulary
The Sovereign–Bank "Doom Loop" and the Bond Spread

The bond yield is the interest rate an investor earns on a bond; the spread is the difference between the yield on one government's bonds and that on a benchmark, in the euro area usually German ten-year bonds. A widening spread signals that investors see more risk in the first government's debt. The sovereign–bank doom loop describes the mutual dependence of governments and banks: banks hold their own government's bonds and rely on its guarantee, while the government depends on its banks' health and may have to rescue them. If bond prices fall, banks lose capital; if banks need rescuing, the government's debt rises, pushing bond prices down further. The term became standard in 2011 and 2012 and shaped the arguments for a banking union, discussed in section 138.12.

Cause and Effect

Why Greece Lost Market Access in 2010

Long-term causes

Decades of high deficits and debt, weak tax collection, an inefficient public administration and clientelist politics, a large current-account deficit, and cheap euro-era credit that postponed adjustment.

Short-term causes

The 2008–2009 recession, the revelation of a far larger 2009 deficit, rating downgrades, and rising bond yields when large repayments fell due in spring 2010.

Event or transformation

Greece lost market access and requested assistance on April 23, 2010; the first assistance program followed on May 2.

Immediate effects

Severe fiscal adjustment, bank deposit outflows, rising unemployment, and the spread of market pressure to Ireland and Portugal.

Long-term consequences

A prolonged contraction, repeated programs, a restructuring of private debt in 2012, and a deep political realignment.

Historical complexity

No single cause suffices: national fiscal weakness, flawed statistics, the design of the euro, global liquidity conditions, and the choices of lenders and governments each contributed. Historians continue to disagree about their relative weights.
Section 138.7

Bailouts, the Troika, and the First Adjustment Programs

The euro-area response to Greece's request was improvised and constrained. The no-bailout clause seemed to prohibit assistance, but governments feared that a Greek default would trigger contagion that could damage French and German banks and threaten the euro. After weeks of delay, partly owing to German political hesitation before regional elections in May 2010, euro-area members and the IMF agreed on a first assistance program on May 2, 2010. It provided up to 110 billion euros in loans over three years, about 80 billion from euro-area governments in bilateral loans and about 30 billion from the IMF. The program was negotiated by a trio of institutions, the European Commission, the ECB, and the IMF, which became known as the Troika.

The loans came with conditions. Greece committed in a memorandum of understanding to reduce its deficit by many percentage points of GDP over several years through spending cuts, including public-sector wages and pensions, and tax increases, including higher value added tax, and to carry out structural reforms in tax administration, labor markets, pensions, and the state sector. Disbursements were made in installments after the Troika certified that conditions had been met. The theory behind such conditionality was that lenders needed assurance that their money would be repaid, and that reforms would restore competitiveness and growth. The IMF's own staff later acknowledged, in an assessment published in 2013, that the first program had underestimated the damage that fiscal consolidation would do to output and that a restructuring of Greek debt should have occurred earlier. This admission has been central to later debates, examined in section 138.10.

To create a mechanism for lending to other states, euro-area governments established the European Financial Stability Facility in 2010, a temporary vehicle that could raise funds on markets backed by guarantees from the member states, alongside a smaller European Financial Stabilisation Mechanism backed by the Union budget. Ireland, whose banking collapse had overwhelmed the state's finances, requested assistance in November 2010 and received a program of about 85 billion euros, which included resources of the Irish government itself. Portugal, whose economy had grown slowly for a decade and which had been hit by rising borrowing costs, requested help in spring 2011 and received a program of about 78 billion euros. In both cases, the Troika imposed fiscal and structural conditions. Ireland and Portugal ultimately completed their programs, in 2013 and 2014 respectively, without a second program, and returned to bond markets.

Greece's first program did not restore market access, because the economy contracted more than projected and the debt burden remained unsustainable. In 2011 the Eurogroup recognized that private creditors would have to bear losses. A second program was agreed in early 2012, providing about 130 billion euros, and a debt restructuring called Private Sector Involvement was completed in March 2012. Holders of roughly 200 billion euros of Greek bonds exchanged them for new bonds with a lower face value, longer maturities, and lower interest, in what was at the time the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history; the nominal reduction was 53.5 percent of the face value, though the economic loss varied by the terms. The restructuring largely excluded official creditors, and Greek banks, pension funds, and other domestic holders suffered substantial losses, which in turn required a further recapitalization of Greek banks with official funds. Political upheaval accompanied this process. In November 2011, Papandreou's announcement of a referendum on the second package alarmed European leaders, and he resigned within days, with a technocratic government headed by Lucas Papademos replacing him.

The programs reflected a particular view of the crisis: that it was a problem of national fiscal and structural weakness that could be solved by adjustment, supported by temporary loans. Critics argued that the programs treated a problem of solvency as one of liquidity, or demanded adjustment at a speed and scale that damaged the capacity to repay. Defenders replied that the lenders were taxpayers in other countries who could not be asked to provide unconditional funds, and that reforms were essential. The disagreement had a political dimension, since creditor and debtor governments did not see their interests identically, as the discussion of sovereignty in section 138.14 shows.

Section 138.8

Contagion across Europe: Spain, Italy, Cyprus, and the Peripheral Economies

By 2011 the crisis had spread beyond the three states that had received full sovereign programs. The central question was whether market pressure would reach large economies that could not be rescued by the facilities then available.

Spain's crisis was primarily a banking crisis. Its public debt had been low before 2008, about 36 percent of GDP, but the collapse of the construction boom left many savings banks, the cajas, with large losses on property loans. The government restructured the sector, merged institutions, and nationalized the large bank Bankia in May 2012, but doubts about the cost of recapitalizing the banks and about regional government finances pushed up borrowing costs. In June 2012, the Spanish government requested financial assistance for its banks, and euro-area members agreed in July on a program of up to 100 billion euros, of which roughly 41 billion was drawn. The funds went to the Spanish bank-restructuring fund and were then lent to the banks, with conditions on banking sector reform. Spain did not receive a full sovereign program, and its assistance ended in January 2014. This distinction is important: the loans addressed the banking sector, although they increased Spain's sovereign debt until the later changes to European rules.

Italy's problem was different again. Its public debt was about 120 percent of GDP, one of the highest in the euro area, but its annual deficits were moderate and its banks were not initially in crisis. Its economy, however, had grown very slowly for years. In the second half of 2011, Italian bond yields rose toward seven percent as investors doubted the government's ability to pursue reform. In August 2011 the ECB's then president and president-designate wrote a confidential letter to Prime Minister Silvio Berlusconi urging measures; Berlusconi resigned in November 2011, and a technocratic government under Mario Monti replaced him. Because Italy's debt was too large for the available rescue funds to cover, its situation was viewed as potentially decisive for the euro. The relief that came in 2012, discussed in the next division, was largely the result of ECB action and was never accompanied by an Italian sovereign program.

Cyprus provided the final acute episode. Its banks, which had grown to roughly seven times the size of the island's economy, held large amounts of Greek government bonds and suffered heavy losses in the 2012 restructuring. In March 2013, a first euro-area proposal to impose a levy on all bank deposits, including insured ones, was rejected by the Cypriot parliament. A revised agreement closed the second-largest bank, Laiki (Popular Bank), and imposed losses on uninsured depositors and bondholders of the Bank of Cyprus, while protecting deposits below 100,000 euros. Cyprus received a program of about 10 billion euros from the new European Stability Mechanism and the IMF and imposed capital controls that were lifted in stages until 2015. The episode showed that depositors, as well as bondholders, could be asked to absorb losses, and it left a precedent that influenced later rules on bank resolution.

Comparing the cases clarifies the types of assistance. Greece, Ireland, Portugal, and Cyprus received full macroeconomic adjustment programs, in which the whole economy was subject to conditions and monitoring. Spain received assistance restricted to its banking sector, and Italy received none, though it was subject to heavy market pressure and informal pressure from European institutions. Hungary, Latvia, and Romania, as non-euro EU members, received balance-of-payments assistance from the Union and the IMF, and Iceland, outside the Union, received an IMF program. Ireland and Portugal later exited their programs cleanly, while Greece required three programs. The reason that national problems could endanger the shared currency lay in the interdependence of the banking systems, the sovereign bond markets, and the common currency itself: a default or exit by one member could cause losses in other countries' banks, and markets might assume that a country that left once could be followed by others.

Section 138.9

The European Central Bank and the Defense of the Euro

The ECB's mandate, set by the treaties, gave it the primary objective of maintaining price stability, and it prohibited the direct purchase of government debt. In the early years of the crisis, the ECB acted as a lender to banks, supplying liquidity against collateral, but it hesitated to act as a backstop for governments. In May 2010, under President Jean-Claude Trichet, it began a limited Securities Markets Programme of bond purchases to calm markets, but the program was controversial inside the Governing Council, and it was suspended and revived as pressure shifted from Greece to Italy and Spain. When Mario Draghi became president on November 1, 2011, the euro area was in the most dangerous phase of the crisis. Italian and Spanish yields were rising, banks were losing access to funding, and some analysts openly forecast that the euro would break up.

Draghi's early measures concentrated on banks. In December 2011 and February 2012, the ECB conducted two long-term refinancing operations, providing banks with about one trillion euros in three-year loans at low interest. These operations prevented a funding collapse and allowed banks, in part, to buy their governments' bonds, which eased pressure on yields for a time but also strengthened the doom loop. By the summer of 2012, yields in Spain and Italy had again risen, and talk of a Greek exit from the euro, or "Grexit," was widespread.

On July 26, 2012, speaking at an investment conference in London, Draghi said that within its mandate the ECB was ready to do "whatever it takes to preserve the euro," and added that this would be sufficient. Markets responded at once, and bond spreads fell. But the speech was not an instrument. The substantive policy came on September 6, 2012, when the ECB announced Outright Monetary Transactions, a program under which it could purchase, in principle without ex ante quantitative limit, the short-term government bonds of countries that were subject to a European assistance program with the ESM or EFSF and which met its conditions. The conditionality was designed to address both the legal prohibition on monetary financing and the concern of the Bundesbank and German policymakers that the ECB would encourage fiscal laxity. The Bundesbank president, Jens Weidmann, opposed the program.

OMT has never actually been used. Its effect worked through expectations: investors who knew that a powerful central bank stood ready to buy bonds had less reason to flee, and the fear of a self-fulfilling default declined. Analysts disagree about whether the promise alone would have sufficed without the political commitments that accompanied it, including the banking-union agreement in June 2012 and the continued adjustment programs. The program was challenged in the German Federal Constitutional Court, which in 2014 referred the question to the Court of Justice of the European Union, and in June 2015 the Court of Justice held that the program was compatible with the treaties, subject to conditions. Later ECB instruments, including negative deposit rates from June 2014 and a large-scale public-sector asset purchase program announced in January 2015, extended the ECB's role in ways that provoked further legal and political disagreement, including a German constitutional court ruling in May 2020.

The ECB's role changed during the crisis in at least three ways. It became, in effect, a lender of last resort in sovereign bond markets, though the legal form of that role was carefully hedged. It became more openly involved in the politics of fiscal adjustment, as in the 2011 letters to the Italian and Spanish governments and its participation in the Troika. And, after 2014, it became a supervisor of banks. Critics, particularly in Germany, argued that these roles compromised its independence and exposed it to political pressure, and defenders argued that the Bank had to act to fulfil its mandate when the transmission of monetary policy had broken down. This debate belongs to the wider question of democratic accountability examined in section 138.14.

Students should be cautious about treating Draghi's speech as having single-handedly saved the euro. It is better understood as one decisive moment in a sequence that included the ECB's liquidity operations, the creation of the European Stability Mechanism, the banking-union decision of June 2012, the German government's decision to support the European rescue framework, and the continuing adjustment of the crisis economies. The speech changed expectations, but only because the underlying instruments, and the political backing of the major governments, existed or were being created.

Section 138.10

Austerity versus Growth: Europe's Great Economic Debate

Between 2010 and the middle of the decade, the dominant fiscal policy in much of Europe was fiscal consolidation: reductions in government deficits through spending cuts, tax increases, or both. The word "austerity," which critics used and supporters often rejected, became the label for this policy and for the broader package of wage restraint and structural reform that accompanied it in the crisis countries. The argument over whether it was necessary, prudent, or damaging was among the most important intellectual and political disputes of the period.

The case for consolidation rested on several claims. Governments with high debts and rising borrowing costs faced a risk that markets would refuse to lend, and fiscal adjustment was a precondition for regaining market confidence. Lenders to crisis countries, whose taxpayers were at risk, could reasonably demand that borrowers reform. Reduced deficits would, supporters argued, reduce risk premiums and eventually encourage private investment. Some economists and policymakers also cited research suggesting that high debt levels were associated with slower growth, notably a 2010 paper by Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff that identified a threshold near ninety percent of GDP. Others pointed to episodes in which deficit reduction had been followed by growth, a line of argument developed by Alberto Alesina and Silvia Ardagna on the possibility of "expansionary fiscal contractions." Germany's government, led by Chancellor Angela Merkel and Finance Minister Wolfgang Schäuble, emphasized rules, reduced moral hazard, and structural reform, and the 2012 Fiscal Compact reflected that preference.

The case against consolidation, made by economists such as Paul Krugman, Joseph Stiglitz, and many trade unions and left-wing parties, emphasized that cutting spending in a depressed economy reduces income and tax revenue, which can offset deficit reduction and deepen the recession. This argument depended on the size of the fiscal multiplier, the effect on output of a change in government spending or taxation. In January 2013, IMF economists published a study concluding that forecasters had underestimated multipliers during the consolidation period, so that fiscal tightening had harmed growth more than anticipated. Critics also challenged the Reinhart–Rogoff result: in 2013 a team at the University of Massachusetts Amherst identified coding errors and methodological choices in the original spreadsheet, and the authors acknowledged some errors while defending their broader conclusions. Others pointed out that causation could run from low growth to high debt, as well as the reverse.

The debate was intertwined with differences in context. Countries that could devalue their currencies, such as the United Kingdom, could offset fiscal tightening with monetary easing, while euro-area countries could not. Economies that were relatively open and whose trading partners were growing could expand exports; economies that were closed or whose trading partners were also consolidating found this harder. Some countries, such as Ireland and Latvia, adopted deep adjustment programs and later resumed growth, and were cited by supporters as successes; critics noted that their recoveries were aided by export demand, by currency conditions, and by large emigration, and that their output losses had been severe. Others, such as Greece, experienced a decline in output of about a quarter, and Spain and Portugal also suffered deep recessions.

Political positions did not follow a single pattern. In 2012, the French president François Hollande, newly elected, secured a "growth pact" at the European Council in June, though it involved modest sums. Draghi, in 2014, urged governments to use fiscal flexibility. By the middle of the decade, the Commission and many governments shifted toward a more flexible approach, and the IMF increasingly emphasized the importance of debt relief for Greece. Whether this shift reflected a change in evidence, a response to political backlash, or a recognition that the monetary policy of the ECB could not alone support recovery remains debated.

Historical Debate

Was Austerity Necessary, or Did It Deepen Europe's Crisis?

The question: Did fiscal consolidation in 2010 to 2014 restore fiscal stability and market confidence at an acceptable cost, or did it deepen the recession, raise debt ratios, and inflict avoidable social damage?

Interpretation A: Consolidation was necessary, though imperfect: Governments facing loss of market access had limited alternatives. Rising yields and the withdrawal of private lenders meant that deficits had to fall, and official lenders could not provide unlimited funds. Adjustment in Ireland, Portugal, and Spain, together with structural reforms, restored current-account balance, regained market access, and prepared the ground for later growth. Political limits on lending in creditor countries meant that conditionality was unavoidable.
Interpretation B: Consolidation was excessive, mistimed, and poorly designed: Simultaneous tightening across many economies, under conditions of weak demand and with no independent monetary policy, produced larger output losses than predicted, raised debt-to-GDP ratios through shrinking denominators, and caused lasting social harm, including long-term unemployment and emigration. Earlier restructuring of Greek debt, a slower pace of adjustment, or a coordinated fiscal expansion by countries with fiscal room, such as Germany, would have been preferable.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same data on deficits, debt, growth, and unemployment, and differ on the counterfactual: what would have happened with different policies. Evidence includes the IMF's revised multiplier estimates (2013), comparisons of countries with different adjustment paths, the experience of non-euro economies, and the question of whether bond yields fell because of fiscal adjustment or because of ECB intervention in 2012. A third view holds that the sequence, a quick fiscal tightening before the banking sector was repaired, was the most important error, and that the timing of repair and consolidation matters more than the choice between them.

Why it matters: The answer shapes the assessment of European institutions' competence and legitimacy, the interpretation of political realignments after 2010, and the design of future fiscal rules.

Section 138.11

The Greek Crisis of 2015: Syriza, the Referendum, and the Third Program

After the 2012 restructuring and the ECB's intervention, Greece's immediate emergency eased but its economy remained severely depressed. Output had fallen by about a quarter from its 2007 peak, unemployment exceeded a quarter of the labor force, public debt remained above 170 percent of GDP, and wages and pensions had been cut sharply. The coalition government led by Antonis Samaras of New Democracy had implemented the programs, with PASOK as junior partner, but support for the established parties had eroded, and the radical left coalition Syriza, led by Alexis Tsipras, had risen from marginal status to leading opposition party.

Syriza won the election of January 25, 2015, with about 36 percent of the vote and formed a government with the small right-wing party Independent Greeks. It promised to end austerity, renegotiate the terms of the program, and seek debt relief, while remaining in the euro. Yanis Varoufakis, an economist, became finance minister. The new government's strategy relied on the belief that euro-area governments would not risk the consequences of a Greek exit, while the creditors believed that the Greek government would, in the end, accept the program. Negotiations were marked by mutual mistrust. In February 2015, the Eurogroup agreed to extend the existing program for four months, and the ECB, which had stopped accepting Greek government bonds as collateral, limited the emergency liquidity that Greek banks could draw. Depositors withdrew funds, the government struggled to pay pensions and salaries, and talks failed to produce agreement by the end of June.

On June 27, Tsipras announced a referendum for July 5 on whether to accept a package of proposals from the Commission, the ECB, and the IMF. The Eurogroup declined to extend the program, which expired on June 30, and the Greek government failed to repay an IMF installment of about 1.5 billion euros that day. The ECB held the level of emergency funding steady, and the Greek government imposed capital controls and closed banks on June 29. Cash withdrawals from automatic teller machines were limited to 60 euros a day. The referendum campaign took place in this atmosphere. The government urged a "No" vote, arguing that it would strengthen its negotiating hand, while opposition parties, many European leaders, and much of the Greek business community urged "Yes," warning that a No vote could lead to exit from the euro.

On July 5, about 61 percent of voters, on a turnout of around 62 percent, voted No. The result was widely read as a repudiation of austerity. But what it meant in practice was contested. The proposal on which voters had been asked to decide was no longer formally on offer, since the program had expired. Tsipras said that he would return to negotiations with a stronger mandate and Varoufakis resigned the next day, and over the following week negotiations reached a crisis. On July 12 and 13, euro-area leaders held a marathon summit. German officials circulated a paper proposing a temporary Greek exit from the euro, and there was open disagreement between Germany and France. The summit concluded with an agreement under which Greece would pass a series of laws on tax, pensions, and market reforms and negotiate a new three-year program from the ESM, with provisions for a privatization fund and for possible debt-relief measures later. Tsipras accepted terms that many in his own party considered harsher than the proposals voters had rejected, and his government passed the measures with support from the opposition. The ESM approved a third program of up to 86 billion euros in August 2015, and Tsipras resigned and won new elections in September.

The episode is often presented as a test of democratic will against external pressure, and it can be read that way. A more precise account separates three things: the voters' rejection of a particular package, the government's decision to remain in the euro, and the terms of the eventual settlement. The referendum showed the strength of popular opposition to the programs. The settlement showed that the government judged that the cost of leaving the euro, including likely bank collapses, inflation, and uncertainty, was greater than the cost of accepting the terms. Whether the creditors would in fact have forced Greece out is unclear, and the German proposal for a temporary exit was not adopted. Participants and historians continue to dispute these interpretations, and memoirs by Varoufakis, Schäuble, and others give conflicting accounts.

Section 138.12

Reforming the Eurozone: Banking Union and New Financial Governance

The euro area changed its institutions significantly during and after the crisis, though less than many reformers wished. The reforms fall into three groups: crisis-lending mechanisms, fiscal governance, and banking union.

In crisis lending, the temporary EFSF was succeeded by the European Stability Mechanism, a permanent international institution based in Luxembourg, established by a treaty among euro-area governments signed in 2012 and inaugurated that October, with a lending capacity of 500 billion euros. The ESM could lend to governments under conditions and, from 2012, to recapitalize banks. Its creation required a treaty change outside the Union treaties, in part because Britain and others would not agree to amend the Union treaties, and its legality was confirmed by the Court of Justice in the 2012 Pringle judgment.

In fiscal governance, the Union strengthened its rules and surveillance in several steps. The "Six-Pack" of legislation in 2011 and the "Two-Pack" of 2013 tightened the Stability and Growth Pact, introduced a procedure for macroeconomic imbalances, and gave the Commission greater powers to review national budgets. The European Semester, introduced in 2011, created an annual cycle in which the Commission and Council reviewed national budgets and reform plans. In March 2012, twenty-five member states signed the Treaty on Stability, Coordination and Governance, usually called the Fiscal Compact, which required signatories to incorporate balanced-budget rules into national law; the United Kingdom and the Czech Republic did not sign, after David Cameron's refusal in December 2011 to accept a treaty change without safeguards for the City of London. These measures increased the supervision of national budgets by European institutions, which critics saw as a diminution of national democratic control and supporters saw as a necessary replacement for the lax enforcement of earlier rules.

The most important institutional innovation was banking union, agreed in principle at the June 2012 summit and built in stages. The Single Supervisory Mechanism, established by Council Regulation 1024/2013, gave the ECB direct supervision of the largest banks in participating countries, about 120 institutions when it became operational on November 4, 2014, with national authorities supervising the smaller ones. The Single Resolution Mechanism, established by Regulation 806/2014 and operational from January 2016, created a Single Resolution Board and a fund, financed by contributions from banks, to handle failing banks, and it operates alongside the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive of 2014, which requires that shareholders and creditors absorb losses before public money is used. These rules reflect the lesson of the crisis that governments had often rescued banks with taxpayers' money. The third intended pillar, a common deposit insurance scheme, was not agreed, because Germany and some other states feared that it would make them liable for the losses of banks elsewhere. Banking union therefore remained incomplete.

The reforms have limits. The euro area still has no substantial common fiscal capacity or common safe asset, though later debates, and the joint borrowing agreed for pandemic recovery in 2020, show that the issue was far from closed. The ESM remained an intergovernmental body outside the main Union institutions. The reliance on national rules and monitoring meant that the political question of who decides, and who pays, was managed rather than resolved. In 2012 the Commission President José Manuel Barroso and others discussed a "genuine economic and monetary union," and the Four Presidents' Report outlined ambitious proposals, but most were not adopted.

Greece completed its third program on August 20, 2018, ending a period of almost eight years during which it had been dependent on official lending. In June 2018, the Eurogroup agreed on measures including deferral of some interest and principal payments and an extension of maturities, along with a mechanism for post-program surveillance. The exit did not mean that the crisis had been fully resolved: Greek public debt remained among the highest in the world as a share of GDP, the banking system carried a large burden of non-performing loans, and the economy was well below its pre-crisis size. The legacy of the crisis was thus partly institutional, in the new European structures, and partly social and political, in the sustained damage to Greek and other societies.

Section 138.13

The Social Consequences of Crisis: Unemployment, Inequality, and Protest

The social effects of the crisis varied by country, region, age, and occupation, and generalizations about "European poverty" conceal as much as they reveal. Germany, Poland, and Sweden, for example, experienced growth and falling unemployment for much of the period, while Greece, Spain, Portugal, Ireland, and Cyprus suffered deep and prolonged downturns. The more accurate picture is one of sharply different national and regional experiences within a single political union.

Unemployment was the central social fact in the crisis countries. At its peak, around 2013, the unemployment rate was approximately 27 percent in Greece and 26 percent in Spain, and unemployment among those under twenty-five exceeded 55 percent in both. Long-term unemployment, in which workers have been jobless for a year or more, rose steeply and carried lasting consequences for skills, income, and health. In Ireland, unemployment peaked near 15 percent, and in Portugal at about 16 percent. Wage cuts and tax increases reduced household incomes, and public spending reductions affected health services, education, and social benefits. In Greece, the health budget and household access to medical care were sharply reduced, and researchers later debated the size of the effects on mortality and mental health, with some studies finding increases in suicide and in untreated illness while others emphasized the difficulty of separating the effects of the crisis from longer-term trends. In Spain, the rise in unemployment was cushioned for many families by household support, and by the tendency of adult children to remain with parents, but the burden fell unevenly.

Emigration became a significant response. Young and highly qualified workers left Ireland, Portugal, Spain, and Greece for Germany, the United Kingdom, northern Europe, Latin America, and elsewhere. Estimates of the numbers vary widely, depending on whether they rely on the departing countries' records or the receiving countries', and on whether returns are counted, so claims about a "lost generation" should be handled with care. The movement also interacted with migration within the Union from central and eastern Europe, a subject examined in Chapter 137, and with debates in receiving countries such as the United Kingdom, as section 138.15 shows. Countries that lost young workers faced a longer-term demographic and fiscal loss.

Protest movements took various forms. In Greece, general strikes were frequent from 2010, and in 2011 crowds occupied Syntagma Square in Athens in a movement known as the "Aganaktismenoi," the indignant. In Spain, the movement known as the Indignados or 15-M began on May 15, 2011, with demonstrations and occupations of public squares across the country, demanding more responsive democracy and an end to corruption and austerity. In Portugal, large demonstrations in March 2011 and September 2012 expressed discontent with austerity and joblessness. In Ireland, protest was more muted, though large demonstrations occurred against the water charges introduced in 2014.

The crisis also altered party systems. In Greece, the two traditional parties, PASOK and New Democracy, lost much of their support: Syriza replaced PASOK as the main left party, and the far-right Golden Dawn entered parliament in 2012 with about seven percent of the vote, before its leadership was prosecuted and, in 2020, convicted of operating a criminal organization. In Spain, Podemos, founded in January 2014, and Ciudadanos disrupted the two-party system that had existed since the 1980s. In Italy, the Five Star Movement won about a quarter of the vote in the 2013 election. In Portugal and Ireland, by contrast, established parties adapted more successfully. Trust in the European Union, measured by Eurobarometer surveys, fell sharply in crisis countries, in Spain, for example, from a clear majority before the crisis to roughly one fifth in 2013, though attitudes toward the euro itself remained more favorable than attitudes toward EU institutions, since many citizens feared the consequences of leaving the currency.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Adjustment in Greece and Spain

Greek households experienced the crisis as a series of overlapping reductions: cuts to public-sector salaries and pensions, increases in income and property taxes and in value added tax, loss of private-sector jobs, and, after 2015, limits on bank withdrawals. Many households relied on pensions of older relatives to support unemployed adult children, and when pensions were cut, the effect spread through extended families. Public hospitals faced shortages of supplies and staff, and some communities organized "social pharmacies" and clinics run by volunteers, as well as solidarity kitchens. Small businesses closed in large numbers, and many professionals emigrated.

In Spain, the crisis combined unemployment with a housing crisis. Families who had taken out mortgages at the peak faced eviction, and a civil society campaign, the Platform for People Affected by Mortgages (Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca), organized to stop evictions and to push for reform of the law governing mortgage debt. Young Spaniards, often holding university degrees, found that they could obtain only temporary contracts or none, and many postponed leaving their parents' homes or forming families. These stories do not represent every household in either country: pensioners with stable incomes, public employees whose jobs were protected, and some export-oriented firms fared differently. Historians therefore combine statistical evidence with oral histories, local studies, and surveys to build a more complete picture.

Section 138.14

National Sovereignty, Democracy, and European Economic Governance

The crisis raised a basic constitutional question: who had the authority to decide on taxation, public spending, pensions, and labor law, when governments depended on loans conditional on specific policies? Elected national governments remained formally sovereign, but their room for maneuver was limited by creditors, markets, and European rules. Critics spoke of a "democratic deficit," and defenders of the arrangements pointed out that governments had accepted the programs voluntarily and that they had been approved by national parliaments.

The tension took several forms. In debtor countries, voters saw policy dictated by outsiders who were not accountable to them. In Greece, the Troika's role in specifying detailed reforms became a symbol of this relationship, and the replacement in November 2011 of elected prime ministers by technocratic governments in Greece, under Lucas Papademos, and in Italy, under Mario Monti, was widely seen as evidence of external pressure, although both transitions followed constitutional procedures and parliamentary votes. Constitutional courts also intervened: Portugal's Constitutional Court struck down several austerity measures in 2012 and 2013 as violating principles of equality, forcing the government to find substitutes, and the Cypriot parliament's rejection of the deposit levy in March 2013 forced a different plan.

In creditor countries, voters and parliaments saw themselves as being asked to underwrite the debts of others. The German Constitutional Court ruled in 2011 and again in September 2012 that German participation in the EFSF and the ESM was compatible with the Basic Law, but insisted that the Bundestag retain control over budgetary commitments, so that the government needed parliamentary approval for major decisions. In Finland, the Netherlands, and Slovakia, parliamentary votes on rescue measures were politically fraught. The creditor perspective stressed that the treaties had not envisaged transfers, that moral hazard needed to be prevented, and that national taxpayers could not be expected to fund unconditional support.

European institutions faced their own legitimacy problems. The Troika was an ad hoc arrangement without a clear basis in the treaties for the Commission's and ECB's participation in negotiating programs, and in 2014 the European Parliament produced a report criticizing its lack of democratic accountability and transparency. Decisions were frequently made in summits of heads of government and meetings of the Eurogroup, an informal body, rather than in institutions directly accountable to the European Parliament. At the same time, the European Parliament, the Commission, and national parliaments claimed different bases of legitimacy, and there was no consensus on whether a European political community existed that could legitimate fiscal redistribution on a large scale.

The debate also concerned alternative models. One school of thought, associated with many in the German and Dutch establishments, favored a rules-based union, in which governments were bound to fiscal discipline and no transfers occurred. Another, favored by many in France, southern Europe, and in the European Parliament, argued for a deeper political and fiscal union, with common borrowing and a common budget, and greater democratic control at the European level. A third, represented by Eurosceptic parties across the continent, sought to return powers to national governments or to dissolve the currency. Governments largely chose a middle path of strengthened rules and limited mutual support. These choices had consequences for the later politics of integration and, in the United Kingdom, fed into arguments about whether staying in the Union meant being drawn into a system in which Britain, as a non-euro member, would be outvoted on matters affecting the City of London.

Section 138.15

Euroscepticism and the Political Origins of Brexit

Britain's departure from the European Union cannot be explained only by the events of 2008 to 2016. The United Kingdom's relationship to European integration had been distinctive from the beginning. Britain declined to join the original European Coal and Steel Community and the European Economic Community in the 1950s, applied to join in the 1960s, was twice vetoed by President Charles de Gaulle, and entered the European Economic Community on January 1, 1973, as part of the same enlargement that included Ireland and Denmark. In 1975, a referendum under the Labour government of Harold Wilson, held after renegotiation of the terms of membership, produced a vote of about 67 percent in favor of staying in. Even then, the parties were divided: much of the Labour left and some Conservatives were opposed.

During the 1980s and 1990s, the relationship grew more contested. Margaret Thatcher negotiated a budget rebate in 1984, supported the single market, and in her Bruges speech of 1988 warned against a European "superstate," as she described it. Britain joined the Exchange Rate Mechanism in 1990 and was forced out on September 16, 1992, "Black Wednesday," after a speculative attack on the pound, an episode that discredited the policy among many Conservatives and strengthened the view that European monetary arrangements were hazardous. The ratification of the Maastricht Treaty was a bruising parliamentary battle for John Major's government, and Britain obtained an opt-out from the single currency and, until 1997, from the Social Chapter. It also remained outside the Schengen area, and secured a distinct arrangement on justice and home affairs. The principle that the UK would not join the euro unless the government passed five economic tests set by Gordon Brown in 1997 and assessed in 2003 meant in practice that Britain never adopted it.

Outside the main parties, the UK Independence Party, founded in 1993 from the earlier Anti-Federalist League, campaigned for withdrawal. For many years it was a small force, but under Nigel Farage it grew, benefiting from the use of proportional representation in European Parliament elections, and in 2014 it came first in the United Kingdom in the European Parliament elections with about 27 percent of the vote. In the 2015 general election it won about 12.6 percent of the vote, though under the first-past-the-post system only a single seat. Within the Conservative Party, the Lisbon Treaty of 2007 and the Eurozone crisis renewed demands for a referendum. The enlargement of 2004, in which Britain, unlike most other member states, did not impose transitional controls on workers from the new central and eastern European members, produced larger inflows than the government had predicted, and immigration became a major political issue, as discussed in Chapter 137.

David Cameron, who became Conservative leader in 2005 and prime minister in a coalition in 2010, sought to manage these pressures. In a speech at Bloomberg in London on January 23, 2013, he promised that, if the Conservatives were re-elected, he would renegotiate Britain's terms of membership and then hold an in-or-out referendum. The Conservatives won an unexpected overall majority in May 2015, and Parliament passed the European Union Referendum Act in December 2015. Cameron negotiated a settlement with the other member states in February 2016, including limits on in-work benefits for new migrants, protections for non-euro states, and an exemption from the aim of "ever closer union." He recommended that Britain remain in the Union on those terms. Many of his own ministers, including Michael Gove and, after some hesitation, Boris Johnson, joined the campaign to leave.

How did the financial crisis bear on these developments? The relationship is real but indirect. The Eurozone crisis, in which the euro area seemed to be moving toward closer integration to save its currency, led many British Eurosceptics to argue that the Union was becoming a political union in which Britain would be a marginal member, and Remain supporters to reply that Britain's separate currency had protected it. The recession and the government's fiscal consolidation after 2010 contributed to economic and regional grievances, and surveys and studies later examined whether areas more exposed to cuts or to weak local economies were more likely to vote Leave. But British opposition to the Union, expressed in parliamentary and party politics, had existed since the 1970s, and the crisis did not create it.

Historical Debate

Did Economic Insecurity and Austerity Cause Brexit?

The question: To what extent did the financial crisis and the policies of the 2010s, as opposed to older questions of sovereignty, identity, and attitudes toward the European Union, produce the vote to leave in 2016?

Interpretation A: Long-standing attitudes toward sovereignty, identity, and integration were decisive: Studies of the British Election Study and of voting by Harold Clarke, Matthew Goodwin, and Paul Whiteley emphasize that attitudes toward the EU, immigration, and national identity, developed over decades, were stronger predictors of the vote than short-term economic conditions. Leave voters, on this view, were often motivated by concerns about control over laws and borders, and by a view of Britain's place in the world that predated the crisis.
Interpretation B: Economic and regional grievance, including austerity, was an important cause: Economists such as Sascha Becker, Thiemo Fetzer, and Dennis Novy found that low-income, low-education areas and areas that had experienced deindustrialization and slow growth voted strongly Leave, and Fetzer argued that areas more exposed to welfare cuts after 2010 showed greater support for UKIP and for Leave. On this view, economic insecurity made voters more receptive to the claim that leaving would allow Britain to "take back control."

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on constituency and local-authority data, survey evidence, and voting patterns. Differences concern causal inference: areas that voted Leave also had older populations, lower qualifications, and different attitudes, and it is difficult to separate these influences. Many scholars, including Eric Kaufmann and others who study cultural values, emphasize a combination of age, education, national identity, and attitudes toward immigration, and the evidence is generally best read as supporting several overlapping explanations rather than a single one.

Why it matters: If Brexit was largely a response to austerity, it would be a direct consequence of the financial crisis; if it was rooted in older constitutional and cultural disputes, the crisis was a context and not a cause. Historians therefore usually describe Brexit as having long-term, medium-term, and short-term causes, only some of which were economic.

Student task: List one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation and one reason why the evidence is difficult to interpret. Then write a short paragraph explaining how a combination of explanations might be assembled without assuming that any single factor was decisive.

Section 138.16

The Brexit Referendum of June 23, 2016

The campaign for the referendum lasted from the passing of the Referendum Act in December 2015 and the announcement on February 20, 2016, of the date, until June 23. The question on the ballot paper was: should the United Kingdom remain a member of the European Union or leave it? The Act did not legally bind the government to act on the result, and in strict constitutional terms the referendum was advisory, although the government had promised to implement the outcome, a distinction that became important afterward.

The official campaign for Leave was Vote Leave, led by Conservative politicians including Michael Gove and Boris Johnson, the Labour MP Gisela Stuart, and the campaign director Dominic Cummings, with the slogan "Take Back Control." A separate group, Leave.EU, supported by Nigel Farage and the businessman Arron Banks, emphasized immigration. Britain Stronger in Europe, the official Remain campaign, was led by cross-party figures, with the support of the prime minister, the chancellor George Osborne, and most of the Labour leadership, although the party leader Jeremy Corbyn was criticized by some for lukewarm engagement. Business organizations, the Bank of England, the IMF, and the Treasury warned of economic damage from leaving, and Leave supporters dismissed such warnings as "Project Fear."

The central arguments concerned sovereignty, immigration, and the economy. Leave campaigners argued that membership constrained Parliament's ability to make law, that the EU's institutions were unaccountable, that migration from the Union was too high and could not be controlled while freedom of movement applied, and that Britain could negotiate trade agreements around the world. One Vote Leave claim, that the UK sent £350 million a week to the EU that could be spent on the National Health Service, was challenged by the UK Statistics Authority as misleading, since it ignored the rebate and receipts. Remain campaigners argued that leaving would harm trade, investment, and living standards, that membership gave Britain influence and security, and that the benefits of the single market outweighed the costs. Claims on both sides were criticized as exaggerated. The murder of the Labour MP Jo Cox on June 16 by a man with far-right views led to a temporary suspension of campaigning.

The result, declared on June 24, showed a vote of 51.9 percent for Leave and 48.1 percent for Remain, on a turnout of 72.2 percent, with about 17.4 million votes for Leave and 16.1 million for Remain. Voting varied markedly by nation. England voted for Leave by about 53.4 percent and Wales by about 52.5 percent, while Scotland voted for Remain by about 62 percent, with every Scottish council area voting Remain, and Northern Ireland voted for Remain by about 55.8 percent, with a sharp divide between predominantly nationalist and predominantly unionist areas. London voted strongly for Remain, while most of the English regions outside London and the south-east voted Leave. Gibraltar voted overwhelmingly to remain.

Post-referendum polling and analysis, using surveys such as those by Lord Ashcroft and YouGov and the British Election Study, indicated that older voters were more likely than younger ones to vote Leave, that those with degrees were more likely to vote Remain, and that voters in certain areas with lower incomes and manufacturing decline leaned Leave, though these are survey-based estimates and not records of how individuals voted. Ethnic minority voters appear to have leaned toward Remain, though patterns varied. Voters gave multiple motivations, including sovereignty and the desire to control immigration, and concern about the economy.

Students should distinguish three things. The first is the electoral result, which is a documented fact. The second is voters' motivations, which can be studied only through surveys and indirect evidence and were varied. The third is the constitutional process required to implement the result, which was not specified by the referendum itself. Beyond these, later inquiries examined aspects of the campaign: the Electoral Commission found that Vote Leave had breached spending rules, and fined it, and fined Leave.EU for other breaches, and there were investigations into the use of data and into alleged foreign interference. Investigators and scholars have reached varying conclusions about these matters, and there is no consensus evidence that they changed the outcome; the conduct of the campaign remains a subject of political dispute. Prime Minister David Cameron resigned on June 24, and the process of withdrawal began under his successor.

Section 138.17

Article 50 and the Constitutional Struggle over Withdrawal

The referendum answered one question, whether to leave, but not the questions that followed: how, when, and on what terms. Article 50 of the Treaty on European Union, introduced by the Lisbon Treaty, provides that a member state may withdraw in accordance with its constitutional requirements, by notifying the European Council, after which the treaties cease to apply two years later unless an agreement is reached or the period is extended. No state had previously used it. After Cameron's resignation, Theresa May became Prime Minister on July 13, 2016, with the slogan "Brexit means Brexit," and in a speech at Lancaster House on January 17, 2017, she announced that the United Kingdom would leave the single market and the customs union, ending the jurisdiction of the Court of Justice of the European Union and ending free movement.

The first constitutional dispute concerned who could trigger Article 50. The government argued that it could do so using the royal prerogative, the powers of the executive in foreign affairs. In the case brought by the businesswoman Gina Miller and others, the High Court in November 2016 and the Supreme Court on January 24, 2017, ruled that an Act of Parliament was required, because withdrawal would remove rights created by statute. The Court also held that the devolved legislatures in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland had no legal veto. Parliament passed a short Act, and on March 29, 2017, May formally notified the European Council. The two-year clock was set to expire on March 29, 2019. In December 2018, the Court of Justice, in a case brought by Scottish politicians, held that a member state could revoke its notification unilaterally, a ruling that gave support to those who wished to reverse the decision.

May's attempt to strengthen her position by calling a general election on June 8, 2017, backfired: the Conservatives lost their majority, winning 317 seats, while Labour under Jeremy Corbyn won 262, and May governed with the support of the Democratic Unionist Party of Northern Ireland through a confidence-and-supply arrangement. This weakened her position in negotiations and in Parliament. The EU's negotiating team, led by Michel Barnier, insisted on sequencing: first the rights of citizens, the financial settlement, and the Irish border, and only then the future relationship. A joint report in December 2017 set out preliminary agreement. In July 2018, the cabinet agreed at Chequers a proposal for a "common rulebook" for goods, which led to the resignations of David Davis and Boris Johnson, and in November 2018 the government agreed a draft Withdrawal Agreement with the EU.

Parliament rejected it. On January 15, 2019, the House of Commons defeated the agreement by 432 votes to 202, the largest government defeat in modern parliamentary history, and it was defeated twice more, on March 12 and March 29. The reasons for rejection varied: some Brexit supporters opposed the Irish "backstop," described in the next section, while some pro-Remain MPs wanted a second referendum or a closer relationship with the EU. The Union agreed to extend the deadline, first to April 12 and then to October 31, 2019. May announced her resignation, and was succeeded as Conservative leader and Prime Minister on July 24, 2019, by Boris Johnson. His attempt to prorogue Parliament for five weeks in September 2019 was ruled unlawful by the Supreme Court on September 24. Johnson negotiated a revised agreement in October, discussed below, but Parliament did not approve it in time, and the election of December 12, 2019, gave the Conservatives 365 seats, an overall majority of 80, which allowed the Withdrawal Agreement to pass.

These events raised competing understandings of the constitution. One view held that the referendum expressed the sovereign will of the people, and that Parliament and the government were bound to implement it. Another held that Parliamentary sovereignty meant that Parliament, not a referendum, was the final authority, and that it was entitled to determine the form of withdrawal, or to decline it. A third view stressed that the referendum had been decided by a narrow margin and without a specified plan, so that the question of what kind of Brexit the public had endorsed was open. The courts, in the Miller cases, affirmed the role of Parliament without overturning the outcome of the referendum.

The effects on the Union's component nations were serious. Scotland had voted 62 percent to remain, and the Scottish government under Nicola Sturgeon argued that Brexit, against Scotland's will, was a material change from the 2014 independence referendum, in which Scotland had voted 55 percent to 45 percent to stay in the United Kingdom, and sought a second independence referendum. In November 2022, the Supreme Court held that the Scottish Parliament could not legislate for such a referendum without the consent of the UK Parliament. In Northern Ireland, the vote to remain, the dependence of the peace settlement on the open border, and the position of the Democratic Unionist Party, which supported Leave, created a unique political situation. The power-sharing institutions of Northern Ireland were suspended from January 2017 to January 2020 and again from 2022 to 2024, with disputes over Brexit arrangements among the causes of the second suspension.

Section 138.18

Negotiating Brexit: The Withdrawal Agreement and the Irish Border

The Irish border proved the most intractable issue in the withdrawal negotiations. Before 1998, the border between Northern Ireland, part of the United Kingdom, and the Republic of Ireland had been militarized during the conflict known as the Troubles. The Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement of April 10, 1998, established power-sharing institutions in Northern Ireland and cross-border bodies, and with both countries in the single market and customs union, the border became almost invisible, with no customs posts or routine controls. A common travel area between Ireland and the United Kingdom, which long predated the European Union, also allowed free movement. Trade across the island, particularly in agriculture and food, was closely integrated. Once the United Kingdom left the single market and the customs union, a border between the EU and a non-member would need customs and regulatory controls, unless an alternative arrangement was found, and any physical infrastructure risked becoming a target and a symbol of division.

The draft agreement of November 2018 included a "backstop," a guarantee that, unless and until another solution was found, the whole United Kingdom would remain in a customs territory with the EU, and Northern Ireland would stay aligned with certain single-market rules, so that no hard border would be needed. Brexit supporters objected that it could trap the UK indefinitely in EU rules, and the DUP objected to any difference between Northern Ireland and Great Britain. The revised agreement that Boris Johnson reached with the EU on October 17, 2019, removed the UK-wide backstop and replaced it with a Protocol on Ireland and Northern Ireland, under which Northern Ireland remained subject to EU rules for goods and applied the EU's customs code, so that checks would take place on goods moving between Great Britain and Northern Ireland, an "Irish Sea border," while Northern Ireland formally remained part of the UK customs territory. The Northern Ireland Assembly would vote periodically on whether to continue the arrangement. The change was a result of a political choice by the UK government to accept a regulatory border in the Irish Sea in order to avoid one on the island, and opponents, particularly unionists, argued that it weakened Northern Ireland's place in the Union.

The Withdrawal Agreement covered the terms of separation: the rights of EU citizens in the UK and UK citizens in the EU who had settled before the end of the transition period; the financial settlement, in which the UK agreed to meet financial obligations incurred during membership, with officially estimated costs in the tens of billions of pounds; the transition arrangements; the Northern Ireland Protocol; and mechanisms for dispute resolution. It did not settle the future trade relationship, which was set out in a non-binding Political Declaration. The Withdrawal Agreement Act passed Parliament in January 2020, and the United Kingdom left the European Union at 11 p.m. GMT on January 31, 2020. A transition period followed, during which the UK continued to apply EU rules and to remain in the single market and customs union while negotiating the future relationship, and it ended on December 31, 2020. The Trade and Cooperation Agreement was concluded on December 24, 2020, one week before the end of the transition, and applied provisionally from January 1, 2021.

The Protocol caused continuing dispute. Businesses in Great Britain complained of new paperwork on goods sent to Northern Ireland, unionists protested, and the UK government delayed some checks and in 2020 passed legislation that would have enabled it to override parts of the Protocol, a step the EU said breached international law. Talks continued until 2023, as the next section describes.

Historical Vocabulary
The Backstop, the Single Market, and the Customs Union

The single market is the area in which EU rules on goods, services, capital, and labor apply, so that products meeting common regulatory standards can move without checks. The customs union is a separate arrangement in which members apply a common external tariff and do not impose tariffs on each other's goods. A state can leave the single market while staying in a customs union, as Turkey has a customs union with the EU without single-market membership. The backstop was a safeguard in the 2018 draft agreement: a fallback arrangement, which would apply unless something better was agreed, designed to prevent a hard Irish border. The word is a borrowing from baseball. It turned out to be politically explosive, because it appeared to mean that the UK could not leave the arrangement unilaterally. The term helps to explain why the border question, a small geographic matter, determined the shape of the whole Brexit settlement.

Section 138.19

Life after Brexit: Trade, Borders, and European Relations

The Withdrawal Agreement governed the terms of departure; the Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA) governs much of the subsequent relationship. The distinction matters. The first dealt with citizens' rights, the financial settlement, the transition, and Northern Ireland. The second, which entered into force on May 1, 2021, after provisional application from the start of that year, established a free trade agreement for goods with no tariffs or quotas on products that meet rules of origin, as well as arrangements for transport, energy, fisheries, law enforcement cooperation, and participation in some EU programs. It did not create regulatory alignment or a customs union, and it covered services, including financial services, only in limited ways.

The practical consequences were substantial. Exporters and importers faced customs declarations, rules-of-origin requirements, and, for food and animal products, health certification and inspections. The UK phased in its own import controls on EU goods over several years, announcing delays on several occasions. Small firms found the costs of paperwork particularly heavy. UK financial services lost the passporting rights that had allowed firms based in London to serve clients across the Union without separate authorization, and some activities and staff moved to Dublin, Paris, Amsterdam, Frankfurt, and other centers, though London remained a leading global financial center. Free movement ended; EU citizens resident in the UK before the end of 2020 could apply to the EU Settlement Scheme, under which several million people obtained status, and new migration was governed by a points-based immigration system. UK participation in the Erasmus+ student exchange program ended and was replaced by a national scheme, the Turing Scheme. UK fishing communities, which had seen Brexit as an opportunity, found that the TCA provided for a phased adjustment in access to waters and a continuing role for EU vessels.

Assessing the economic effects is complicated by the pandemic of 2020 and 2021, the energy price shock after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, and other changes, and economists use statistical comparisons and models to isolate the effect of Brexit. The UK's Office for Budget Responsibility has assumed that the TCA will reduce the long-run trade intensity of the UK economy, the ratio of trade to GDP, by roughly 15 percent relative to continued EU membership, and that this will reduce long-run potential productivity by about 4 percent compared with remaining. This is a model-based counterfactual estimate, not an observed fall of four percent in productivity. Other studies, using different methods, have reported varied estimates of the effects on trade, investment, and output, and there is wide agreement that the effects were negative on average, but disagreement on their size and on the extent to which they have been offset by new trade relationships and regulatory freedom.

The Northern Ireland Protocol was substantially revised by the Windsor Framework, agreed by Prime Minister Rishi Sunak and Commission President Ursula von der Leyen on February 27, 2023. It created a "green lane" for goods remaining in Northern Ireland, reduced customs paperwork, allowed some goods to circulate under UK rules, and introduced the "Stormont brake," a mechanism by which members of the Northern Ireland Assembly could ask the UK government to block the application of new EU rules. The DUP, after a boycott, agreed in early 2024 to return to the power-sharing institutions, and the Executive was restored in February 2024. The framework did not remove the Irish Sea border or the Protocol's legal basis, and unionist opinion remained divided about it.

After the Labour Party won the general election of July 2024 under Keir Starmer, the government sought what it called a "reset" of relations. At the first UK–EU summit, held in London on May 19, 2025, the two sides agreed a joint statement establishing a security and defense partnership, committing to negotiate an agreement on a common sanitary and phytosanitary area to reduce checks on food and agricultural products, to work toward linking their emissions trading systems, to extend reciprocal access for EU and UK fishing vessels, and to explore a youth experience scheme and cooperation on electricity markets. The fisheries understanding was subsequently made legally operative through Decision No 1/2025 of the Specialised Committee on Fisheries, adopted on June 19, 2025, providing reciprocal access from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2038. That decision was already concluded and should not be confused with the later SPS, emissions-trading, or youth-experience negotiations. See the published committee decision. They also announced further work on border facilitation. On December 17, 2025, the two sides announced an agreement on the United Kingdom’s association with Erasmus+ beginning in 2027; this announcement did not restore participation during 2025 or 2026. See the House of Commons Library briefing. The summit statement committed the parties to hold annual summits.

Developments in 2026 showed that the process was slower than hoped. According to reporting and parliamentary briefings, negotiations on the SPS and emissions trading agreements advanced during 2026, but the EU linked their conclusion to agreement on a youth experience scheme, and the parties disagreed on the number of participants and other terms. The summit originally expected in mid-2026 was postponed amid a change of British leadership: Keir Starmer announced his resignation on June 22, 2026, and Andy Burnham became prime minister on July 20. The leadership change therefore preceded the rescheduled November summit rather than causing a postponement only after July. Burnham said that the status quo with the EU was not good enough, ruled out a return to free movement without a referendum and said that no such referendum would take place during the current parliament, and confirmed that a second UK–EU summit would take place in Brussels on November 20, 2026. As of November 18, 2026, that summit had not taken place. Proposals on SPS alignment, emissions-trading linkage and youth mobility remained unimplemented, and any provisions reported in draft form were not binding outcomes. The separate, completed 2025 fisheries access decision, the 2025 Erasmus+ agreement for 2027, and other operative agreements must not be placed in that pending category. This chapter therefore distinguishes between agreements that have been concluded, namely the Withdrawal Agreement, the TCA, the Windsor Framework, the binding June 2025 fisheries decision and the December 2025 Erasmus+ association agreement for 2027, alongside the 2025 political statement, and those still being negotiated, namely the SPS, ETS, and youth experience agreements and any wider changes to the relationship, whose final terms, ratification, and implementation had not occurred by the cutoff. The TCA itself provides for periodic joint reviews, and a review process was due in 2026.

Section 138.20

Historical Synthesis: Crisis, Sovereignty, and the Future of European Integration

The chapter has examined three developments: a global banking crisis that exposed the vulnerability of European finance, a sovereign-debt crisis that tested the design of the euro, and the British decision to leave the European Union. They had different origins and followed different courses. The banking crisis began in the interconnected financial markets of the United States and Europe and was addressed largely by national rescues and central-bank liquidity. The sovereign-debt crisis arose from a monetary union without fiscal union, in countries whose weaknesses ranged from Greece's fiscal mismanagement to the private credit booms of Ireland and Spain, and it was addressed by a combination of loans, adjustment programs, ECB intervention, and institutional reform. Brexit arose from a long British tradition of contested integration, interacting with economic insecurity, immigration debates, party politics, and the particular political decision to hold a referendum.

Comparing the institutional responses to the two crises is instructive. In the euro area, the member states did not break the currency or fully federalize it. They created new institutions, the ESM, the Single Supervisory Mechanism, and the Single Resolution Mechanism, stronger fiscal rules, and a new role for the ECB, achieving what has been described as survival by adaptation. The measures were incomplete, and a fully integrated fiscal and banking union did not emerge. In the British case, withdrawal was legally permitted by Article 50, but the process revealed how deeply integrated the legal and economic systems had become: it took more than four years from the vote to the end of the transition, required three major legal instruments, and was still generating renegotiation in 2026. The experience suggests that integration is reversible but that reversal is costly and complex, and that the terms of separation must be negotiated, not simply declared.

The crises also exposed a tension that Part XX has traced. Economic interdependence required common rules and shared institutions; democratic legitimacy remained largely national. When European decisions were seen as imposed, as in the programs for Greece or in the perceived loss of control in Britain, political backlash could follow. Yet the countries differed in their responses: Greek voters rejected a package and then accepted a harsher settlement; Irish and Portuguese voters supported parties that implemented programs; Spanish and Italian voters created new parties; British voters chose to leave. There was no single European pattern of response.

The euro itself endured. No member state left, and membership expanded after the crisis, with Latvia joining in 2014, Lithuania in 2015, Croatia in 2023, and Bulgaria on January 1, 2026. This persistence shows that the costs of exit were judged by governments and voters as higher than those of staying, and that the institutional improvements, with the ECB's role, reassured markets. But the political consequences, including the transformation of party systems and the erosion of trust, outlasted the financial emergency. Part XX continues in Chapter 139 with a different set of pressures, Russia's war against Ukraine, energy security, and Europe's changed global position, which would further alter the relationship between national governments and European institutions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

European Integration from Maastricht to the Post-Brexit Order

What changed?

  • The ECB moved from a narrow monetary role toward lender-of-last-resort functions and banking supervision
  • The euro area acquired a permanent rescue fund, fiscal surveillance, and banking-union institutions
  • The Union lost a member state for the first time, and the United Kingdom's relationship moved from membership to a trade and cooperation partnership
  • Party systems in several countries were reshaped, and public trust in European institutions fell and, in some places, partly recovered

What remained continuous?

  • Fiscal policy and democratic legitimacy remained primarily national
  • The euro area remained a currency union without a full fiscal union
  • Disagreements between creditor and debtor states, and between integrationists and advocates of national sovereignty, persisted
  • The UK and EU remained economically interdependent neighbors, with trade, security, and migration ties that required continuing negotiation

Who experienced change most directly? Unemployed young people in southern Europe, pensioners in Greece, depositors in Cyprus, and businesses and migrants affected by new borders experienced change most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Households and firms in economies that were less affected, such as much of Germany, and sectors protected by existing arrangements. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that institutional adaptation can preserve a structure while leaving unresolved the political questions that provoked the crisis.

Which structures endured, which changed, and what the crises revealed about the limits and resilience of integration? The euro, the single market, and the Union's institutions endured and, in some respects, gained powers. The monetary and financial architecture changed substantially, though not completely. The crises revealed resilience, in that governments chose to defend the currency and to expand institutional capacity, and limits, in that they were unwilling to pool fiscal resources on a large scale or to resolve questions of democratic accountability. Brexit showed that exit was possible, and also that the political, legal, and economic costs of separation were considerable and long-lasting. The historian's task is to explain how these outcomes arose from choices made under pressure, and to resist the temptation to treat either survival or withdrawal as inevitable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that European integration survived the financial and political crises of the early twenty-first century through institutional adaptation, but that the political consequences of those crises revealed lasting tensions between economic interdependence and national democratic authority. It has treated three developments as connected but separate: the global banking crisis of 2007 to 2009, the euro-area sovereign-debt crisis of 2009 to 2013, and the British decision to leave the European Union.

The banking crisis began in the housing and credit markets of the United States but spread to Europe through securitized assets, high leverage, and dependence on short-term wholesale and dollar funding. The signs appeared in August 2007, when BNP Paribas suspended funds and the ECB injected liquidity, and in September 2007, when depositors queued at Northern Rock. After Lehman Brothers failed on September 15, 2008, interbank lending froze, and governments in Britain, Ireland, Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Iceland rescued, guaranteed, or nationalized banks. Europe had property booms of its own in Ireland, Spain, and the United Kingdom, so the American shock acted on existing vulnerabilities.

The recession that followed affected countries very differently. Germany suffered a sharp fall in output driven by exports but protected employment through short-time work and recovered quickly. Ireland and Spain experienced the collapse of construction booms, with unemployment rising to about fifteen and twenty-six percent at their peaks. Iceland's banks failed on a scale that overwhelmed the state, and in eastern Europe, Poland avoided recession while Latvia, Hungary, and Romania suffered severe disruption. The responses in these countries, from bank guarantees and nationalization to quantitative easing and stimulus, determined how far private banking losses became public debt.

The sovereign-debt crisis arose from the design of monetary union. The Maastricht Treaty created a single currency and central bank, a prohibition on monetary financing, a no-bailout clause, and fiscal reference values, but no common fiscal authority, and left bank supervision and resolution at the national level. In Greece, a long record of deficits, weak tax collection, and unreliable statistics combined with the revelation of a far larger deficit in 2009 to produce a loss of market access in spring 2010. Ireland and Portugal followed, with different causes, and pressure extended to Spain, Italy, and Cyprus. The programs of the Troika imposed conditionality, and the 2012 Greek restructuring was the largest sovereign debt restructuring to that date.

The ECB changed the course of the crisis in 2012. Mario Draghi's commitment of July 26 and the announcement of Outright Monetary Transactions on September 6 reduced fears of euro breakup without the program ever being used, but they worked in conjunction with the European Stability Mechanism, the June 2012 banking-union decision, and continuing adjustment. The ECB's role changed to include quasi lender-of-last-resort functions for sovereigns and, after 2014, direct supervision of banks, which raised new questions of legitimacy that courts, notably in Germany, addressed.

Austerity became the central object of economic and political controversy. Supporters argued that fiscal consolidation was necessary for lenders and for restoring confidence; critics argued that simultaneous tightening in depressed economies, without independent monetary policy, deepened the recession and raised debt ratios. The IMF's 2013 acknowledgment that multipliers had been underestimated, the controversy over the Reinhart–Rogoff paper, and the experience of countries with different adjustment paths all contributed to the debate, which students should evaluate using evidence and counterfactual reasoning, not partisan assumption. The Greek crisis of 2015, in which a government elected to oppose austerity held a referendum, imposed capital controls, and then accepted a third program, illustrated the limits of national choice within monetary union, though the referendum result, the government's decision, and the final settlement must be distinguished.

The reforms of 2011 to 2018 created the ESM, new fiscal surveillance through the European Semester, the Fiscal Compact, the Single Supervisory Mechanism, and the Single Resolution Mechanism. They were significant but incomplete: no common deposit insurance and no substantial common fiscal capacity emerged. Greece left its final program in August 2018 with the highest debt ratio in the euro area. The social costs, including mass unemployment, emigration, reduced public services, and protest movements such as the Indignados, transformed party systems in Greece, Spain, and Italy and reduced trust in European institutions in crisis countries.

Brexit had distinct roots. Britain's relationship with European integration had been contested since accession in 1973, through the 1975 referendum, Black Wednesday, the Maastricht opt-outs, and the rise of UKIP. David Cameron's promise of a referendum, made in 2013 and fulfilled in 2016, was a response to party pressures and to the rise of UKIP. The financial crisis and austerity formed part of the setting, and scholars disagree about their weight in explaining the vote, with the strongest evidence suggesting overlapping explanations: sovereignty and identity, immigration, regional economic inequality, age, and education. The referendum of June 23, 2016, produced a narrow 51.9 to 48.1 percent result, with England and Wales voting Leave and Scotland and Northern Ireland voting Remain.

Implementation proved far more complicated than the vote. Article 50 was notified on March 29, 2017, after the Supreme Court required parliamentary authorization; the Withdrawal Agreement was rejected three times by the Commons, and the Irish border question produced the backstop and then the Northern Ireland Protocol. The UK left the Union on January 31, 2020, and the transition ended on December 31, 2020, with the Trade and Cooperation Agreement providing a framework for goods trade and cooperation but ending free movement and the single market. The Windsor Framework of 2023 revised the Protocol, and the summit of May 19, 2025, began a reset under which negotiations on food standards, emissions trading, and youth mobility continued through 2026. As of November 18, 2026, the second summit, scheduled for November 20, had not occurred, and the agreements under negotiation had not been concluded.

The chapter's conclusion is that the euro and the Union survived, and the British exit demonstrated that integration was reversible. The crises produced institutional adaptation and political realignment, and left unresolved the question of how to combine shared economic governance with democratic accountability. Chapter 139 turns from financial crisis to the security and energy challenges posed by Russia's war against Ukraine.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Securitization
The pooling of loans, such as mortgages, and the sale of bonds backed by their payments, spreading and sometimes concealing credit risk.
Leverage
The use of borrowed money to finance assets, which magnifies gains and losses relative to a lender's own capital.
Wholesale funding
Borrowing by banks from other financial institutions and markets, as opposed to retail deposits; a source of vulnerability when markets freeze.
Bank run
A rush by depositors or lenders to withdraw funds because of fear of an institution's failure, as at Northern Rock in September 2007.
Liquidity and solvency
Respectively, the ability to meet immediate payments and the condition of having assets worth more than liabilities.
Quantitative easing
A central bank's creation of money to buy government or other bonds, intended to lower long-term interest rates and support demand.
Kurzarbeit
The German short-time work scheme, in which the state subsidizes reduced hours to preserve employment during downturns.
Stability and Growth Pact
The 1997 agreement elaborating the Maastricht fiscal rules, including limits on deficits and debt, weakly enforced in the 2000s and strengthened after 2011.
No-bailout clause
The treaty provision (now Article 125 TFEU) stating that the Union and member states are not liable for the commitments of other member states' governments.
Monetary financing
Direct central-bank financing of governments, prohibited by the treaties (now Article 123 TFEU).
Sovereign debt
Debt owed by a national government, usually in the form of bonds.
Bond yield and spread
The interest return on a bond, and the difference between one government's yield and a benchmark, usually German bonds.
Doom loop
The reinforcing dependence between weak banks and weak governments, each threatening the other's solvency.
Troika
The European Commission, the ECB, and the IMF, which negotiated and monitored assistance programs for Greece, Ireland, and Portugal.
Conditionality
The requirement that financial assistance be matched by specified policy commitments, monitored by lenders.
European Stability Mechanism (ESM)
The permanent euro-area rescue fund, established in 2012, which can lend to governments and banks under conditions.
Private Sector Involvement
The March 2012 restructuring of Greek bonds held by private investors, with a nominal reduction of 53.5 percent.
Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT)
The ECB's 2012 framework for conditional purchases of short-term government bonds, never activated.
Fiscal consolidation (austerity)
Policies to reduce government deficits through spending cuts and tax increases; "austerity" is the term used mainly by critics.
Fiscal multiplier
The change in national output resulting from a change in government spending or taxation.
Capital controls
Restrictions on the movement of money, such as the limits on cash withdrawals and transfers imposed in Cyprus (2013) and Greece (2015).
Banking union
The euro-area system of common bank supervision (SSM) and resolution (SRM), with common deposit insurance still not agreed.
Fiscal Compact
The 2012 Treaty on Stability, Coordination and Governance, requiring signatories to adopt balanced-budget rules in national law.
European Semester
The annual cycle, created in 2011, in which EU institutions review national budgets and reform plans.
Euroscepticism
Opposition to or doubt about European integration, ranging from criticism of particular policies to demands for withdrawal.
Article 50
The Treaty on European Union provision allowing a member state to withdraw, triggered by the UK on March 29, 2017.
Withdrawal Agreement
The treaty governing the UK's departure, covering citizens' rights, the financial settlement, the transition, and the Northern Ireland Protocol.
Backstop
A fallback arrangement in the 2018 draft agreement to avoid a hard Irish border, replaced in 2019 by a revised Protocol.
Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA)
The treaty governing most of the post-Brexit EU–UK relationship, applied from January 1, 2021, and in force from May 1, 2021.
Windsor Framework
The February 2023 agreement revising the Northern Ireland Protocol, with green and red lanes and the Stormont brake.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an integrated Europe responded when its shared institutions were tested by events that no member state had caused alone. The financial crisis revealed how far European economies and banking systems had become interdependent, and the sovereign-debt crisis revealed how far political institutions had lagged behind that interdependence. The responses, improvised in emergencies, set the pattern for later crises.

The chapter also reveals the persistence of national political communities. Parliaments, courts, voters, and parties continued to claim authority over fiscal and constitutional questions, and the contest between that authority and European rules produced some of the most important political conflicts of the period. The British vote to leave was the most dramatic expression, but the Greek referendum, the Portuguese constitutional rulings, and the German constitutional judgments showed that the issue was general. Within Volume VI, the chapter contributes to the argument that post-1945 integration was a contingent and contested process, deepened by crisis but also strained by it.

Many consequences were still unfolding at the chapter's cutoff of November 18, 2026. The euro area had enlarged, the banking union was incomplete, and the United Kingdom and the Union were still negotiating the practical terms of their relationship. Students should hold open the possibility that historians will revise judgments about the crisis as archives open and as later events change the perspective.

Chapter 139 turns to a different set of pressures: Russia's war against Ukraine, energy security, climate policy, and Europe's changing global position. The experience of financial crisis, with its lessons about collective action, institutional improvisation, and the limits of democratic consent, forms part of the background to those events.
