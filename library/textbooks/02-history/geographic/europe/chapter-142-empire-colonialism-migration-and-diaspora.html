Empire, Colonialism, Migration, and Diaspora

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Empires moved people. They moved soldiers, officials, merchants, settlers, captives, workers, and exiles, and they bound together populations that spoke different languages and held unequal legal standing. The people who were moved, and the people who were governed where they arrived, in turn remade the societies that empires claimed to rule.

Consider five documents, each produced inside a different imperial system. The first is a small bronze diploma of the kind issued by the Roman government to auxiliary soldiers who had completed some twenty-five years of service. It certified that the holder, recruited from a provincial population and often posted far from his birthplace, had received Roman citizenship and legal recognition of his marriage. The second is a letter written in 1526 by Afonso I, the Christian king of Kongo in west central Africa, to King João III of Portugal, protesting that Portuguese traders were seizing and selling people from his realm. The third is a ledger, a ship's muster roll, or an insurance record of the kind that survives in the archives of the Dutch and English East India companies, showing how sailors, clerks, soldiers, and enslaved laborers from many countries were assembled into a single commercial machine. The fourth is a petition addressed in 1919 to the statesmen assembled at the Paris Peace Conference by Vietnamese activists living in France, asking that the French empire extend to its Indochinese subjects the rights its own republican principles proclaimed. The fifth is the passenger list of the Empire Windrush, which reached Tilbury in June 1948 carrying several hundred people from the Caribbean who held British imperial nationality and who now sought work in the imperial center.

These documents belong to very different historical worlds. The Roman diploma reflects an ancient system that incorporated conquered peoples into its army and, over time, its citizenry. Afonso's letter belongs to the early phase of European Atlantic expansion, when an African ruler negotiated with a European monarch as a diplomatic correspondent and found his authority undermined by the trade that Portuguese commerce created. The company records show how commercial enterprises came to govern territory and populations. The 1919 petition illustrates how colonized peoples appropriated the universal language of the empires that ruled them. The Windrush list records movement within an empire that still governed much of the Caribbean: its passengers traveled as British subjects under nationality arrangements already in force, not under a postcolonial nationality that had outlasted British rule. The British Nationality Act of 1948 was enacted only after the ship arrived and took effect in 1949. None of these documents can stand for all empires. Together they show the central problem of this chapter: empires organized political power over diverse populations, generated and redirected human movement under conditions ranging from opportunity to coercion, and in doing so changed what it meant to belong to a place, a state, a faith, or a continent.

This chapter therefore asks a question with two directions. Europeans and European states established, joined, inherited, and resisted empires, and those empires transformed societies in Africa, Asia, the Americas, and Oceania. But imperial relationships also transformed Europe. They brought wealth and commodities, enslaved labor and its profits, new ports and financial institutions, new languages and religious communities, new ideas of race and citizenship, and eventually new populations whose presence challenged inherited ideas of who counted as European. The history of empire is not an episode that occurred somewhere beyond Europe's edges. It is part of how European societies were made and repeatedly redefined.

The chapter also resists two common simplifications. The first reduces empire to overseas colonialism after 1492. Rome, Byzantium, the Carolingians, the Habsburgs, the Ottomans, the Russian tsars, and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth all governed diverse populations, and their histories belong in any account of European empire. The second reduces migration to the modern immigrant who arrives in search of work. Movement within and across empires has included the voluntary relocation of merchants and settlers, the enforced removal of conquered communities, the sale and transport of millions of enslaved Africans, the flight of persecuted minorities, the exile of political dissidents, and the large scale expulsions and exchanges of the twentieth century. Treating all these as a single phenomenon would erase the difference between choice and coercion, and the difference between a settler claiming land and an enslaved person being carried across the Atlantic.

The evidence is correspondingly varied. Historians draw on inscriptions, papyri, and diplomas for the Roman world, on the letters and legal records of medieval merchant communities such as those preserved in the Cairo Geniza, on company archives, notarial records, and ship manifests for the early modern period, on the digitized voyage records of the transatlantic slave trade, on colonial legal codes and administrative reports, on petitions, newspapers, and pamphlets produced by colonized and migrant communities, on emigrant letters and passenger lists, on government records of deportation and resettlement, and, for the late twentieth century, on census data, oral histories, and the records of public memory. Each type of source reveals some perspectives and conceals others, and the chapter attends throughout to whose voice a document preserves and whose it omits.

Chapter 142 is the third of five comparative chapters that conclude the textbook. Chapter 140 traced the changing role of Christianity, religious pluralism, and secularization, and Chapter 141 examined the long relationship among war, states, law, and sovereignty. This chapter carries those themes into the unequal political relationships that empires created and into the movements of population that they set in motion. Chapter 143 will turn to class, gender, family, labor, and social change, and Chapter 144 will reconsider Europe's changing boundaries and its place in the wider world. The detailed narratives of Roman expansion, Atlantic conquest, the Scramble for Africa, the world wars, and decolonization appear in earlier chapters, and this chapter draws on them comparatively without retelling them. Its twenty sections are grouped into five analytical divisions, which should be read as comparative clusters and not as a claim that all empires passed through the same stages.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define and distinguish empire, imperialism, colonialism, settler colonialism, migration, forced displacement, diaspora, and postcolonial society, and explain why these terms describe related but different processes.
  2. Explain how the Roman Empire incorporated conquered populations through settlement, military service, and citizenship, and why imperial citizenship did not amount to equality.
  3. Compare Byzantine, Carolingian, and later medieval imperial claims, and the Mediterranean commercial and religious networks that connected Europe with North Africa and western Asia.
  4. Distinguish dynastic composite monarchies, continental land empires, and maritime commercial empires as different ways of organizing authority over diverse populations.
  5. Analyze Atlantic slavery as a system of coerced migration and trace its effects on African, American, and European societies.
  6. Evaluate competing scholarly interpretations of how empire contributed to European economic transformation.
  7. Assess how racial ideologies and legal categories structured membership, rights, and exclusion within modern European empires.
  8. Interpret primary sources that express colonial, colonized, and anticolonial claims to authority and rights, recognizing the limits of any single document.
  9. Trace the demographic and territorial consequences of imperial collapse, genocide, war, decolonization, and postcolonial migration across the twentieth century.
  10. Contextualize contemporary diasporas and debates over imperial memory within a long history of empire and migration, distinguishing established evidence from political memory.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did different empires organize unequal authority over populations with different languages, religions, and legal statuses, and what made some systems more durable than others?
  2. In what ways did imperial systems generate migration, and how should historians distinguish movement shaped by opportunity from movement imposed through conquest, enslavement, expulsion, or deportation?
  3. What is the difference between legal membership in an empire and equal membership in a political community, and how did that difference change from Rome to the twentieth century?
  4. How did overseas empire change European economies, institutions, consumption, and social structures, and why do historians disagree about the scale of that change?
  5. What evidence allows historians to recover the experiences of enslaved, colonized, and displaced people, and what are the limits of sources produced mainly by imperial governments and merchants?
  6. How were racial and civilizational ideologies constructed to justify imperial rule, and how did colonized thinkers and European critics contest them?
  7. Why did the collapse of continental empires in 1918 and of overseas empires after 1945 produce such large movements of population, and why did collapse not end imperial hierarchies?
  8. How have diasporas and the memory of empire reshaped contemporary European ideas of national belonging, and how should historians separate evidence from political argument in those debates?

Historical Context

This chapter synthesizes material developed across the textbook, and students should recall the principal earlier treatments. Rome's conquest of Italy and the Mediterranean was examined in Chapters 16 and 17, Roman society and slavery in Chapter 18, and the empire at its height, with its provincial societies and frontiers, in Chapter 22. The medieval formation of successor kingdoms, the Carolingian empire, and the Orthodox and Byzantine worlds appeared in Chapters 29 through 41, and the Crusades and the commercial revival of the High Middle Ages in Chapters 43 and 47. Part XI, Chapters 68 through 73, traced Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, British, and French expansion, Atlantic slavery, and the transformation of European consumption. Chapters 74 through 80 examined dynastic states, composite monarchy, and the balance of power. Chapters 103 through 106 treated race, the New Imperialism, and the European empires in Asia and Africa. Chapters 111 through 113 covered the collapse of the eastern empires and the post-1918 settlement, Chapters 119 through 123 the war, occupation, and genocide of the Second World War, Chapter 128 decolonization, and Chapter 137 migration, demography, and identity. This chapter does not repeat those narratives. It asks what they have in common and where they differ.

Two conceptual problems frame the analysis. The first concerns the word empire. Historians use it for political systems that exercised unequal authority over multiple territories or peoples, but the systems so described differ enormously. The Roman Empire absorbed conquered elites into its governing class over several centuries. The Ottoman Empire governed Muslim, Christian, and Jewish populations through differentiated arrangements. The Spanish monarchy joined distinct kingdoms under a single dynasty. The Dutch East India Company was a commercial corporation that exercised sovereign powers. The British Empire in the nineteenth century combined settler colonies, directly ruled territories, protectorates, and economic spheres of influence. Treating these as examples of one phenomenon obscures more than it reveals, and the chapter therefore emphasizes comparison, not a single definition.

The second problem concerns belonging. Throughout most of the period covered here, people did not belong to nation-states in the modern sense. They belonged to households, villages, religious communities, guilds, dynasties, estates, and empires. A merchant in Venetian Candia, a Jewish trader in Fustat, a Serb frontier soldier in the Habsburg Military Frontier, a Dutch clerk in Batavia, and an Algerian laborer in Marseille each held a different position within systems of rights and obligations that rarely corresponded to the later category of citizen. The relationship between imperial membership, religious identity, language, and legal status is therefore central. It was only in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries that the nation-state became the dominant model of political membership, and it did so in tension with, and often within, the empires that continued to exist.

Finally, students should recognize that the movement of people was built into the structure of empire. Armies and garrisons required recruitment and supply. Administrations required officials and clerks. Taxation and tribute drew on labor and produce collected over distances. Commercial systems needed merchants, shippers, financiers, and local intermediaries. Settlement policies deliberately relocated populations for strategic or economic reasons, and conquest regularly produced captives, refugees, and exiles. At the same time, migrants sometimes created networks and communities that outlived the empires that had enabled them. The history of empire is therefore inseparable from the history of diaspora, the maintenance of relationships among dispersed communities, which this chapter examines as a distinct and continuing phenomenon.

The chapter also depends on terminology that students should treat with care. Words such as colony, colonist, settler, native, subject, citizen, and race had different meanings in different periods and empires. The Latin word colonia described a settlement of Roman citizens, not an exploited dependency in the later sense. The word diaspora, from a Greek term for dispersal, entered scholarly use in connection with Jewish history and has since been applied, sometimes loosely, to many communities. Where a term is historically contested, the chapter explains its use. Where evidence is incomplete, as it is for most demographic estimates before the nineteenth century, the chapter gives ranges and distinguishes documented figures from scholarly reconstruction.

Section 142.1

What Is an Empire? Conquest, Incorporation, and Population Movement

Any comparative history of empire must begin with definitions, because the words used to describe imperial systems carry both analytical meaning and political charge. In its broadest scholarly sense, an empire is a political system in which one center or ruling group exercises unequal authority over multiple territories or peoples. The inequality is the defining feature. A kingdom may rule a single population under a shared law, and a confederation may bind equals together, but an empire governs differently situated communities through different arrangements, and it typically reserves ultimate power to a ruler, a dynasty, an elite, or a metropolitan state. Empires are usually, though not always, large, and most have been created by conquest, but many were also extended through dynastic inheritance, treaty, commercial pressure, or the gradual incorporation of neighbors who sought protection or advantage.

Historians distinguish several recurring features. Imperial systems tend to be politically hierarchical, with a center that claims precedence over provinces, colonies, or subordinate realms. They tend to incorporate populations unevenly, offering some groups privileged access to office or citizenship and others only subordinate status. They rely on military power, though rarely on force alone, since durable empires also depend on negotiated relations with local elites, religious institutions, and intermediaries. They extract resources through taxation, tribute, labor service, or commerce. And they govern across difference, administering communities with distinct languages, religions, and legal traditions through arrangements that vary by region and period. These features overlap with those of other political forms, and the boundaries are not sharp. The Kingdom of Castile was in some respects a nation-state in formation and in others the core of a global empire. A commercial network such as that of the Hanseatic League, examined in Chapter 54, exercised economic power and negotiated privileges without ruling territory, and so was not an empire, though it participated in imperial relationships. This chapter treats such overlaps as part of the history and not as a problem to be resolved by a single definition.

The related terms imperialism and colonialism are best understood as describing processes within and around empire. Imperialism refers to the policies, practices, and ideologies by which imperial power is extended or maintained, and it can occur without formal annexation, as when a stronger state controls a weaker through finance, treaty, or military threat. Colonialism describes a form of domination in which a territory and its people are placed under external control, usually involving settlement, extraction of resources, or institutional subordination. Settler colonialism, examined in Section 142.9, is a particular subtype in which migrants establish permanent communities on lands already inhabited and seek to control them. Not all empires were colonial in this sense. The Roman Empire planted settlements called coloniae but did not generally treat its provinces as sources of settler land in the manner of later Atlantic colonization, and the Ottoman Empire resettled populations for strategic purposes without constructing a settler regime of the kind found in the Americas.

Historical Vocabulary
Empire, Colonialism, and Imperialism

Empire is a political structure, imperialism is the set of policies and ideas that build or sustain it, and colonialism is one form of domination that empires have used. The three overlap but are not synonyms. A state can practice imperialism without ruling colonies, and an empire can govern without settlers. Ancient and medieval rulers rarely used these terms as modern scholars do, and the word imperialism acquired its modern critical meaning only in the nineteenth century. The chapter uses them as analytical tools and identifies the historical actors' own vocabulary, such as imperium, basileia, or Reich, when it matters. Students should be cautious of two misleading equivalents: treating every large state as an empire, and treating every empire as identical to the British or French overseas empires of the nineteenth century.

Movement is the second central subject. Empires create migration in at least four ways. They recruit and deploy people, as when armies, officials, and merchants are sent across imperial space. They resettle populations deliberately, to secure frontiers, populate territory, or remove potential opponents. They enable voluntary migration by lowering barriers, creating common legal frameworks, or opening opportunities for trade and land. And they produce flight, expulsion, and enslavement, through conquest, persecution, and the commercial exploitation of coerced labor. These categories are not mutually exclusive, and an individual's movement could combine choice and constraint. A soldier recruited under obligation might choose to settle where he was discharged, and an emigrant leaving a famine-stricken region exercised choice within sharply limited alternatives. But the analytical distinction between movement that people chose, however constrained, and movement imposed on them by force, such as the transport of enslaved Africans, is indispensable. The chapter consistently asks about the degree of agency available to those who moved, and it does not use the language of exchange or mobility to conceal violence.

Finally, the chapter asks what these processes did to belonging. Imperial systems created new categories of membership, such as Roman citizen, subject of a Habsburg crown, Ottoman reaya, colonial indigène, and Commonwealth citizen, and they distributed rights unequally among them. Movement within empires also created new communities, in which migrants and their descendants negotiated identities that fit neither their places of origin nor the societies where they settled. By the twentieth century the idea that political membership should coincide with national identity had become dominant, and the tension between that ideal and the diverse populations that empires had assembled helped shape the world wars, the collapse of empires, and the migrations that followed. These questions run through the remaining sections.

Section 142.2

The Roman Empire: Conquest, Settlement, Citizenship, and Mobility

The Roman Empire, whose political development was traced in Chapters 16 through 22, offers an early and well documented example of imperial incorporation. By the second century CE it governed perhaps fifty to seventy million people, a figure that is a scholarly estimate and not a documented count, across a territory extending from Britain to Egypt and from Iberia to Mesopotamia. It did so with a comparatively small central administration, relying heavily on provincial elites, municipal self-government, and a professional army stationed principally along the frontiers. The result was a system that depended on movement. Soldiers were recruited in one province and posted to another, officials and merchants traveled on state and commercial routes, and conquered populations were drawn into a shared political and legal framework without losing their local identities.

Settlement was a deliberate instrument of rule. From the Republic onward, Rome founded coloniae, communities of citizens, often discharged soldiers, settled on conquered or confiscated land. Roman colonial foundations spanned distinct political periods: Lugdunum in Gaul was founded in 43 BCE, under the late Republic; Augusta Emerita in Iberia was founded under Augustus in 25 BCE; and the Rhine settlement known as Colonia Claudia Ara Agrippinensium received colonial status under Claudius in 50 CE. Across these periods, colonies helped establish Roman law, language, and civic institutions in provincial settings. Augustus's Res Gestae, his own account of his achievements, emphasizes the extensive veteran settlements established during his rule. Such colonies served strategic and social purposes at once: they secured territory, provided land for demobilized soldiers, and spread Roman institutions. But they also displaced or subordinated local populations, and the foundations of some colonies involved the expropriation of land from existing communities. The relationship between Roman settlement and indigenous inhabitants varied by region, and historians caution against treating the Roman colony as the equivalent of a modern settler colony while recognizing that conquest, land seizure, and the settlement of loyal populations were part of its operation.

The army was the single most important engine of mobility and incorporation. Legionaries were Roman citizens, but the auxiliary units that made up roughly half the army's strength recruited non-citizen provincials, who received citizenship and recognition of their marriages on honorable discharge after about twenty-five years. Hundreds of bronze diplomas recording these grants survive, and they provide direct documentary evidence of how service converted provincials into Romans. The wooden writing tablets found since 1973 at the fort of Vindolanda on the northern frontier in Britain, dating from around 90 to 120 CE, preserve the correspondence of soldiers, officials, and their households, including supply requests, military reports, and a birthday invitation from one officer's wife to another. They also show that Roman soldiers sometimes referred to local Britons with a contemptuous diminutive, a reminder that incorporation and hierarchy coexisted. Frontier garrisons drew people from across the empire, and the soldiers' households, trading connections, and religious cults, including those of Mithras and of eastern deities, created communities that cut across ethnic and linguistic boundaries.

Roman citizenship offers a particularly clear case of the difference between legal membership and equality. Citizenship was extended gradually, first across Italy after the Social War, then to provincial elites, to veterans, and to whole communities through municipal charters. Provincial families entered the senatorial order, and the emperors Trajan, from Italica in Hispania, and Septimius Severus, from Leptis Magna in North Africa, came from provincial backgrounds. A surviving bronze inscription from Lugdunum preserves a speech of the emperor Claudius in 48 CE arguing for the admission of Gallic notables to the Senate, which shows that such inclusion was contested within Rome's governing class. In 212 CE, the emperor Caracalla issued an edict, partly preserved in a Greek papyrus now in Giessen, that extended citizenship to most free inhabitants of the empire. The later historian Cassius Dio attributed the measure to fiscal motives, and scholars continue to debate its purposes and its consequences. Yet citizenship did not eliminate the empire's profound inequalities. Slavery, examined in Chapter 18, remained a basic institution, and estimates of the enslaved share of the population vary widely, though in some regions it may have been substantial. Free people also held different legal and social standing, and from the late second century the law increasingly distinguished between honorable classes and humbler ones in punishment and privilege.

Mobility transformed languages and cultures. Latin became the administrative language of the western provinces and the army, while Greek retained its position in the eastern Mediterranean, and local languages such as Punic, Celtic dialects, Aramaic, and Coptic persisted for centuries. Historians have debated whether the older concept of Romanization, in which provincials passively adopted Roman culture, adequately describes this process. More recent scholarship emphasizes that provincial populations adapted, selected, and transformed Roman institutions for their own purposes, producing regional variants of Roman culture, as discussed in Chapter 22. Religious communities also moved along imperial routes. Jewish communities existed in cities across the Mediterranean, and Jewish and early Christian networks followed trade and administrative connections, with consequences examined in Chapter 23. The suppression of Jewish revolts in 66 to 73, 115 to 117, and 132 to 135 CE led to destruction, enslavement, and restricted access to Jerusalem, and contributed to the long history of Jewish diaspora, although dispersal predated the revolts and continued through voluntary migration as well as displacement. Rome's example shows how an empire could incorporate and enrich provincial populations while maintaining slavery, conquest, and legal hierarchy, and it provided a model that later rulers invoked, adapted, or rejected.

Section 142.3

Successor Empires, Byzantine Continuity, and Medieval Imperial Ideals

The transformation of the western Roman world, treated in Chapters 27 and 28, did not end the idea of empire. It dispersed it. In the east, the Roman state continued from Constantinople for a thousand years more. Its inhabitants called themselves Romaioi, Romans, and generally did not use the modern scholarly label Byzantine. This Eastern Roman Empire governed Greek-speaking, Armenian, Slavic, and other populations in the Balkans, Anatolia, and, at times, Italy and the eastern Mediterranean, and it preserved Roman law, taxation, and imperial ceremony in modified form. It also used population movement as a tool of government. Emperors resettled Slavic, Armenian, Syrian, and other groups in depopulated or strategic regions. In the eighth century Constantine V moved Syrian and Armenian populations into Thrace, and later emperors settled dissident Christian communities, including Paulicians, on the Balkan frontier. After Basil II conquered Bulgaria in 1018, the empire incorporated Bulgarian territory and its populations, as discussed in Chapters 37 and 39. Byzantine armies and administrations recruited soldiers from beyond the empire, and the Varangian Guard drew Scandinavian, Rus, and, after the Norman Conquest of 1066, English warriors to imperial service.

In the west, rulers invoked Roman precedent in different ways. The Carolingian empire, examined in Chapter 31, culminated in the coronation of Charlemagne as emperor by Pope Leo III on 25 December 800. The title expressed a Frankish king's claim to rule a Christian community in succession to the Roman emperors and without reference to Constantinople, whose own rulers regarded it with suspicion, and the Byzantine court did not accord recognition until the early ninth century. Charlemagne's conquests, notably of Saxony, incorporated new populations by force. Frankish annals record deportations of Saxons and the settlement of Franks and others in their place, together with compulsory baptism and harsh legal penalties, and the combination of conquest, conversion, and resettlement that characterized the Saxon wars anticipated later patterns of frontier incorporation. Imperial coronation was revived in 962 when Otto I was crowned emperor in Rome, beginning a line of rulers whose realm, examined in Chapter 46, came to be called the Holy Roman Empire. That name emerged gradually, with the adjective sacrum appearing in the twelfth century, and the territory it described was never a unified state governed by a single administration. It was a flexible political order of princes, bishops, cities, and imperial institutions in which imperial authority operated through negotiated relationships.

Medieval empires should not be treated either as imperfect nation-states or as replicas of Roman administration. The Holy Roman Empire was not an incomplete Germany, and Byzantium was not a Greek national state. Their rulers governed populations defined primarily by religion, dynastic allegiance, and local custom, not by modern nationality. Imperial ideology drew on multiple sources: Roman inheritance, Christian kingship, dynastic legitimacy, and the prestige of military success. The result was a medieval political landscape in which the word emperor carried meanings that ranged from universal Christian ruler to the head of a particular realm, and in which the papacy, the Byzantine emperor, and several western rulers contested the right to define legitimate imperial authority. The Great Schism, examined in Chapter 40, and the conflict between emperors and popes, examined in Chapter 44, show how contested those claims were.

Movement across and within these political systems was extensive. Clerics, monks, and missionaries traveled across Latin Christendom and Orthodox lands, as in the Slavic missions treated in Chapter 38. Merchants from Italian cities, Scandinavia, and the Rus lands traded in Constantinople. Vikings moved across Europe as raiders, traders, and settlers, founding the duchy of Normandy in the early tenth century, settling in the Danelaw in England, and contributing to the formation of Rus polities. From the late eleventh century Normans conquered southern Italy and Sicily. From the twelfth century German-speaking settlers, invited by Polish, Bohemian, Hungarian, and other rulers or recruited by the Teutonic Order, moved east into territories inhabited by Slavic and Baltic populations, a long process historians call the eastern settlement or Ostsiedlung. This movement involved peaceful migration, the foundation of towns under German law, and conquest and forced conversion in Prussia and the Baltic, and its meaning has been heavily shaped by later national memory. Historians now emphasize that the settlers were not a single ethnic wave and that local rulers often sought them. The evidence includes charters of settlement, town privileges, and archaeological surveys, and it shows a movement more varied than either older nationalist narratives of civilizing mission or opposing narratives of simple colonization allowed.

Section 142.4

Medieval Mediterranean Empires, Religious Communities, and Diasporic Networks

The Mediterranean basin was the principal medieval zone in which Byzantine, Muslim, and Latin Christian political systems overlapped, and it was a space shaped by movement. In 711 Muslim forces crossed from North Africa into the Iberian Peninsula and within a few years brought most of it under Muslim rule. The Umayyad emirate, and from 929 caliphate, of Córdoba governed a population of Muslims, Christians, and Jews, many of whom adopted Arabic as a language of administration and culture. Sicily came under Muslim control from the ninth century, with Palermo captured in 831, and remained so until Norman conquest between 1061 and 1091. The Norman kings, notably Roger II, who was crowned in 1130, ruled a multilingual realm in which Greek, Arabic, and Latin were all used in administration. These societies were not simple frontiers between hostile blocs. They produced intellectual exchange, translation, and commercial relationships alongside warfare, and their later conquest by Latin Christian rulers brought new settlers, forced conversions, and the displacement of Muslim populations. After the capture of Toledo in 1085 and of Córdoba in 1236, Muslims living under Christian rule, called mudéjares, retained limited religious and legal privileges that eroded over time. In Sicily the emperor Frederick II relocated Muslim communities to Lucera in Apulia in the thirteenth century. These episodes belong to the Iberian expansion discussed in Chapter 47, and they show that conquest inside Europe also produced diaspora.

Commercial and religious communities connected these political systems. Jewish merchants and scholars maintained correspondence and trade across the Mediterranean and beyond. The documents recovered from the storeroom, or geniza, of a synagogue in Fustat in Egypt, some three hundred thousand fragments dating mainly from the tenth to the thirteenth centuries, record letters, contracts, accounts, and legal queries, and offer one of the richest sources for medieval Mediterranean commerce and everyday life. They show merchants of different religions and regions collaborating, Jewish communities in Iberia, North Africa, Egypt, and the Levant maintaining continuous relations, and individuals moving for trade, marriage, study, or safety. In northern Europe, Jewish communities settled in Rhineland towns and elsewhere from the tenth century, as examined in Chapter 49, with privileges granted by rulers and with periodic violence, including the massacres accompanying the First Crusade in 1096. Expulsions followed: from England in 1290, from France in 1306 and again in 1394, and from the Spanish kingdoms in 1492, when the Alhambra Decree forced Jews to convert or leave. Historians estimate the number who left Spain in the tens of thousands up to perhaps a hundred thousand or more, with estimates varying, and many others converted. Exiles went to Portugal, North Africa, Italy, and the Ottoman Empire, whose rulers admitted many, and formed Sephardic communities that preserved Iberian languages and customs while building commercial networks from Amsterdam to Salonica. Later, Muslims and Christian converts of Muslim ancestry, the Moriscos, were expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614, in a movement of perhaps three hundred thousand people by most estimates.

Other mobile communities also sustained diasporic networks. Armenian merchants and clerics traveled across the Black Sea, Anatolia, Iran, and Europe, and Armenian communities existed in Crimea, in Lviv and other cities of Poland-Lithuania, and in Venice, where the first printed Armenian book appeared in 1512. The Armenian community of New Julfa near Isfahan, established in the early seventeenth century, operated trading links stretching to Amsterdam and Manila. Greek-speaking communities moved between Ottoman and Venetian territories, and after the fall of Constantinople in 1453 Greek scholars and clerics contributed to the humanist culture examined in Chapter 57. Italian merchants created settlements that functioned as semi-colonial enclaves. Venice governed Crete from 1211 and later Cyprus, and Genoa held trading colonies such as Caffa on the Crimean coast and the island of Chios. In Cyprus and Crete, sugar plantations drew on enslaved and dependent labor, including people brought from the Black Sea region, and Mediterranean commerce in enslaved persons, who came largely from the Black Sea and Balkan hinterlands, provided precedents for later Atlantic practice. Historians debate how far these Mediterranean institutions were direct models for Atlantic plantation slavery, though continuities of personnel, techniques, and commercial finance are well documented, notably in the sugar economies that moved from the Mediterranean to Madeira and the Atlantic islands in the fifteenth century.

Historical Vocabulary
Diaspora, Migration, and Displacement

Migration describes movement of people, and the term covers both voluntary relocation and movement under heavy constraint. Forced displacement refers to removal or flight caused by coercion, violence, persecution, or the threat of them, and includes expulsion, deportation, enslavement, and flight from war. Diaspora refers to something different: a dispersed population that maintains, or creates, relationships among its locations through kinship, correspondence, religion, commerce, memory, or political attachment. A community may migrate without forming a diaspora, if its members assimilate and lose connection with their origins, and a diaspora may persist for centuries after the movements that created it. The term originated in Greek, referring to dispersion, and entered scholarly vocabulary especially in connection with Jewish history. It has since been applied to Armenian, Greek, African, South Asian, Irish, and other communities, and scholars disagree about how far these histories resemble one another. Students should avoid using the word as a synonym for any emigrant population and should ask, for each case, who maintained what connections, and under what circumstances the dispersal occurred.

Religious affiliation, commercial relationships, legal privilege, and patronage allowed such communities to survive and operate across political boundaries. Rulers granted privileges to minorities who contributed to commerce, finance, or administration, and withdrew them under pressure, so that the position of such communities was inherently precarious. Ottoman practice, which expanded in the fifteenth century, accommodated Orthodox Christians, Armenians, and Jews under arrangements often grouped under the term millet, though modern historians caution that the term, and the idea of a fixed system, projects later categories backward and that practice varied by period and region. The central point is that a diaspora is a continuing relationship across space. It is not equivalent to a past act of relocation, and it depended on the political frameworks, whether Byzantine, Muslim, Latin Christian, or Ottoman, within which dispersed communities carried on their lives. The Mediterranean of the late Middle Ages therefore prepared several features of later European empire: commercial colonies, coerced labor, mobile minorities, and states that governed religious difference through differentiated rights.

Section 142.5

Dynastic Empires, Composite Monarchies, and Imperial Allegiance

Between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries many European rulers governed multiple territories joined by dynastic marriage, inheritance, conquest, or negotiated union and not by a single administrative system. Historians describe such realms as composite or multiple monarchies. The category does not apply to every state, but it captures a political reality that the later nation-state tended to obscure: the same ruler could be king in one territory, duke in another, and count in a third, with separate laws, assemblies, taxation, and officials in each. The Habsburg lands, examined in Chapters 75 and 78, are the best known example. Charles V inherited the Burgundian Netherlands, the Spanish kingdoms with their Italian and American possessions, and the Habsburg Austrian lands, and was elected Holy Roman Emperor in 1519. His successors divided these holdings, and the Spanish and Austrian lines continued under different arrangements. The Spanish monarchy itself was composite. Castile and Aragon were united by the marriage of Isabella and Ferdinand in 1469 and the accession of Ferdinand in 1479, yet each kingdom, and the territories attached to them such as Catalonia, Valencia, Naples, Sicily, and the American viceroyalties, retained distinct institutions. When Philip II inherited the Portuguese crown in 1580, Portugal retained its own laws and its overseas empire until it resumed separate rule in 1640.

Composite monarchy had profound consequences for allegiance and movement. Subjects owed loyalty to a dynasty and its representatives, not to a nation, and their rights depended on the status of the territory in which they lived. Nobles, merchants, soldiers, and clergy moved among the dominions of a single ruler, so that Castilian administrators served in Italy, Italian bankers financed campaigns in the Netherlands, and Flemish and German soldiers served in Spanish armies. Fiscal and military obligations were negotiated with local assemblies, which could refuse or limit demands, and the failure of a ruler to respect local privileges could provoke revolt, as it did in the Dutch Revolt examined in Chapter 66 and in the Catalan and Portuguese rebellions of 1640. The cohesion of such realms depended not on cultural uniformity but on the ruler's ability to maintain the consent of numerous distinct elites. This is one reason why dynastic allegiance should not be confused with later national citizenship.

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth offers a comparable but differently constructed example, treated in Chapters 54 and 79. The dynastic union of Poland and Lithuania, begun at Krewo in 1385, was transformed at the Union of Lublin in 1569 into a single Commonwealth with a shared elected monarch and parliament, while retaining separate offices, laws, and armies. It governed a population of Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants, Jews, and Muslims speaking Polish, Lithuanian, Ruthenian, German, Yiddish, and other languages, and in 1573 the Warsaw Confederation guaranteed a measure of religious toleration among the nobility. Its Jewish population, among the largest in Europe, developed autonomous communal institutions, and the Union of Brest in 1596 created a Greek Catholic church that bridged Orthodox liturgy and papal authority and became a source of lasting religious and political tension. The Commonwealth also experienced violent conflict, including the Cossack uprising of 1648 and the massacres of Jews and others that accompanied it, and the eventual partitions of 1772, 1793, and 1795 divided its territory among Russia, Prussia, and Austria. Its history shows how a composite polity could accommodate diversity and also be torn by it.

Other composite systems existed across Europe. The Danish-Norwegian monarchy governed Denmark, Norway, Iceland, and later Atlantic and Caribbean dependencies, and Sweden in the seventeenth century built an empire around the Baltic that included Finland, Livonia, and parts of northern Germany, together with a short-lived colony on the Delaware River. The British Isles moved from the Union of Crowns in 1603 to the Acts of Union of 1707 and 1801 under a process of political incorporation that was accompanied by the plantation of English and Scottish settlers in Ireland from the sixteenth century, including the Ulster plantation of 1609. That plantation connected domestic conquest and Atlantic colonization, and it reminds students that the practices of land confiscation and settlement later applied overseas had precedents within the British Isles. Taken together, these cases show that European empire was never exclusively overseas, and that rulers governed diversity through negotiated and varied arrangements long before the era of mass nationalism.

Section 142.6

Continental Expansion, Imperial Borderlands, and Resettlement

The Ottoman, Habsburg, and Russian empires were the great continental powers of early modern eastern and southeastern Europe, and their competing expansion created borderlands in which populations were repeatedly moved, converted, taxed, and recruited. The Ottoman Empire, treated in Chapters 41 and 79, conquered Constantinople in 1453 and extended its rule across the Balkans, Hungary, the Black Sea coast, and much of the Mediterranean. It was simultaneously a European, Anatolian, Middle Eastern, and North African power, and its governance of southeastern Europe lasted centuries. The Ottomans administered conquered Christian populations through a combination of taxation, land grants to cavalry holders, local religious hierarchies, and, for a period, the devshirme, the recruitment of Christian boys from the Balkans who were converted to Islam and trained for service in the army and administration. They also deliberately moved people. After 1453 the sultans transferred populations to repopulate Constantinople, and Anatolian Turkmen groups and others were settled in the Balkans, a policy known as sürgün. Conversion to Islam, voluntary or encouraged by tax advantage, changed the religious composition of regions such as Bosnia and Albania, and Muslim communities of the Balkans became permanent features of European society. Ottoman control of Hungary from the sixteenth century until 1699 made the Danube basin a frontier region of imperial rivalry.

The Habsburg monarchy responded by organizing its southern frontier as a militarized border. The Military Frontier, developed from the sixteenth century, settled Orthodox Christians, many of them Serbs and Vlachs, as soldier-peasants who held land in return for permanent service. After the Habsburg advance of the 1680s and the Ottoman retreat, many Orthodox Serbs migrated north in 1690 under the patriarch Arsenije III, a movement whose size is debated and often given in the tens of thousands. The Treaty of Karlowitz of 1699 and later settlements transferred Hungary and then the Banat to Habsburg rule, and in the eighteenth century the Habsburg state, particularly under Maria Theresa and Joseph II, recruited settlers from German lands, the so-called Danube Swabians, to repopulate territories depopulated by war. These policies fused strategic defense, religious politics, and economic development. They also created mixed populations whose later national identities were shaped by these movements, though in the eighteenth century the settlers' identities rested primarily on religion, privilege, and service to the dynasty.

The Russian Empire expanded in several directions. The conquest of Kazan in 1552 and Astrakhan in 1556 brought Muslim and other non-Russian populations under Muscovite rule and opened the Volga to colonization, and the expansion into Siberia continued through the seventeenth century. In the west, Russia's relationship with the Cossack Hetmanate in Ukraine, formalized at Pereyaslav in 1654, has been interpreted very differently in later national histories, and the later partitions of Poland-Lithuania brought millions of Poles, Lithuanians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, and Jews under Russian rule. Catherine II, by manifestos of 1762 and 1763, invited foreign settlers, and tens of thousands of Germans moved to the Volga region and, later, to the lands of New Russia north of the Black Sea, which were conquered from the Ottomans and the Crimean Khanate. The annexation of Crimea in 1783 led to waves of Crimean Tatar emigration to Ottoman territory. In 1791 Russian authorities restricted Jewish residence largely to western provinces, in what became the Pale of Settlement, and by the census of 1897 the empire contained more than five million Jews. In the Caucasus, Russian conquest, which advanced through the early nineteenth century and culminated in the mid-1860s, was followed by the displacement of large numbers of Circassians and other Muslim peoples, who moved or were driven into Ottoman territory, a process whose scale and character remain contested in scholarship, with estimates ranging from several hundred thousand to over a million.

These borderlands were not peripheral. They were the places where imperial institutions were tested and transformed, and where languages, religions, and communities were continually reshaped. In the Balkans, Orthodox Christians lived under Ottoman and Habsburg rule, and Muslims, Jews, Catholics, and Protestants formed mixed communities in towns and countryside. In the Black Sea region, trading communities of Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and Italians connected Ottoman, Russian, and western European economies. In Ukraine, Poland-Lithuania, Russia, and the Ottoman Empire all contended for influence, and Cossack societies moved among them. The borderlands also illustrate that continental empires used methods that resemble colonialism, including land grants, deliberate settlement, and the legal differentiation of native and settler, even where the territories involved were adjacent and not overseas. Whether these cases should be described as colonization remains debated: some historians emphasize the similarity of the practices, while others stress differences of geography, legal framework, and the absence of a metropolitan-colonial divide. Chapter 79 and Chapters 106 and 139 place these relationships within the wider diplomatic and military history of the region.

Section 142.7

Atlantic Expansion and the Creation of European Overseas Empires

The Portuguese and Castilian Atlantic systems, whose conquest histories were told in Chapters 68 and 69, are examined here for what they reveal about empire and population. Their expansion began in the fifteenth century with the Portuguese seizure of Ceuta in 1415, the settlement of Madeira and the Azores, and exploration along the West African coast, while Castile conquered the Canary Islands between 1402 and 1496 and enslaved or displaced much of the indigenous Guanche population. Traditional accounts that credit the Portuguese prince Henry, called the Navigator, with directing a systematic program of exploration have been qualified by historians, who point to multiple actors and motives, including commerce, crusading ideology, the search for gold, and rivalry among Atlantic powers. By the mid-fifteenth century Portuguese ships were carrying enslaved Africans to Portugal and the Atlantic islands, and papal bulls such as Romanus Pontifex of 1455 gave religious sanction to conquest and enslavement. The Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494, which divided the claims of the two crowns, was an agreement between European monarchs that took no account of the peoples who lived in the regions it purported to apportion.

Spanish rule in the Americas rested on conquest, on the labor of indigenous populations, and on an elaborate institutional framework that connected the colonies with the metropolis. The crown created the Casa de Contratación in Seville in 1503 to regulate trade and the Council of the Indies in 1524 to supervise governance, and established viceroyalties in New Spain in 1535 and Peru in 1542. The encomienda, which assigned tribute and labor of indigenous communities to Spanish holders, was criticized by clerics, notably Bartolomé de las Casas, and was regulated by the Laws of Burgos of 1512 and the New Laws of 1542, with uneven effect. Alongside the encomienda, forced labor systems such as the mita supplied workers to silver mines at Potosí, whose output after 1545 reshaped global commerce. Indigenous populations declined catastrophically in many regions after contact, principally from epidemic disease, with violence, forced labor, and social disruption contributing. Estimates of pre-contact populations vary widely, and the demographic collapse varied by region, but in central Mexico and the Andes the loss within a century is estimated to have been very large, and historians generally agree that disease was the principal cause. Indigenous societies were not simply conquered passively. Allies such as the Tlaxcalans were essential to Spanish campaigns, Indigenous elites negotiated legal standing, communities used Spanish courts to defend land, and resistance, from the Inca state at Vilcabamba to the Mixtón War in New Spain and the Mapuche in Chile, continued for decades or longer.

The Portuguese colonization of Brazil, which began seriously in the 1530s with the captaincy system and expanded with sugar plantations, developed a different relationship with indigenous and African labor, relying increasingly on enslaved Africans. Both Iberian empires produced mixed societies, with legal and social categories of ancestry that structured status, usually described as the casta system in Spanish America, though historians warn that its rigidity varied and that categories were negotiated in practice. European migration to the colonies was substantial but, in the sixteenth century, numbered only some hundreds of thousands, and estimates vary. Emigrants included soldiers, officials, clergy, merchants, artisans, and a smaller number of women, and the Crown attempted to regulate who could go, excluding in principle Jews, Muslims, and converts. Missionary orders, including Franciscans, Dominicans, and later Jesuits, established missions and, in Paraguay, the Jesuit reducciones, which gathered indigenous communities into settlements under religious supervision.

Atlantic colonization differed in important respects from continental empire, though not entirely. It involved crossing an ocean, which made metropolitan control dependent on shipping, naval power, and delegation. It introduced a drastic demographic disparity between a small European presence and a large indigenous population, and, in plantation regions, between enslaved and free. And it linked European states to the extraction of precious metals and the production of commodities for global markets. At the same time, it shared with continental empires the use of land grants, resettlement, legal differentiation, religious conversion, and the incorporation of local intermediaries. The Iberian empires also affected the metropoles. Silver financed Habsburg wars and inflated European prices, Lisbon and Seville became cosmopolitan ports with African, Asian, and American populations, and, by some estimates, enslaved Africans made up roughly a tenth of Lisbon's population in the sixteenth century. These effects, and the relationship of Atlantic empire to European society, are developed further in Sections 142.10 and 142.12.

Section 142.8

Chartered Companies, Maritime Commerce, and Networks of Imperial Power

In the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the Dutch, English, French, and other European states created commercial empires in the Atlantic and Indian Ocean in which chartered companies exercised powers usually associated with governments. The English East India Company was chartered on 31 December 1600, and the Dutch East India Company, the VOC, was founded in 1602 by merging several trading ventures. The VOC received a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and powers to make treaties, wage war, build forts, and coin money, and it raised capital through shares in a pioneering joint-stock enterprise whose records, held in the Dutch national archives, are among the most extensive of any early modern business. Its counterpart in the Atlantic, the Dutch West India Company, founded in 1621, participated in the slave trade, in the conquest of parts of Brazil, and in the colonization of New Netherland. The French created East and West India companies, and Denmark, Sweden, and the Austrian Netherlands established smaller ones, such as the Danish company at Tranquebar and the Ostend Company, showing that participation in commercial empire extended beyond the largest powers.

Company empire developed in contact with powerful Asian states and commercial systems. European merchants in the Indian Ocean did not conquer a space they could dominate but entered an existing network of Indian, Arab, Persian, Chinese, and Southeast Asian traders. The Portuguese, who captured Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511, and Hormuz in 1515, built a chain of fortified ports and a licensing system, the cartaz, to tax maritime trade. The Dutch took a more aggressive course in the Indonesian archipelago, founding Batavia in 1619 and, in 1621, conquering the Banda Islands to control the nutmeg trade in a campaign that killed or enslaved much of the local population. They also established a settlement at the Cape of Good Hope in 1652 to supply their ships, from which developed a settler colony. In India the English Company began as a trading enterprise operating through Mughal and regional concessions. After the battle of Plassey in 1757 and the acquisition of the diwani, the right to collect revenue in Bengal, in 1765, it became a territorial power, governing millions with an army that was largely composed of Indian soldiers. Parliament increased its oversight through the Regulating Act of 1773 and the India Act of 1784, ended the Company's monopoly on Indian trade in 1813 and on Chinese trade in 1833, and transferred its territories to the Crown in 1858 after the rebellion of 1857. The VOC, by contrast, was dissolved in 1799.

Commercial empires were staffed and sustained by people moved from many places. Archival reconstructions indicate approximately 972,000 outward passages from Europe to Asia aboard VOC ships between 1602 and 1795. This counts outward passages, not necessarily 972,000 different people. Many who served were recruited beyond the Dutch Republic, including in German lands and Scandinavia, and many never returned because of disease, shipwreck, other causes of death, or permanent settlement abroad. The companies also employed Asian and African crews, soldiers, clerks, interpreters, and brokers, and they relied on enslaved labor in settlements such as Batavia and the Cape, where enslaved people from India, Indonesia, and Madagascar formed a large part of the labor force. Local intermediaries were indispensable. Indian bankers and merchants financed the Company's operations, Chinese traders supplied Batavia, Armenian and Jewish merchants connected the networks of different empires, and Sephardic Jews took part in Dutch Atlantic ventures, including the community at Recife during the Dutch occupation of northeastern Brazil, and its dispersal after Portuguese reconquest in 1654. These intermediaries were not agents of European plans alone but pursued their own interests, which sometimes aligned with those of the companies and sometimes diverged.

Historians distinguish formal territorial empire, in which a state exercises sovereignty, from trading enclaves, protectorates, commercial privileges, and informal influence. The categories were fluid. Dutch and English possessions began as trading posts and in some cases became territorial dominions, while the Portuguese Estado da Índia remained an assemblage of ports. In an influential article of 1953, the historians John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson introduced the concept of informal empire to describe how Britain, through free trade and diplomatic pressure, could dominate regions in the nineteenth century without annexing them. Comparing these arrangements shows that imperial power took many institutional forms, and that corporate and commercial structures could govern as effectively as monarchical states. It also shows how European merchants, sailors, financiers, soldiers, and local collaborators connected distant markets into systems whose effects reached back into European port cities, as examined in Chapters 71 through 73 and in Section 142.12.

Section 142.9

Colonial Conquest, Land, and Settler Societies

Settler colonialism is an arrangement in which migrants establish lasting communities and institutions on territories already inhabited by others, and in which access to land becomes the central contested resource. The concept, developed in particular by the historian Patrick Wolfe, who described the settler project as a structure and not a single event, has been widely used and also criticized, and students should treat it as an analytical lens that clarifies some cases better than others. Its value lies in drawing attention to the difference between colonies of extraction, in which a small European presence governed or exploited a large indigenous population, and colonies of settlement, in which the colonizers aimed to build a society of their own and treated indigenous inhabitants as obstacles, laborers, or subjects to be displaced or absorbed.

Historical Vocabulary
Settler Colonialism

Settler colonialism describes colonization in which migrants establish permanent communities and seek to control the land of indigenous inhabitants, often building political institutions that serve the settlers and displace or subordinate the original population. It differs from migration in general, because the settlers claim sovereignty or exclusive land rights, and from extractive colonialism, in which colonial rule aims chiefly at taxes, labor, or commodities. In practice the categories overlapped, and many colonies combined extraction and settlement. The term is modern and was not used by the colonizers themselves, who spoke of plantation, civilization, improvement, and the occupation of waste or vacant land. Students should avoid assuming that every European emigrant was a colonizer in the same sense, and should also avoid treating settler colonialism as uniform: the Spanish colonial order, the British colonies of North America, the French in Algeria, and the Europeans in southern Africa developed different relationships with indigenous peoples, with the metropolis, and with enslaved or dependent labor.

The settler experiences of the early modern Atlantic diverged sharply. In Spanish America, indigenous communities remained a majority in many regions, and the Crown, which claimed to protect them as vassals, created separate legal arrangements, the so-called republics of Indians and of Spaniards, that coexisted with the exploitation of indigenous labor and land. Spanish settlers formed a small elite whose status depended on control of indigenous tribute and labor. In British North America, by contrast, the number of settlers grew rapidly, reaching roughly two and a half million, including some five hundred thousand enslaved people, by 1776, and settlement involved land purchase, treaty-making, warfare, and the progressive dispossession of indigenous nations, with legal doctrines that treated lands as available for settlement. New France remained demographically small, with perhaps seventy thousand settlers by 1760, and depended on alliances with indigenous nations in the fur trade, which gave indigenous groups significant political power but did not prevent their displacement in other regions. The Portuguese in Brazil combined settlement on the coast with the enslavement of indigenous people and, increasingly, of Africans. These differences show that the transfer of European migrants and institutions into the Americas took multiple forms.

The nineteenth century brought new settler projects. British settlement of Australia began with the arrival of the First Fleet at Sydney in January 1788 with convicts and guards, and the colonial authorities treated the continent as legally unoccupied, a doctrine known as terra nullius that the High Court of Australia rejected in the Mabo decision of 1992. Pre-contact Aboriginal populations are estimated at between roughly three hundred thousand and over a million, and the colonial period brought disease, violence, and dispossession. In New Zealand, the Treaty of Waitangi of 1840 between the Crown and many Māori chiefs, which exists in English and Māori texts that differ in important respects, became the foundation for continuing disputes over sovereignty and land. In southern Africa, the Dutch settlement at the Cape evolved through British conquest, the migration of Boer settlers in the 1830s, and the dispossession of African communities, culminating in twentieth-century legal segregation. France invaded Algeria in 1830, and by mid-century declared it part of France, organizing the northern territory into departments. European settlers, who included many of Spanish, Italian, and Maltese as well as French origin, numbered about a million by the 1950s among a population of roughly nine or ten million, and their legal position was privileged over that of the Muslim majority. These examples illustrate the diversity of settler regimes, and their relationship to the metropolis varied from dependence, to partnership, to open conflict with imperial authorities who sometimes sought to restrain settlers' demands for land.

The idea of settlement also illuminates movements within Europe. The Ulster plantation, the German agricultural settlement in the Habsburg Banat, the Russian colonization of New Russia, and the medieval eastern settlement all involved recruitment of migrants and the allocation of land in territories with existing populations. Historians debate whether to describe these as settler colonial. A broad definition draws attention to similarities of land policy and legal privilege, while a narrower one reserves the term for overseas systems with distinct metropoles and a deliberate logic of replacement. The debate matters because it affects whether European continental expansion and overseas colonialism are understood as parts of a single imperial history or as distinct systems that shared some practices. The chapter's approach is to use the comparison for analysis while preserving distinctions, and to recognize that in every case land, law, and labor were the central stakes, and that indigenous and local populations responded through resistance, negotiation, adaptation, and, sometimes, alliance.

Section 142.10

Atlantic Slavery, Forced Migration, and the African Diaspora

The transatlantic slave trade was the largest forced migration in early modern history and the central point at which European imperial expansion, commercial finance, and coerced population movement converged. The digitized records of slaving voyages compiled in the Slave Voyages database, which draws on ship registers, customs records, and insurance and merchant papers, estimate that between roughly 1500 and 1866 more than twelve million Africans were embarked on ships bound for the Americas and that some ten to eleven million survived the crossing. These are modern scholarly estimates derived from incomplete records, and they continue to be revised. Portuguese and Brazilian-based traders carried the largest share, followed by British, French, Spanish, Dutch, and Danish merchants, and slaving was also pursued by North American, Swedish, and other participants. Mortality on the Middle Passage averaged more than one in ten among those embarked, and mortality in the period of seasoning and in harsh plantation regimes was often very high, particularly in the sugar colonies. Chapter 70 narrates the trade and its structure, and the task here is to place it within a comparative history of empire and migration.

European participation was not marginal or accidental. Governments chartered and regulated trading companies, issued monopolies, and collected duties. Merchants in Lisbon, Liverpool, Bristol, Nantes, Bordeaux, Amsterdam, Copenhagen, and other ports financed voyages. Banks, insurers, and shipbuilders profited, and plantations in the Caribbean, Brazil, and the American South supplied sugar, tobacco, coffee, cotton, and other commodities to European consumers. But the trade also depended on African political and commercial actors. African rulers and merchants controlled much of the supply of captives on the African coast, as warfare, raiding, and judicial enslavement provided people for sale, and states such as Dahomey and Asante participated in the trade and were transformed by it. African rulers also resisted. In 1526 Afonso I of Kongo wrote to the Portuguese king to protest that his subjects were being kidnapped and sold, a letter that is among the earliest African protests against the trade. Africans were therefore historical actors in the political economy of slavery, but their agency operated within a system created largely by European demand and capital. Historians emphasize the human consequences, which included depopulation, insecurity, and the militarization of societies along the African coast.

Enslaved Africans did not simply endure. They created new communities, languages, religions, and political forms in the Americas under conditions of extreme violence. Cultural traditions from West and Central Africa were reconstituted in religions such as Candomblé and Vodou, in music and craft, and in languages and creoles. Enslaved people resisted by flight, sabotage, and rebellion. Maroon communities, such as Palmares in Brazil and the Maroons of Jamaica, maintained autonomy for decades, and the revolution that began in 1791 in the French colony of Saint-Domingue led to the abolition of slavery there and to the independence of Haiti in 1804, the first state founded by formerly enslaved people. France later demanded an indemnity from Haiti in 1825, initially of 150 million francs and reduced in 1838, which burdened the new state for generations. The African diaspora in the Americas was thus a creation of forced migration and also of continuing agency, and it was connected to Europe by commerce, ideas, and the presence of Black people in European cities, including Lisbon, London, Paris, and Amsterdam, where the evidence of parish registers, legal records, and published writings, such as the autobiography of Olaudah Equiano of 1789, documents lives lived in the shadow of slavery and sometimes in resistance to it.

The effects on Europe were substantial, though their extent is debated, as Section 142.12 shows. Slavery shaped wealth and political debate. In Britain, abolitionist campaigning drew on religious dissent, particularly Quakers and Evangelicals, on the testimony of formerly enslaved people, and on mass petitions. Parliament abolished the British slave trade in 1807 and slavery itself in the empire in 1833, with an apprenticeship system lasting to 1838 and with compensation of about twenty million pounds paid to slaveholders and nothing to the enslaved, a fact preserved in the compensation records that scholars now use to trace the economic and social reach of slave ownership in Britain. Denmark prohibited its slave trade from 1803, France abolished slavery in 1794, saw it restored by Napoleon in 1802, and abolished it again in 1848. The Dutch abolished slavery in 1863, Spain in Cuba in 1886, and Brazil in 1888. Emancipation did not end coerced labor, which continued through indentured contracts, forced labor, and debt systems, and abolitionism itself became tied to imperial expansion, since opposition to slavery was used to justify intervention in Africa, a topic revisited in Section 142.13. Atlantic slavery also contributed to the development of racial ideology, in which categories of color were used to justify the enslavement of Africans and their descendants, a theme examined in Section 142.14. Europeans were also enslaved and traded in other systems, including by North African corsairs and by Crimean Tatar raiding of eastern European populations. The scale of these systems is debated, and they differed from the transatlantic trade in organization, and in the way they were integrated into the economies of imperial and commercial states.

Multiple Perspectives

The Atlantic Slave Trade: Differing Experiences and Claims

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions based on patterns in surviving evidence. They are not quotations from any single named person, and they do not carry equal moral weight or equal power.

From the perspective of enslaved Africans: Captured through warfare, raiding, or judicial enslavement, they were separated from kin, marched to the coast, confined in forts and ships, and sold as property. Survivors endured plantation labor under violent discipline and responded through flight, sabotage, rebellion, and the creation of new families, religious practices, and languages. Their experience is documented mainly through a limited number of narratives, legal testimony, runaway notices, and archaeological evidence, and much of it was never recorded in writing.
From the perspective of European merchants and shipowners: The trade was a profitable but risky commercial enterprise, supported by state charters, financed through credit, and justified by contemporary legal and religious arguments that most merchants did not question. Surviving account books and insurance records treat human beings as cargo, and show how risk, mortality, and profit were calculated. These sources document the institutional and financial structure of the trade but conceal the experience of its victims.
From the perspective of colonial administrators and planters: Plantation owners relied on enslaved labor and defended it as an economic necessity, while colonial officials sought to preserve order, collect revenue, and manage the fear of revolt. Law codes, such as the French Code Noir of 1685, regulated baptism, punishment, and the status of enslaved people, and reveal both the pretense of religious and legal supervision and the underlying reliance on coercion.
From the perspective of abolitionists: Abolitionists included Quakers, Evangelical Christians, Enlightenment critics, and, importantly, formerly enslaved and free Black writers and activists. They argued from religious conviction, natural rights, and testimony, and organized petitions, boycotts, and publications. Their movement succeeded only in combination with enslaved people's resistance, economic change, and political circumstance, and many abolitionists held views about race and empire that later generations have criticized.
Section 142.11

European Emigration and the Formation of Overseas Diasporas

Alongside the forced movement of Africans, Europeans themselves moved overseas in large numbers, and the scale of this migration was without precedent. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century migrants to the English colonies of North America and the Caribbean included many indentured servants, who exchanged several years of labor for passage and who made up a large share of white migrants to the Chesapeake colonies. Britain also transported convicts, some fifty thousand to North America before 1776 and some one hundred sixty thousand to Australia between 1788 and 1868. Huguenots fled France after the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, with perhaps a couple of hundred thousand leaving for Protestant states and colonies, and religious refugees such as the Puritans and the Pilgrims settled in New England. The Portuguese gold rush drew migrants to Brazil in the eighteenth century, and Spaniards continued to travel to the Americas. But the great flow came in the nineteenth century. Estimates suggest that around fifty million Europeans left between 1815 and the 1930s, mostly for the Americas, with substantial numbers going to Australia, New Zealand, southern Africa, and Siberia and Central Asia within the Russian Empire. These are rough aggregate estimates, and figures differ by country and by how return migration is counted.

The causes were complex. Population growth, agricultural change, loss of land, industrial dislocation, famine, religious or political persecution, and the attraction of land and wages overseas all contributed, and improvements in transportation, notably steamships and railways, together with falling fares, made emigration feasible for people of limited means. Ireland provides the most dramatic case. The Great Famine of 1845 to 1852 killed around a million people and drove roughly a million more to emigrate, and Ireland's population fell from over eight million in 1841 to some six and a half million in 1851, and continued to decline afterwards. Germans left in large numbers, some five million to the United States over the nineteenth century, and Scandinavians, notably Norwegians, emigrated at high rates in proportion to their populations. After 1880 Italian emigration surged from the south and northeast, with more than thirteen million leaving between 1876 and 1915 according to the usual count, many of whom returned. From the eastern parts of Europe, millions of Poles, Slovaks, Hungarians, Ukrainians, and Jews left, and Spaniards and Portuguese went to Latin America. Scottish Highlanders were pressed off the land during the clearances. Emigrants moved for different reasons, and some, such as the Irish famine emigrants, left in circumstances of near compulsion, while others, such as skilled artisans, left in hope of better opportunities.

Overseas communities developed distinct strategies for preserving or changing their cultures. Immigrants established churches, schools, newspapers, mutual aid societies, and associations based on village, region, or religion, and often maintained languages for a generation or two. German-language newspapers flourished in the United States, Italian immigrants created neighborhood communities and associations, and Jewish immigrants founded Yiddish newspapers such as the Forverts of New York, established in 1897, and landsmanshaftn, mutual aid societies of people from the same town. Over time many groups assimilated, adopting the dominant language and often broadening their identities from regional to national, so that Italians, who had arrived as Sicilians or Neapolitans, became Italian Americans partly through the experience of migration. Some migrants returned, bringing capital and ideas, and some maintained transnational family economies that remitted money to relatives at home, which sustained rural households in Ireland, Italy, and elsewhere.

European emigration was entangled with empire and dispossession. Settlers took indigenous land in the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Argentina, and southern Africa, and governments encouraged settlement through land grants and legislation, such as the American Homestead Act of 1862. The migrants themselves were not equal. Some arrived with capital or connections, others as impoverished laborers who faced discrimination, and Irish Catholics and Jews, among others, encountered prejudice in the societies they entered. Yet in settler societies, European migrants eventually gained access to categories of belonging and privilege, often defined by race, that were denied to indigenous people, enslaved Africans and their descendants, and migrants from Asia, who were targeted by exclusion laws such as the Australian Immigration Restriction Act of 1901. The European diasporas thus illustrate both the diversity of European experience and the ways in which settler colonial systems redistributed land and rights. The movement also affected Europe: it relieved demographic pressure in some regions, created networks of remittances, and shaped political debates about emigration, national identity, and loyalty.

Everyday Life and Social History

Letters, Households, and Migration Networks

Decisions to migrate were usually made within households and communities and not by individuals alone. A family might send a son first, to be followed by siblings, a spouse, or parents, and the pattern, now called chain migration, meant that people tended to settle near those from their own village or region. Emigrant letters, which survive in large numbers in family collections and archives, show how information traveled: they described wages, land, and illness, urged relatives to follow or to stay, enclosed money or tickets, and negotiated marriages across distance. The records of ports and receiving stations, such as passenger lists and the registers of Ellis Island, which opened in 1892, allow historians to trace the age, occupation, and destination of migrants, though they record only what officials asked.

Migration changed the lives of those who stayed as well. In regions of high male emigration, women managed farms and households alone, relied on remittances, and sometimes waited years for reunion, and in southern Italy women whose husbands were abroad were sometimes called white widows. Return migration, which was common, brought new tastes, skills, and capital, and sometimes friction with neighbors who had not left. Networks of kin, religion, and village sustained newcomers in unfamiliar places, supplying lodging, employment, and credit. At the same time, these networks could be exploitative, as when labor agents, known in some immigrant communities as padroni, profited from newcomers who knew no English. Letters and household records therefore offer an alternative to histories organized around states and empires: they show migration as an accumulation of family decisions made under pressure, with imperial structures in the background.

Section 142.12

Empire, Wealth, Consumption, and the Transformation of European Societies

The preceding sections have emphasized how empire moved people and reshaped societies overseas. This section reverses the direction and asks how imperial systems changed Europe. The effects were visible first in ports. Seville, Lisbon, Amsterdam, London, Bristol, Liverpool, Glasgow, Nantes, Bordeaux, Copenhagen, and Hamburg expanded as commercial centers whose fortunes depended on Atlantic and Asian trade. They developed shipbuilding, warehousing, refining, banking, and insurance, and some, such as Bordeaux and Liverpool, grew rich on the slave trade and on the colonial commodities it supported. Financial institutions, including marine insurance markets and credit networks, financed voyages and plantation economies. Merchant fortunes funded country houses, urban development, charitable foundations, and, in some cases, universities and museums, and the surviving legacies of such wealth are still visible in many European cities.

Colonial commodities transformed consumption. Sugar, once a luxury, became a staple of European diets over the eighteenth century, and tobacco, coffee, tea, chocolate, and, in the nineteenth century, cotton reshaped habits of sociability, nutrition, and clothing. The development of coffeehouses and tea drinking, discussed in Chapter 73 and in the study of consumer society in Chapter 85, linked new patterns of public life to colonial production. The historian Sidney Mintz argued that sugar consumption helped reorganize working class diets and the rhythms of industrial work, an interpretation that scholars have refined and contested but that identifies a real connection between plantations and European households. Cotton, produced largely by enslaved labor in the American South until the 1860s, provided the raw material for the British textile industry, and the Lancashire cotton famine of the early 1860s, when the American Civil War cut supplies, shows how dependent European manufacturing had become on that system. Empire also influenced European science and culture, through botanical gardens, collections, and the circulation of knowledge about plants, medicine, and peoples, and through the migration of soldiers, officials, and missionaries who returned home with experience and capital.

Imperial competition also shaped European states. Wars for colonies and trade, such as the Seven Years' War examined in Chapter 80, required taxation, borrowing, and naval power, and strengthened fiscal institutions, as discussed in Chapter 74. Colonial service offered careers to younger sons, soldiers, and administrators, and the empire created constituencies with an interest in its expansion. Political debates, from abolitionism to the regulation of company rule in India, forced states to define their obligations and powers. Imperial identity entered national culture, through literature, exhibitions, and popular imperialism, and affected European ideas of themselves in relation to other peoples. But the benefits were distributed unequally within Europe. Merchants and shareholders gained, while many workers experienced the industrial and commercial changes of the period as dislocation, and rural populations in imperial metropoles such as Ireland or the Scottish Highlands experienced dispossession and emigration, a reminder that empire's effects within Europe were uneven.

The relationship between empire and industrialization has been among the most contested questions in modern historiography.

Historical Debate

How Did Empire Contribute to European Economic Transformation?

The question: To what extent did Atlantic slavery, colonial trade, and imperial expansion cause or enable the industrial and commercial transformation of Europe, and especially Britain, between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries?

Interpretation A, empire as a central driver: Eric Williams, in Capitalism and Slavery (1944), argued that profits from the slave trade and plantations helped finance British industrialization and that economic change later undermined slavery. Later historians, including Joseph Inikori and, for cotton, Sven Beckert, emphasized that Atlantic markets, slave-grown cotton, and the commercial and financial networks built around slavery provided raw materials, demand, and capital that were crucial to European industry. From this perspective empire was not peripheral but constitutive of European capitalism.
Interpretation B, domestic factors as primary: Historians such as Stanley Engerman and Patrick O'Brien argued that estimates of colonial profits show them to have been only a small share of total British investment, and that industrialization depended mainly on domestic factors, including agricultural productivity, institutions, technological innovation, and internal markets. Robert Allen has stressed the role of high wages and cheap coal in encouraging labor-saving technology. In this view, colonial trade and slavery mattered but were not necessary conditions for industrial takeoff.
Interpretation C, empire and resource windfalls: Kenneth Pomeranz, in his work on the Great Divergence, argued that Europe's access to New World land and resources, which provided what he termed ghost acres of production, together with accessible coal, helped Britain escape ecological constraints that limited other advanced economies. This interpretation partly bridges the other two, linking domestic and imperial factors.

Evidence: The debate rests on estimates of profits from slavery and trade, the proportion of investment drawn from colonial sources, the share of exports sent to colonial markets, the supply and price of raw cotton, wage and energy data, and comparative evidence from other regions. Much of the quantitative evidence is fragmentary, and conclusions depend heavily on what is counted and how profits are defined. The debate also turns on conceptual questions, including whether the relevant effect was the direct financing of industry or the broader creation of markets, commodities, institutions, and incentives.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the relationship between European prosperity and the exploitation of colonized and enslaved peoples, how far industrialization can be treated as an internal European development, and how responsibility and legacy should be assessed. A strong version of either position is difficult to sustain, and most current scholarship accepts that empire was historically consequential for European economies while disagreeing about its relative weight and mechanisms.

Whatever the weight assigned to empire in industrial growth, the transformation of European society by imperial relationships is not in doubt. The movement of goods, capital, and people reshaped port cities, consumption, state finance, and political debate, and it left institutions and fortunes whose history has become a subject of public discussion in recent decades. Section 142.13

Nineteenth-Century Imperial Expansion and the Global Colonial Order

The nineteenth century transformed European empire. After the Napoleonic Wars, examined in Chapters 90 through 92, Britain emerged with the strongest navy and an expanding network of territories, including the Cape Colony, Ceylon, Malta, and a growing position in India, while Spain and Portugal lost most of their American empires and the Atlantic slave systems were gradually dismantled. The new phase of expansion rested on industrial technology: steamships, railways, the telegraph, quinine against malaria, and repeating and later machine guns enlarged the capacity of Europeans to project force, communicate over long distances, and operate in tropical regions. It also rested on commercial interests, investment, strategic rivalry, and ideologies of civilization and progress. The principal purpose here is not a second narrative of conquest, which Chapters 104 and 105 provide, but a comparison of imperial structures.

British imperial development combined several systems. India, governed by the East India Company until 1858 and then by the Crown, was the greatest imperial possession, and its army, revenues, and markets sustained British power across Asia. Settler colonies in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and southern Africa acquired growing self-government, while other territories were ruled directly as Crown colonies, or indirectly through local rulers and protectorates. Britain also exercised informal influence in Latin America, China, and the Ottoman Empire through trade and finance. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 and the occupation of Egypt in 1882 shifted British attention toward the Red Sea and Africa. France, which invaded Algeria in 1830, expanded into West and Equatorial Africa, Indochina, and Madagascar, and pursued a policy that officially stressed assimilation, even though in practice it governed most colonial subjects under separate legal regimes. The Netherlands consolidated control of the East Indies, including the long Aceh war that began in 1873. Belgium acquired the Congo through King Leopold II, whose personal Congo Free State, recognized in 1885, became notorious for forced labor in rubber extraction and for atrocities that produced very high mortality, estimated by historians in the millions with wide uncertainty, and led to its annexation by the Belgian state in 1908.

The partition of Africa, which accelerated after the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885, saw European powers claim most of the continent within a few decades. The conference did not itself partition Africa, but its General Act established rules for the recognition of claims, including the principle that occupation should be effective, and encouraged competition to establish control. Germany acquired colonies in Togo, Cameroon, South West Africa, and East Africa, and Italy took Eritrea and Somaliland and, after its defeat by Ethiopia at Adwa in 1896, Libya in 1911 to 1912, and, in the 1930s, Ethiopia itself. Colonial conquest met sustained resistance, from the long Algerian struggle of Abd al-Qadir in the 1830s and 1840s to the Maji Maji rising in German East Africa in 1905 to 1907. In South West Africa, German forces responded to the Herero and Nama uprisings of 1904 with campaigns of extermination now widely recognized by scholars as genocide, a classification that the German government formally accepted in 2021. In southern Africa, the South African War of 1899 to 1902 pitted Britain against the Boer republics and involved British camps in which many thousands of civilians died.

Imperial ideology combined coercion with claims of mission. The French invoked a mission civilisatrice, the British a trusteeship over less developed peoples, and the Belgians and others the suppression of slavery and the spread of Christianity and commerce. These claims were sometimes sincerely held and also served to legitimize conquest and economic exploitation. They coexisted with persistent resistance and with critics in Europe, including liberals, socialists, and missionaries. Britain and France were the greatest imperial powers, but smaller states, including Portugal, which retained Angola and Mozambique, Spain, which lost its remaining major colonies in 1898, Sweden, which sold its last Caribbean colony in 1878, and Denmark, which kept several Caribbean islands until 1917, show the range of participation. Russia continued its continental expansion into Central Asia, with the conquest of Tashkent in 1865 and the incorporation of Turkestan, in a movement that resembled overseas conquest in its ideology of civilization and its settlement of Russian peasants.

Section 142.14

Race, Civilizational Hierarchies, and Imperial Categories of Belonging

Modern empires did not simply rule diverse populations. They classified them. From the eighteenth century, naturalists and anthropologists such as Johann Friedrich Blumenbach proposed divisions of humanity into races, and in the nineteenth century these classifications hardened into theories of biological hierarchy. Arthur de Gobineau's Essay on the Inequality of the Human Races, published in the 1850s, argued for the primacy of a supposed white race, and the interpretation of Darwin's theory of natural selection by thinkers such as Herbert Spencer, together with Francis Galton's eugenics, contributed to what is called Social Darwinism and scientific racism, topics examined in Chapter 103. These theories were often used to justify empire, by presenting colonized peoples as lower on a scale of civilization and Europeans as natural rulers or guardians. They were never uncontested. Enlightenment universalism, Christian ideas of human unity, and the arguments of Black and Asian intellectuals, such as the Haitian anthropologist Anténor Firmin, whose book On the Equality of the Human Races appeared in 1885, rejected racial hierarchy. Modern scholarship has established that racial categories are historical constructions and not objective biological divisions.

Colonial legal systems turned these ideas into categories of membership. The French code of the indigénat, introduced in Algeria in 1881 and later extended to other colonies, subjected colonized people to summary penalties and restrictions that did not apply to French citizens. In the French empire, a limited number of colonial subjects could obtain citizenship by meeting conditions of education, service, or assimilation, a system that created a small group of so-called évolués while excluding the majority. Portuguese law in Angola and Mozambique distinguished assimilados from indígenas until the system was abolished in 1961. In the Dutch East Indies, the legal order separated Europeans, foreign Orientals, and natives, and applied different law to each, while the so-called Ethical Policy introduced in 1901 promised welfare and education within a continuing hierarchy. The British developed both settler self-government and indirect rule, defended by Frederick Lugard in 1922 as a dual mandate to develop colonies for the benefit of both the local and the metropolitan populations. Across these systems, a person's rights depended on legal and racial classification, and the categories were enforced by courts, taxation, and the control of land and movement.

Racial categories also shaped migration. Settler societies restricted the entry of non-Europeans, as in the Australian Immigration Restriction Act of 1901 and the Chinese head tax in Canada. In Britain the Aliens Act of 1905 was directed largely at Jewish immigration from eastern Europe, showing that European diasporas, as well as colonial subjects, were affected by racial thinking. Within Europe, theories of race were applied to Jews, Roma, Slavs, and others, and institutions such as the Swedish State Institute for Racial Biology, founded in 1922, studied the Sámi, so that colonial categories and metropolitan prejudices influenced one another. Ethnological expositions, in which colonized people were displayed in European exhibitions, popularized racial hierarchy. The relationship between empire and European antisemitism and fascism is debated, and Section 142.18 addresses one aspect of that debate.

Empire also interacted with nationalism. Nationalists in Europe often supported colonial expansion as a sign of national strength, and the ideal of the nation-state coexisted with the governance of overseas subjects who were not regarded as part of the nation. Yet imperial ideologies also produced universal claims that colonized people could appropriate. When colonial authorities proclaimed the rights of man, or claimed to bring law and civilization, colonized intellectuals and activists asked why these principles did not apply to them. Dadabhai Naoroji, elected to the British House of Commons in 1892 as its first Asian member, argued that British rule drained India's wealth. Pan-African and anticolonial thinkers, including W. E. B. Du Bois, who helped to organize Pan-African Congresses in Paris in 1919, challenged both racism and imperial rule. The categories of empire were thus contested from the beginning, and the contest, which continued in the metropolitan public sphere as well as in the colonies, is the subject of the next section.

Section 142.15

Colonial Government, Labor, Resistance, and Anticolonial Politics

Colonial states differed in structure but shared the problem of governing large populations with limited European personnel. Direct administration, associated with France and Portugal, placed European officials in charge at regional and local levels, while indirect rule, associated with Britain in northern Nigeria and elsewhere, preserved existing rulers under supervision, which could entrench or reshape local hierarchies. Settler-dominated systems, as in Algeria, Kenya, and southern Africa, gave settlers a strong voice in policy and land allocation. Concessionary systems, notably in the French and Belgian Congo, granted private companies rights over land and labor in exchange for revenue. Common to all were taxation, often imposed in cash to force people into wage labor, control of land, and the use of coerced labor. Forced labor remained widespread, in the form of compulsory work on roads and railways, contract labor on plantations, such as the cocoa estates of São Tomé that provoked international protest in the early twentieth century, and obligatory service under tax and tribute systems. The International Labour Organization adopted a Forced Labour Convention in 1930, though its application to colonies was limited and uneven.

Colonial rule depended on indigenous participation. Local rulers collected taxes and kept order, interpreters and clerks mediated between rulers and ruled, missionary schools trained a literate class, and soldiers recruited from colonized populations, such as the Indian army, the Senegalese tirailleurs, and the Dutch colonial army in the East Indies, made up much of the force that held the empires together and later fought in European wars. Entrepreneurs and cultivators took advantage of colonial markets, and many people lived within the colonial system while seeking to modify it. These intermediaries were not simply collaborators or victims. They pursued varied interests and sometimes became leaders of reform or anticolonial movements.

Resistance took many forms. Early armed resistance, from the Indian rebellion of 1857 to the Herero and Maji Maji risings, was often suppressed with great violence. Others employed petitions, newspapers, strikes, boycotts, religious movements, and legal challenges. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, began as a body seeking reform and grew into a mass movement. Mohandas Gandhi's Hind Swaraj of 1909 criticized modern civilization and imperial rule, and campaigns of civil disobedience mobilized millions. Writers associated with the Négritude movement, such as Aimé Césaire and Léopold Sédar Senghor, redefined Black identity in the French-speaking world from the 1930s. Indonesian nationalists, led by Sukarno, organized a national party in 1927, and Vietnamese nationalists included figures who ranged from reformers to communists. In the Middle East and North Africa, the Egyptian revolution of 1919 and movements in the Maghreb challenged British and French control. Anticolonial nationalism drew on European ideas of national self-determination, socialism, and rights, and also on indigenous traditions, and many of its leaders had studied or lived in European cities, which became centers of anticolonial organization.

Creators: European governments at the Berlin Conference; Vietnamese activists in Paris; the General Assembly of the United Nations · Dates: 1885, 1919, 1960 · Type: An international legal agreement, a petition, and a declaration of principle

Context: The three documents were produced in very different circumstances. The General Act of the Berlin Conference was signed on 26 February 1885 by representatives of European powers, the United States, and the Ottoman Empire, with no African participants, at the end of a conference convened to regulate trade and claims in the Congo basin and West Africa. The 1919 petition, known by its French title as the demands of the Annamite people, was submitted in June 1919 to the delegations at the Paris Peace Conference. It was issued under the name Nguyen Ai Quoc, a name later associated with Ho Chi Minh, and was prepared, according to most accounts, with the assistance of other Vietnamese activists in France. The Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples, resolution 1514 (XV), was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 14 December 1960, by a vote of 89 in favor, none against, and nine abstentions, among them several colonial powers.

Paraphrase of the General Act of the Berlin Conference, 1885 The Act set out rules for navigation and trade in the Congo and Niger basins and declared that the signatory powers would work to protect indigenous populations, improve their moral and material conditions, and suppress the slave trade. It also provided that powers taking possession of coastal territory should notify the others and maintain authority sufficient to protect existing rights, a provision known as effective occupation. This is a summary of the Act's provisions and not a translation of a single passage.
Paraphrase of the Vietnamese demands to the Paris Peace Conference, 1919 The petition asked France to grant its Indochinese subjects a general amnesty for political prisoners, equal legal guarantees with Europeans, freedom of the press, of association, and of assembly, freedom of education, the replacement of rule by decree with rule by law, and representation in the French parliament. It framed these requests within the principles of national self-determination that Allied leaders had proclaimed. This is a summary of its principal demands and not a translation.
Paraphrase of United Nations General Assembly resolution 1514 (XV), 1960 The Declaration stated that subjecting peoples to alien domination and exploitation denies fundamental human rights and conflicts with the United Nations Charter, affirmed the right of all peoples to self-determination, and declared that inadequate political, economic, social, or educational preparedness should never serve as a pretext for delaying independence. This is a summary of its central claims and not a translation.

Analysis: The three documents make different claims about who may exercise authority and on what grounds. The Berlin Act presents European rule as a legitimate project of regulation, trade, and civilization, and it defines the conditions of recognition among Europeans while excluding the peoples whose territories were involved. Its language of protection and moral improvement coexisted with the conquests and exploitation that followed, and the Congo Free State, whose existence the conference helped to recognize, became a notorious example. The 1919 petition accepts the legitimacy of French law and republican principles and uses them to demand rights, a strategy of reform within the system and not outright rejection, and it shows that colonized people could appeal to the claims of empires against their practice. The 1960 Declaration, adopted in a General Assembly in which newly independent states had become a majority, reverses the language of preparedness on which imperial arguments had relied. Reading the three together shows a shift in the sources of legitimacy from imperial recognition to self-determination. No single document, however, represents the experiences of an entire population. The Berlin Act is a diplomatic text produced by governments, the petition reflects the views of a small group of activists whose representativeness is a historical question, and the Declaration is a statement of principle that did not itself bring about decolonization, which followed from political and military struggles.

Questions for students:

  1. What does each document say about who holds legitimate authority and who may speak for colonized peoples?
  2. Who created each source, and for what possible purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from these documents about imperial ideology, anticolonial strategy, and international law?
  4. What can historians not establish from these documents alone, and what other sources would be needed to understand the experiences of colonized populations?
Section 142.16

Migration, Exile, and Transnational Communities in the Age of Empire

The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw movement of population on a vast scale, both overseas and within Europe and its imperial borderlands. Labor migration within Europe was substantial. Irish workers moved to Britain, Polish agricultural and industrial workers moved to eastern Germany and the Ruhr, and Italians moved to France, Switzerland, and Germany, as well as across the Atlantic, often seasonally. Within the Russian Empire, millions of peasants moved east to Siberia and Central Asia, encouraged by the railways and, after 1906, by government policy, so that several million relocated before 1914. Empires also moved laborers across oceans. After the abolition of slavery, colonial economies recruited indentured workers from India and China, with approximately 1.3 million Indian indentured laborers going to colonies such as Mauritius, Guyana, Trinidad, Fiji, and Natal between the 1830s and 1920, usually under conditions of constraint that critics compared to slavery. Asian and African seamen, known in British ports as lascars, formed communities in European harbor cities, and students and activists from the colonies came to study and live in London and Paris.

Jewish migration from eastern Europe was among the largest movements of the period. Pogroms in the Russian Empire in 1881 and 1882, restrictive legislation such as the May Laws of 1882, further violence at Kishinev in 1903, and economic hardship drove some two to two and a half million Jews from eastern Europe between 1881 and 1914, mainly to the United States, but also to Britain, Argentina, South Africa, and Palestine. The movement had political dimensions. The first wave of Zionist immigration to Palestine began in 1882, Theodor Herzl published Der Judenstaat in 1896 and presided over the First Zionist Congress at Basel in 1897, and the Jewish Labor Bund was founded in the same year, offering a socialist alternative within eastern Europe. These movements illustrate how migration generated competing political projects, and how diaspora communities in Europe and overseas developed intellectual, religious, and cultural networks through newspapers, philanthropic organizations, and political parties.

Imperial transformation caused displacement in the Ottoman and Russian borderlands. Russian expansion in the Caucasus and the Balkan wars of the nineteenth century sent Muslim refugees, known as muhacirs, into Ottoman territory, where they were settled in Anatolia and the Balkans, and the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 produced further movements of Muslims, Christians, and Jews. In the First World War, Ottoman authorities deported and killed large numbers of Armenians in 1915 and 1916, in events that most historians characterize as genocide, with estimates of deaths commonly in the range of about one million to one and a half million. Survivors formed communities in Syria, Lebanon, France, the United States, and elsewhere, and the memory and recognition of these events became central to Armenian diaspora identity. These events are examined further in Section 142.17 and their relation to the collapse of the empires belongs to the same history.

Political exile was another form of movement. After the failure of the Polish uprising of 1830 to 1831, thousands of Polish officers and intellectuals formed the Great Emigration, centered in Paris. Italian nationalists, including Giuseppe Mazzini, organized from London and elsewhere. Participants in the revolutions of 1848, examined in Chapter 98, fled to Britain, Switzerland, and the United States, and Karl Marx lived and worked in London. Russian revolutionaries, such as Alexander Herzen, who published his journal Kolokol in London from 1857, and later Lenin, operated in exile, and émigré presses smuggled publications back to their home countries. Colonial subjects who traveled to Europe also became politically active. Migration thus generated new identities, associations, printing networks, and political movements that linked Europe with places outside it, and exiles and migrants contributed to nationalist, socialist, and anticolonial movements on several continents. The age of empire was, in this respect, also an age of transnational communities, whose members belonged simultaneously to the societies in which they lived and to networks that spanned empires.

Section 142.17

World War I, Imperial Collapse, and the Problem of National Self-Determination

The First World War destroyed four continental empires. The Russian monarchy fell in 1917 and the Habsburg, Ottoman, and German imperial orders collapsed in 1918, as examined in Chapters 111 and 112. The war had mobilized the resources of empires, including millions of colonial soldiers and laborers, who served on European fronts and in the Middle East and Africa, and it had intensified the political claims of national movements within the empires. The peace settlements, treated in Chapter 113, created or enlarged states in central and eastern Europe, including Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, and Romania, and reduced others, such as Hungary, which at the Treaty of Trianon in 1920 lost roughly two thirds of its former territory. President Woodrow Wilson's call for national self-determination inspired many, but its application was uneven. The victors drew borders according to strategic, economic, and political calculations as well as ethnic ones, and few of the new states were ethnically homogeneous. Millions of people found themselves minorities in states dominated by other nationalities, and the peace treaties, beginning with the Polish treaty of 28 June 1919, imposed minority protection obligations on several states under League of Nations supervision, which were resented by the governments that bore them and which were inconsistently enforced.

Population movement became an instrument of policy. The Greek and Turkish wars that followed the Ottoman collapse, including the burning of Smyrna in 1922, produced hundreds of thousands of refugees, and the Convention of Lausanne of 1923 mandated an exchange of populations, determined by religion, between Greece and Turkey. About one and a quarter million Orthodox Christians and some four hundred thousand Muslims were moved, and the exchange, widely viewed at the time as a pragmatic solution, set a precedent for later transfers while inflicting severe hardship and cultural loss. Refugees from the Russian Civil War, perhaps a million or more, dispersed to Paris, Berlin, Prague, Belgrade, and Harbin, and the League of Nations introduced the Nansen passport in 1922 as an identity document for stateless refugees. Armenian survivors of the genocide became stateless, and many settled in the Middle East and western Europe. The Soviet state, established as a union in 1922, retained much of the former Russian imperial territory under a new ideology and formal federal structure, and its nationalities policy promoted national cultures within a centralized communist system, a combination whose development is a subject for the separate study of Russian and Soviet history.

Overseas empires survived and, in some respects, expanded. The League of Nations assigned former German colonies and Ottoman territories to victorious powers as mandates, a system that Article 22 of the League Covenant described as a sacred trust of civilization, and that placed Syria and Lebanon under France, and Iraq, Palestine, and Transjordan under Britain, together with former German territories in Africa and the Pacific. Mandates were supposed to prepare peoples for eventual self-government, but they were in practice colonial administrations whose imposition conflicted with the language of self-determination, and they met resistance, as in the Syrian revolt of 1925. Britain and France faced mass movements in their empires, including the Egyptian revolution of 1919, nationalist campaigns in India, and the shooting of unarmed demonstrators at Amritsar on 13 April 1919, whose political repercussions were substantial. The peace thus applied national self-determination selectively, extending it in much of central and eastern Europe and restricting it elsewhere. The collapse of some empires did not end imperial structures or hierarchical international relations. It rearranged them, produced new minorities and refugees, and left unresolved questions of borders and belonging that shaped the politics of the interwar period and the origins of the next war.

Section 142.18

World War II, Genocide, Displacement, and the Reshaping of Europe

The Second World War produced the largest movements of population in modern European history. Chapters 119 through 123 narrate the war, occupation, the Holocaust, and postwar reconstruction, and this section considers them from the perspective of empire and migration. Nazi Germany aimed at territorial conquest in eastern Europe, the expulsion, enslavement, and murder of large populations, and the resettlement of German colonists, ideas set out in plans collectively known as Generalplan Ost and associated with the doctrine of living space. Hitler and others sometimes compared this project to earlier colonial expansion, and some historians, following Hannah Arendt and Aimé Césaire, who argued in 1950 that Europeans had come to tolerate in Europe methods they had long applied in colonies, have explored connections between imperial violence and Nazi policies. Others stress differences. Nazi ideology was centered on a racial worldview that treated Jews as an existential enemy and aimed at extermination, a purpose that has no exact parallel in earlier colonial systems, and the scale and speed of the German project, within the geographic space of Europe, distinguish it. Scholars continue to debate how far the German genocide in South West Africa and other colonial violence influenced later policy, with evidence for some continuities of personnel and ideas and against any direct line of descent. The two systems should not be equated, and the comparison is useful chiefly in clarifying the role of racial hierarchy and conquest.

The Holocaust, in which the Nazi regime and its collaborators murdered about six million Jews, along with large numbers of Roma, estimated in a wide range from about two hundred thousand to half a million, and other victims, is treated in Chapter 121. Its demographic effect was to destroy the communities of eastern and central Europe that had been the center of Jewish life for centuries. Millions of other people were moved by force. The German Reich used some twelve million foreign civilians and prisoners of war in forced labor, according to modern estimates, and the Soviet government deported entire peoples, among them Volga Germans in 1941, Chechens and Ingush in February 1944, and Crimean Tatars in May 1944, as well as Poles and others from the territories it occupied in 1939 to 1941. Occupation, bombing, and the fighting itself displaced further millions.

After the war, Allied decisions redrew borders and populations. Poland's frontiers were shifted westward, with the Soviet Union retaining territory in the east and Poland receiving former German lands, and the Potsdam Agreement of August 1945 provided for the orderly transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary. Between twelve and fourteen million Germans fled or were expelled from eastern Europe, in processes that involved violence, and the number who died is disputed, with scholarly estimates ranging from roughly half a million to about two million. Poles and Ukrainians were exchanged across the new Polish-Soviet border, and Operation Vistula in 1947 dispersed Ukrainians within Poland. Italians left Istria and Dalmatia, which passed to Yugoslavia. Millions of displaced persons, former forced laborers, concentration camp survivors, and refugees were in Germany and Austria in 1945, and the Allies and the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration, and later the International Refugee Organization, repatriated many and resettled others. Jewish survivors, many unwilling to return to countries where they had suffered persecution and in some cases violence, lived in displaced persons camps and migrated to Palestine, the United States, and elsewhere, and the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948 further changed the geography of the Jewish diaspora.

These changes produced a Europe more ethnically homogeneous in many states than before the war, through murder, expulsion, and border changes, and they created new diasporas of Germans, Poles, Ukrainians, Jews, and others. They also left a legacy of memory and contested narratives. The empires that Germany and the Soviet Union had built or sought to build were reorganized in the postwar order. The Soviet Union extended its influence over eastern Europe, as examined in Chapter 125, and Britain and France, though victorious, found their overseas empires weakened. The movement of populations thus connects the imperial dimensions of the war with the next stage in the history of empire, which was its decline.

Section 142.19

Decolonization, Postcolonial Migration, and the Remaking of European Society

Between the 1940s and the 1970s, most European overseas empires ended, a process surveyed in Chapter 128 and approached here comparatively. The war had weakened the imperial powers economically, discredited claims of racial superiority, and strengthened the anticolonial movements that had mobilized during it, while the United States and the Soviet Union, whatever their own imperial practices, generally opposed European colonialism in principle. Decolonization nonetheless followed different paths. Britain, which granted independence to India and Pakistan in August 1947, in a partition accompanied by the displacement of perhaps fifteen million people and by massacres whose death toll is estimated in a very wide range, from some two hundred thousand to two million, later withdrew from most of its remaining colonies, with Ghana becoming independent on March 6, 1957, as the first British colony in sub-Saharan Africa to gain independence during the post-1945 decolonization wave. This distinction should not erase earlier independent African states, including Liberia. In 1960 Prime Minister Harold Macmillan spoke in Cape Town of a wind of change blowing through Africa. Britain also fought colonial wars, including the suppression of the Mau Mau rising in Kenya between 1952 and 1960, in which detention camps and abuses were later documented in litigation and archival research.

France fought longer and more costly wars. Defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954 ended its rule in Indochina, and the Algerian war of 1954 to 1962, in which settlers, Algerian nationalists, and the French army and state were deeply divided, ended in Algerian independence on 5 July 1962 and the departure of some eight hundred thousand to a million Europeans, the so-called pieds-noirs, together with a smaller number of Algerian auxiliaries, the harkis, many of whom were abandoned or poorly received. The Netherlands, which fought two military campaigns to retain Indonesia after the Indonesian declaration of independence in August 1945, transferred sovereignty in December 1949, and some three hundred thousand people of mixed or European background moved to the Netherlands over the following years, with a further group of South Moluccans arriving in 1951. Belgium withdrew from the Congo in June 1960 in a transition that was rapidly followed by crisis. Portugal, under an authoritarian regime that insisted its colonies were overseas provinces, fought wars in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea-Bissau from 1961, until the revolution of 25 April 1974, led by army officers, overthrew the regime and led to the independence of its colonies in 1974 and 1975. Hundreds of thousands of Portuguese settlers and others, the retornados, estimated at around half a million or more, returned to Portugal. These varied outcomes show that decolonization was not a single process. It was shaped by the nature of anticolonial movements, the presence of settlers, the political situation in the metropole, international pressures, and the economic importance of colonies.

Migration between former colonies and European societies took several forms and is not equivalent to other postwar migration streams. In Britain, migrants from the Caribbean arrived as British subjects under pre-existing nationality arrangements. The Empire Windrush reached Tilbury in June 1948, with a manifest of more than one thousand passengers; contemporary Ministry of Labour records identified 492 Caribbean migrants, mainly from Jamaica but also other Caribbean territories, within the arrivals they were assisting. The British Nationality Act received royal assent on July 30, 1948, after the ship arrived, and took effect on January 1, 1949, creating the status of citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies while preserving the broader designation of Commonwealth citizen. In the decades that followed, thousands migrated to Britain from the Caribbean, India, Pakistan, and later East Africa. As migration increased and encountered discrimination, including riots in Notting Hill in 1958, Parliament restricted entry through the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act of 1971, while also passing race relations legislation. In France, Algerians, who held French nationality with restricted rights before independence, migrated for work in large numbers, and migrants from Morocco, Tunisia, sub-Saharan Africa, and the overseas departments followed, the last in part through a state agency established in 1963. The Netherlands received migrants from Indonesia, Suriname, and the Antilles, with a large movement from Suriname around its independence in 1975. Belgium received migrants from the Congo and Rwanda, and Portugal from its former colonies. Beyond these, Germany recruited guest workers from Italy beginning in 1955 and from Turkey from 1961 until it halted recruitment in 1973, and eastern European communist states recruited contract workers from Vietnam, Mozambique, and Cuba. These movements were not postcolonial in the same legal and historical sense, though they interacted with them in the formation of multiethnic societies.

Postcolonial migrants entered societies that were rebuilding after the war and short of labor, and they worked in transport, health services, manufacturing, and construction. They also faced discrimination in housing, employment, and education, as well as questions about their legal status. Over time, communities developed institutions, businesses, religious centers, and political organizations, and second and third generations, born and educated in Europe, challenged exclusion and redefined national identities. Debates over citizenship, nationality law, and integration followed in each country, and the specific legal heritage of empire, whether the British Commonwealth framework or the French republican model of assimilation, shaped the responses.

Cause and Effect

Decolonization and Postcolonial Migration to Europe

Long-term causes

Imperial networks of language, law, nationality, education, and labor had connected colonies with metropoles for generations, while colonial economies had distorted local development and generated migration to ports and cities.

Short-term causes

Wartime weakening of imperial powers, the growth of anticolonial movements, international pressure, and postwar labor shortages in western Europe created the conditions for independence and for migration.

Transformation

Colonies became independent through negotiation, war, or revolution, and nationality law was redefined, with some populations retaining rights of entry and others losing them.

Immediate effects

Settlers and auxiliaries from Algeria, Indonesia, Angola, and Mozambique relocated to Europe, and workers from former colonies moved to take jobs in European economies, often facing discrimination.

Long-term consequences

Multigenerational communities, restrictive immigration laws, antiracist legislation, new political movements, and continuing debates about citizenship, integration, and national identity.

Historical complexity

Postcolonial migration cannot be reduced to a single cause. It also reflected labor demand, family reunification, and the policies of states that sometimes encouraged and sometimes restricted entry, and it varied across the empires involved.

Historical complexity: Migration from former colonies was shaped by decisions in both Europe and the former colonies, and by legal frameworks inherited from empire. Treating it as an unplanned reversal of colonization, or as a purely economic phenomenon, obscures the role of nationality law, labor policy, and the agency of the migrants themselves.

Section 142.20

Diaspora, Imperial Memory, and Belonging in Contemporary Europe

The legacies of empire are visible in the populations, languages, institutions, and public culture of contemporary Europe, and in continuing debates over who belongs. Diasporic communities differ widely and should not be treated as interchangeable. Jewish communities, whose long presence in Europe predates most modern states, were devastated by the Holocaust and since 1945 have been reshaped by migration to Israel and the Americas, by the immigration of Soviet Jews, of whom about a million moved to Israel after 1989, and by a revival of communal life in some countries, alongside continued antisemitism. African and Afro-European communities include descendants of people who have lived in European cities for centuries and later migrants from Africa and the Caribbean. Armenians form a worldwide diaspora whose identity has been shaped by the genocide and its recognition. Roma, whose languages show an origin in the Indian subcontinent and whose presence in Byzantine and Balkan lands is documented from the later Middle Ages, were enslaved in the Romanian principalities until the mid-nineteenth century, persecuted under Nazi rule, and continue to face discrimination, and their history shows that diaspora does not always involve a distant homeland. Muslim communities in Europe include long-established populations in the Balkans, descendants of the Ottoman past, and migrants and their descendants from North Africa, Turkey, South Asia, and elsewhere, with different languages, national origins, and relations to the states where they live. European emigrants and their descendants form their own diasporas, which sometimes maintain ties through citizenship laws and cultural connections, as in the case of Irish descent abroad. Diaspora identities may overlap with national citizenship, and individuals may hold several affiliations at once.

The public memory of empire has been contested. Governments, museums, and communities have debated how to recognize colonial violence and slavery. In France, a law of 2001 recognized the slave trade and slavery as crimes against humanity, while a law of 2005 requiring teaching about the positive role of colonization was criticized and its relevant provision repealed in 2006, and President Emmanuel Macron described colonization in Algeria as a crime against humanity in 2017. In Britain, the toppling of the statue of the slave trader Edward Colston in Bristol on 7 June 2020 prompted discussion about monuments, and the Windrush scandal of 2018, in which long-resident Commonwealth citizens were wrongly treated as illegal immigrants, showed how the legal legacy of empire affected people in the present. Belgium has reconsidered its memory of Leopold II, and the Royal Museum for Central Africa at Tervuren reopened in 2018 after renovation that sought to address its colonial origins. In the Netherlands, Prime Minister Mark Rutte apologized for the Dutch role in slavery on 19 December 2022, and King Willem-Alexander did so on 1 July 2023. Germany recognized the colonial killings in Namibia as genocide in 2021 and negotiated a declaration with Namibia, which remains politically contested, and in 2022 agreed to transfer ownership of looted Benin objects to Nigeria. Restitution progressed elsewhere: in June 2025 the Netherlands returned 119 Benin objects to Nigeria; in February 2026 the University of Cambridge announced its transfer of legal ownership of 116 such objects to Nigeria, with physical return arrangements to follow. Ownership transfers and physical repatriations are distinct actions, and restitution remains a major issue for museums across Europe. These are developing matters. With a historical information cutoff of November 19, 2026, this chapter distinguishes established evidence about slavery, conquest, and colonial violence from ongoing political and institutional debates about their commemoration.

Contemporary debates about migration and identity, discussed in Chapter 137 and, for Brexit, Chapter 138, are rooted partly in this history. They involve disagreement about whether imperial history should be a source of national pride, of shame, or of complex inheritance, about how far citizens of immigrant background are recognized as fully belonging, and about how European institutions, including the European Union, should relate to their member states' colonial pasts. Historians contribute by providing evidence, comparison, and context, though they do not agree on all interpretations, and their conclusions about the past do not by themselves settle political questions in the present. Some critics argue that emphasis on empire distorts the history of European societies, while others argue that earlier national histories ignored it. The more careful historical position is that both Europe's internal development and its imperial relationships mattered, and that they cannot be separated.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Diaspora, Imperial Memory, and Belonging from Antiquity to the Present

What Changed

  • The principal form of political membership shifted from imperial and dynastic allegiance, and from religious or legal status, toward national citizenship in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries
  • The scale and speed of movement increased with steamships, railways, and states able to regulate borders and populations
  • Formal overseas empires largely disappeared between 1945 and 1975, but their populations, languages, and legal ties remained
  • Public memory of empire changed from celebration to contested reassessment, with new emphasis on slavery, conquest, and colonial violence

What Stayed the Same

  • Imperial systems consistently produced population movement, ranging from voluntary migration to enslavement and expulsion
  • Legal distinctions between categories of people persisted, from Roman provincials to colonial subjects and, in modified form, contemporary immigrants
  • Diasporic networks continued to rely on religion, kinship, commerce, and print to sustain connections across borders
  • Questions about who counts as a member of the community remained contested

Who experienced change most directly: Enslaved people, conquered communities, expelled minorities, refugees, and colonized peoples experienced the most abrupt changes, including loss of home, status, and often life. Who experienced greater continuity: Elite groups in metropoles and settler communities often retained wealth and institutions across regime changes, though settlers in decolonizing territories, such as Algeria and Angola, faced sudden loss.

Why the pattern matters: The long view shows that debates about belonging in contemporary Europe are not new, and that imperial history and migration are continuous with earlier patterns of incorporation, hierarchy, and diversity. It also warns against assuming that the nation-state was the natural or permanent framework of European life.

The chapter's argument can now be stated directly. Europe's imperial history was not simply the history of European states expanding outward. It was also the history of populations, resources, institutions, and cultural relationships moving across imperial boundaries and transforming Europe from within. Empire and migration were therefore not external episodes in European history. They were integral to the formation and repeated redefinition of European societies, and any account of what Europe has been must include the people who were moved, the people who were governed, and the people who moved themselves.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how empire, colonialism, migration, and diaspora shaped European societies and ideas of belonging across more than two thousand years. Its central argument is that empires organized unequal political power over diverse populations, that imperial systems generated and redirected human movement under conditions ranging from opportunity to coercion, and that the resulting encounters, settlements, and dispersals transformed both the societies Europeans ruled and Europe itself. The chapter treated empire as a family of related political forms, not a single European invention, and distinguished empire, imperialism, colonialism, settler colonialism, migration, forced displacement, diaspora, and postcolonial society as analytically separate though overlapping concepts.

In antiquity, the Roman Empire provided an early example of incorporation through military service, veteran settlement, provincial elites, and the gradual extension of citizenship, culminating in the edict of 212 CE. The evidence of military diplomas, the Vindolanda tablets, and inscriptions shows how movement and legal change converted provincials into Romans, while slavery and legal hierarchy persisted. Roman citizenship illustrates the central distinction between imperial membership and equality. In the centuries after the fragmentation of western Roman rule, the Byzantine Empire, the Carolingians, and the Holy Roman Empire claimed Roman inheritance in different ways, and their histories show resettlement, conquest, conversion, and frontier colonization, but should not be treated as incomplete nation-states.

The medieval Mediterranean connected Byzantine, Muslim, and Latin Christian systems through trade, conquest, and the movement of religious communities. Muslim rule in Iberia and Sicily, Latin conquests, the Cairo Geniza documents, Jewish and Armenian networks, and Italian commercial colonies show that diaspora is a continuing relationship across space and not simply a past relocation, and that conquest inside Europe produced displacement and expulsion. Mediterranean sugar plantations and the trade in enslaved people prepared some features of later Atlantic empire, though historians debate how direct the connection was.

From the fifteenth century, dynastic composite monarchies, continental land empires, and maritime commercial empires organized authority in distinct ways. The Habsburg, Spanish, and Polish-Lithuanian realms governed through negotiated relations among territories with their own laws, and dynastic allegiance differed from later national citizenship. The Ottoman, Habsburg, and Russian empires created borderlands in which conquest, resettlement, deportation, religious administration, and colonization continually remade populations. Portuguese and Spanish expansion in the Atlantic, followed by chartered Dutch, English, and other companies, created overseas systems that combined conquest, missions, forced labor, and commerce, with local intermediaries and indigenous societies as active participants.

Settler colonialism, as an analytical category, distinguishes colonization aimed at permanent settlement and control of land from extractive colonialism, and the chapter compared Spanish, British, French, Portuguese, and later settler societies, noting that their relations with indigenous peoples, enslaved labor, and metropolitan authorities differed. The Atlantic slave trade, which carried more than twelve million Africans across the ocean according to modern estimates, was the largest forced migration of the early modern period. It was organized by European states, merchants, and financiers and by African and American participants in different roles, and it produced an African diaspora whose resistance, creativity, and survival shaped the Americas and connected to European politics through abolitionism, racial ideology, and the long legacy of slave-derived wealth.

European emigration moved tens of millions overseas, in circumstances from famine and persecution to ambition and state encouragement, and created diasporas that preserved, transformed, or abandoned inherited identities. Many migrants benefited from settler colonial systems that dispossessed indigenous people and excluded others. Empire in turn transformed Europe, through ports, finance, consumption, state institutions, science, and political debate. The scale of its contribution to industrialization is disputed, with interpretations ranging from emphasis on slavery and colonial markets as central drivers to emphasis on domestic factors, and with positions that join the two. The evidence is fragmentary, and a strong claim in either direction is difficult to sustain.

In the nineteenth century, industrial technology, commercial and strategic rivalry, and ideologies of civilization produced a new phase of expansion and the partition of Africa. Racial classification, scientific racism, and legal categories distinguished colonial subjects, settlers, and citizens, while colonial states combined direct and indirect rule, concessions, forced labor, and taxation, and depended on indigenous intermediaries and soldiers. Colonized peoples resisted through armed struggle, petitions, strikes, religious movements, and nationalist organization, often using the language of rights that empires proclaimed. The primary sources examined, from the Berlin Act to the Vietnamese demands of 1919 and the United Nations Declaration of 1960, show how claims about legitimate authority shifted, while illustrating the limits of any single document. Migration in the age of empire included labor movements, Jewish emigration from eastern Europe, deportations and refugee flows in the Ottoman and Russian borderlands, political exile, and the formation of transnational communities.

The First World War destroyed the Habsburg, Ottoman, Romanov, and German imperial orders and produced new states, minorities, refugees, and population exchanges, while overseas empires continued under mandates and other arrangements. National self-determination was applied unevenly. The Second World War, Nazi conquest and genocide, wartime deportations, and postwar expulsions produced the largest population movements in modern European history and redrew borders and demographic maps. Comparison between Nazi racial empire and earlier colonial systems has been useful for analyzing racial hierarchy and conquest, but the Nazi project of extermination and the specific history of the Holocaust should not be equated with colonial rule.

Decolonization between the 1940s and 1970s followed different paths in the British, French, Dutch, Belgian, and Portuguese empires, shaped by anticolonial movements, wars, settlers, metropolitan politics, and international pressure. It was accompanied by migrations of settlers and by movements of workers and others from former colonies to Europe, which created multigenerational communities and debates over citizenship, discrimination, and national identity. Today, Jewish, African, Armenian, Roma, Muslim, postcolonial, and European emigrant diasporas shape European societies, and public debates about monuments, museums, restitution, and migration show that imperial legacies remain active. The chapter has distinguished established historical evidence from political memory and institutional claims. Its principal conclusion is that empire and migration were integral to the making and remaking of European societies, and that European identity has been formed through relationships with the populations, institutions, and ideas that imperialism and movement brought into Europe, a theme that leads into the social history of Chapter 143.

Policies, practices, and ideologies that extend or sustain imperial power, including conquest, commercial pressure, and informal influence.
Colonialism
External political domination of a territory and its people, often combined with settlement, extraction of resources, and institutional subordination.
Settler colonialism
Colonization in which migrants establish permanent communities and seek control of indigenous lands, as in North America, Australia, Algeria, and southern Africa.
Colonia
In the Roman Empire, a settlement of citizens, often veterans, founded in conquered territory, and not a dependency in the modern colonial sense.
Auxiliary
A Roman soldier recruited from non-citizen provincials who received citizenship on honorable discharge, illustrating incorporation through service.
Constitutio Antoniniana
The edict of 212 CE, associated with Caracalla, extending Roman citizenship to most free inhabitants of the empire.
Ostsiedlung
The medieval eastward settlement of German-speaking migrants and the spread of German-law towns into Slavic and Baltic lands.
Diaspora
A dispersed population that maintains or develops relationships and identifications among its different locations over time.
Forced displacement
Removal or flight caused by coercion, violence, or persecution, including deportation, expulsion, enslavement, and flight from war.
Composite monarchy
A realm of several territories under one ruler, each keeping its own laws, assemblies, and privileges, as in the Spanish and Habsburg monarchies.
Military Frontier
The Habsburg border zone against the Ottoman Empire, settled by soldier-peasants, many of them Orthodox Christians, in return for military service.
Pale of Settlement
The western region of the Russian Empire in which Jewish residence was largely restricted from the late eighteenth century until 1917.
Encomienda
A Spanish colonial grant assigning the tribute and labor of indigenous communities to a Spanish holder, nominally in exchange for protection and instruction.
Chartered company
A corporation holding state-granted monopoly and sometimes sovereign powers, such as the Dutch and English East India Companies.
Informal empire
Imperial influence exercised through trade, finance, and diplomatic pressure without formal annexation.
Middle Passage
The transatlantic voyage that carried enslaved Africans to the Americas, marked by high mortality and extreme violence.
Indentured labor
A system of fixed-term contracted labor, used by European colonies for European, Indian, and Chinese workers, often under coercive conditions.
Chain migration
A pattern in which migrants follow relatives and neighbors, forming networks that direct settlement and sustain communities.
Scientific racism
Nineteenth-century theories claiming to demonstrate biological hierarchies among human groups, used to justify imperial rule and exclusion.
Indigénat
The French colonial legal regime that subjected colonized people to special penalties and restrictions, introduced in Algeria in 1881.
Indirect rule
Colonial government exercised through existing local rulers and institutions under European supervision.
Civilizing mission
The imperial claim that European rule brought progress, law, education, and religion to colonized peoples.
Mandate
A League of Nations arrangement under which Britain, France, and other powers administered former German and Ottoman territories after 1919.
Population exchange
A negotiated or imposed transfer of populations between states, exemplified by the Greek-Turkish exchange authorized at Lausanne in 1923.
Displaced persons
People uprooted by the Second World War and its aftermath, including forced laborers and survivors, who were housed in camps and repatriated or resettled after 1945.
Decolonization
The process by which colonial territories achieved independence or changed political status, through negotiation, conflict, or both.
Retornados and pieds-noirs
Settlers and others who relocated to Portugal and France respectively after the independence of their colonies, in the mid-1970s and in 1962.
Postcolonial society
A society shaped by the historical consequences and continuing relationships of colonial rule, whether in a former colony or a former metropole.
Restitution
The return of cultural objects or other property taken in colonial or wartime contexts, a major issue in contemporary museum debates.

Major People and Places

Identification & Significance

Augustus
First Roman emperor, whose veteran colonies and provincial settlement policies established patterns of imperial incorporation.
Caracalla
Roman emperor traditionally credited with the edict of 212 CE extending citizenship, though his motives remain debated.
Vindolanda
Fort on the northern frontier of Roman Britain whose writing tablets document soldiers' and households' lives around 90 to 120 CE.
Charlemagne
Frankish king crowned emperor in 800, whose conquest of Saxony involved deportation and forced conversion.
Roger II of Sicily
Norman king, crowned in 1130, whose court used Greek, Arabic, and Latin and governed a multilingual realm.
The Cairo Geniza
Collection of medieval documents from a Fustat synagogue that illuminates Jewish and Mediterranean commerce, correspondence, and law.
Mehmed II
Ottoman sultan who captured Constantinople in 1453 and resettled populations to repopulate the city.
Charles V
Habsburg ruler whose composite possessions in Europe and the Americas exemplified dynastic empire.
Bartolomé de las Casas
Dominican friar who criticized Spanish treatment of indigenous peoples and influenced debates over colonial law.
Afonso I of Kongo
African Christian king whose 1526 letters to Portugal protested the enslavement of his subjects.
The Dutch East India Company (VOC)
Chartered company founded in 1602 that exercised commercial and governing powers across the Indian Ocean and Indonesia.
Plassey and the diwani
The 1757 battle and the 1765 grant of revenue rights through which the English East India Company became a territorial power in Bengal.
Saint-Domingue and Haiti
French Caribbean colony where an enslaved majority revolted from 1791, producing independent Haiti in 1804.
Olaudah Equiano
Formerly enslaved author whose 1789 narrative became an influential abolitionist text, though details of his early life are debated.
Berlin Conference, 1884 to 1885
Meeting of European powers whose General Act set rules for recognizing claims in Africa, without African participation.
Leopold II of Belgium
King who ruled the Congo Free State as a personal possession until its transfer to Belgium in 1908.
Dadabhai Naoroji
Indian political leader elected to the British House of Commons in 1892 who criticized the economic effects of British rule.
Nguyen Ai Quoc
Name under which the 1919 Vietnamese demands were issued, later associated with Ho Chi Minh.
Empire Windrush
Ship that brought Caribbean migrants to Britain in June 1948, now a symbol of postwar migration from the empire.
The Pale of Settlement
Region of the Russian Empire where most Jews were required to live, and a major origin of emigration after 1881.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that European history cannot be understood only from within the borders of European states. Rome, Byzantium, the dynastic monarchies, the Ottoman, Habsburg, and Russian empires, and the Atlantic and Asian commercial and colonial systems each brought together populations that were unequal in law and power, and each generated movement that changed the societies it touched. The chapter has revealed a continuous theme: the question of who belongs, and on what terms, was answered differently by different systems, and the answers were contested by those whom they excluded.

The chapter also shows how the histories examined in earlier volumes converge. The Roman citizenship of Volume I, the medieval diasporas and frontier societies of Volume II, the maritime empires and Atlantic slavery of Volume III, the industrial and imperial expansion of Volume IV, and the wars, expulsions, and genocides of Volume V all contributed to the demographic and political landscape of Volume VI. The legal, economic, and cultural relationships created by empire shaped postwar reconstruction, migration, and the politics of identity, and they continue to shape debates in contemporary Europe. These developments were not inevitable outcomes, and they depended on specific decisions, conflicts, and resistance.

Within Part XXI, the chapter extends the analysis of states, law, and sovereignty in Chapter 141 by asking who was governed, displaced, or transformed by imperial power. It prepares the way for Chapter 143 by showing how labor, family, status, and gender were bound up with imperial hierarchy and with migration, and it leaves to Chapter 144 the larger question of Europe's place in the world. The chapter's contribution to the argument of the whole textbook is to show that Europe's diversity and its connections to other regions were not additions to its history but part of its structure.

Finally, the chapter invites humility about interpretation. The evidence for imperial and migratory history is uneven, many of the people it concerns left few records, and the debates about empire's economic effects, about the scale of population movements, and about public memory remain open. Students who learn to distinguish documented facts from estimates, and evidence from political argument, are better prepared to understand both the past and the arguments about it in the present. Chapter 143 now turns to the social structures within which people lived and worked, and to the transformations of class, gender, family, and labor that empire and migration helped to shape.
