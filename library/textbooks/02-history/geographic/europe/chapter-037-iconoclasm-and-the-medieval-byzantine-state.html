Iconoclasm and the Medieval Byzantine State

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In the apse of the church of Hagia Eirene in Constantinople, where later Byzantine churches would place a figure of the Virgin or Christ, a large plain cross of dark tesserae stands against a gold ground. It is among the few monumental survivals of an age whose visual culture was deliberately reshaped, and it stands for a period in which the Roman state was rebuilt while its Christians argued over what could, and could not, be depicted in sacred space.

The church of Hagia Eirene, "Holy Peace," stood beside Hagia Sophia and had been damaged by an earthquake that struck Constantinople in the early 740s. It was rebuilt, and its apse received a monumental cross, a design usually attributed by art historians to the reign of the emperor Constantine V. A visitor to the church in the later eighth century, looking toward the altar, would have seen no figure of Christ, the Virgin, or the saints in that position. The cross is a small piece of surviving evidence, but it points toward a much larger story: a half century of imperial religious policy, a generation of bitter controversy among bishops, monks, soldiers, and officials, and a longer process, running beneath the controversy, in which a battered Roman state learned how to feed, tax, defend, and govern a smaller territory.

Chapter 36 traced the contraction of the Roman Empire between the reign of Justinian and the failed Arab siege of Constantinople in 717 and 718. By the end of that chapter the empire had lost Egypt, Syria, Palestine, and much of North Africa, most of the Balkan interior had passed out of imperial control, and Italy was only partially held. What survived was a state centered on Constantinople, Anatolia, Thrace, parts of Greece and the Aegean, and scattered western possessions. Chapter 36 explained how that state survived. This chapter explains what it became. Survival in 718 was not recovery. The emergency arrangements of the seventh century, regional military commands, shifting fiscal practices, and the heavy reliance on the emperor as the center of Christian Roman legitimacy, had to be turned into durable institutions, and that process unfolded across roughly the century and a quarter between 717 and 843.

The central thread of the chapter is the controversy over sacred images, usually called Iconoclasm, from Greek words meaning "image breaking." The controversy shaped the imperial court, the church, and the monasteries from the 720s or 730s until 843, with an intermission between 787 and 815. It is a famous episode, and it has often been told as a dramatic story of fanatical emperors and heroic monks. Modern scholarship has made that version much harder to sustain. Almost all of the surviving narrative evidence was written or reshaped by the eventual iconophile victors, the supporters of images, who had every reason to blacken their opponents. This chapter therefore treats the image controversy as a genuine religious and political conflict, but also as a problem of evidence that students must learn to read critically.

Iconoclasm was not the whole of the period, however, and the chapter argues that it should not be allowed to crowd out everything else. In the same decades, the empire developed the military districts later called themes, created elite regiments called tagmata, collected taxes from rural communities whose organization is only dimly visible in our sources, endured a catastrophic plague in 746 and 747, fought Arabs, Bulgars, and rebels, and watched a woman, Irene, rule as regent and then in her own name. Historians now generally reject the older picture of the eighth century as a "Dark Age" of cultural and institutional collapse, while also recognizing how fragmentary the evidence is and how severe the pressures were. Cambridge-level surveys frequently describe the period as a "state of emergency," a phrase that captures both the danger and the creativity of the age.

Students will meet several kinds of evidence in this chapter. Chronicles, above all that of Theophanes the Confessor, supply a narrative skeleton, but Theophanes wrote as a committed iconophile in the early ninth century and used dating conventions that have to be corrected by modern scholars. Church councils, notably Hieria in 754 and Nicaea in 787, preserve theological argument, though the earlier council is known chiefly through the quotations of its opponents. Saints' lives, law codes, seals, coins, church buildings, and the letters and treatises of figures such as John of Damascus, Patriarch Nikephoros, and Theodore of Stoudios add other layers. Almost none of this material is neutral. Part of the work of the chapter is to show how historians reconstruct a past from sources written by people who thought they knew how the story had to end.

Within Part VI, this chapter occupies the pivotal middle position. Chapter 36 described contraction and survival. Chapter 37 describes consolidation and the recovery of capacity. Chapter 38 will then examine how a Christian Roman state with renewed institutional confidence became a major transmitter of Christianity, literacy, and liturgy into the Slavic world. Within Volume II, the chapter belongs to the parallel story of eastern Christendom, which must be read alongside the formation of Latin Christendom in Part V rather than as a footnote to it. The Franks, the papacy, and the Lombards appear in these pages only where their histories intersect with imperial policy, and the longer story of Latin and Greek divergence belongs to Chapter 40.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why survival in 718 did not by itself amount to recovery, and describe the territorial and fiscal condition of the empire after the siege of Constantinople.
  2. Evaluate what historians can and cannot know about the religious policy of Leo III, and explain why the traditional story of the Chalke image is disputed.
  3. Distinguish among the theological concepts at issue in the icon controversy, including image, prototype, veneration, and worship.
  4. Trace the institutionalization of Iconoclasm under Constantine V, including the Council of Hieria in 754, and explain the relationship between emperor and church.
  5. Assess the problem of persecution, explaining which groups were targeted, how later monastic writers shaped the record, and why simple narratives of universal monastic resistance are inadequate.
  6. Describe the development of themes and tagmata and explain why historians no longer describe themes as hereditary soldier estates created by Heraclius.
  7. Analyze rural society, village communities, land tenure, and taxation, using the Farmer's Law with appropriate caution about its date and representativeness.
  8. Distinguish among state stabilization, population recovery, and economic recovery, and place the plague of 746 and 747 within that analysis.
  9. Compare the iconophile restoration of 787 with the permanent restoration of 843, explaining why 787 was an intermission rather than an ending.
  10. Interpret the reign of Nikephoros I as an example of both state consolidation and the reversibility of recovery, with attention to the bias of Theophanes.
  11. Contextualize Byzantine developments within relations with the Abbasid caliphate, the Bulgars, the Franks, and the papacy without turning those relations into separate national histories.
  12. Evaluate competing explanations for the origins of Iconoclasm and for Byzantine recovery, and explain why neither can be reduced to a single cause.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What can historians securely say about the origins of Iconoclasm, given that almost all narrative sources were shaped by its opponents?
  3. In what ways was the icon controversy a theological dispute, and in what ways was it a dispute about imperial authority, the church, and the organization of holy power?

  8. Why was the restoration of images in 843 more durable than the restoration of 787?

Historical Context

The empire that emerged from the siege of 717 and 718 was the product of roughly a century of shocks. Persian and Arab conquests in the 610s, 620s, and 630s had stripped away the wealthiest eastern provinces. Slavic settlement and Avar pressure had transformed the Balkan interior, while the Bulgars established a state south of the Danube late in the seventh century, an episode examined from the Bulgarian side in Chapter 39. In Italy, the Lombards held much of the peninsula, and Byzantine authority survived in places such as Ravenna, Rome, Naples, Venice, Sicily, and Calabria, though in increasingly local forms. The state that governed what remained, centered on Constantinople, had a smaller tax base, a more regional military system, and a much narrower territorial core than the empire of Justinian, as Chapter 36 explained.

The people of this state called themselves Romans. They spoke Greek in the administrative and ecclesiastical center, though Armenian, Syriac, Slavic, Latin, and other languages were heard in different regions, and they regarded their emperor as the legitimate successor of Augustus and Constantine. The label "Byzantine" is a convention of later Western scholarship, reflecting the ancient name of the city on the Bosporus. It is used in this textbook because it is the standard modern term for the medieval Roman state centered on Constantinople, but students should remember that contemporaries did not think of themselves as members of a new empire or a separate "Byzantine nation." The term Roman, in their usage, carried claims about law, Christian imperial legitimacy, and a continuous political order.

Religious life was inseparable from imperial legitimacy. The emperor was understood as God's appointed ruler, responsible for the welfare of the Christian Roman people, and the defeats of the seventh century raised painful questions about divine favor. Several earlier controversies had already shaped imperial involvement in doctrine, including the Christological disputes treated in Chapter 26 and the debates over the will of Christ associated with the reign of Heraclius and his successors. Emperors had convened councils, appointed patriarchs, and promulgated doctrinal formulas, and few Byzantines considered such actions improper in principle, although they disagreed about particular decisions. The question of sacred images arose within a world in which the emperor, the patriarch of Constantinople, bishops, monks, and the people of the capital all claimed some voice in religious matters.

Veneration of holy images had also been growing. Painted panels of Christ, the Virgin, and the saints, mosaics in churches, and cherished objects believed to preserve a miraculous connection with their prototypes had become important in private and public devotion during the sixth and seventh centuries. Some images were credited with protecting cities and armies. Other Christians, including some bishops in Anatolia, felt that the practice had gone too far. The seventh century also saw Christian communities living under Muslim rule and Jewish communities in both Byzantine and Muslim lands, all of whom had their own debates about images and sacred representation. These wider contexts are part of the background, although they are not a sufficient explanation, as the Historical Debate feature below explains.

Finally, students should recognize the unusual character of the evidence. For the eighth century, the Byzantine historical record is thin. Contemporary historical writing by authors who lived through the reigns of Leo III and Constantine V has largely not survived. The main narrative sources, the Chronographia of Theophanes the Confessor and the Breviarion of Patriarch Nikephoros, were composed around 810 to 815 and draw on earlier materials, some of which were lost, and both authors were convinced iconophiles. Theophanes arranged events by "Anno Mundi" years, counted from the supposed creation of the world, and his year numbers do not always line up with the true sequence of events, so that modern historians correct him with other evidence. This textbook uses BCE and CE dates throughout and flags the cases in which the chronology is uncertain. Beyond chronicles, historians rely on coins, seals, church buildings, law codes, hagiography, theological treatises, and papal and Arab sources that give outside perspectives. The resulting picture is necessarily partial.

Section 37.1

The Empire after 718

When the Arab fleet and army withdrew from Constantinople in 718, the empire's new emperor, Leo III, had held the throne for little more than a year. Leo had been the military commander, or strategos, of the Anatolikon, one of the large regional commands of central Anatolia, and he had used that position to seize power from Theodosios III in 717 after a period of rapid imperial turnover. Later tradition, reflected in Theophanes, gave Leo a Syrian origin, associated with Germanikeia in the eastern frontier zone, and the name "Isaurian dynasty," still seen in older books, rests on a mistaken later identification. Modern historians often prefer to speak of the dynasty of Leo III without insisting on a precise ethnic or regional label. The empire he inherited had survived the greatest threat of the era, but it was still fragile.

Territorially, the state rested on three central pillars. The first was Constantinople itself, with its walls, harbors, fleet, and court, still the largest city of the Christian world and the center of imperial ceremony and administration. The second was Anatolia, a large peninsula whose upland plateau, river valleys, and coasts supplied food, manpower, horses, and taxes. The third consisted of surviving Balkan and Aegean possessions, including Thrace, the coastal regions around Thessalonica, parts of Greece and the Peloponnese, and the islands and coasts of the Aegean. These regions are sketched geographically in Chapter 2 and in the preceding chapter, and the Map Study later in this chapter asks students to trace their approximate limits. To the west, Sicily, Calabria, parts of Apulia, the duchies of Naples, Rome, and Venice, and the exarchate of Ravenna constituted a shifting and increasingly autonomous western periphery. The exarchate fell to the Lombards in 751, which shows how thinly central power could be stretched.

The strains of the situation were evident. Arab armies raided Anatolia nearly every year in the so-called summer campaigns, and the plateau and its fortified places experienced repeated destruction. Border zones were depopulated or exchanged hands, and the empire's response was typically to avoid pitched battle, fortify central places, and strike back when raiders were encumbered or divided. Leo III's victory at Akroinon in 740 is often cited as a sign that the empire could still win significant engagements, though the exact scale of the victory is difficult to establish from the surviving evidence. In the Balkans, the Bulgar khanate was emerging as a major power, and Slavic communities had settled across much of the peninsula, with varied degrees of dependence on imperial authority. The Bulgar khan Tervel, who had helped Leo during the siege of 718 according to our sources, remained a neighbor with his own agenda and interests. In the northern Aegean and the Mediterranean, Arab naval power continued to pose a threat to islands and coasts.

The fiscal and manpower base had shrunk severely. The loss of Egypt, Syria, and North Africa had removed the grain and tax revenue that had sustained the earlier empire. The state now depended on the produce, labor, and revenue of a smaller number of regions, many of them rural and many of them exposed to raiding. The military commands that had developed in response to this crisis, discussed more fully in Section 37.6, were organized on regional lines. This regionalization brought flexibility and local knowledge, but it also gave provincial commanders substantial independent power, and in the decades after 718 emperors repeatedly faced the problem of commanders who might become rivals. In short, the empire at the opening of this chapter had survived by improvisation. The task of the following century was to convert improvisation into a stable system.

That task had to be accomplished without abandoning the ideology that justified the state. The emperor continued to claim the full authority of a Roman ruler and the particular responsibility of a Christian ruler for the faith and welfare of his people. Imperial ceremonial, taxation, law, and the court in Constantinople provided continuity with the earlier Roman empire. The state also drew strength from the memory of its survival. After the failure of the Arab siege in 717 and 718, the Virgin was increasingly credited with protecting the city, a belief that made the later quarrel over holy images especially charged, since the defense of the capital had become tied to sacred protection. It is against this background of fragile survival, expensive defense, and strong religious expectations that the first signs of imperial hostility toward certain images must be read.

Historical Vocabulary
Roman and Byzantine

Byzantine is the conventional modern term for the medieval Roman state centered on Constantinople. It derives from Byzantion, the older Greek city on the site, and came into general use in Western scholarship centuries after the empire's end. The people who lived in the empire called themselves Romans (Romaioi) and their state the Roman empire, and Western Europeans, after the coronation of Charlemagne in 800 examined in Chapter 31, increasingly called them "Greeks." This textbook uses "Byzantine" as a convenient scholarly label while insisting that the empire was Roman in law, ideology, and self-understanding. The word should not be used to imply that a new people or civilization suddenly replaced Rome in the seventh or eighth century.

Section 37.2

Leo III, Imperial Authority, and the Beginnings of Iconoclasm

The standard older account of the origins of Iconoclasm can be summarized in a single sentence: a volcanic eruption in the Aegean, perhaps combined with Arab victories, persuaded Leo III that God was punishing the empire for idolatry, and in 726 he ordered the removal of the image of Christ over the Chalke Gate of the imperial palace, provoking riots in Constantinople and beginning a general campaign against images. Almost every element of that sentence has been questioned. This section therefore does not present the traditional story as established fact. It asks instead what historians can actually know.

The evidence for Leo's religious policy is poor. Contemporary or near-contemporary sources that describe his actions in detail are scarce. The accounts of Theophanes and Patriarch Nikephoros, both written long afterward by iconophiles, present Leo as the origin of the heresy, and Theophanes places a volcanic eruption at Thera in the mid-720s and connects it with the emperor's change of mind. Other stories developed later still, including accounts of the removal of the Chalke image, the killing of a group of people who protested, and the forced resignation of Patriarch Germanos in 730. Some of these narratives are detailed and vivid, which is part of the difficulty: later authors had strong incentives to supply an origin story with a clear villain. Some historians argue that the Chalke episode, in the form we have it, is largely a later literary construction, and that if an image above the gate was removed, it may have occurred at a different date and in a less dramatic way. The letters ascribed to Leo and to Pope Gregory II concerning images are also treated with suspicion by most specialists, since they survive in a problematic textual tradition.

What can be said with greater confidence is that concern about religious images was present in parts of the Christian world around 700, and that at some point during his reign Leo III took a stand against some uses of images. Several bishops in Anatolia, including Constantine of Nakoleia and Thomas of Claudiopolis, are associated in our sources with early criticism of image veneration, and they are among the figures whom iconophile sources portray as influencing the emperor. Germanos of Constantinople, the patriarch, defended images and, according to tradition, left office in 730. Modern scholarship tends to see in the reign of Leo III a period of growing controversy rather than a single decisive campaign, with imperial measures that were probably limited, local, or rhetorical in the early stages. Evidence for aggressive, systematic destruction of images under Leo III is thin when compared with what later writers claimed.

Leo's reign was in any case occupied with other priorities. He is credited with strengthening military defenses, defeating the Arabs at Akroinon in 740, and issuing, together with his son Constantine, a short legal handbook known as the Ekloga, usually dated to about 741 although a date in the 720s has also been argued. The Ekloga adapted earlier Roman legal material to the conditions of a smaller, more rural empire, with emphasis on family law, property, and punishments that often favored mutilation over death. Its preface speaks in a strongly Christian idiom about the emperor's duty to justice. The document is a reminder that the imperial government of the 720s and 730s was concerned with law, fiscal administration, and defense as much as with religious policy.

Leo's relations with the West and the papacy further complicate the picture. Popes of the period, including Gregory II and Gregory III, are portrayed in later sources as opposing imperial policy on images and taxation. Some later writers claim that Leo responded by transferring the dioceses of Sicily, Calabria, and Illyricum from papal to Constantinopolitan jurisdiction, but that transfer is poorly dated and has been much debated. For the purposes of this chapter, what matters is that tensions between Rome and Constantinople were real, that they involved fiscal and jurisdictional disputes as well as theology, and that the Lombard conquest of Ravenna in 751 and the papacy's growing turn toward the Frankish rulers shifted the political context. These themes are developed in Chapter 40 and should not be read here as a straight road to 1054.

The central question of this section can therefore be stated simply: what can we actually know about Leo III's religious policy? We can say that he is associated by later tradition with the beginning of the controversy, that he probably expressed hostility to certain kinds of image veneration, that some of his bishops did the same, and that the patriarch Germanos opposed him. We cannot confidently say that he launched a systematic empire-wide campaign of destruction in 726. The responsible conclusion is that Leo III's reign was a formative period for a controversy whose institutional form emerged later, under his son.

Section 37.3

What Was the Icon Controversy About?

Before returning to the political narrative, students need a clear picture of the theology, because the controversy cannot be understood without it. The Greek word eikon means "image," and in Byzantine usage it covered a wide range of representations, including painted panels, mosaics, wall paintings, small portable objects, and images stamped on coins or seals. An icon of Christ, the Virgin, or a saint was not merely decoration. For many believers it was a point of contact with the holy person depicted, kissed, lit with candles, carried in procession, and invoked in need. This was the practice that the controversy placed in question.

Supporters of images are called iconophiles ("lovers of images") or iconodules, while their opponents are called iconoclasts ("image breakers") or, in the polemical Greek term used by their enemies, iconomachs ("image fighters"). All these terms carry the biases of the sources that produced them. The iconoclasts did not call themselves image breakers, and they regarded their position as the defense of true Christian worship. They accused their opponents of idolatry, and their opponents accused them of heresy. Historians must therefore take care when using these labels not to adopt the vocabulary of one side as though it were neutral.

The central iconophile argument rested on a distinction between the image and its prototype, the person represented. The honor shown to an image, in this view, passed to the one depicted. Iconophile writers such as John of Damascus, Patriarch Nikephoros, and Theodore of Stoudios distinguished between proskynesis, a relative veneration that could be offered to created things such as the cross, the gospel book, saints, and images, and latreia, the absolute worship owed to God alone. Kissing an icon of Christ was therefore not worship of paint and wood, but an act of respect directed to Christ. The deeper argument was Christological. Because God had become human in the incarnation, he had become visible, and it was legitimate to depict the human Christ. To refuse to depict him, Theodore of Stoudios argued, was in effect to deny the reality of the incarnation.

Iconoclast arguments, as far as we can reconstruct them from the hostile reports preserved in later iconophile texts, were more varied than the polemics suggest. They included the scriptural prohibition against making images of God, the fear that veneration of created matter amounted to idolatry, and Christological arguments that images of Christ either attempted to depict the divine nature, which cannot be circumscribed, or depicted only the human nature and thereby separated it from the divine, a dilemma which iconoclasts associated with ancient heresies. Constantine V's theological writings, which are lost, are known chiefly through the refutations of Patriarch Nikephoros. The council of 754, discussed below, is reported to have argued that the true image of Christ is the Eucharist, a position that iconophiles contested. These arguments should be taken seriously as the arguments of Christians who believed they were defending the faith.

It is also essential to avoid the caricature of iconoclasts as people who "hated art." Crosses remained central, and the cross in the apse of Hagia Eirene shows how imperial patrons could commission monumental decoration in an iconoclast idiom. Decorative and secular imagery, including scenes of hunting, chariot races, and imperial portraits, continued to be produced. Coins under iconoclast emperors showed the emperor and his heirs rather than Christ, and the image of Christ, which had appeared on gold coinage in the late seventh century, returned to imperial coins only after the final restoration. Recent scholarship has stressed the continued importance of imperial images during the period, which shows that the issue was not representation as such but the proper object and form of sacred representation. The dispute was about what holy images could be, what they did, and who controlled the sacred.

Historical Vocabulary
Icon, Iconoclast, and Iconophile

An icon is a sacred image, most often a painted panel, mosaic, or other representation of Christ, the Virgin, or a saint, used in worship and private devotion. An iconoclast (from Greek eikon, image, and klaein, to break) opposed the veneration of such images, and an iconophile or iconodule defended it. These terms are partly products of polemic, since most of the surviving writing about the controversy was produced by supporters of images, and they should be used with awareness of their origin. The term "iconoclasm" is sometimes used for the whole period c. 726 to 843, though historians prefer to speak of a first iconoclast period (to 787) and a second (815 to 843), separated by the iconophile intermission of the intervening decades. Modern English "iconoclast," meaning someone who attacks cherished beliefs, derives from this controversy and should not be confused with its original religious meaning.

Historical Debate

What Caused Byzantine Iconoclasm?

The question: Why did a controversy over sacred images erupt in the Byzantine empire in the early eighth century, and why did emperors adopt it as policy?

Interpretation A, religious and theological developments: Some scholars emphasize long-term changes in Christian practice. During the sixth and seventh centuries, the cult of holy images, relics, and saints had grown rapidly, and with it came anxieties about idolatry, about the status of matter, and about the proper limits of mediation between humans and the divine. In this view, Iconoclasm was a genuine reform movement, and the arguments of iconoclast bishops should be taken at face value.
Interpretation B, imperial authority and crisis: Other historians stress the political context. Defeats and disasters raised questions about divine favor, and emperors may have sought to restore it by purifying worship. Iconoclasm also gave emperors a way to assert control over the church, over powerful holy men and holy images, and over the competing sources of sacred authority that threatened the throne. In this view, imperial policy was inseparable from the crisis of the seventh and early eighth centuries.
Interpretation C, wider religious interaction: A third line of inquiry considers whether contact with Jewish and Muslim traditions of aniconism, or reports of an edict against images attributed to the caliph Yazid II, influenced Byzantine opinion. Some medieval Byzantine writers already claimed that Leo copied Muslim practice. Modern scholarship generally treats this as a polemical explanation and not a sufficient one. Christian communities at the eastern frontier, including those of Anatolia and Armenia, and Christian critics of images within the empire, are at least as important as any direct Islamic influence.

Evidence: The debate rests on the same limited evidence: later iconophile chronicles, council acts, theological treatises, a handful of surviving artifacts such as the Hagia Eirene cross, and a very few contemporary or near-contemporary external sources. Because evidence is scarce, historians often work by comparing likelihoods, and no single explanation has been demonstrated.

Why it matters: How historians explain the origins of Iconoclasm affects how they judge the emperors, the church, and the broader religious culture of the empire. Obsolete explanations, such as the claim that Leo simply copied Islam, mislead students into treating a complex Christian controversy as an external import. The strongest conclusion is that Iconoclasm had no single securely demonstrated cause.

Section 37.4

Constantine V and the Institutionalization of Iconoclasm

If Leo III's role in the origins of Iconoclasm is uncertain, Constantine V's is much better documented. Born in 718, the son of Leo III and his wife Maria, Constantine succeeded his father in June 741. He was almost immediately challenged by Artabasdos, his brother-in-law and the commander of the Opsikion theme, who seized Constantinople with the support of regional troops and held it for roughly two years, from 741 to 743. According to the sources, Artabasdos restored the veneration of images in the city, which indicates that image questions were already entangled with political rivalry, although we should be careful about the motives of a usurper seeking allies. Constantine eventually retook the capital, defeated and blinded Artabasdos, and turned to consolidating his power.

The war against Artabasdos revealed a structural problem, and Constantine's response to it shaped his reign. The regional military commands, especially Opsikion, the large district closest to the capital, could provide the manpower for a challenger. Constantine reduced the power of such commands by dividing Opsikion, and he strengthened the elite regiments based in and near the capital, as Section 37.6 explains. His military record was also strong by the standards of the age. In the 740s and 750s he took the offensive against the Arabs, capturing Germanikeia and Melitene and exploiting divisions in the caliphate that accompanied the Abbasid revolution of 750. Against the Bulgars he led repeated campaigns in the 760s and 770s, with mixed results. These victories gave him prestige among soldiers, who later remembered him fondly, and they explain why even iconophile sources had difficulty denying his competence.

His religious policy developed alongside these military and administrative achievements. By the early 750s, Constantine was articulating a theological case against religious images, drawing on a Christological argument about the impossibility of representing Christ's nature, and he summoned a council to give it authority. The Council of Hieria met in 754 in the imperial palace at Hieria on the Asian shore of the Bosporus, with the bishops reportedly numbering 338. The patriarchal throne of Constantinople was vacant at the time, and neither the pope nor the patriarchs of the eastern sees were represented, a fact which later iconophiles used to deny the council's claim to be ecumenical. Its decrees, preserved in quotations in the acts of the Second Council of Nicaea, condemned the making of images of Christ and the saints as inconsistent with the faith, and they pronounced anathemas on prominent defenders of images, including John of Damascus. The Primary Source Window later in this chapter examines these documents together.

Imperial sponsorship was essential to Hieria's convocation and enforcement, but its bishops themselves claimed conciliar authority for their decisions. This is a significant point about the relationship between emperor and church. Byzantine emperors had long summoned councils, and Constantine did not do anything unprecedented by claiming doctrinal leadership. Yet the controversy exposed the limits of imperial authority. Many clergy, monks, and laypeople believed that the emperor could not decide a doctrinal matter alone, and that a council required a broad consensus of the churches. According to iconophile sources, after Hieria Constantine required oaths against the veneration of images and replaced bishops who resisted, and the same sources report that images in churches were covered, scraped, or replaced with crosses and decorative patterns, while some ecclesiastical officials and monks were punished. The extent and consistency of this enforcement are discussed in the next section, since the sources for it are heavily colored by later hagiography.

Constantine V's reign must also be understood as a period of administrative consolidation. Constantine resettled Syrians and Armenians from eastern frontier regions in Thrace, strengthening population there. Separately, after the plague of 746 and 747, sources report that he repopulated Constantinople with families brought especially from Greece and the Aegean islands. He kept up fortification and frontier defense, managed fiscal demands, and drew the army's loyalty. Later Byzantine soldiers, according to a story reported in iconophile sources, hailed his tomb as a place of victory. Historians therefore describe him as one of the most effective emperors of the period, whose religious policy was part of a broader program of imperial assertion. Iconoclasm and recovery were thus intertwined in his reign, and one of the main tasks of this chapter is to show how they intersected without treating either as an explanation for the other.

Constantine died on campaign in 775 and was succeeded by his son Leo IV, whose reign (775 to 780) was shorter and less assertive. Leo IV was married to the Athenian-born Irene and appears to have loosened pressure on monasteries and bishops, though he too held to the iconoclast position, and late in his reign he reportedly punished courtiers suspected of venerating images. His premature death in 780 left a nine-year-old heir, Constantine VI, and a regent who would change the direction of the church.

Section 37.5

Monks, Saints, Images, and the Problem of Persecution

No part of the image controversy has been more shaped by later narrative than the question of persecution. Iconophile hagiography portrays the iconoclast emperors, especially Constantine V, as cruel persecutors of monks, and the story of Stephen the Younger, a monk of Mount Auxentios near Constantinople who was killed in 765, became the centerpiece of this tradition. His life, composed in the early ninth century by Stephen the Deacon, depicts him as a heroic opponent of imperial policy, and it also includes vivid accounts of parades of mutilated monks and nuns, forced marriages, and the destruction of monasteries. These stories have exercised a powerful influence on later writers, including modern ones, who have often accepted them as evidence of a systematic campaign against monasticism.

Recent scholarship urges a more cautious reading. First, not all monks were iconophiles, and not all iconophiles were monks. Monastic communities differed widely in location, wealth, political connections, and theological opinion, and some monks and abbots cooperated with imperial policy. Secular clergy, officials, soldiers, and laypeople took positions of every kind. Second, the evidence indicates that some prominent figures and some networks of monks suffered punishment, exile, or confiscation, but the scale and continuity of persecution are hard to determine. Many of the sources that describe mass persecution were written in the early ninth century by monastic authors who had political and religious reasons to emphasize the sufferings of their predecessors, and some of those accounts appear to have been revised during the second iconoclast period. Third, relations between the emperor and the monasteries were complicated by questions of property, loyalty, and politics. Monasteries were landowners, centers of local influence, and sometimes places of refuge for opponents of the regime, so imperial action against them could have fiscal and political as well as doctrinal motives.

None of this means that persecution did not occur. The more careful formulation is that there was a sustained imperial effort against dissenters in certain periods, especially in the 760s, and that individuals suffered for their convictions. But the question is not whether persecution occurred, but how extensive it was, who was targeted, when, and for what combination of religious and political reasons. Stephen the Younger himself, for example, appears in our sources to have been connected with political opposition to Constantine V, and the circumstances of his death were probably entangled with conspiracies in the capital. Distinguishing the religious from the political in such cases is difficult, and perhaps would not have been meaningful to the participants.

Images and holy persons were also contested because of what they represented socially. Saints, relics, and venerated images offered an alternative locus of holy power that was not directly controlled by the emperor. Holy men could attract followings, advise rulers, and oppose them. Whether or not this was an intended target of imperial policy, it makes sense of the intensity of the conflict. A monastery with a famous holy man or a revered icon was both a religious community and a political actor. Likewise, ordinary laypeople had stakes in the matter, since devotion to particular icons was bound up with family and local identity. For them, the question of images was a question about their access to sacred protection.

Source Criticism

Can We Trust the Victors' History of Iconoclasm?

The problem: The iconophiles won. Their church councils, saints' lives, chronicles, and treatises survived, were copied, and became the authoritative account of the period. The iconoclasts lost, and their own writings were mostly destroyed or neglected. Their arguments survive chiefly as quotations embedded in hostile refutations, notably in the acts of Nicaea II and in the treatises of Patriarch Nikephoros.

What this means in practice:

  • Iconoclast theology must be reconstructed from fragments selected and framed by opponents, which can distort or simplify the arguments.
  • Emperors condemned as heretics, especially Constantine V, appear in iconophile sources as monsters, and some famous episodes may be retrospective inventions or exaggerations.
  • The image of universal monastic resistance was partly constructed by later monastic writers, who had an interest in presenting their communities as consistent defenders of orthodoxy.
  • Even details of chronology depend on authors, such as Theophanes, who had their own systems of dating and their own agendas.

How historians respond: They compare different kinds of evidence, including coins, seals, buildings, and non-Byzantine sources, in order to check literary claims. They read hostile quotations carefully for the traces of the argument they report. They ask who wrote a text, when, and for whom, and they treat vivid stories with particular caution. This approach does not make the iconophile sources worthless, but it requires that they be used critically. It also offers an unusually clear opportunity to teach how historians reconstruct defeated viewpoints.

Section 37.6

Themes, Tagmata, and the Army of the Medieval Empire

Chapter 36 described the emergency measures of the seventh century and deliberately deferred a fuller account of the military institutions that emerged from them. This section supplies that account. The key point is that the army of the medieval empire was not designed on a single plan. It developed through a sequence of improvisations, in which regional field armies became territorial commands and, over time, administrative districts, while emperors also built and rebuilt central forces whose first loyalty was to Constantinople.

From the later seventh into the eighth century, surviving field armies were increasingly based and maintained in Anatolian regions. Military commands became associated with particular territories, while older civil and fiscal structures changed unevenly alongside them. Only gradually did these commands develop into the territorial military-administrative units later known as themes. The commands bore the names of the armies or groups associated with them. The Anatolikon, the Armeniakon, the Thrakesion, and the Opsikion, along with a naval command based in the southern Anatolian and Aegean coasts, stand out in the sources. Each was headed by a military commander, the strategos. Specialists sometimes speak of "embryonic themes" in the earlier eighth century, since the strategos acquired civil and fiscal authority over a territory only gradually, and the term thema, "theme," came to apply to the district rather than just the army as that process matured. The process was gradual and uneven, and the surviving evidence does not permit a precise map for the eighth century. The Map Study later in this chapter therefore asks students to work with approximate regions.

The soldiers of these commands were supported by the provisioning and taxation of the districts in which they were stationed. Some scholars have imagined that soldiers received hereditary plots of land in return for military service, a "soldier-farmer" system created by the emperor Heraclius, and some older textbooks presented this as the origin of the themes. Modern scholarship has abandoned that view. The evidence does not show that Heraclius created themes, and it does not show a system of hereditary military estates for this period. Soldiers were supported by a mixture of state payment, local supply, and in some cases their own property, and the arrangements varied by region and time. The themes should not be described as feudal estates, since the Byzantine state retained a tax system, salaried officials, and a powerful central administration, none of which resembled the dispersed lordship discussed in Chapter 34.

The weakness of the thematic system was the autonomy of its commanders. The revolt of Artabasdos in 741 and other rebellions of the period showed that a theme commander could pose a threat to the emperor, particularly when his district lay near the capital. Emperors responded in several ways. They divided large commands into smaller units, so that no single commander controlled enough troops to threaten the throne, and they promoted loyal officers and relatives. And they developed the tagmata, professional regiments that were stationed in or near Constantinople and paid by the central treasury. Constantine V is generally credited with enlarging and reorganizing these units, including the Scholai and the Excubitors, and making them an effective field force as well as a palace guard. The tagmata were a visible instrument of imperial authority, and their presence helps explain why Constantine could suppress opposition and conduct offensives while keeping the themes in check. Their loyalty was not unconditional. In 786, soldiers of the tagmata disrupted a council that Irene had summoned to restore images, which shows that armies had opinions about religious policy too.

This system had implications for the broader state. Provisioning the army required tax collection in kind and in coin, and the assessment of rural communities depended on the officials who surveyed land and households. Defense in depth meant fortified refuges, watchtowers, and signaling systems along the frontier with the caliphate, while the capital's land and sea walls protected the center. The empire fought a defensive war much of the time, with raiding and counterraiding along the Taurus and Anti-Taurus mountains, rather than wide campaigns of conquest. A good comparative question for students is therefore the following: how did emperors balance provincial military power with forces loyal to Constantinople, and what were the costs of each approach?

Historical Vocabulary
Theme (thema) and Tagma

A theme (Greek thema) was originally a regional army corps and later a territorial military and administrative district governed by a strategos. The origins and development of the system are debated, but the military commands from which the themes developed are visible by the later seventh century, while their territorial and civil-administrative form emerged gradually, and historians agree that they were not a single reform enacted by Heraclius. A tagma (plural tagmata) was a professional regiment, usually stationed in or near Constantinople and paid by the central government, such as the Scholai or the Excubitors. Themes provided regional strength and local knowledge, while tagmata gave emperors forces less dependent on provincial commanders. Neither term should be confused with Western feudal institutions.

Section 37.7

Rural Society, Villages, Land, and Taxation

Most inhabitants of the empire lived in the countryside, and the state depended on their labor and produce. Yet rural society is difficult to study for this period, since almost none of the sources were written by peasants, and the surviving evidence is small. Historians draw on a few law codes, saints' lives that describe village incidents, archaeological surveys, seals of tax officials, coins, and occasional comments in chronicles. They have also learned to combine these with comparative evidence from later centuries, a method requiring care since conditions changed. What emerges is a varied countryside of villages, small and middle-sized holdings, larger estates, pastoral districts, and monastic lands, with local arrangements differing from Anatolian uplands to Aegean coasts and from Thrace to the Peloponnese.

The village was the main unit of rural life and an important fiscal community. Villagers farmed arable plots, tended vines and olive trees where the climate allowed, and grazed animals on shared pastures, as outlined in the environmental survey in Chapter 2. They also shared responsibilities, for example for boundaries, watercourses, and local disputes. Taxes were assessed on landholders and households, while forms of communal responsibility could make neighbors liable when obligations went unpaid. Payment was collected in coin and sometimes in kind. A household tax, often called the hearth tax (kapnikon), is attested in this period, and later sources suggest the importance of communal responsibility for payment, although scholars disagree about when particular practices began. Military supply also fell on the countryside, since soldiers had to be fed, equipped, and sometimes lodged, and raids could destroy crops, livestock, and the fortified places on which village security depended.

The Farmer's Law (Nomos Georgikos) is one of the most valuable sources for village life, and it must be used carefully. It is a short collection of rules dealing with fields, boundaries, livestock, theft, hired labor, vineyards, and damage caused by animals, and it provides a rare glimpse of rural disputes and penalties. Its date is uncertain, and most scholars place it somewhere between the seventh and ninth centuries, with many favoring the later seventh or eighth. It is not clear how widely it applied, who issued it, or how far it reflected the whole empire rather than particular regions. It therefore cannot be used to prove that Byzantine society consisted of uniform communities of free independent peasant proprietors. The text itself refers to hired laborers, shepherds, and farmers of different status, and the existence of large landowners and monasteries is attested elsewhere.

Large estates existed alongside village holdings. Aristocrats, officials, churches, and monasteries held land and often relied on tenants or dependents to work it. Monasteries were particularly important landholders and could accumulate property through gifts and bequests, which is one reason why imperial attitudes toward monasteries had economic as well as religious dimensions. At the same time, the late seventh and eighth centuries probably saw some disruption of the wealthiest landed families, as Persian, Arab, and Slavic pressures altered the social order and the emperors favored new men. Regional variation was substantial: Anatolian frontier zones, with their fortified settlements and military populations, differed from the more settled and monetized Aegean coasts, and Slavic communities in parts of the Balkans stood in varying relations to imperial authority.

The analytical point is that the medieval Byzantine state depended heavily on taxable rural production, but the countryside contained varied forms of property, dependency, and local organization. A state that could collect taxes reliably from peasants, support an army, and pay officials had a sound basis for recovery. But it also depended on the stability and productivity of those same peasants, who were vulnerable to raids, plague, famine, and the fiscal demands of the state itself. That vulnerability is part of what made the intersection of rural society, taxation, and military organization so important to recovery.

Section 37.8

Plague, Population, Settlement, and Economic Recovery

Chapter 36 described an economy that had contracted sharply. Cities had shrunk, many had been fortified and reduced in size, and monetary circulation had declined, particularly in small copper coinage. Chapter 37 asks whether and how this changed. The answer is that recovery was uneven, and it needs to be analyzed in several ways: stabilization of the state, recovery of population, and recovery of the economy. These did not necessarily occur at the same time, and much of what we think we know rests on evidence that is hard to interpret.

The plague that reached Constantinople in 747, after beginning in the central and western Mediterranean in 746 according to Theophanes, was a major interruption. The disease is usually identified as a return of the plague that had first struck in the sixth century, as discussed in Chapter 36, and our principal narrative account describes heavy mortality in the capital. Theophanes and other sources say that Constantine V repopulated the city by bringing settlers from Greece and the islands. The figures in such sources are not reliable, and modern scholars generally avoid assigning precise death tolls, but there is little doubt that the outbreak was severe. Some historians suggest that plague and the demographic shock influenced political and religious conditions, for example by intensifying fears about divine anger, though the evidence is indirect.

After the plague, signs of recovery are visible in several respects. The state continued to mint gold coinage, the nomisma, which remained a stable standard of value. Copper coinage, which had fallen sharply in circulation, shows some recovery in the ninth century in places such as Constantinople, although the pattern differs from region to region. Rural settlement appears to have stabilized in parts of Anatolia and the Aegean, and agricultural recovery is suggested by evidence for new fortified settlements and, in some places, expanding cultivation. Regional commerce, including the movement of grain, oil, wine, textiles, and metalwork, continued, and the capital remained linked to Mediterranean and Black Sea trade. Urban networks changed, with some old cities declining and others, especially fortified and strategically placed ones, surviving or growing. Archaeological and numismatic evidence is rapidly changing, and students should treat claims about specific numbers or dates as provisional.

Conceptually, three processes should be kept apart. State stabilization refers to the capacity of the government to collect taxes, pay soldiers, suppress rebellion, and defend its core. Population recovery refers to a rise in numbers after the losses of war, plague, and flight. Economic recovery refers to the expansion of production, exchange, and monetization. The Byzantine state could be stabilizing while population remained low, or it could tax effectively while commerce stagnated. In the period of this chapter, state stabilization came earlier than the clearer signs of demographic and economic growth, which become more visible in the ninth and tenth centuries. That sequence is a useful corrective to any story of straightforward recovery.

Section 37.9

Irene and the Second Council of Nicaea, 787

When Leo IV died in 780, his widow Irene became regent for their young son, Constantine VI. She had to build a political base from a position of weakness, since she was a woman, an outsider to the dynasty, and dependent on the support of court officials and the military. Her program included the restoration of images, a task for which she required the church, the army, and the court. She promoted Tarasios, a lay official, to the patriarchate in 784, and he agreed to work toward an ecumenical council. Irene also faced military threats. In 782, an Abbasid army reached the Bosporus under Harun al-Rashid, and the empire made a truce on costly terms, which reveals the dangers that accompanied her domestic changes.

The first attempt to hold a council, in Constantinople in 786, failed when soldiers of the tagmata, loyal to the iconoclast tradition of Constantine V, broke into the church and dispersed the bishops. Irene, apparently waiting for a better moment, reorganized the units, sending the offending troops to the eastern frontier and replacing them. The next year, she convened a council at Nicaea, in northwestern Anatolia, where the first ecumenical council had met in 325. The Second Council of Nicaea of 787 was attended by a large number of bishops, traditionally numbered at about 350, as well as monks, and by papal legates commissioned by Hadrian I, together with two eastern monks, John and Thomas, who acted in the name of the eastern apostolic sees though they had not been directly commissioned by the three eastern patriarchs. This participation allowed iconophiles to claim an ecumenical authority which Hieria lacked, although the eastern representation is itself a reminder that the council's claims were shaped by its organizers. The council annulled the decrees of Hieria.

The council's acts are the most important surviving source for the dispute, since they contain quotations from the decrees of Hieria, patristic texts, and arguments presented by both sides, together with the council's own definition. They affirmed that holy images could be given veneration of honor, proskynesis, but not the worship, latreia, which belongs to God alone. Their defense of images was grounded in the tradition of the church, in the incarnation, and in the claim that honor given to an image passes to its prototype. The acts also include an indictment of the iconoclast council. Because the acts were produced by the winning side, they are partly a work of selection and polemic, and students should use them accordingly, as the Primary Source Window below demonstrates.

Nicaea II was not received without difficulty. Western reactions were mixed. A Latin translation of the acts that circulated at the court of Charlemagne was imperfect, and the Frankish response, the Libri Carolini, criticized what its authors understood the council to have decided, and a Frankish synod at Frankfurt in 794 rejected aspects of Greek practice. These developments, which belong to the story of Carolingian Europe in Chapter 31 and to the longer separation of Latin and Greek Christianity in Chapter 40, show that the council was understood differently by different audiences. The papacy, by contrast, welcomed the restoration of images. Within the empire, many clergy accepted the change, though some resented the speed with which iconoclast bishops were pardoned and reinstated, a tension that anticipates later disputes between rigorist monks and more flexible patriarchs.

It is crucial not to call 787 the permanent end of Iconoclasm. The council reestablished image veneration under Irene and Tarasios, but the political structures that had supported iconoclast policy remained in place, including the army's memory of Constantine V, and the opposition within the church was only suppressed, not eliminated. The decades after 787 were therefore an iconophile intermission, not a final settlement. Irene herself remained a controversial ruler. In 797 she had her son blinded and deposed, and she ruled in her own name, an action that shocked many contemporaries. The fact that Charlemagne was crowned emperor in Rome in 800, partly on the argument that the imperial throne in Constantinople was occupied by a woman, is part of the larger western story, but historians debate the extent to which that argument was actually decisive. In 802, officials led by Nikephoros, the finance minister, overthrew Irene and sent her into exile.

The acts of Nicaea II were produced by the victors and are the most important single source for the theology of the controversy. They are available in English translation in scholarly editions, and students should consult a reliable translation rather than rely on paraphrase.

Paraphrase, not a quotation The definition attributed to Hieria argued, in summary, that any painted image of Christ was either an attempt to depict his divine nature, which cannot be circumscribed, or a depiction of his human nature alone, which would separate it from the divine, and that the true image of Christ was the Eucharist. The definition of Nicaea II, again in summary, declared that images of Christ, the Virgin, angels, and saints could be set up in churches and honored with veneration and kisses, that this was not the absolute worship due to God alone, and that the honor paid to an image passes to the one it represents.

Analysis: Read together, the two documents show the structure of the dispute. Hieria argued from the impossibility of representing Christ without error about his natures, while Nicaea argued from the incarnation, which made representation of the incarnate Christ legitimate and, in Theodore of Stoudios's later formulation, necessary. Both sides appealed to scripture and the fathers. Nicaea II's distinction between veneration and worship gave iconophiles a response to the charge of idolatry. But the documents are limited. Hieria is known through hostile quotation, which may have selected the passages most easily refuted. Nicaea II claims to speak for the whole church but was shaped by the political circumstances of Irene's regency, and neither text reveals much about what ordinary people did in front of images.

Questions for students:

  1. What does each council say, in summary, about the relationship between an image and the person it represents?
  2. Who created each source, and what purposes might they have served, including political purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from these texts about the theology and about the councils' claims to authority?
  4. What can historians not establish from these sources alone, especially about the iconoclast side, daily practice, and the extent of enforcement?
Section 37.10

Nikephoros I, the Bulgars, and the Consolidating State

The overthrow of Irene in 802 brought to the throne Nikephoros, who had served as the genikos logothetes, the general finance minister. His career, unlike that of Leo III or Constantine V, was bureaucratic rather than military, and the sources record that his rule was marked by an intense concern with revenue. The principal source is Theophanes, who disliked Nikephoros intensely. Theophanes lists a number of fiscal measures as an indictment of the emperor, including increases in various taxes, the reassessment of some obligations, the imposition of burdens on churches and monasteries, requirements affecting the poor who were to equip soldiers, and the forced movement of populations to Slavic regions in 809. Language such as "oppressive taxes" belongs to Theophanes, and this chapter attributes it to him instead of adopting it as neutral fact. Modern historians generally treat the measures as plausible in outline but caution against assuming that all of them were novel, that they formed a single reform program, or that they were as harsh as the hostile source suggests.

What Nikephoros's reign does reveal is the importance of state extraction in recovery. A state that fought on several frontiers and maintained an army and a court required revenue, and emperors who could increase revenue gained resources for defense. Nikephoros's measures should be understood in that context. They also show tensions between the state and the church, since monasteries and churches were substantial landholders whose exemptions limited the treasury. The relative tolerance toward iconophile monasteries in his reign did not mean sympathy with ecclesiastical privilege, and his dealings with the patriarch Tarasios's successor, Nikephoros, reflected the limits of imperial patience with church independence.

The Bulgar frontier was the setting of the reign's crisis. Under Khan Krum, who came to power around 803, the Bulgar state grew stronger and more assertive. Our sources for Krum are largely Byzantine and hostile, and the political history of the Bulgars is treated in Chapter 39 and should not be reconstructed here. For present purposes, what matters is the conflict with the empire. Nikephoros campaigned north of the Balkan range in 811, captured and sacked the Bulgar court at Pliska, and then withdrew, only to be trapped in the passes by Krum's forces on 26 July 811. The emperor was killed, and his heir Staurakios was gravely wounded. Byzantine sources report, with a mixture of horror and perhaps embellishment, that Krum made a drinking cup of the emperor's skull. The defeat shook the regime and led to a short reign by Michael I Rhangabe and then the accession of Leo V in 813, after another defeat at Versinikia and a Bulgar advance to the walls of Constantinople.

Pliska shows that Byzantine recovery was reversible. An empire that had a more effective fiscal bureaucracy, a more mature military system, and a clearer sense of its own institutions could still lose an emperor and an army in a mountain pass. Success depended on leadership, logistics, intelligence, and the loyalty of troops, as much as on formal institutions. The stronger state was not an invulnerable one. At the same time, the empire did not collapse. The walls of Constantinople held, Leo V stabilized the frontier, and Krum's death in 814 allowed a period of negotiation. Nikephoros's reign thus offers a case study in the argument of this chapter: Byzantine recovery involved state extraction as well as military success, and it was always contingent.

Section 37.11

The Second Iconoclasm, 815–843

Leo V, an Armenian officer who came to power in 813, presented himself as a defender of the empire in the aftermath of military disaster. Within two years he revived the policy of opposing images. The traditional explanation, found in iconophile sources, is that Leo wished to emulate the military successes of Constantine V and Leo III, which had come under iconoclast emperors, and he reportedly believed that the failures of the intervening period reflected divine displeasure at the restoration of images. Whether that was his reasoning or a later invention is hard to say, but the pattern is plausible in a society for which military success and divine favor were closely linked. In 815, a council in Hagia Sophia reaffirmed Hieria, and the patriarch Nikephoros, who refused to comply, was deposed and replaced by Theodotos Melissenos.

The second iconoclast period was not simply a repeat of the age of Constantine V, and historians have increasingly emphasized its distinct features. Its theology was more cautious. It appealed to the authority of the earlier tradition of the church but did not repeat all of Hieria's positions, and it seems to have allowed some forms of image devotion in practice, concentrating on prohibiting veneration of images placed within reach of worshippers. The political circumstances were different, with weaker emperors and a more fragmented empire. The intensity of enforcement also appears to have varied. Opposition was led by figures such as Theodore of Stoudios, the abbot of the great Constantinopolitan monastery of Stoudios, who defended limits on imperial intervention in doctrine and church discipline when imperial commands conflicted with what he regarded as church law, and who was exiled and mistreated, and by Patriarch Nikephoros, who wrote extensively against iconoclast arguments from exile. Yet there was no single response, and many clergy complied or stayed quiet.

Leo V was assassinated in 820 by conspirators in the palace and succeeded by Michael II, a former comrade who became the founder of a new dynasty. Michael faced a major rebellion led by Thomas the Slav, which lasted from 821 to 823 and at one stage besieged Constantinople. In the same period, an Andalusi exile community that had established itself in Alexandria later conquered Crete at a disputed date, usually placed between about 824 and 828, and in 827 Muslim forces from North Africa landed in Sicily, beginning a conquest that would take decades. These losses, treated further in later chapters, show that the empire's recovery coexisted with serious reverses in the Mediterranean. Michael II seems to have taken a moderate position on images, discouraging public dispute and maintaining the policy without much enthusiasm.

His son Theophilos, who reigned from 829 to 842, was a more vigorous ruler, noted for his love of justice, his interest in ceremony, and his attention to the army. Under him, iconoclast policy was enforced more actively than under Michael II, though iconophile sources are again hostile and probably exaggerated. Theophilos faced a significant Arab offensive in 838, when the caliph al-Mu'tasim sacked the city of Amorion, the capital of the Anatolikon theme, which Byzantine tradition remembered as a great disaster. Theophilos also engaged in diplomacy with the caliphate and with other powers. His reign illustrates that the empire could still sustain high-level diplomacy, building programs, and administrative activity while experiencing serious military setbacks.

When Theophilos died in 842, he left an infant heir, Michael III, and his widow Theodora became regent. In 843, with the support of leading officials and a restored patriarchate under Methodios, Theodora oversaw the restoration of images. The restoration was commemorated by a procession and a service on the first Sunday of Lent, later known as the Feast of Orthodoxy or Triumph of Orthodoxy, which remains part of the liturgical calendar of Orthodox churches. The traditional account depicts the restoration as a total victory for iconophile orthodoxy, and it includes the story that Theodora protected the memory of her husband by claiming that he had repented. As with other episodes, historians treat details with caution. What is clear is that the settlement of 843 proved permanent. It is also important not to read 843 as the moment at which Byzantium "became Orthodox" in the later confessional sense, since the term Orthodox was used in the sources for right belief, and the later separation from the Latin church lay in the future.

Multiple Perspectives

The Image Question: How Different Groups Experienced the Controversy

Note: these are analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the evidence, not quotations from named individuals. Groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

Emperors and court officials: Emperors justified policy as the defense of true worship and the welfare of the Christian Roman people, and they also acted to maintain control over the church, the army, and the sources of sacred authority. Iconoclast emperors had real theological reasons, and their policies were also tied to military credibility and dynastic legitimacy.
Bishops, patriarchs, and monks: Responses varied widely. Some bishops initiated criticism of images, and others resisted at the cost of their office. Monastic leaders such as Stephen the Younger and Theodore of Stoudios became symbols of resistance, though monasteries differed in their positions and political entanglements, and later writers amplified their sufferings.
Soldiers and provincial populations: Many soldiers admired iconoclast emperors who won victories, and the army shaped the course of the controversy, as the 786 episode shows. Rural and provincial communities experienced the dispute through local clergy, officials, and the loss or survival of particular images, and we know much less about their views than about those of the elites.
Women and the household: Women played active roles in the controversy, as rulers such as Irene and Theodora, as nuns and abbesses, and as householders who maintained devotional practice. Iconophile sources sometimes emphasize women's role in devotion, an emphasis that may reflect both reality and polemical interest.
Jews, Muslims, and religious minorities: Jewish communities, Christians living under Muslim rule such as John of Damascus, and heterodox Christian groups in Anatolia observed or were drawn into the dispute in various ways. Later Byzantine writers sometimes accused iconoclasts of sympathy with Jews or Muslims, a polemical charge that modern historians treat with caution, and reports of forced measures against Jews in the period are poorly documented.
Section 37.12

From Emergency State to Medieval Recovery

A comparison between the empire of 718 and that of 843 offers a way to synthesize the chapter. In 718, Constantinople had survived a siege, but the empire's territory was reduced, its army was organized in regional commands that threatened the throne, its revenue was limited, and the church was beginning a conflict that would divide it. By 843, the empire had endured further Arab and Bulgar attacks, lost Crete and begun to lose Sicily, and experienced two periods of iconoclast policy. Yet it also had a more mature provincial military organization, central elite regiments, an effective fiscal bureaucracy, a more stable rural tax base, a continuing government in Constantinople, a restored church with a defined position on images, and institutions with growing capacity for expansion and cultural influence.

It would nevertheless be a mistake to say that Byzantium entered a golden age in 843. The empire remained under pressure, and the following decades included major challenges and opportunities that later chapters examine. The better conclusion is that the empire had moved from survival to renewed capacity. During the reign of Michael III (842 to 867) and the regency of Theodora, the court showed renewed confidence in learning, diplomacy, and ecclesiastical organization, and the empire was increasingly able to project clergy, books, liturgy, and diplomacy beyond its borders. That capacity prepares directly for Chapter 38, but the actual missions of Cyril, Methodius, and others belong to that chapter and are not narrated here.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Byzantine Power Recover?

Long-term causes

The survival of Constantinople and its defenses, the agricultural and manpower base of Anatolia, continuity in Roman administrative and legal traditions, and a political ideology that gave the emperor a central role in the Christian Roman order.

Short-term causes

The defeat of the Arab siege in 718, the Abbasid revolution and the shift of the caliphate's center eastward, divisions among Arab and Bulgar powers, emperors who repaired the army and fiscal system, and diplomacy that exploited rivals.

Immediate effects

Greater central control through tagmata and fiscal administration, successful campaigns under Constantine V, population movements and resettlement, and a government able to survive the plague and civil conflict.

Long-term consequences

A state with the institutional foundation for later expansion, a church that had resolved the image question, and a culture capable of projecting influence into neighboring regions, as Chapters 38 and 39 explain.

Historical complexity: Recovery cannot be attributed to the themes, the tagmata, taxation, diplomacy, or a particular emperor alone. These factors interacted, and each was shaped by luck, external events, and the actions of many people. It would also be misleading to label every fiscal or military measure a "reform" merely because later historians group measures together.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Byzantine State in 718 and 843

What Changed

  • The army was more territorially organized, with more mature themes alongside elite tagmata.
  • The relationship between emperor and church was tested by the image controversy and settled with a restored, conciliar definition.
  • The rural economy showed signs of stabilization, though the state still depended on a smaller and more vulnerable base.
  • The administration had become more capable of mobilizing resources and projecting influence beyond the defensive core.

What Remained Continuous

  • Roman imperial identity, the emperor, and Constantinople as the political and ceremonial center.
  • The taxation system, the army as a major expense, and the importance of Roman law.
  • The Christian political ideology that tied the welfare of the empire to correct belief and worship.

Who experienced change most directly? Soldiers, officials, and the clergy and monks of Constantinople felt institutional and religious change most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Many villagers experienced the period as a continuation of taxation, raiding, and seasonal labor, with change arriving mainly through officials, soldiers, and local clergy. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that recovery was uneven across social groups and regions, which prevents a simple narrative of national revival.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of the Roman state centered on Constantinople between the siege of 717 and 718 and the restoration of images in 843. Its governing argument is that the eighth and early ninth centuries were neither a Byzantine "dark age" nor merely an age of religious persecution. They were a period in which emperors, soldiers, clergy, monks, rural communities, and fiscal officials rebuilt a smaller Roman state while debating the legitimate place of sacred images within Christian worship. Iconoclasm was deeply connected to imperial authority and ecclesiastical politics, but it did not explain every political, military, or social development of the period.

The chapter began by establishing that survival in 718 did not equal recovery. The empire rested on Constantinople, Anatolia, and surviving Balkan and Aegean regions, with a reduced fiscal and manpower base, under pressure from Arab raids and Bulgar power. Its inhabitants regarded themselves as Romans, and the modern label "Byzantine" is a convention that should not obscure that self-understanding.

On the origins of Iconoclasm, the chapter urged caution. The traditional narrative of Leo III destroying the Chalke image in 726 depends heavily on later iconophile sources and is much disputed. What can be said is that concern about images existed, that some Anatolian bishops and Leo III were hostile to certain uses of images, and that Patriarch Germanos opposed them. Systematic, empire-wide enforcement under Leo III is poorly evidenced. The theological controversy itself turned on the distinction between image and prototype, between veneration and worship, and on the Christological question of whether Christ could be represented. Iconoclasts were not enemies of all art, and imperial portraits, crosses, and decoration flourished.

Under Constantine V, Iconoclasm became formal imperial policy, defined at the Council of Hieria in 754 and enforced through oaths, replacement of bishops, and the alteration of church decoration. His reign also brought military success, frontier consolidation, resettlement, and the strengthening of the tagmata, which made the emperor less dependent on provincial theme commanders. The traditional caricature of Constantine as merely a fanatical destroyer fails to capture this administrative and military dimension. Persecution of monks occurred, but its scale and character have been exaggerated by hagiographical sources, and monastic resistance was not uniform.

The chapter's treatment of military and rural organization stressed gradual development. Themes began as regional army corps and became territorial districts over time. They were not hereditary soldier estates created by Heraclius and should not be called feudal. The tagmata gave emperors elite troops loyal to the capital. Rural society consisted of villages, small and large holdings, and monasteries, supporting the state through taxation, while the Farmer's Law provides valuable but undatable evidence that cannot be treated as proof of uniform peasant proprietorship. The plague of 746 and 747 interrupted recovery, and the chapter distinguished state stabilization, population recovery, and economic recovery as processes that did not necessarily coincide.

Irene's regency and the Second Council of Nicaea in 787 restored the veneration of images, distinguishing proskynesis from latreia. Yet 787 was an intermission, not an ending. Nikephoros I, a finance official who overthrew Irene, strengthened fiscal administration, though Theophanes's hostile portrait should be attributed rather than adopted. His death at Pliska in 811 showed that stronger institutions did not make the empire invulnerable. The second iconoclast period, beginning under Leo V in 815, differed in circumstances and intensity from the first, and it ended in 843 when Theodora and the church restored images in a settlement that proved permanent.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized the problem of evidence. Because the victors wrote most of the surviving narrative sources, historians must read them critically, compare them with coins, buildings, seals, and outside sources, and recognize that defeated viewpoints are often known only through hostile quotation. This methodological lesson is as important as any narrative detail, and it is one of the richest opportunities in the textbook to see how historical knowledge is constructed.

By 843, the empire had moved from survival to renewed capacity. It had a more mature provincial military organization, elite central regiments, an effective fiscal bureaucracy, a more stable tax base, a continuing government in Constantinople, and a settled position on images. It had not entered a golden age, and it continued to suffer losses in the Mediterranean and pressure on its frontiers. But it was better able to project clergy, books, diplomacy, and culture beyond its borders. That capacity prepares for Chapter 38, which examines Orthodox Christianity and the Slavic world, and for Chapter 39, which examines the political communities that emerged in interaction with Byzantium. The longer separation of Latin and Greek Christianity, to which the image controversy contributed in part, belongs to Chapter 40 and should not be read as a predetermined path to schism.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Icon
A sacred image of Christ, the Virgin, or a saint, used in public worship and private devotion and regarded by supporters as a point of contact with the person depicted.
Iconoclasm
The opposition to the veneration of sacred images, and the imperial policy and religious controversy associated with it in the Byzantine empire between the 720s or 730s and 843.
Iconophile (iconodule)
A supporter of the veneration of sacred images; the label was used by supporters themselves and by later writers who inherited their vocabulary.
Iconomach
A polemical Greek term, "image fighter," used by iconophiles for their opponents.
Prototype
The person represented by an image, in iconophile argument the one to whom honor shown to the image ultimately passes.
Proskynesis
Relative veneration or reverence, shown by bowing or kissing, which iconophiles said could be given to images, saints, and the cross.
Latreia
The absolute worship owed to God alone, which iconophiles distinguished from the veneration of images.
Incarnation
The Christian doctrine that God became human in Christ, the basis of the iconophile claim that depicting the human Christ was legitimate.
Theme (thema)
A regional army corps that developed into a territorial military and administrative district governed by a strategos.
Strategos
The military commander of a theme, who also held substantial civil authority.
Tagmata
Professional regiments stationed in or near Constantinople and paid by the central government, used by emperors as a counterweight to provincial commands.
Opsikion
A large Anatolian command near the capital, whose commanders posed a political danger, as the revolt of Artabasdos showed.
Logothete
A senior financial or administrative official; the general logothete oversaw the fiscal administration, a post held by Nikephoros I before he became emperor.
Nomisma
The Byzantine gold coin, a stable standard of value that continued to be minted throughout the period.
Kapnikon
A household or hearth tax attested in this period, with disputed origins and application.
Farmer's Law (Nomos Georgikos)
A short collection of rules on fields, livestock, boundaries, and village disputes, valuable for rural life but of uncertain date and scope.
Ekloga
A brief Byzantine legal handbook, issued in the names of Leo III and Constantine V, adapting Roman law to the conditions of the smaller empire.
Horos
The formal definition of doctrine issued by a church council, as at Hieria in 754 and Nicaea in 787.
Ecumenical council
A council claiming to represent the whole church; iconophiles denied that Hieria qualified, since it lacked the patriarchs and the pope.
Council of Hieria (754)
The council convened by Constantine V that condemned religious images, later annulled at Nicaea II.
Second Council of Nicaea (787)
The council convened under Irene that restored the veneration of images and defined the distinction between veneration and worship.
Iconophile intermission
The period between 787 and 815, when images were venerated under imperial sanction before iconoclast policy was revived.
Triumph of Orthodoxy
The restoration of images in 843, commemorated on the first Sunday of Lent as a feast of the Orthodox church.
Regent
A person who rules on behalf of a minor emperor, as Irene did for Constantine VI and Theodora for Michael III.
Hagiography
Writing about saints, such as the life of Stephen the Younger, valuable as evidence but shaped by devotional and polemical purposes.
Anno Mundi
A dating system counting years from the supposed creation of the world, used by Theophanes and requiring correction by modern scholars.
Chalke Gate
The ceremonial entrance to the imperial palace in Constantinople, associated in tradition with an image of Christ whose removal is a disputed episode.
Libri Carolini
A Frankish treatise criticizing what its authors understood to be the decisions of Nicaea II, produced at Charlemagne's court.
Byzantine
The modern scholarly label for the medieval Roman state centered on Constantinople, whose inhabitants called themselves Romans.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state that had survived near collapse became a durable medieval power without ceasing to be Roman. The study of Iconoclasm provides a window into that process, but the larger lesson is that religious controversy, imperial authority, military organization, taxation, and rural society moved together, each with its own rhythm. Understanding that interaction prevents the reduction of a complex century to a struggle over images, and it also prevents the equally mistaken view that institutions developed apart from belief.

The chapter also contributes to the larger argument of Volume II, which treats medieval Europe as a world with multiple Christian traditions, political systems, and regional centers. While the Franks and the papacy were constructing Latin Christendom, as Part V described, the Roman state at Constantinople was working out a distinct relationship between emperor, church, army, and countryside. These developments were not isolated from each other. Frankish criticism of Nicaea II, papal appeals to Frankish rulers, and the loss of Ravenna show how closely eastern and western stories intersected, even as they diverged. The divergence should not, however, be turned into a predetermined road to schism.

Many continuities survived beyond 843. The themes and tagmata, the fiscal bureaucracy, the role of the emperor, and the settled position on images became foundations of the later medieval empire. The experience also left a legacy of memory, in which the Triumph of Orthodoxy became a founding celebration of the church, and in which the image of the iconoclast emperors as heretics endured for centuries. Historians must therefore keep distinguishing what happened from how it was remembered, a task that this chapter has tried to model.

Chapter 38 now turns outward. A state that had recovered institutional confidence, resolved its dispute over images, and developed a capable church and court could extend its influence toward its Slavic neighbors through missionary activity, literacy, liturgy, and diplomacy. Understanding the consolidation described in this chapter is essential for understanding why and how that outward movement became possible, and for seeing the Orthodox world as an integral part of the history of medieval Europe.
