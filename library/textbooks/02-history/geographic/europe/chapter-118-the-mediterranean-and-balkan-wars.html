The Mediterranean and Balkan Wars

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe
Chapter Contents

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1940 a small island less than one hundred kilometers south of Sicily became one of the most contested places in Europe. Malta mattered not for its size but for its position, and the war that formed around it, and around Greece, Crete, Libya, Egypt and Yugoslavia, showed how geography, supply and political division could decide the fate of armies and of civilians alike.

Malta, a British possession with a naval dockyard, lay astride the sea lanes that joined the western and eastern Mediterranean. From its harbors and airfields, ships and aircraft could threaten the convoys that carried fuel, ammunition and soldiers from Italy to Libya. Whoever could use the island, or neutralize it, gained leverage over a war that was being fought hundreds of kilometers away in the desert. When Italy entered World War II on June 10, 1940, Malta was among the first places to feel the consequences, and it would endure repeated air attack for much of the next two years. The island is a useful place to begin because it shows, in miniature, the central lesson of this chapter: the Mediterranean war was a war of communications as much as a war of battles.

Chapter 117 traced Germany's victories in Poland and western Europe and Britain's survival against the Luftwaffe. This chapter follows the war outward, across the sea that had once been the heart of the Roman world and that now linked a Europe at war with Africa, the Middle East and the British imperial system. Between June 1940 and September 1943 the conflict spread from Libya to Egypt, from Albania to Greece, from the Adriatic coast to the mountains of Bosnia, from the island of Crete to the beaches of Morocco, Algeria and Sicily. These were different campaigns with different participants, but they were joined by shipping routes, by the strategic choices of London, Rome and Berlin, and by the lives of the populations who found armies marching across their homes.

The central argument of the chapter is that the Mediterranean and Balkan wars were not peripheral diversions from the real European conflict. Italian initiatives drew in Germany, Britain, Commonwealth and colonial forces, local armies and, eventually, the United States. Axis conquests in the Balkans and Crete appeared decisive in the spring of 1941, yet conquest produced occupation, and occupation produced famine, reprisals, collaboration, civil conflict and resistance. Allied victory in North Africa and Sicily, by contrast, did not end the war in the Mediterranean but opened a new phase, one that brought down Mussolini and exposed the fragility of Axis control across southern and southeastern Europe.

Equally important are the people whose experiences cannot be recovered from the careers of generals. Greek soldiers fought the Italian army in the Pindus mountains in the winter of 1940 and 1941, and Greek civilians then endured one of the worst famines of occupied western Europe. Cretan villagers took part in the defense of their island and suffered reprisals afterward. Libyan, Egyptian, Algerian and Moroccan civilians lived on lands that became supply zones and battlefields under colonial administrations. Indian, Australian, New Zealand, South African and other soldiers fought for imperial governments whose political purposes they did not always share. Serbs, Croats, Jews, Roma, Bosnian Muslims, Albanians, Macedonians, Slovenes and others experienced the dismemberment of Yugoslavia in sharply different ways, and the violence of those years left a long shadow over the region.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. Operational orders, war diaries, signals intelligence, naval convoy records and official histories allow historians to reconstruct campaigns. Occupation decrees, relief reports, municipal and church records, police files and trial documents show how occupied societies were governed. Memoirs, letters, diaries and later oral testimony reveal how soldiers and civilians understood events, though memory is shaped by later politics. Historians must be careful here. Postwar Yugoslavia, Greece, Italy, Britain and Germany each constructed national memories of these years, and the reputations of figures such as Rommel, Montgomery, Tito and Mihailović were shaped by wartime propaganda, Cold War politics and later national mythology. This chapter uses those sources critically.

Within Part XVIII, this chapter occupies a bridging position. It follows the western victories of 1939 and 1940 and precedes the German-Soviet war (Chapter 119), the general history of occupation, collaboration and resistance (Chapter 120), the Holocaust and Nazi genocide (Chapter 121) and the Allied return to Europe and the defeat of Germany (Chapter 122). It introduces Balkan and Mediterranean manifestations of persecution and occupation where they shaped local societies, but the fuller systematic treatments belong to those later chapters. Within Volume V, it shows that the catastrophe of the Second World War was already a continental and imperial war long before 1944.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Mediterranean became a major theater of World War II and how Gibraltar, Malta, Sicily, Crete, Alexandria and Suez linked European and imperial strategy.
  2. Distinguish Italian strategic ambitions in 1940 from Italian industrial, naval and logistical capabilities, avoiding generalizations about national military character.
  3. Trace the sequence from the Italian invasion of Greece to German intervention in the Balkans and assess the limits of claims that this intervention caused later German failure in the Soviet Union.
  4. Analyze the Axis conquest of Yugoslavia, Greece and Crete in spring 1941 as military operations and as the beginning of long occupations.
  5. Evaluate the role of logistics, shipping, intelligence and air power in the North African campaigns from Operation Compass to El Alamein.
  6. Describe the contributions and experiences of Commonwealth, colonial and local populations in North Africa, including civilians, laborers and soldiers.
  7. Compare the Ustaša regime, the Chetniks and the Partisans, distinguishing collaboration, resistance, civil conflict and crimes against civilians.
  8. Explain the causes and consequences of famine, reprisals and persecution in occupied Greece and the differing policies of German, Italian and Bulgarian authorities.
  9. Assess the significance of Operation Torch, the Tunisia campaign and Operation Husky for the fall of Mussolini and the Allied return to southern Europe.
  10. Interpret the Italian armistice, distinguishing its signing, announcement and military consequences, and evaluate competing views of the strategic importance of the Mediterranean war.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a war that began as an Italian bid for Mediterranean predominance expand into a struggle involving Germany, Britain, the Commonwealth, the United States and many local populations?
  2. How did the control of sea lanes, ports and airfields determine what armies could accomplish in Libya and Egypt?
  3. Why did rapid Axis conquest in Yugoslavia and Greece in April 1941 not produce stable political control?
  4. In what ways did the experiences of civilians in occupied Greece, Yugoslavia, Crete and North Africa differ from the military narratives of the campaigns?
  5. How should historians distinguish resistance, collaboration, civil war and mass violence in occupied Yugoslavia without collapsing distinct movements into one story?
  6. What evidence supports or limits explanations of the desert war that center on individual commanders such as Rommel and Montgomery?
  7. Why did the collapse of Mussolini's government on July 25, 1943 not immediately end Italy's war, and what followed the armistice announced on September 8?
  8. Was the Mediterranean theater strategically central to the outcome of World War II, a costly diversion, or something more complicated than either description?

Historical Context

The Mediterranean of 1940 was a sea divided among overlapping empires. Britain held Gibraltar at its western entrance, Malta at its center, Cyprus and Palestine in the east, and exercised a dominant but contested influence in Egypt, where a treaty of 1936 permitted British forces to protect the Suez Canal. France held Algeria, Tunisia, Morocco, Syria and Lebanon. Italy held Libya, which it had conquered from the Ottoman Empire in 1911 and 1912 and subdued through severe repression, together with the Dodecanese islands and, from 1936, Italian East Africa, created after the conquest of Ethiopia. In April 1939 Italy also occupied Albania, which became a base from which Mussolini could pursue influence in the Balkans. Fascist propaganda spoke of the Mediterranean as "our sea," but Italy's access to the wider oceans was controlled by the British-held chokepoints of Gibraltar and Suez.

Southeastern Europe had been reorganized by the peace settlements discussed in Chapter 113. Yugoslavia, formed in 1918 as the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, was a multinational state whose politics were strained by disputes over centralization, culminating in the Cvetković-Maček agreement of August 1939, which created an autonomous Croatian province. Greece had experienced republic, monarchy, coups and, from 1936, the authoritarian regime of Ioannis Metaxas. Romania, Hungary and Bulgaria were revisionist or economically vulnerable states for which Germany was both a threat and a market. Their economies depended heavily on agricultural exports and, in Romania's case, on the oil fields around Ploiești, which gave Germany a powerful strategic interest in the region. Historians should resist reading this diversity as a sign that the region was doomed to fragment. Its later violence was the product of specific decisions, invasions and occupations.

The fall of France in June 1940, examined in Chapter 117, transformed the Mediterranean balance. The French fleet, previously a partner in the Allied naval position, was now divided between the Vichy government and those who rejected the armistice. British forces attacked French ships at Mers-el-Kébir on July 3, 1940, to prevent their use by Germany, an action that embittered Franco-British relations. French North Africa and the Levant remained under Vichy authority. With Italy now a belligerent, Britain had to defend Egypt, Malta and its eastern Mediterranean positions with limited resources while also preparing for possible invasion at home. Italian forces in Libya and East Africa seemed numerically formidable, but they were widely dispersed and were poorly supplied with the modern equipment needed for a prolonged war.

Germany's priorities lay elsewhere. Hitler was already considering an attack on the Soviet Union, and German planners initially regarded the Mediterranean as an Italian sphere. The Axis, formalized by the Pact of Steel in 1939 and the Tripartite Pact of September 1940, was a partnership of unequal and sometimes divergent aims. Mussolini wished to wage a "parallel war" that would secure Italian gains without German domination, but Italian setbacks repeatedly forced him to seek German assistance. The result was the progressive entanglement of Germany in a theater that it had not planned to fight. This is the context for the chapter's opening, in which Italian ambition meets Italian limits and Britain's position, though strained, proves harder to dislodge than Axis leaders expected.

The sources for this chapter are uneven in character and must be handled accordingly. British, Australian, New Zealand and South African official histories are detailed but sometimes reflect national and institutional perspectives. German records, including the war diaries of the Afrika Korps, were captured and studied after the war. Italian archives and memoirs were for decades less used in English-language writing, which contributed to unflattering stereotypes of Italian performance. For Yugoslavia, Titoist historiography after 1945 emphasized the Partisan struggle, while émigré and nationalist writing defended Chetnik or Ustaša causes, and the numbers of dead became politically charged. Greek memory of the occupation has likewise been shaped by the civil war that followed. Students should keep these distortions in mind throughout.

Italy's War and the Mediterranean Strategic Theater

June 1940–early 1941
Section 118.1

The Mediterranean War Begins: Italy, Britain, and the Strategic Geography of Empire

Italy declared war on Britain and France on June 10, 1940, with effect from the following day, when German armies had already broken the French front. Mussolini's calculation was that the war was nearly over and that Italy must be a belligerent to claim a share of the settlement. His aims included expanded territory in North Africa, Corsica, Nice, Savoy and the Balkans, and the weakening of British control over the Mediterranean. Italian officials spoke of a "parallel war," conducted alongside Germany rather than under German direction. The phrase expressed a political ambition that Italy's resources could not sustain. The Franco-Italian armistice was signed on June 24, 1940 and took effect on June 25, but the war between Italy and Britain continued.

The Mediterranean was an interconnected geography. Gibraltar controlled the western entrance, Malta the narrows between Sicily and Tunisia, Alexandria the eastern fleet anchorage, and the Suez Canal the shortest route between Britain and its Asian and Pacific empire. Libya, Egypt, Tunisia and Algeria formed a coastal corridor along which armies, ports and railways were concentrated, with the desert to the south. Sicily and southern Italy faced Libya across the central sea, while Greece, Crete and the Aegean lay to the northeast. A map of this region is best read as a network of ports, airfields and sea lanes, not as a series of separate fronts.

The allocation of authority in Egypt illustrates how imperial strategy could coexist with contested sovereignty. Egypt had achieved formal independence in 1922, yet the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty preserved important British military privileges, including positions along the canal. Egyptian governments therefore confronted an expanding war that they had not chosen and pressures from both the British command and Egyptian nationalist critics. Alexandria, the Western Desert railway and the canal zone were not merely marks on a military map; they were working environments with dock laborers, railway employees, farmers and families. Their services and vulnerabilities underpinned Allied movement. In Libya, by contrast, Italian colonial rule rested on conquest and earlier campaigns of forced removal and confinement, leaving deep divisions between colonial authority and the people over whom it claimed power.

The geography also made distance deceptive. An army advancing several hundred kilometers from its port might appear to have gained territory while making its own resupply progressively harder. Water had to be delivered, vehicles maintained, fuel carried and damaged roads repaired. A port's nominal capacity meant little if shipping could not reach it or if enough trucks were unavailable to distribute cargo inland. Consequently control of a single island or coastal road could matter more than an expanse of open terrain. The same logic would later influence Allied decisions about invading Sicily and securing airfields before moving onto the European mainland.

Italy's strengths and weaknesses must be separated. The Italian navy, the Regia Marina, was a large and in some respects modern fleet, with fast ships and many submarines. But it lacked radar, had limited fuel reserves, and had weak cooperation with the air force. The army included many divisions, but its artillery, antitank weapons, armor and motor transport were often outdated or insufficient. Italian industry could not match the output of Britain, still less of the United States, and Italy imported much of its coal, oil and steel. These were structural limits on a state that had gone to war with extensive ambitions. They do not justify the stereotype that Italian soldiers were uniformly incompetent. Italian units fought with determination in many places, and their performance varied with leadership, equipment, supply and terrain.

Britain's position was defined by empire. Egypt, the Suez Canal and Palestine were the keys to the Middle East, and Malta, Gibraltar and Alexandria protected the sea route to them. After Italy's entry the Mediterranean was no longer a safe route for British shipping to the Middle East, and most supplies had to travel around the Cape of Good Hope. Britain relied on forces drawn from across its empire: Indian divisions, Australian and New Zealand troops, South African units, and many colonial and local personnel. These forces reflected the imperial character of the war and also its tensions, since governments and populations in India, Egypt, Palestine and elsewhere had their own political aims, as Section 118.11 discusses.

The Mediterranean was therefore both a European and an imperial theater. Its northern shores belonged to the European continental war, its southern shores to colonial societies in Africa and the Middle East, and its waters linked the two. Understanding the campaign requires attention to all three dimensions: the great-power competition, the empires that supplied it, and the local populations on whose lands it was fought.

Historical Vocabulary
Theater of War, Maritime Communications, Naval Blockade, and Imperial Supply

A theater of war is a geographic area within which military operations are linked strategically, such as the Mediterranean theater. It need not coincide with political borders, and campaigns in one theater often depend on those in another. Maritime communications are the sea routes and associated ports, escorts and stores by which armies and economies are supplied. Naval blockade is the use of naval power to prevent a state's access to sea transport, a method used by Britain against Axis-controlled Europe and, in effect, against occupied Greece, with severe civilian consequences. Imperial supply refers to the movement of men, food, fuel and equipment across the British and other empires to sustain operations. These terms matter because Mediterranean campaigns were decided less by individual battles than by whether supplies could cross the sea and the desert.

Section 118.2

Naval Warfare, Malta, and the Struggle for Mediterranean Communications

The naval contest in the Mediterranean was fought between the British Mediterranean Fleet, based at Alexandria, a western squadron at Gibraltar (Force H, formed after the fall of France), and the Italian Regia Marina, supported by air forces from Sicily, Sardinia and Libya. Neither side could risk heavy losses without endangering its supply routes. The Italian fleet generally avoided a decisive fleet engagement while protecting convoys to Libya. British leaders sought to maintain control of the eastern Mediterranean and strike Axis shipping. The resulting war was one of convoys, submarines, mines and aircraft more than of great fleet battles.

Malta lay at the center of this struggle. Its harbors, dockyard and airfields allowed British aircraft, submarines and surface forces to attack the shipping that supplied Axis armies in Libya. For that reason the island came under sustained Italian and, from the end of 1940 and early 1941, German air attack, with the German Fliegerkorps X deployed to Sicily. Civilians sheltered in rock caves and tunnels, food and fuel were rationed, and the island's survival depended on convoys that had to run through narrow seas under air attack. In April 1942 King George VI awarded the George Cross to the island as a whole. The award recognized endurance, but it also reflected the island's strategic value, a point examined further in Section 118.11.

Seaborne logistics depended on tasks rarely visible in accounts of admirals. Dockworkers loaded crates and fuel drums, merchant mariners navigated mined waters, naval escorts adjusted routes according to aircraft sightings, and repair crews returned damaged ships to service. Losses could be severe even when a convoy arrived: survivors sometimes lacked the specialized tankers, ammunition or aircraft spares needed at the front. Radar and codebreaking improved British opportunities, but commanders could not attack every convoy without revealing intelligence or endangering scarce warships. Italian escort forces were often effective in getting ships across; the quantity and timing of cargo reaching distant desert units remained the harder measure of success.

Malta's own needs created competing priorities. Aircraft and submarines operating from the island needed aviation fuel, torpedoes, spare parts and rested crews, while residents required flour, water, medicine and shelter. In 1942, when Axis air attacks intensified, defenders sometimes struggled simply to keep aircraft serviceable and runways usable. Operation Pedestal in August brought desperately needed supplies at heavy naval and merchant-shipping cost, showing why control of maritime communications could not be reduced to an isolated battle result. Britain also had to choose among reinforcing Malta, protecting Egypt and escorting shipping around Africa, where the long route consumed vessels and time.

Two naval episodes of this period are often cited. On the night of November 11–12, 1940, aircraft from the carrier HMS Illustrious attacked the Italian fleet at Taranto, damaging three battleships. The raid demonstrated the effectiveness of naval aviation and later attracted attention from naval planners elsewhere, though its effect on the balance of the Mediterranean was less decisive than is sometimes claimed, because Italian ships were repaired in part and German air power soon arrived. In March 1941, British forces intercepted an Italian squadron off Cape Matapan (March 27–29), sinking three heavy cruisers and two destroyers, partly aided by intelligence and radar. Matapan discouraged Italian surface operations in the eastern Mediterranean, but the sea remained contested, and within two months the Royal Navy suffered heavy losses off Crete.

The relationship between shipping and land operations explains why these events mattered. An army in the desert required fuel, ammunition, food, vehicles and replacements, and the great majority of these arrived by sea. Losses of Axis shipping reduced the fuel and equipment available to Italian and German forces in Libya, while threats to British convoys to Malta and the eastern Mediterranean limited British options. The desert war was thus a campaign of the sea as much as of the sand. Historians avoid interpreting any single naval engagement as decisive, since the balance shifted repeatedly with air power, intelligence and the commitments of each side elsewhere.

Section 118.3

Italy's Offensive in Egypt and the First British Counteroffensive

On September 13, 1940, Marshal Rodolfo Graziani's Tenth Army advanced from Libya into Egypt, under strong pressure from Rome. The Italians covered roughly one hundred kilometers and halted at Sidi Barrani, where they built a chain of fortified camps. The advance was limited by water and fuel, by the scarcity of motor transport and by Graziani's own caution. Italy lacked the logistical base for a rapid move toward the Nile, and the British, who withdrew to Mersa Matruh, avoided a decisive battle. The two armies thus settled into a pause during which each reorganized.

British commander General Archibald Wavell, in command in the Middle East, prepared a limited offensive under General Richard O'Connor's Western Desert Force. Operation Compass began on December 9, 1940, with an attack on the Italian camps around Sidi Barrani, which fell the next day. The attackers included the British 7th Armoured Division and the 4th Indian Division, later joined by the 6th Australian Division. They attacked the Italian positions from the flank and rear, using concentrated armor and infantry, including heavy Matilda tanks against which Italian antitank guns were ineffective. The Indian division was withdrawn for service in East Africa, and Australian troops took Bardia on January 3–5, 1941, and Tobruk on January 21–22.

One reason Compass succeeded was that Italian camps around Sidi Barrani were separated by gaps that British and Indian formations could exploit. The use of Matilda infantry tanks gave attackers protection against many available antitank weapons, while coordinated artillery and mobility isolated strongpoints that might otherwise have held longer. The offensive was initially conceived as a limited raid, not as an inevitable march across Cyrenaica. Commanders extended it after observing the extent of Italian disorganization, and each extension imposed new burdens on repair workshops, fuel convoys and units already stretched thin. Italian soldiers' surrender must be understood in that tactical setting, not treated as proof of an inherent unwillingness to resist.

The campaign also exposed how imperial forces are often hidden inside national labels. Indian troops fought in the initial assault, Australian infantry participated in later operations and local Egyptian labor supported the transport network. East African campaigns likewise depended on Sudanese, Indian, Ethiopian and other African forces whose political relationships to Britain and Ethiopia differed sharply. Ethiopian resistance had never disappeared after Italy's conquest, and Haile Selassie's return in 1941 could not be explained solely as the work of an external liberating army. Examining their differing aims prevents the campaign from becoming a story of European commanders acting on an otherwise empty landscape.

The final phase came in February 1941. British armored forces drove across the desert of Cyrenaica to cut the coastal road at Beda Fomm, where the retreating Tenth Army was trapped and surrendered on February 5–7. British and Commonwealth accounts reported well over a hundred thousand Italian and colonial prisoners over the campaign, a rounded figure that reflects Allied counts and should be treated as an estimate. The campaign showed how a relatively small, mobile and well-planned force could defeat a larger but less mobile and less well-coordinated opponent. It was not a demonstration that Italian soldiers were uniformly poor; it reflected the weaknesses of Italian equipment, command and supply in this particular theater, as well as British surprise and operational skill.

The war against Italy extended into East Africa, where British, Commonwealth, Indian, Sudanese, South African and Ethiopian forces attacked Italian East Africa from the north and south beginning in January 1941. Fighting at Keren in Eritrea was hard, and the campaign continued until Italian forces in Ethiopia surrendered at Amba Alagi in May, with the last garrisons holding out at Gondar until November 1941. Ethiopian resistance, which had continued since the conquest of 1935 and 1936, was now supported by British forces and the exiled emperor Haile Selassie, who returned to Addis Ababa in May 1941 after the capital had been taken in April. East Africa must not be collapsed into the Western Desert, since its geography, forces and political meaning were different. For Ethiopians, it was a campaign for the restoration of independence.

Compass also had consequences elsewhere. Success in Cyrenaica encouraged London to consider assisting Greece, which meant diverting forces that might have advanced further toward Tripoli. The decision, examined in Section 118.4, would be one of the most debated of the war.

Section 118.4

Italy Invades Greece: The Greco-Italian War, 1940–1941

Mussolini ordered the invasion of Greece partly in response to Hitler's unannounced entry into Romania, and partly to demonstrate Italian independence from Germany. On the night of October 27–28, 1940, the Italian minister in Athens presented an ultimatum to the Greek prime minister, General Ioannis Metaxas, demanding the right to occupy strategic sites. Metaxas rejected it, and Italian forces crossed from Albania at dawn on October 28, a day still commemorated in Greece as Ohi Day. Metaxas headed an authoritarian regime that had suppressed political opposition, but its rejection of the ultimatum was widely supported, and the war united much of Greek society behind the army.

The Italian invasion went poorly. Italian planners underestimated Greek strength and will, and relied on poor roads, bad weather and inadequate transport in the mountainous region of Epirus and the Pindus. Heavy autumn rains and early snow impeded the advance, and Italian forces were short of artillery, mules and supplies. Greek forces, partly mobilized and well prepared for defense, halted the Italians, and in November they counterattacked, eventually taking Korçë (Koritsa) on November 22 and advancing into southern Albania. Greek civilians, including many women, carried supplies up mountain tracks to the front, one of the best known features of the campaign. By the winter the front lay deep in Albania, and an Italian spring offensive in March 1941 failed to restore the position.

The fighting in Epirus involved more than a simple confrontation between modern machines and impassable mountains. Greek commanders mobilized infantry familiar with difficult terrain, supported by local knowledge and transport networks heavily dependent on pack animals. Small roads became bottlenecks for artillery, food and medical evacuation. Italian troops were also fighting in difficult conditions and at times mounted determined counterattacks. When Greece advanced into Albania, supply lines lengthened for the defenders-turned-attackers, and the severe winter created problems for both sides. The resulting stalemate by early 1941 demonstrated the limits of projecting power through the mountains even when national morale was high.

Political decisions complicated the military picture. Metaxas's regime remained authoritarian, yet Greek resistance to Italian aggression drew support far beyond those who endorsed his domestic rule. Britain feared that abandoning Greece would undermine its credibility across the eastern Mediterranean, while commanders in Egypt warned that an expedition would use scarce aircraft and trained formations. German planners had to assess British airfields, Romanian oil, the approaching campaign against the Soviet Union and Italy's position together. The historical question is not whether intervention had a single cause, but how several risks were weighed when the time available to respond was diminishing.

Britain's assistance began with air units and, from March 1941, ground forces dispatched from Egypt. Metaxas died on January 29, 1941, and his successor continued the policy of resistance while seeking to avoid giving Germany a pretext for intervention. Greek leaders hoped for British help but feared that a large British presence would provoke Germany. Britain, for its part, was constrained by obligations in Africa and by limited resources, and the commitment of Commonwealth forces to Greece was strategically controversial then and since.

Germany's eventual intervention is best understood as a response to several concerns, not as a single calculation. Hitler was worried that British air bases in Greece could threaten the Romanian oil fields, that Italy's defeat might damage the Axis, and that the Balkan flank should be secured before the planned campaign against the Soviet Union. In December 1940, Germany issued directives to prepare an operation against Greece through Bulgaria. These considerations were separate from Italian prestige, though Italian failure created the occasion. Historians generally distinguish between the Italian invasion of October 1940, the German intervention of April 1941 and the subsequent conquest and occupation, since each had different causes and consequences.

Cause and Effect

Italian Setbacks and Germany's Balkan Intervention

Long-term causes

Italian ambitions in the Balkans since the 1920s, Italian dominance in Albania after April 1939, German economic and strategic interest in Romanian oil, and the weaknesses of Italian logistics and industry.

Short-term causes

Mussolini's wish to act independently after German moves in Romania, Italian failure in Greece, the British presence on Greek airfields and Hitler's plans for the Soviet Union.

Event or transformation

Italian defeat in Albania and Greece leads to German planning for Operation Marita and, after the Yugoslav coup, to a wider Balkan campaign in April 1941.

Immediate effects

Rapid conquest of Yugoslavia and Greece, deployment of German forces to the Balkans, and a more dependent Italian position within the Axis.

Long-term consequences

A long and costly occupation of southeastern Europe, resistance and civil conflict, and a heightened German commitment to the Mediterranean.

Historical complexity

Some accounts claim that the Balkan campaign delayed Operation Barbarossa and caused Germany's defeat before Moscow. Historians treat this cautiously. The invasion was postponed from May to June 1941, but spring floods, the state of German logistics, preparation of forces and strategic decisions also bore on the timing. The Balkan campaign cannot be shown to be the single cause of later failure.

1940–June 1941
Section 118.5

Southeastern Europe before the Axis Invasions

By early 1941, the states of southeastern Europe occupied very different positions. Romania, Hungary and Bulgaria had joined the Tripartite Pact, in November 1940 for the first two and on March 1, 1941 for Bulgaria. Romania, which had lost territory to the Soviet Union, Hungary and Bulgaria in the summer of 1940 and whose government fell to General Ion Antonescu in September, became a German military and economic partner, with German forces entering the country in October. Hungary, under Regent Miklós Horthy, pursued territorial revision and cooperation with Germany. Bulgaria sought to recover territory lost after World War I, especially in Macedonia and Thrace, and aligned with Germany for that reason as well as from fear of its power.

Other states tried to preserve independence. Greece was at war with Italy but not with Germany. Turkey remained neutral, concluding a friendship treaty with Germany in June 1941, and its position, controlling the Turkish Straits, mattered to both sides. Yugoslavia, under the regency of Prince Paul, tried to avoid war through negotiation. These categories must be distinguished: Axis allies, countries under military occupation, neutral states and governments attempting to preserve independence through diplomacy. They were not identical, and the choices of each reflected national interests, fear and opportunity, not a single "Balkan" pattern.

Romania's position was especially consequential because oil production around Ploiești supplied German military operations. After territorial concessions in 1940, Antonescu invited German military expertise and placed Romania within the Axis alignment while pursuing its own aims and repressing political enemies. Hungary also anticipated gains in territories lost after World War I, but tensions between Hungary and Romania survived their common alignment with Berlin. Bulgaria secured southern Dobruja from Romania in 1940 and expected further territorial rewards, yet its government still weighed the domestic costs of entering different fronts of the war. Joining a pact did not make the three states identical in their military commitments or attitudes toward Germany and the Soviet Union.

Turkey presents the opposite problem of strategic exposure without entry into the war. Its control of the Straits under the Montreux Convention gave it influence over naval access between the Black Sea and Mediterranean. Turkish leaders pursued treaty and trade relationships while avoiding direct participation, judging the competing threats to sovereignty and the risks of provoking a stronger neighbor. The region's rail and river routes were similarly political resources: the Danube linked oil and grain markets, while the passage through the Vardar valley connected central Europe to the Aegean. A map that shows only national borders conceals the economic and military corridors through which Berlin attempted to exercise leverage before firing a shot.

Economic ties tied the region to Germany. Southeastern European states exported grain, minerals, timber and, in Romania's case, oil, and imported German manufactured goods, which German trade policy used to shape their dependence. Romanian oil, centered on Ploiești, was vital to the German war economy, and its security gave Berlin a strong interest in the stability of the Balkans. Roads, railways and the Danube formed transportation routes of strategic importance, and German diplomacy sought to secure them without military expenditure.

Yugoslavia illustrates the dangers of retrospective explanation. It was a multinational state of Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, Bosnian Muslims, Macedonians, Montenegrins, Albanians, Hungarians, Germans and others, with Orthodox, Catholic and Muslim populations and a significant Jewish community. Political tension over the balance between Serb-dominated central government and Croatian autonomy was real, and the Cvetković-Maček agreement of 1939 addressed it only partly. But the state's later dismemberment was not inevitable. It followed from foreign invasion in April 1941, and from the decisions of the occupiers and local movements that exploited and deepened existing divisions. Historians therefore examine the interwar conflicts as background, not as an explanation sufficient in itself.

Section 118.6

Yugoslavia's March Crisis and the Axis Invasion of April 1941

By early 1941 German pressure on Yugoslavia was intense. With Bulgaria joining the Pact and German troops on its borders, Prince Paul's government agreed to adhere to the Tripartite Pact, and the agreement was signed in Vienna on March 25, 1941. The terms were negotiated to limit the obligations of Yugoslavia, which would not be asked to permit the passage of troops, but many Serbian officers and politicians regarded the signing as capitulation. Two days later, on March 27, a group of air force and army officers led by General Dušan Simović overthrew the regency, declared the seventeen-year-old King Peter II to be of age and formed a new government. Crowds demonstrated in Belgrade and other cities, in particular in Serb-populated areas, while reactions in Croatia and Slovenia were more mixed.

The new government tried to avoid war and made attempts to reassure Berlin about the Tripartite Pact, although the coup and public repudiation created diplomatic uncertainty. Hitler treated the coup as a provocation and on March 27 ordered the destruction of Yugoslavia as a military and national unit. The attack began on April 6, 1941, with air attacks on Belgrade, which caused heavy civilian casualties, followed by invasion from Germany, Austria, Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria, and by Italian forces from the west. Hungary joined the attack on April 11. Yugoslav forces were deployed along the frontiers in an attempt to defend all territory, were poorly equipped for mobile war, and were weakened by uncertainty over the loyalty of some units. German armor moved rapidly, and Belgrade was taken on April 12.

The diplomatic sequence after the coup needs careful separation from the symbolism of Belgrade's street demonstrations. The Simović government did not immediately and unequivocally rescind Yugoslavia's adherence to the Tripartite Pact. It attempted to reassure Germany and avoid an impossible confrontation, even as popular demonstrations and different ministers sent conflicting signals. Hitler, who regarded the coup as a strategic and political challenge, ordered military preparations on March 27 rather than waiting for a formal denunciation. This combination of contested public legitimacy, attempted diplomatic accommodation and an aggressive German decision makes the invasion intelligible without presenting Yugoslavia as having deliberately chosen war with Germany.

Yugoslav forces faced an extremely difficult military problem. The army's extensive frontier dispositions left few mobile reserves against simultaneous thrusts; transport and aircraft were insufficient, and coordination among commands weakened quickly. German units descending from Bulgaria and advancing from the north cut key communications, while Italian and Hungarian participation increased the number of threatened fronts. The bombing of Belgrade struck civilians as well as military targets and disrupted governmental communications. Local political divisions mattered but did not operate in a vacuum: they were exploited by attacking states and by separatist movements that expected power after defeat. The surrender of field forces did not extinguish the country's legal claims or prevent the rapid growth of organized resistance.

The Yugoslav high command surrendered on April 17, with the armistice taking effect the next day. The government and the young king left the country and continued, from exile, to be recognized as the legitimate Yugoslav authority by Britain and other Allied governments. The military capitulation must be distinguished from what followed. The invasion destroyed the state's central institutions, but the partition and administration of Yugoslav territory, discussed in Section 118.13, were decisions made afterward by Germany, Italy, Hungary, Bulgaria and local collaborators. Some units fought on, and in the countryside weapons and officers remained, which would form part of the basis for later resistance.

The speed of conquest, about eleven days, has often been taken as evidence that Yugoslavia was an artificial state ready to collapse. Historians are cautious here. Military collapse resulted from surprise, technological disadvantage, an overextended deployment and the political divisions within the army, but a quick defeat is not proof of national disunity. Many Yugoslav soldiers fought, and the later resistance showed that the occupation did not command acquiescence.

Section 118.7

Germany Invades Greece: The Collapse of the Mainland Defense

Operation Marita, the German invasion of Greece, began on April 6, 1941, from Bulgarian territory. Greece's strongest forces were concentrated in Albania against the Italians, and the fortifications of the Metaxas Line, along the Bulgarian frontier, were manned by a limited number of troops. The Greek government had accepted British assistance, and by early April a force including British, Australian and New Zealand units, usually called W Force and commanded by General Henry Maitland Wilson, had been landed in Greece and was deployed along a line in the north that was meant to be held jointly with Greek and Yugoslav forces.

The line collapsed when Yugoslavia was invaded, since German forces advanced through southern Yugoslavia and into Greek Macedonia, outflanking both Greek and Allied positions. German troops reached Salonika on April 9, cutting off the Greek forces in eastern Macedonia, which capitulated. The Greek army in Epirus and Albania, still engaged against Italian forces, was caught between enemies, and its commander, General Georgios Tsolakoglou, concluded an armistice with the Germans on about April 20–21, later extended to include Italy. The decision, taken by a general without clear authority from the government, was controversial and Tsolakoglou later headed the first collaborationist government.

The Metaxas Line was not an imaginary defense or one that every garrison abandoned after the first German assault. Greek troops held fortified positions against direct attacks in several sectors and continued fighting after their operational situation became untenable. Germany's advance through Yugoslavia bypassed the strongest defenses and threatened Thessaloniki, changing the strategic significance of local successes. This distinction matters because the failure of a defense system can result from encirclement rather than its fortifications being individually ineffective. The defeat also reflected the earlier concentration of Greek units against Italy in Albania, where they had sustained a demanding winter campaign with little opportunity for rapid redeployment.

British-led forces confronted a parallel dilemma. Their presence was intended to support Greece politically and militarily, but the available divisions lacked the air cover and depth to withstand the full German offensive after the Yugoslav front collapsed. Rearguard actions protected the southward withdrawal, yet repeated evacuations lost heavy equipment and required Royal Navy ships vulnerable to air attack. The Greek army's separate negotiations with German commanders and the departure of the lawful government created a legitimacy crisis before occupation even began. Historians should distinguish the obligation to support an ally from the more specific question of whether the size and timing of the expedition could realistically alter the campaign's outcome.

The British, Australian and New Zealand troops withdrew southward, fighting rearguard actions at Thermopylae and elsewhere, while the Royal Navy and merchant ships carried out an evacuation from beaches in the Peloponnese and Attica. German paratroops seized the Corinth Canal bridge on April 26, and German forces entered Athens on April 27, raising the swastika on the Acropolis. Around fifty thousand Allied troops were evacuated, but vehicles, heavy equipment and many soldiers were lost. The Greek government and king left for Crete and later for Egypt. A new government in Athens was established under German and Italian authority.

The mainland campaign must be distinguished from the war on Crete and from the occupation that followed. German victory was swift, but Greece was not conquered in the sense that its society had been pacified, as the famine, resistance and reprisals of Section 118.16 show. For the Allies, the campaign was a military defeat and a political commitment that had cost forces that might have been used in Africa. Whether it was nevertheless justified by its political and symbolic importance to Greece and to Britain's standing has been debated since, a question discussed in the historical debate at the end of this chapter.

Section 118.8

The Battle of Crete: Airborne Assault, Evacuation, and Strategic Consequences

Crete lay in a strategic position in the eastern Mediterranean, controlling the approaches to the Aegean and offering airfields from which aircraft could attack shipping and, potentially, the Romanian oil fields. After the evacuation from Greece, the island was held by Commonwealth, British and Greek forces, among them many soldiers evacuated from the mainland who were poorly equipped, under the command of the New Zealand general Bernard Freyberg. Allied intelligence, which included decrypted German signals, gave warning of an airborne attack, but the defenders had too few aircraft, anti-aircraft guns and transport to prepare fully, and the information was difficult to use without revealing its source.

Operation Mercury, the German airborne invasion, began on the morning of May 20, 1941, with paratroops and glider troops landing around Maleme airfield, Chania (Canea), Rethymno and Heraklion. The Luftwaffe had air superiority, and German losses on the first day were severe, in some places catastrophic. The fate of the operation turned on Maleme. A miscommunication and the withdrawal of New Zealand troops from key positions near the airfield during the night of May 20–21 allowed German forces to hold it, and transport aircraft began to land reinforcements. Command decisions on the Allied side, including the interpretation of risk of seaborne landings and the handling of counterattacks, have been debated at length.

The struggle for Maleme airfield illustrates how a local tactical uncertainty could become a decisive operational change. The defenders inflicted severe losses on the first German airborne wave, and many parachutists landed without their heavy weapons. Confusion over communications and the assessment of positions on Hill 107 contributed to the withdrawal of the New Zealand force overlooking the airfield. German aircraft could then land reinforcements and supplies, allowing surviving airborne troops to establish a more secure position. This sequence is more illuminating than a general assertion that air power alone captured Crete; the result depended on command decisions, ground resistance, contested information and the exploitation of one vital landing site.

The evacuation at sea and the subsequent treatment of civilians are equally central. Royal Navy crews operated near hostile airfields and suffered losses while transporting troops, and not every Allied soldier reached an evacuation vessel. Cretan civilians had joined fighting in many locations; German commanders later punished villages and hostages in brutal reprisals. Surviving Greek and Allied soldiers went into hiding or escape networks, helping connect the 1941 campaign to a longer history of resistance. The experience of the island therefore had two chronologies: the brief battle that ended in German victory and the occupation in which German control remained violent and disputed.

The Royal Navy intercepted German convoys of troops crossing the sea and turned them back with heavy loss of life among the Germans, but exposed ships to air attack and itself suffered substantial casualties, including the loss of cruisers and destroyers. Greek civilians took part in the defense, fighting with improvised weapons and sheltering troops, and Cretan civilians were later the targets of German reprisals. The Allied forces withdrew across the island, and most of those who could be taken off were evacuated from Sphakia on the south coast until the organized defense ended on about June 1. Many soldiers were left behind and became prisoners, and some, with local help, evaded capture.

Crete was a German success achieved at a high cost. Elite paratroop units sustained heavy losses, and historians commonly argue that this contributed to German reluctance to undertake further large airborne operations, though the extent of Hitler's reservations is debated. German aircraft from Crete later attacked British shipping, but the island did not become the base for an offensive toward Egypt, since limited supply capacity and the demands of the planned attack on the Soviet Union constrained the Axis. For Crete's population the consequences were immediate. German forces carried out reprisals in villages such as Kondomari, in early June 1941, and the island entered a long occupation marked by hardship and by resistance that included local groups and British intelligence agents.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Crete before and after the German Invasion

What Changed

  • Control of the island passed from Greek and Allied authority to German military administration, with Italian forces later holding the east.
  • Villages faced requisitioning, hostage-taking and reprisals, while food supplies and movement became restricted.
  • Crete became a base for Axis air and naval activity in the eastern Mediterranean and a place where local guerrilla warfare and British clandestine operations took root.

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture, olive and grain production and the village economy remained the basis of life for most islanders.
  • Strong local loyalties, extended family networks and traditions of armed independence continued, shaping both resistance and its costs.
  • The island's strategic position meant that it remained, as for centuries before, a place contested by outside powers.

Who experienced change most directly: villagers near the landing zones and those subject to reprisals experienced the sharpest change, while families in remote mountain communities experienced somewhat greater continuity, though they too bore requisition and the risks of resistance. Why the pattern matters: Crete shows that a swift military victory could begin, not end, a period of political and social turmoil.

Section 118.9

Rommel, the Afrika Korps, and the Siege of Tobruk

The collapse of the Italian position in Cyrenaica led Hitler to send a German blocking force to Libya. The first units, under Lieutenant General Erwin Rommel, arrived at Tripoli in February 1941, forming what became known as the Afrika Korps. The German force was initially small and was intended to prevent an Italian collapse, not to conquer Egypt. Italian troops, however, formed the majority of Axis forces in North Africa and remained nominally under Italian command, so that cooperation between German and Italian headquarters was a constant source of tension. Rommel, an energetic and unorthodox commander, exploited a moment of British weakness: Wavell's forces had been reduced by the Greek commitment, and the desert army was in transition.

Rommel's offensive began at the end of March 1941, in part against the instructions of his superiors, and it moved with surprising speed across Cyrenaica, recapturing Benghazi and driving the British and Commonwealth forces back to the Egyptian frontier. Two British generals, including O'Connor, were captured in the retreat. Only the port of Tobruk remained in Allied hands, held by a garrison built around the 9th Australian Division, together with British and Indian units. The Axis forces invested the town from April 11, 1941.

Tobruk's defense was sustained by a system of perimeter positions, mobile counterattacks and supply by sea. Australian formations initially formed much of the garrison, while later British, Polish and other Allied units contributed as the siege evolved. Axis forces could threaten the port from land but could not use it to shorten their supply line while it remained in Allied hands. Naval supply and medical evacuation were hazardous; nevertheless, the garrison's ability to receive limited reinforcement prevented the siege from being only a passive defensive ordeal. It also tied down German and Italian formations that otherwise might have advanced eastward.

Rommel's energetic moves produced surprise and sometimes substantial tactical gains, but they also exposed the gap between battlefield opportunity and strategic sustainability. Fuel consumed by vehicles reaching the front had first to survive the sea crossing and then a long road journey from a functioning port. Italian formations, including artillery and infantry, carried much of the fighting and cannot simply be erased behind the Afrika Korps label. A comparison of German, Italian and British war diaries shows commanders adjusting plans to these limits; later heroic memoirs often obscure them.

Tobruk mattered because of its harbor, the best on the coast between Benghazi and Alexandria, and its position on the coastal supply road. Its garrison, supplied by sea at night, tied down Axis forces and prevented Rommel from advancing deeply into Egypt without leaving a threat behind him. The defenders held a fortified perimeter with antitank ditches, minefields and strongpoints, and repelled German assaults in April and May. The siege lasted for most of 1941, and the term "Rats of Tobruk," originally intended as an insult in German propaganda, was adopted by the defenders. Australian, British, Indian and later Polish and Czechoslovak troops formed part of the garrison over time, and the Royal Navy and merchant crews paid for supply with heavy losses.

Rommel's battlefield reputation, established in these months, should be distinguished from the wider constraints of the Axis campaign. His tactical skill, willingness to lead from the front and use of mobile formations were real, and Allied commanders respected him. But his operations were limited by supply difficulties, the priority given to the Eastern Front after June 1941, tensions with the Italian command and the German high command, and the scale of Allied air and naval interdiction. The popular image of the "Desert Fox" owes much to wartime propaganda on both sides and to postwar memoir and film. Historians therefore evaluate Rommel as a capable operational commander within a system that limited what any commander could achieve.

Section 118.10

Operation Crusader, Gazala, and the Changing Desert Front

British attempts to relieve Tobruk and drive the Axis from Cyrenaica began with limited operations in May and June 1941, which failed, and led to the replacement of Wavell by General Claude Auchinleck. In the autumn the British Eighth Army was formed under Lieutenant General Alan Cunningham, and on November 18, 1941, it launched Operation Crusader. The battle was a confused struggle across the desert south of Tobruk, in which armored formations fought dispersed engagements, and for some days it was unclear who was winning. Cunningham was replaced by Neil Ritchie, and the New Zealand Division, the South African units and the Australian and other forces made important contributions. The relief of Tobruk was achieved at the beginning of December, and the Axis forces withdrew westward to El Agheila by the end of the year.

The reversal was short-lived. Rommel, whose force had been resupplied and strengthened, struck back in January 1942, regaining much of Cyrenaica. The British established a line of fortified positions, the Gazala Line, running south from the coast west of Tobruk and anchored in the south on the isolated position of Bir Hakeim, held by Free French troops. Rommel's attack on May 26, 1942, went around the southern flank. The battle was fought in the area of the "Cauldron" and around Bir Hakeim, which held until it was evacuated in June. British armor was committed piecemeal and suffered heavy losses, and by mid-June the Eighth Army was in retreat.

Operation Crusader demonstrated the difficulty of maintaining control over a fast-moving battlefield. Armored formations repeatedly engaged, withdrew and regrouped while infantry fought for fortified positions along the border and the routes to Tobruk. British losses in tanks did not automatically mean the offensive had failed, because the overall operational purpose was to force the Axis to abandon the siege. Likewise, Axis tactical victories could consume fuel and vehicles that could not be replaced quickly. Commanders had to balance aggressive movement with the need to keep infantry, artillery and supply services connected in a theater where the absence of a clear front line created uncertainty.

At Gazala in 1942, strong Allied defensive boxes and extensive minefields were intended to constrain Axis movement. The German-Italian maneuver around the southern flank created opportunities, but Axis forces also faced a dangerous position near the minefields before breaking through. The capture of Tobruk on June 21 changed the available port capacity and damaged Allied confidence, yet it did not solve the logistics of an extended offensive into Egypt. The sequence should be read as interacting victories and errors, not as proof that Rommel consistently outthought every opponent.

Tobruk, which had been besieged the previous year, fell on June 21, 1942, when its garrison, which included the 2nd South African Division, surrendered after a short assault. Roughly thirty thousand men were taken prisoner, a catastrophe for the Allies. Rommel was promoted to field marshal, and the Axis advanced into Egypt, reaching the El Alamein line around ninety kilometers from Alexandria. The news reached Churchill in Washington, and its political effects in Britain were considerable.

The campaign was a sequence of reversals, not a series of unbroken Axis victories. The advance and retreat across Cyrenaica occurred three times between 1941 and 1942 because supply lines lengthened as an army advanced and shortened as it retreated, giving the retreating side an advantage in resources. Armored warfare, antitank guns, minefields, artillery, infantry positions and air power all influenced outcomes. Allied performance suffered from poor coordination of armor, infantry and artillery, and from the dispersal of formations, while German forces exploited concentration, tactical flexibility and effective antitank use. Intelligence also mattered, including signals intercepts used by both sides. Troops from Britain, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, India, Free France, Poland and elsewhere fought alongside each other, and the composition of the army was one of the features of the campaign.

Section 118.11

Logistics, Intelligence, Malta, and the Imperial Dimensions of Desert Warfare

Supply determined the desert war. An army in Libya needed not only ammunition and fuel but also water, food, spare parts and replacements, and each had to be moved over very long distances. Axis supplies crossed the Mediterranean to Tripoli, Benghazi and Tobruk, and then traveled hundreds of kilometers by road or coastal shipping to the front. Allied supplies came around Africa, reached Suez, Alexandria or Port Said, and moved by rail and road to the front. The port capacity of Tripoli, the limited roads and the shortage of trucks all constrained Rommel, while the long distance from the Allied base in Egypt to the front in Cyrenaica constrained the British. Historians such as Martin van Creveld have emphasized that the farther an army moved from its supply base, the more its effective power diminished.

Malta's offensive role was central. When air and naval forces were strong on the island, in late 1941 and again in the second half of 1942, Axis shipping losses rose sharply, and fuel shortages affected Rommel's operations. When the Luftwaffe established air superiority over the island in spring 1942, Malta nearly ceased to be an effective base, and convoys to the island were costly. Operation Pedestal, carried out from August 10 to 15, 1942, brought fourteen merchant ships escorted by a large naval force, of which five reached Malta, including the damaged tanker Ohio, which arrived on August 15 under tow. The convoy's cost was high, but it prevented the island's surrender and restored its capacity to disrupt Axis supply.

Imperial labor systems formed part of those supply calculations. Maintenance depots, rail lines and road convoys depended on workers whose names rarely entered operational communiqués. Egyptian laborers and officials had to negotiate the British military presence in a country with formal sovereignty but limited freedom of action in the canal zone. Libyan communities lived with Italian rule, military requisitions and the hazards of movement through contested territory. In French North Africa, the status of Muslims, European settlers and Jewish communities was unequal under colonial law, and the arrival of American forces did not automatically abolish these arrangements. A history focused only on generals and trucks would miss the social relationships that made transport possible.

Intelligence also required interpretation rather than mere possession. Signals intercepts might reveal a convoy's sailing time but not what cargo would reach a desert unit after unloading and road transport. Allied commanders had to decide when to exploit information without exposing methods; Axis staffs also learned from reconnaissance, prisoner reports and captured documents. This complex decision chain explains why information advantage could be substantial yet not always deliver an immediate battlefield result.

Intelligence formed a second dimension. British signals intelligence, including decrypted German and Italian messages, gave information on convoy movements, and aerial reconnaissance and submarine patrols helped to target them. The Axis likewise benefitted from intercepts of Allied messages at certain times. Historians stress that intelligence did not by itself decide battles, because it needed to be interpreted, protected, and acted upon with sufficient forces. Its importance lies in how it interacted with industrial supply, air power and command.

The war was not fought only by soldiers and commanders. Dockworkers at Alexandria, Port Said, Tripoli and other ports, merchant sailors, railway workers, drivers, construction laborers and clerks sustained the armies. Egyptian, Sudanese, Palestinian, Cypriot, Indian and East African laborers and auxiliaries were part of Allied support, and many people were recruited under conditions that varied from voluntary service to compulsion. In Libya, local Arab and Berber communities, long subject to Italian colonial rule and to the severe repression of the 1920s and 1930s, found their lands crossed by armies, their wells and livestock requisitioned, and their villages affected by minefields, which remained dangerous long after the war. Italian settlers, colonial soldiers and Jewish communities in Libya also experienced the war in different ways. In Egypt, nominally independent but under British influence, the war brought inflation, shortages and political tensions, including the British intervention at the royal palace in February 1942.

Wartime imperial rhetoric spoke of common struggle, but the interests of those involved were not the same. Indian troops fought in an army whose government had declared war on India's behalf without consulting its political leaders, and nationalist leaders such as those of the Indian National Congress were divided in their response. Australian and New Zealand governments committed forces partly out of loyalty to Britain and partly out of concern for their own security, which was soon threatened in the Pacific. Egyptian nationalists were divided between hostility to British control and fear of Axis occupation. Historians therefore distinguish the common military effort from the diversity of political purposes.

Section 118.12

The Battles of El Alamein and the Turning of the Desert War

El Alamein was chosen as a defensive position because the Qattara Depression, an impassable area of soft sand and salt marsh, lay about sixty kilometers to the south, preventing the Axis from outflanking the line by moving around the southern end. The Axis forces, though exhausted, attacked in the First Battle of El Alamein, beginning on July 1, 1942. Auchinleck, who had taken direct command of the Eighth Army, used the remaining reserves and the strength of British artillery and air power to halt the advance, and further attacks in July failed to break through. The Axis forces were also stopped in the Battle of Alam el Halfa, from August 30 to September 5. These battles stabilized the front and protected Alexandria and the Suez Canal.

In August 1942 Churchill reorganized the command. General Harold Alexander became commander-in-chief in the Middle East and Lieutenant General Bernard Montgomery took command of the Eighth Army after the death of the originally designated commander. Montgomery restored morale, concentrated forces, and insisted on thorough preparation and the coordination of infantry, armor, artillery and air support. By October, the Eighth Army had a substantial superiority over the Axis forces in tanks, guns, aircraft and supplies, and the Axis position suffered from fuel shortages and from the sinking of tankers by Allied aircraft and submarines.

The first battle at El Alamein and Montgomery's later offensive should not be merged. In July, British and Commonwealth defenders halted an Axis army already exhausted by its advance from Libya; their success established a defensible position between the Mediterranean and the Qattara Depression. The terrain limited wide flanking movements and made artillery, minefields and coordinated infantry attacks more important than some earlier desert maneuvers. The September fighting at Alam el Halfa further constrained Axis options. When Montgomery began the October offensive, the Eighth Army therefore benefited not simply from a new commander but from months of attrition, intelligence, logistics and improving relative strength.

The second battle imposed heavy costs on both armies and required repeated attacks to create lanes through minefields. The Allied breakout also depended on Axis shortages of fuel, reduced air support and the difficulty of replacing trained personnel. For participants from India, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and Britain, the victory was experienced through different national and unit histories. Later political claims sometimes presented El Alamein as the moment when the entire war turned; historians should instead place it beside Stalingrad, American entry and the imminent Torch landings, each operating on a different scale and timetable.

The Second Battle of El Alamein began on the night of October 23, 1942, with a massive artillery barrage and an attack through the Axis minefields, Operation Lightfoot. The initial infantry attack made slower progress than planned, and Rommel, who had been on sick leave in Germany, returned to command after the death of his stand-in, General Georg Stumme. After several days of attrition, Montgomery launched a second blow, Operation Supercharge, on November 2, and the Axis line broke. The Italian and German forces began a retreat on November 4, and Italian infantry units lacking transport were often left behind. By mid-November the Eighth Army had retaken Tobruk and Benghazi.

The victory was important for Egypt and the Suez Canal, which was no longer under threat, and for Allied morale, which had suffered from repeated reversals. It also ended Axis hopes of reaching the Middle East. But its significance should not be exaggerated. The battle was won with superior resources, and the Eighth Army's pursuit was slow, allowing Axis forces to escape into Tunisia, where the fighting continued until May 1943. Churchill's later remark that before Alamein the Allies had hardly a victory and afterward hardly a defeat is memorable, but historians note that it simplifies a more complex war, including the Soviet victories at Stalingrad and the Pacific campaigns. Alamein was a turning point in the Mediterranean theater, not necessarily in the war as a whole.

Historical Vocabulary
Attrition, Operational Breakthrough, Supply Line, and Strategic Turning Point

Attrition is a method of war that seeks victory by wearing down the enemy's men and matériel faster than they can be replaced, which at Alamein meant a deliberate and costly "crumbling" of Axis positions before breakthrough. An operational breakthrough occurs when an attacker penetrates the enemy's defensive line and can exploit the gap with mobile forces; the Eighth Army achieved one only after several days of fighting. A supply line is the route by which armies receive their needs, and its length and security often decide how far an army can advance. A strategic turning point is an event after which the balance of a war shifts, though such labels are retrospective and contested. Together, these terms help explain why Alamein, a battle won by material superiority and persistent pressure, changed the desert campaign without ending it.

1941–1943, with selective later developments
Section 118.13

The Partition of Yugoslavia and Greece: Occupation and Political Reconstruction

Yugoslavia did not become a single occupied territory. Germany, Italy, Hungary and Bulgaria divided it, and Axis-sponsored local regimes governed parts of it. Germany annexed northern Slovenia and placed Serbia, including the Banat, under a military administration, later supported by a Serbian government led by General Milan Nedić from August 1941. Italy annexed southern Slovenia, including Ljubljana, much of the Dalmatian coast and several islands, occupied Montenegro and installed an administration there, and attached Kosovo and parts of western Macedonia to Italian-controlled Albania. Hungary annexed the Bačka and areas on the northern borders. Bulgaria took most of Vardar Macedonia and parts of southeastern Serbia. Each occupier ruled differently, and the populations affected experienced very different regimes.

On April 10, 1941, the Ustaša, a Croatian nationalist movement that had been in exile and had enjoyed little electoral support before the war, proclaimed the Independent State of Croatia (NDH) in Zagreb, with German approval. Its leader, Ante Pavelić, returned from Italy and assumed the title of Poglavnik. The NDH included Croatia, Slavonia and Bosnia-Herzegovina, and had a large Serb minority, Muslims and a Jewish community. It was formally independent but depended on Germany and Italy, and its territory was divided into German and Italian zones of military influence. Italy, which received concessions on the Adriatic coast, treated the NDH with considerable condescension and its forces in the Italian zone were at times at odds with the Ustaša.

The map of Yugoslavia after April 1941 was not only divided by international frontiers but by overlapping military jurisdictions. German authorities established a military government in Serbia and pursued control of strategic communications and raw materials. Italian officials administered annexed and occupied territories through varying civilian and military arrangements; Hungary incorporated northern districts, and Bulgaria occupied much of Vardar Macedonia and the Pirot area. In Croatia and Bosnia, the nominal sovereignty claimed by the NDH did not eliminate Italian and German military influence. Distinguishing direct annexation, military occupation, local client administration and disputed incorporation is essential for tracing who ordered requisitions, controlled police and punished civilians.

These categories also affected how people identified themselves and their prospects for survival. Some residents encountered imposed nationality rules, restrictions on language or schooling, and the confiscation of property. Others were driven across newly asserted boundaries, joined armies or resistance movements, or attempted to maintain local trade despite checkpoints and military patrols. In occupied Greece, Bulgarian authorities pursued policies of forced assimilation and displacement in eastern Macedonia and Thrace, while German authorities prioritized key transport links and ports and Italian forces controlled extensive mainland zones. General descriptions of 'Axis occupation' therefore conceal distinct chains of command and different degrees of danger in adjacent districts.

Greece was divided into three zones. Germany held Athens, Salonika, parts of Macedonia, Crete's main centers and strategic islands, and the northeastern border region along the Turkish frontier. Italy occupied most of the mainland and the Ionian islands, along with the eastern part of Crete. Bulgaria annexed western Thrace and eastern Macedonia, where it imposed Bulgarianization policies, closed Greek schools and expelled or pressured Greek residents. A Greek collaborationist government, headed first by Tsolakoglou, administered the areas under occupation without independent power. The legal claims of the occupiers must be distinguished from the control actually exercised. In Bosnia, in the mountains of Montenegro and in parts of Greece, the occupiers held towns and main roads, while rural regions were contested or controlled by insurgents.

Albania, annexed by Italy in 1939 and enlarged in 1941 with Kosovo and western Macedonian territories, had a separate position. It was ruled by Italian officials and a local government, with the Albanian king Zog in exile. Albanian nationalists welcomed unification with Kosovo, while many Slavic inhabitants of those regions experienced the changes as oppression, and communist and nationalist resistance groups emerged. Historians should treat these changes as results of occupation and war, not as outcomes of timeless ethnic hostility, and must also recognize that occupiers deliberately exploited local rivalries.

Section 118.14

The Ustaša Regime, Ethnic Persecution, and Mass Violence

The Ustaša movement under Ante Pavelić combined Croatian nationalism with an authoritarian, racial and Catholic-clerical rhetoric, and drew on Italian Fascist and German National Socialist models. Almost immediately after taking power, the regime issued racial laws, in April and May 1941, that defined Jews and Roma as outside the national community, and discriminated against Serbs through measures that closed Serbian Orthodox institutions, banned the Cyrillic alphabet and imposed expulsion and forced conversion. The goal expressed by some Ustaša leaders was to remove or assimilate the large Serbian population. Not all Croats supported these policies, and some, including members of the Catholic clergy and the peasant party, objected, while others participated.

Mass killing began in the spring and summer of 1941. In villages in Bosnia, Herzegovina, Lika and Slavonia, Ustaša units and local militias massacred Serbian civilians, burned villages and churches and drove survivors from their homes, while other Serbs were deported to Serbia. The regime established a system of camps in 1941, of which the Jasenovac complex, on the Sava River, was the largest, with its first camps opened in August 1941 and operating, with changes, until April 1945. Victims included Serbs, Jews, Roma and Croatian and other political opponents. Jews were rounded up and killed in Jasenovac and other camps, or deported to German camps, and the great majority of Croatia's Jewish community, estimated at some thirty to forty thousand, was killed. The Roma population suffered mass killings as well, in Jasenovac and elsewhere.

The Ustaša campaign against Serbs was not limited to formal internment. Local militias, police and officials participated in expulsions, coerced religious conversions and massacres, while the regime enacted racial measures against Jews and Roma. Jasenovac was a major site of incarceration and killing, but it cannot stand for every place where Ustaša forces murdered civilians. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum estimates approximately 77,000 to 99,000 people were killed in the Jasenovac camp complex between 1941 and 1945. The Jasenovac Memorial Site has identified more than 83,000 victims by name, a documentary achievement that should not be mistaken for a final, comprehensive total.

Occupation politics further complicated the violence. In some Italian-controlled areas, military commanders clashed with the Ustaša and offered temporary refuge to threatened civilians, yet Italian forces also carried out reprisals, internment and coercive operations of their own. Some communities attempted to survive by moving among occupation zones or negotiating with whichever armed group controlled a road or village. The NDH regime's dependence on outside powers did not remove its responsibility for policy choices or the initiative taken by its own security forces. Its crimes must be distinguished from those of the Nazi German regime, other Axis authorities and rival local militias.

Quantitative evidence requires care. The number of people killed at Jasenovac and in the NDH as a whole has been disputed since the war. Postwar Yugoslav authorities gave very high figures, some nationalist writers have minimized the killing, and others have inflated it. Named-victim research and demographic reconstruction establish the large scale of the killing, but different totals can describe camp murders, other massacres, or the war-wide losses of particular populations and must not be conflated. Students should treat any single figure with caution, identify who produced it and by what method, and distinguish deaths in camps from killings in villages, wartime deaths from disease and the victims of different perpetrators.

Responsibility must also be distinguished. The Ustaša regime planned and carried out many of the crimes on its own authority, but it did so within a state created under Axis protection. German forces and officials took part in the deportation of Jews and in some killings, and German pressure on the regime in that respect is documented. Italian officers in their zone sometimes protected Serbs and Jews from the Ustaša, for reasons that ranged from humanitarian impulse to rivalry and political interest, though the Italian occupation was itself repressive. The regime's actions also provoked Serb uprisings and strengthened both the Chetniks and Partisans, which transformed Bosnia and Croatia into a zone of intense violence. This history belongs both to Yugoslav history and to the wider history of the Holocaust (Chapter 121).

Section 118.15

Yugoslav Resistance: Tito's Partisans, the Chetniks, and the Civil War within the War

Two main resistance movements emerged in occupied Yugoslavia. The Chetniks, led by Colonel Draža Mihailović, who had refused to surrender after April 1941 and established a headquarters on Ravna Gora in Serbia, were royalist and Serb-oriented, identified with the exiled government, which made Mihailović minister of war in January 1942. Their name recalled earlier irregular bands, and their organization was decentralized, with local commanders enjoying considerable autonomy. Mihailović's strategy was to build up forces and avoid premature action that would provoke reprisals against civilians, awaiting an Allied landing. Many Chetnik leaders were committed to a postwar Yugoslavia with a dominant Serb role.

The Partisans, led by Josip Broz Tito, secretary-general of the Communist Party of Yugoslavia, began armed resistance after Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, with uprisings in Serbia, Montenegro and Bosnia. The movement was Communist-led but recruited broadly among Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, Muslims and others, on a platform of national liberation and a federal postwar Yugoslavia. The Partisans' multinational character and willingness to engage the occupiers, even at the cost of reprisals, helped them to grow. In late 1941 they established a liberated area around Užice in western Serbia, which German forces crushed in autumn 1941, forcing the Partisans to retreat into Bosnia and Montenegro.

The Partisans developed a political organization alongside their military units, including committees through which they claimed to represent a future Yugoslavia based on a federation of peoples. This appealed to some communities exposed to nationalist persecution, but their authority depended on practical control, recruitment and the ability to supply men and women in difficult mountain areas. Chetnik units often claimed loyalty to the royal government and prioritized preservation of their own forces; in particular districts commanders reached accommodations with Italian or German authorities while fighting Partisans. Other Chetnik groups had earlier attacked the occupation or resisted particular measures. The variation cannot be resolved by assigning one unchanging label to every unit.

German and Italian anti-partisan operations made the civilian landscape central to military strategy. Large sweeps, including operations in Bosnia in 1942 and 1943, aimed to restrict guerrilla mobility by controlling villages, food supplies and roads. Such actions generated displacement and reprisals and could simultaneously strengthen resistance recruitment. The British shift toward supporting Tito's movement grew out of changing assessments of its military activity, supported by liaison reports and intelligence, as well as broader diplomacy. Historians compare those contemporary assessments with local records rather than treating later partisan or royalist memoirs as neutral descriptions.

The relationship between the movements was complex. They cooperated briefly in autumn 1941, but fell into conflict over strategy, leadership and the future of Yugoslavia, and by late 1941 were fighting each other. Some Chetnik units, especially in Italian-occupied zones of Montenegro, Dalmatia and Herzegovina, concluded local arrangements with Italian forces against the Partisans, and later with Germans, while others continued to resist or remained passive, and Mihailović's own position on these arrangements has been debated. The Partisans, for their part, committed killings of political opponents and suspected collaborators. A civil war thus developed within the war, entangled with the ethnic violence unleashed by the Ustaša and the repression by occupiers. Allied assessments changed over time. British liaison officers sent to both movements reported by 1943 that the Partisans were fighting the Axis more actively, and Allied support moved gradually from Mihailović toward Tito, a shift reinforced by the Tehran conference at the end of that year.

Civilians bore the heaviest burden. Axis forces, particularly in major offensives in the winter and spring of 1943, carried out reprisals, burned villages, took hostages and executed civilians in numbers far above any attacks on their own troops. Armed groups requisitioned food, and villages might be under the control of different forces within a few months. Bosnia, Montenegro, Serbia, Croatia and the Adriatic hinterland were the principal theaters, and the rugged mountains and forests provided refuge and made occupation difficult. Neither movement was uniform across place and time: Partisan units differed widely, Chetnik groups ranged from resisters to collaborators, and the same commander might change policy as circumstances changed. In November 1942 and November 1943 the Partisans proclaimed a political body, the Anti-Fascist Council (AVNOJ), at Bihać and Jajce, laying the foundation for postwar government.

Historical Vocabulary
Partisans, Collaboration, Resistance Movement, and Civil War

Partisans, in the Yugoslav context, refers to the Communist-led People's Liberation Army and Partisan detachments, and more generally to irregular fighters operating behind enemy lines. Collaboration means cooperation with an occupying power, which ranged from official government service, through economic and local accommodation, to armed alliance, and historians distinguish among motives and degrees. A resistance movement is an organized effort to oppose an occupier or regime, whether by armed struggle, sabotage, intelligence work or civil refusal, and such movements often had political goals beyond expelling the occupier. Civil war describes armed conflict among groups from the same society, here intertwined with foreign occupation. These terms matter because Yugoslav and Greek movements cannot be understood as simply heroic or villainous; they were political and military organizations that sometimes resisted, sometimes accommodated, and sometimes fought each other.

Section 118.16

Greece under Occupation: Famine, Resistance, Persecution, and Civilian Survival

Occupied Greece suffered one of the most severe famines in wartime western and southern Europe. Greece had normally imported part of its grain, and the invasion disrupted farming and transport. The occupiers requisitioned food and other supplies, Bulgaria took over the productive agricultural regions of Macedonia and Thrace, the Allied naval blockade cut off imports, and the Greek currency collapsed in an inflation that wiped out savings and wages. The winter of 1941 and 1942 was the worst period, especially in Athens, Piraeus and the islands, where the urban population depended on food that was no longer available. Estimates of the number of deaths vary greatly, depending on the period, method and area, and run from around a hundred thousand to considerably higher figures; they should be treated as uncertain.

Relief gradually emerged. After international appeals and negotiations among the Greek Red Cross, the Swedish and international Red Cross, the occupiers and the Allied governments, Britain relaxed the blockade for humanitarian shipments, and in the following months Swedish ships carried grain from Canada under the supervision of a joint relief commission, with soup kitchens operating in cities. The mortality fell, though hardship continued. The experiences of different groups diverged: rural families with access to land and the black market fared better than urban wage-earners, and those with gold or property to trade sometimes did well, while refugees, children and the elderly suffered most.

A close examination of famine shows why national totals require careful definitions. Greece relied on imported grain even before the invasion; requisitions, disrupted domestic transport, inflation and the Allied blockade contributed to an acute shortage during the winter of 1941–1942. Mortality differed sharply between Athens, Piraeus, some islands and agricultural regions. Demographic scholarship cited in comparative research estimates approximately 100,000–200,000 famine deaths for the acute 1941–1942 crisis; estimates covering longer intervals or indirect causes are higher and must not be mixed with that figure. A reduced birth rate represents a grave demographic consequence but is not a recorded death. The most defensible presentation names the place, interval and cause before quoting a number.

Relief was politically contested as well as humanitarian. Neutral relief organizations, Greek civic networks and the International Committee of the Red Cross negotiated permission to move food while occupying powers retained control of roads and ports. Households depended on ration cards, soup kitchens, barter, rural relatives and sometimes dangerous informal journeys. In Thessaloniki, meanwhile, German authorities progressively stripped the city's Jewish community of freedom and property, and deportations in 1943 destroyed much of that historic population. Bulgarian policies in their occupied territories differed from what happened to most Jews within Bulgaria's prewar borders; those distinct jurisdictions must not be collapsed into a single national outcome.

Resistance developed alongside hunger. The National Liberation Front (EAM), founded in September 1941 and dominated by the Communist Party of Greece, formed a military arm, the Greek People's Liberation Army (ELAS), which grew into the largest resistance force and organized civil institutions in mountain areas. The National Republican Greek League (EDES), led by Napoleon Zervas, was republican and nationalist, strongest in Epirus, and opposed to the Communist leadership of EAM. A smaller organization, EKKA, also existed. In November 1942, ELAS and EDES units cooperated with British Special Operations Executive officers to destroy the Gorgopotamos railway viaduct, interrupting supply to the Axis in North Africa, a rare moment of collaboration among them. Soon after, the movements fell into rivalry over the postwar political order, setting the stage for civil conflict that belongs to later chapters.

Reprisals were severe. German and Bulgarian forces responded to attacks by burning villages and executing hostages, and some of the worst massacres, such as those at Kalavryta in December 1943 and Distomo in June 1944, came later in the occupation. Persecution of Jews followed distinct paths in different zones. Salonika had one of the oldest and largest Jewish communities in Europe, and the German authorities began anti-Jewish measures in 1941 and 1942, concentrated the community in ghettos in early 1943, and deported most of its approximately forty-five thousand members to Auschwitz-Birkenau between March and August 1943. In the Bulgarian zone, thousands of Jews from Thrace and Macedonia were arrested in March 1943 and deported to Treblinka. In the Italian zone, Jews were generally less threatened until the German takeover after September 1943. The systematic history of the Holocaust belongs to Chapter 121, but these events shaped occupied Greece directly. Bulgaria's treatment must be understood precisely: it deported Jews from the territories it occupied, while the Jews within its prewar borders were not deported, owing to a combination of political pressure and public opposition, and an unqualified narrative of rescue would be inaccurate.

Everyday Life and Social History

Household Survival under Occupation and Famine in Greece

Families adapted to scarcity through improvisation. Urban households sold furniture, jewelry and clothing, or traveled to the countryside to barter for flour, olive oil and vegetables. Rations distributed through the municipal system were, in the worst months, far below the level needed for health. Children and elderly people were especially vulnerable, and many households sent members to relatives in villages. Women organized food collection, managed shrinking budgets and joined relief and resistance networks, while schools, churches and voluntary groups provided meals and shelter.

Rural experiences varied. Villages with productive land fed themselves better than cities, but faced requisition, conscripted labor and the danger that an attack on occupying troops nearby would bring reprisals. The Greek Orthodox Church, with its local clergy, formed part of the relief network, though its leaders' responses to the occupiers varied. Historians reconstruct this social history from relief agency records, municipal documents, oral histories, memoirs and letters, and must remember that famine mortality is hard to measure because registration systems broke down.

Allied Victory in North Africa and the Crisis of Fascist Italy

November 1942–September 1943
Section 118.17

Operation Torch: Allied Landings in French North Africa

Operation Torch, the Anglo-American invasion of French Morocco and Algeria, began on November 8, 1942. American planners had initially favored a cross-Channel invasion of northwestern Europe, but Britain argued that this was premature, and President Roosevelt wished to engage German forces in 1942. Torch was the compromise. Task forces landed at Casablanca, Oran and Algiers under the supreme command of the American general Dwight Eisenhower, with British naval and ground contributions, and the operation was designed to take the ports and then advance east into Tunisia to trap Rommel's army between two fronts.

The landings were complicated by the political status of French North Africa, which remained under authorities loyal to the Vichy government. Allied planners hoped that French forces would not resist, and had made contact with sympathetic French officers, but in some places the landings were opposed, with fighting at Casablanca, Oran and Algiers. In Algiers, a coup by pro-Allied French conspirators neutralized some local forces on the night of the landing. A cease-fire was arranged within days, in part through an agreement with Admiral François Darlan, the Vichy commander-in-chief who happened to be in Algiers. The arrangement was criticized in Britain and America as a deal with a collaborator, and Darlan was assassinated on December 24, 1942. Later, General Henri Giraud and then Charles de Gaulle's Free French organization shared leadership in the French Committee of National Liberation, established in 1943.

The initial landings were shaped by the French political problem as much as by beaches and harbors. Allied planners hoped to limit resistance from Vichy forces, but orders from French commanders in Morocco and Algeria were not uniform. At Oran fighting proved costly, while arrangements at Algiers helped reduce resistance there. The deal with Admiral François Darlan, a senior Vichy figure present in North Africa, allowed a rapid political settlement but caused controversy among antifascist and Free French supporters. The decision illustrated the tradeoff between immediate military advantage and the legitimacy of wartime political partners.

North African societies had no single reaction to the landings. European settlers enjoyed political advantages denied to many Muslim residents; colonial policing and land relations did not disappear when Allied troops arrived. Jews in Algeria had been deprived of French citizenship by Vichy measures reversing the Crémieux Decree, and the restoration of rights was neither immediate nor uncomplicated. The persecution of Jews in Tunisia intensified during the German-Italian occupation that followed the Torch landings. These differences help explain why liberation from Axis military control was not equivalent to an immediate end to colonial inequality.

North Africa was not a blank space for these campaigns. Algeria was legally part of France, with a European settler population and a large Muslim majority that lacked equal rights, while Morocco and Tunisia were protectorates. Vichy had imposed anti-Jewish laws, and in Algeria it had revoked the citizenship of Algerian Jews granted by the Crémieux decree in 1870. Many of these measures remained in force for months after the landings, a sign of how Allied priorities shaped the political outcome. Local populations experienced wartime shortages, requisitioning and the arrival of large foreign armies. Some Muslim political leaders hoped that the Allied presence and the Atlantic Charter's language of self-determination would open political change, an expectation that was largely disappointed in the short term.

Torch's strategic importance was that it created a second front in Africa. Axis forces responded immediately, rushing troops by air and sea to Tunisia, and on November 11, 1942 German and Italian forces occupied the previously unoccupied zone of metropolitan France (Chapter 117 introduced Vichy). The French fleet at Toulon was scuttled on November 27 to prevent its capture. Vichy France thus lost the remaining shadow of independence, and the Axis powers committed forces that might otherwise have been used elsewhere.

Section 118.18

Tunisia, Kasserine Pass, and the Final Axis Defeat in North Africa

The Axis response to Torch was swift. German troops and Italian units were flown and shipped into Tunisia from November 9, 1942, forming a bridgehead around Tunis and Bizerte under General Hans-Jürgen von Arnim, soon to be reinforced by Rommel's retreating army. The Allied advance eastward, by the British First Army and American and French units, was slowed by winter rains, poor roads, long supply lines and German air power, and stalled in December short of Tunis. The campaign thus extended through the winter, with the Axis holding a shrinking perimeter that could be supplied across the narrow sea from Sicily.

In mid-February 1943 the Axis counterattacked in the west. The battle at Kasserine Pass, from February 19 to 24, saw German and Italian armored forces defeat inexperienced American units, inflicting heavy losses, and push through the pass, before being stopped by Allied reinforcements, artillery and air support. Rommel, who commanded the attack, withdrew and then attacked the Eighth Army at Medenine in March, where the attack failed. Kasserine was a shock to American forces, but it prompted rapid reforms in command, training, coordination with British forces and the use of air power, and a change of commanders, including the replacement of Major General Lloyd Fredendall by George Patton at II Corps.

Kasserine exposed problems specific to a new coalition army rather than a permanent weakness in American forces. Corps and division commanders lacked common procedures for combining infantry, artillery, armor and air support, and units were dispersed across difficult terrain. The subsequent changes involved stronger command relationships and more integrated planning, not merely a replacement of one general by another. The British First Army, French units and Eighth Army were learning their own lessons about coordination at the same time. Rain, mountains and poor roads made the Tunisian campaign a different kind of war from the earlier open-desert advances.

The decisive change by spring 1943 lay in the balance of supply and strategic initiative. Axis commanders defended positions close to Sicily but faced steadily more effective Allied air and naval interdiction, while their forces had limited replacements after losses elsewhere. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives approximately 267,000 German and Italian prisoners following the May surrender; this is a defined institutional estimate for the final campaign, not a number to be added uncritically to other reported casualties. For Tunisia's population, including its Jewish communities subjected to forced labor, fines and property seizures during Axis occupation, the surrender also marked a major political and human turning point.

The Eighth Army, advancing from Libya, reached Tripoli in January 1943 and attacked the Axis-held Mareth Line in southern Tunisia in March. Allied air forces and navies increasingly strangled Axis supply across the Sicilian narrows, and fuel and ammunition shortages mounted. In April and early May, coordinated attacks by the British First and Eighth Armies, the American II Corps and French units broke the Axis perimeter. Tunis and Bizerte fell on May 7, and the Axis forces in Tunisia surrendered on May 13, 1943. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records approximately 267,000 German and Italian soldiers taken prisoner in the final surrender, a figure whose reporting period and categories must be distinguished from overall campaign casualties.

The North African victory had considerable consequences. It opened the Mediterranean to Allied shipping, shortening the route to the Middle East and India, and removed the risk to Egypt and Suez. It gave American and British forces experience in coalition warfare, and allowed Allied planners to prepare an invasion of Sicily and Italy. It also removed a significant body of Axis troops, though the Axis lost them at a time when the Eastern Front, after Stalingrad, was absorbing most German resources. Whether the campaign delayed a cross-Channel invasion by a year or enabled it to succeed is one of the questions addressed in the debate below.

Section 118.19

The Invasion of Sicily and the Collapse of Mussolini's Government

At the Casablanca Conference in January 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill agreed that the next Allied operation would be an invasion of Sicily. Operation Husky began on July 10, 1943, with amphibious landings on the southeastern coast of the island by the British Eighth Army under Montgomery and the American Seventh Army under George Patton, together with Canadian forces, preceded by airborne landings that were hampered by wind and heavy losses. It was then the largest amphibious operation of the war. The defenders consisted of Italian forces and German divisions, including the Hermann Göring Division and others, commanded in the field by Italian General Alfredo Guzzoni, with German commanders exercising growing influence.

The campaign was not a simple advance. Montgomery's forces were slowed on the eastern coast by the German defense around Catania and Mount Etna, while Patton's army moved across the west and north, taking Palermo on July 22 and then pressing along the northern coast. German forces fought a skillful delaying action, and most of them, together with a substantial number of Italian soldiers and a good deal of equipment, were evacuated across the Strait of Messina to the mainland. The Allies completed their occupation of the island on August 17, 1943. The friction between American and British commanders, and arguments about the Allied failure to trap the Axis forces, have been discussed at length by historians.

Sicily tested the Allies' capacity to land and sustain large armies on an enemy-held coast. Airborne operations were intended to seize key routes, but scattered drops and friendly-fire incidents complicated their initial contribution. Engineers, naval gunfire, beach logistics and the rapid delivery of vehicles and ammunition were essential after the first landings. Italian coastal units varied markedly in training and equipment, while German mobile formations resisted effectively even as commanders planned evacuation routes. Evaluating the campaign solely by the territory gained obscures the large number of Axis soldiers withdrawn across the Strait of Messina before August 17.

Sicilian civilians encountered bombing, bombardment, requisitioning and the breakdown of Fascist local administration. In some places Allied forces were welcomed as an end to immediate fighting; elsewhere uncertainty about food, order and political authority remained intense. In Rome, the bombing of July 19 and accumulated military disasters accelerated elite discussions of Mussolini's future, yet the July 25 dismissal was not itself a declaration of peace. The king and Badoglio expected Germany to react militarily and faced the problem of communicating with the Allies while German divisions were already moving into the peninsula.

Sicily's capture precipitated a political crisis in Italy. The country had endured military defeats, air raids on cities including Rome (bombed on July 19), shortages and a decline in confidence in the regime. Within the Fascist leadership and among the conservative elite, including the monarchy and senior officers, many had come to believe that Mussolini must be removed if Italy were to escape the catastrophe. On the night of July 24–25, the Fascist Grand Council voted for a motion that called for the restoration of constitutional powers to the king, and the next day King Victor Emmanuel III dismissed Mussolini and had him arrested. Marshal Pietro Badoglio became head of government.

It is important to distinguish the collapse of Mussolini's government from Italy's withdrawal from the Axis. Badoglio announced that the war would continue, while secretly seeking contact with the Allies. German forces moved additional divisions into Italy in the weeks that followed, anticipating Italian defection. The coup was carried out by the regime's own elite, not by an uprising, and it left key questions unresolved, including how to extricate Italy from a war in which German troops occupied much of the territory and Allied demands were severe. Those questions determined the events of September.

Section 118.20

The Italian Armistice and the Strategic Transformation of the Mediterranean War

Secret negotiations, conducted through intermediaries in Lisbon and then at Cassibile in Sicily, produced a short military armistice that was signed on September 3, 1943, by the Allied chief of staff Walter Bedell Smith for General Eisenhower and by the Italian General Giuseppe Castellano for Marshal Badoglio. The agreement was kept secret so that it could coincide with the Allied landing planned on the Italian mainland. Its public announcement was made on the evening of September 8, first by Eisenhower on Allied radio and then by Badoglio from Rome, after Italian hesitation. The distinction matters. Signing was a legal and diplomatic act, announcement was a political act, and the military consequences followed from the gap between them: the Italian forces had received no clear orders to prepare, and the German forces had.

Following the September 8 armistice announcement, German forces implemented Operation Achse, beginning the occupation and disarmament of Italian forces across Italy and Italian-held territories. On September 9 the Allies landed at Salerno, near Naples, and the king and Badoglio departed Rome for the south, leaving the army without clear direction. German forces occupied Rome and most of northern and central Italy, and a large number of Italian soldiers, several hundred thousand by common estimates, were captured and sent to Germany as military internees, many of them to forced labor. Mussolini was freed by German paratroopers on September 12 and installed in a German-backed puppet state, the Italian Social Republic. Italy thus became a divided country and a battlefield, a subject of Chapter 122.

The short armistice can be read as a statement of Allied military priorities. Article 1 required the cessation of Italian hostilities, while Article 2 required efforts to deny facilities to Germany and Article 3 addressed Allied prisoners. Article 4 directed Italian naval and air forces to specified locations; Articles 6 and 7 supplied Allied rights over Italian territory, ports and airfields, and Article 8 required Italian armed forces to withdraw from participation in the war. Articles 10 and 11 reserved broad Allied powers to impose military controls and disarmament, while Article 12 anticipated further political, economic and financial terms. These numbered provisions should be interpreted in their specific language, not reduced to a vague statement that Italy 'surrendered.'

The agreement also exposes the gap between state authority and armed forces on the ground. Italian units in the Dodecanese, the Balkans and France frequently received contradictory or delayed orders while German commands acted on prepared plans. Some Italian officers cooperated with the Allies or local resistance; others surrendered or attempted to lead soldiers home. The consequences could include internment, forced labor and massacre. That divergence is why the September 3 signing, September 8 announcement and September 9 Allied landings are separate events in the historical chronology, and why the subsequent conflict requires Chapters 120 and 122 rather than a final paragraph of easy victory.

Italian garrisons in Greece, Yugoslavia and Albania faced an acute crisis. Some units surrendered to the Germans, some were disarmed and deported, some joined partisans and resistance groups, and some resisted. The most notorious case occurred on the island of Cephalonia, where soldiers of the Italian Acqui Division fought German forces for several days and, after surrendering, were shot in large numbers, with estimates of those killed running to several thousand. On other islands, including Rhodes and the Dodecanese, there was fighting between Germans and Italians, and British forces attempted unsuccessfully to hold some islands. In Yugoslavia and Greece, the Italian collapse transferred weapons and territory to Partisan and ELAS forces in some areas, while the Germans took over the Italian zones and intensified anti-partisan operations and, in Greece, the persecution of Jews formerly in the Italian zone. The armistice therefore did not end fighting in these territories; it changed who was fighting whom and increased the danger to civilians.

The strategic situation had altered markedly since 1940. Axis forces had been driven from North Africa, Sicily had been lost, Mussolini had fallen, and Allied armies stood on the European mainland. Axis domination of the Mediterranean had proved fragile despite the successes of 1941: it depended on shipping that could not be protected, on conquered territories that could not be pacified, and on an alliance whose members had divergent aims and unequal power. Germany retained great military strength and now had to garrison Italy, the Balkans and the south of France while fighting in the east. The next phase of the war, the Italian campaign, is examined in Chapter 122, while the wider system of occupation, collaboration and resistance, including developments in the Balkans after September 1943, is the subject of Chapter 120.

Historical Debate

Was the Mediterranean War Strategically Central or a Costly Diversion?

The question: Did the Mediterranean and Balkan campaigns decisively shape the outcome of World War II in Europe, or did they absorb resources that might have been better used elsewhere, whether by Britain in 1940 and 1941, by Germany in 1941, or by the Allies in 1942 and 1943?

Interpretation A — The Mediterranean was strategically significant: Historians who stress its importance argue that the theater protected Suez and the imperial communications of Britain, tied down German and Italian forces and shipping, forced Italy's collapse, gave the Allies a place to train for coalition warfare and opened the southern flank of Europe. On this view, the Mediterranean was the main theater in which Britain and the Commonwealth could engage the Axis on land between 1940 and 1942, and the victories there set conditions for later operations.
Interpretation B — It was a costly secondary effort: Others argue that the central struggle was on the Eastern Front, where the great bulk of German forces were engaged and destroyed, and that the Mediterranean absorbed Allied shipping and troops that might have enabled a cross-Channel invasion earlier. Critics point to the commitment of forces to Greece in 1941, the prolonged Italian campaign after 1943 and the relatively small German commitment in Africa compared with the East. For Germany, the Balkan and North African ventures were distractions that deepened obligations to Italy without securing decisive gain.

Evidence: The debate rests on comparisons of numbers of divisions engaged, shipping tonnage, aircraft and supplies, on German and Allied planning documents, on the effect of Mediterranean losses on Axis fuel supply and on timelines for the invasion of Normandy. Both views accept the Eastern Front's centrality to German military power; they differ on how to weigh indirect effects such as the fall of Italy and the protection of imperial routes. Historians also note that some commonly repeated claims, such as the idea that the Balkan campaign alone delayed Barbarossa decisively, are not well supported.

Why it matters: How the question is answered shapes judgments of British strategy under Churchill, of the competing priorities of the British and American commands, and of whether the Mediterranean campaigns should be taught as part of the core European war or as a subordinate theater. It also affects the weight given to the experiences of occupied peoples in Greece, Yugoslavia and North Africa, whose histories exceed any strategic ledger.

Student task: Select two pieces of evidence from this chapter that support Interpretation A and two that support Interpretation B. Then explain what additional evidence, such as shipping statistics or planning papers, would help you decide, and whether the two interpretations might be partly compatible.

The chapter's argument can therefore be stated briefly. Military geography, imperial resources, supply systems, political division and civilian resistance shaped the course of the war in the Mediterranean as much as battlefield tactics. Italian offensives, German intervention and Axis conquest transformed southeastern Europe and North Africa, but occupation created continuing instability. Allied victories in North Africa and Sicily weakened the Axis position, helped to bring down Mussolini and opened a new phase of the war whose consequences extended well beyond September 1943.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the expansion of World War II across the Mediterranean basin, North Africa and southeastern Europe between June 1940 and September 1943. It began with Italy's entry into the war on June 10, 1940, and Mussolini's hope of a parallel war that would secure Italian predominance in the Mediterranean. The sea itself, linking Gibraltar, Malta, Sicily, Alexandria and Suez, was both a European and an imperial theater, and the struggle to control its communications determined what armies could do on land.

Italian ambitions exceeded Italian means. The advance into Egypt in September 1940 stalled, and Operation Compass destroyed the Italian Tenth Army in Cyrenaica by February 1941, while the campaign in East Africa ended Italian rule there and restored Haile Selassie. At sea, Taranto and Cape Matapan showed British strengths, but the Mediterranean remained contested, and Malta's survival under air attack proved central. The chapter has cautioned against treating Italian setbacks as proof of national incapacity, stressing instead equipment, logistics, command and theater.

The Italian invasion of Greece on October 28, 1940 failed against Greek defense in the mountains of Epirus, and its failure drew Germany into the Balkans. Following the Yugoslav coup of March 27, 1941, the Axis invaded Yugoslavia and Greece on April 6, conquered both within weeks, and took Crete in an airborne operation that began on May 20 and cost German forces dearly. These were rapid conquests, but they were the beginning of long occupations. The claim that the Balkan campaign decisively delayed Barbarossa and caused German defeat before Moscow was shown to be unsupported without qualification.

In North Africa, the arrival of Rommel and the Afrika Korps reversed British gains in 1941. The siege of Tobruk, Operation Crusader, the battle of Gazala and the fall of Tobruk in June 1942 formed a sequence of advances and retreats governed by supply, as armies moved away from or toward their bases. Malta, intelligence, shipping and the labor of ports and railways were as important as commanders, and the war involved British, Commonwealth, Indian, Free French, Italian and German forces on lands whose inhabitants had no say in the conflict. El Alamein halted and then broke the Axis advance, through material superiority, preparation and attrition.

Division IV turned to the occupied Balkans. Yugoslavia and Greece were partitioned among Germany, Italy, Bulgaria and Hungary, with the Independent State of Croatia created as a dependent regime. The Ustaša persecuted Serbs, Jews and Roma, killed large numbers of people in villages and camps including Jasenovac, and provoked uprisings. Resistance took different forms: the Partisans under Tito and the Chetniks under Mihailović had different goals, fought the occupiers and each other, and varied in conduct by place and time. In Greece, famine, requisitioning and reprisals defined the occupation, resistance was divided between EAM–ELAS and EDES, and Jews of Salonika and other communities were deported.

Operation Torch in November 1942 created a western front in North Africa, brought the United States into the Mediterranean land war and led to the German occupation of unoccupied France. The Tunisia campaign, including the defeat at Kasserine Pass and the subsequent American reforms, ended with the Axis surrender on May 13, 1943. The Allied invasion of Sicily on July 10 and the loss of the island by August 17 precipitated the vote of the Fascist Grand Council and the arrest of Mussolini on July 25, though Italy remained formally in the Axis until the armistice.

The armistice was signed on September 3 and announced on September 8, 1943. The gap between those dates, together with the absence of clear Italian preparation, allowed Germany to disarm Italian forces and seize much of Italy and the former Italian occupation zones in the Balkans. The Allied landing at Salerno on September 9 began the Italian campaign. In Greece, Yugoslavia and Albania the armistice did not end the fighting but changed its terms, increasing opportunities for resistance and dangers for civilians.

The chapter's central argument is that the Mediterranean and Balkan wars were not peripheral. They drew in many states and peoples, tied down Axis resources, brought down the Fascist regime and opened the southern flank of Europe. Historians debate their relative weight alongside the Eastern Front, and this chapter has set out both views. What is not in doubt is that the military victories of 1941 did not produce durable political control, and that the societies of Greece, Yugoslavia, Crete and North Africa bore costs that no campaign narrative can fully capture. These themes continue in the German-Soviet war (Chapter 119), occupation and resistance (Chapter 120), the Holocaust (Chapter 121) and the Allied advance (Chapter 122).

Key Terms

Mediterranean theater
The area of linked military operations around the Mediterranean Sea, including North Africa, southern Europe and the Balkans, in which Axis, British, Commonwealth and later American forces fought between 1940 and 1945.
Maritime communications
The sea routes, ports, escorts and shipping by which armies and economies were supplied, whose security determined operations in North Africa.
Convoy
A group of merchant ships sailing together under naval and sometimes air escort to reduce losses from submarines, aircraft and surface raiders.
Naval blockade
The use of naval power to cut off an opponent's seaborne trade, which Britain applied to Axis-controlled Europe, with severe consequences for occupied Greece before relief was arranged.
Suez Canal
The artificial waterway in Egypt linking the Mediterranean and Red Seas, a vital route of the British Empire and a principal object of defense in the desert war.
Malta
The British island base south of Sicily from which aircraft, submarines and ships attacked Axis supply lines, and which endured intense air siege in 1941 and 1942.
Operation Compass
The British and Commonwealth offensive beginning December 9, 1940, which destroyed the Italian Tenth Army in Egypt and Cyrenaica by February 1941.
Regia Marina
The Royal Italian Navy, a large fleet constrained by fuel shortages, lack of radar and weak air cooperation.
Greco-Italian War
The conflict that began with the Italian invasion of Greece from Albania on October 28, 1940, in which Greek forces pushed the Italians back into Albania before German intervention in April 1941.
Operation Marita
The German invasion of Greece beginning April 6, 1941, which led to the fall of Athens on April 27.
Tripartite Pact
The alliance of Germany, Italy and Japan signed on September 27, 1940, which Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and, briefly, Yugoslavia joined.
Operation Mercury
The German airborne invasion of Crete from May 20, 1941, which captured the island at heavy cost to German paratroop forces.
Airborne assault
An attack in which troops are landed by parachute, glider or transport aircraft behind or around enemy positions, as on Crete and, by the Allies, in Sicily.
Afrika Korps
The German expeditionary force sent to Libya in 1941 under Rommel, later part of a combined German-Italian Panzer Army.
Siege of Tobruk
The Axis investment of the Libyan port from April to December 1941, held chiefly by Australian troops with British, Indian and later other forces.
Operation Crusader
The British offensive of November 18, 1941, that relieved Tobruk and pushed Axis forces back to El Agheila.
Desert warfare
Combat in arid terrain with few fixed features, where mobility, water, fuel and supply distance dominated operations and where armor, antitank guns and mines were decisive.
Supply line
The route and organization by which an army receives food, fuel and munitions, whose length and security limit its operational reach.
Attrition
A strategy of wearing down the opponent's strength by sustained losses, used by the Eighth Army at Alamein before breakthrough.
El Alamein
The Egyptian position where the Axis advance was halted in July 1942 and broken in the second battle that began October 23, 1942.
Axis occupation
The military and administrative control exercised by Germany, Italy, Bulgaria and Hungary over conquered territories, varying from direct rule to dependent local regimes.
Independent State of Croatia
The Ustaša-led state proclaimed April 10, 1941, nominally independent but dependent on Germany and Italy, covering Croatia and Bosnia-Herzegovina.
Ustaša
The Croatian nationalist movement led by Ante Pavelić that governed the NDH and carried out mass persecution of Serbs, Jews and Roma.
Partisans
The Communist-led, multinational resistance movement under Tito that fought occupiers, the Ustaša and Chetniks and laid foundations for postwar Yugoslavia.
Chetniks
The royalist, predominantly Serb movement associated with Draža Mihailović, whose decentralized units varied between resistance and local accommodation with occupiers.
Civilian reprisals
Collective punishments by occupying forces, including executions, hostage-taking and village destruction, in response to attacks or suspected support for resistance.
Operation Torch
The Anglo-American landings in French Morocco and Algeria beginning November 8, 1942.
Tunisia Campaign
The fighting from November 1942 to May 13, 1943, ending in the surrender of Axis forces in North Africa.
Operation Husky
The Allied invasion of Sicily beginning July 10, 1943, completed August 17.
Italian armistice
The military agreement signed September 3, 1943 and announced September 8, by which Italy ceased fighting the Allies, followed by German occupation of much of Italy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the Second World War in Europe cannot be understood from Berlin, London or Moscow alone. The war around the Mediterranean connected Italy's ambitions, British imperial strategy, German opportunism, American entry and the lives of villagers, dockworkers and soldiers from several continents. It revealed how geography and supply could limit even powerful armies.

It also revealed the cost of conquest. In Greece, Yugoslavia and Crete the Axis won quickly and then faced hunger, reprisals, persecution and resistance that no victory parade could end. The violence of the Ustaša regime, the civil conflicts within Yugoslav and Greek resistance, and the deportations of Jews from Salonika and Thrace were products of occupation, not accidents of a distant theater. These developments shaped postwar politics in the Balkans and anticipate the fuller treatments of Chapters 120 and 121.

For Volume V, the chapter contributes the point that catastrophe was continental and imperial before it was total. Between 1940 and 1943 the war moved across a connected space from the Atlantic gateway of Gibraltar to the Aegean, the Adriatic and the Nile, and by September 1943 the collapse of Italy had brought Allied armies onto the mainland of Europe. Contingency remained important at every stage: Italian misjudgment, the Yugoslav coup, command decisions on Crete, supply shortages and the timing of an armistice all shaped what followed.

Chapter 119 now turns to the war that consumed most German strength, the German-Soviet war on the Eastern Front, beginning with Operation Barbarossa. Readers should keep in mind the Mediterranean and Balkan wars as a parallel theater whose consequences for occupied peoples and for the Allied return to Europe, examined in Chapter 122, continued long after 1943.
