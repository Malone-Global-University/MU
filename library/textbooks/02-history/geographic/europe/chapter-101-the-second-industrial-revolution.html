The Second Industrial Revolution

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

A demonstration of electric power at Frankfurt in 1891 showed that a technology could be proved in a laboratory and an exhibition hall long before it reshaped everyday life. Between the proof and the transformation lay engineers, investors, manufacturers, municipal councils, rail and cable networks, and decades of uneven adoption.

In the summer of 1891, visitors to the International Electrotechnical Exhibition in Frankfurt am Main encountered something that had little to do with the machinery halls they might have expected. Electric current generated at a hydroelectric installation at Lauffen am Neckar, roughly 175 kilometers away, was carried by overhead lines to the exhibition grounds, where it lit lamps and drove a motor. The demonstration used three-phase alternating current, and it brought together Swiss and German engineering firms, among them Maschinenfabrik Oerlikon and the Allgemeine Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft (AEG), as well as engineers from several countries. The exhibition's technical organizer, Oskar von Miller, would later help found the Deutsches Museum in Munich, an institution devoted to the history of science and technology.

The Frankfurt demonstration is a useful beginning for this chapter because of what it was and what it was not. It was not a continental electricity grid. It did not mean that Europeans suddenly lived in electrified homes, and it did not end the dominance of coal and steam. It was a carefully staged proof of a technical possibility, produced by a network of people and institutions: university-trained engineers, firms able to build large generators and transformers, investors willing to finance experimental equipment, exhibition organizers who understood publicity, and telegraph and railway systems that had already taught European societies to think in terms of networks. The distance between an exhibition and a working power system, measured in cables, generating stations, franchises, capital, and customers, is one of the central themes of the chapter.

Chapter 101 examines what historians call the Second Industrial Revolution, a set of overlapping developments in the decades between roughly 1850 and 1914, with particular emphasis on the years after 1870. Cheap steel, synthetic dyes and other products of industrial chemistry, electrical machinery and power systems, petroleum and internal-combustion engines, telegraphs, telephones and wireless signaling, and new methods of organizing factories and firms transformed what European industries could make and how they were managed. The chapter asks how those technologies, together with research laboratories, corporations, banks, and government policies, changed European industrial production, and why the benefits and costs of that change were distributed so unevenly among countries, regions, firms, and social groups.

The chapter's argument depends on distinguishing the first and second industrial revolutions without turning them into two separate eras. As Chapters 93 and 94 explained, the earlier transformation rested on coal, iron, steam power, textile machinery, and railways, and Chapter 95 examined the factory towns and working class it created. Those foundations did not disappear after 1850. Coal remained indispensable, steam engines multiplied, and railways extended. What changed was the addition of new materials, new energy systems, and new relationships between scientific knowledge and production, along with larger and more financially complex enterprises. This chapter treats industrialization as a system rather than a list of inventions: a successful technology needed raw materials, skilled labor, factories, transport, legal institutions, capital, and customers.

The geography of the story is equally important. Britain began the period as the world's leading industrial economy, but Germany, Belgium, Switzerland, France, Sweden, and parts of Austria-Hungary, Italy, and the Russian Empire developed distinctive industrial strengths, and the United States emerged as a powerful comparator whose experience will appear throughout the chapter as an external point of reference rather than part of the European narrative proper. Industrial regions such as the Ruhr, Lorraine, the Sambre-Meuse valley, Upper Silesia, Bohemia, the Donets basin, and the Baku oil district became increasingly connected by rails, cables, and capital while remaining strikingly unequal. Much of Europe, including large agricultural zones, stayed only partly touched by the newest technologies even in 1914.

Evidence for this subject is unusually varied. Historians use patents and patent specifications, which show what inventors claimed but not what was actually produced; company archives and annual reports, which often reflect corporate self-presentation; trade and production statistics, whose national categories and reliability differ; engineering journals, exhibition catalogues, and parliamentary inquiries; surviving machines and industrial buildings preserved in museums and as heritage sites; and the letters, memoirs, and testimony of workers, managers, and engineers. Each type of source illuminates some aspects of industrial change and obscures others, and the chapter notes those limits as it proceeds.

Within Part XVI, this chapter opens the examination of Europe in the age of empire by establishing the industrial and financial capacities that underlay mass politics, imperial expansion, and international rivalry. Chapter 102 will turn to organized labor, socialism, and suffrage movements; Chapters 104 and 105 will examine imperial conquest and colonial systems; and Chapter 107 will trace the alliances and crises leading to war. Within Volume IV, the chapter extends the industrial narrative begun in Part XV and shows how economic transformation, state power, and social conflict became increasingly intertwined before 1914.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what historians mean by the Second Industrial Revolution and distinguish its continuities with, and differences from, earlier industrialization.
  2. Analyze how scientific knowledge, technical education, and industrial research became more closely connected to production, while distinguishing discovery, development, and commercialization.
  3. Trace the development of steelmaking, industrial chemistry, electrical engineering, and petroleum and internal-combustion technology, and explain why each mattered.
  4. Compare the industrial trajectories of Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, Switzerland, Russia, and other European economies without assuming a single path of development.
  5. Interpret the geography of industrial power, identifying major coalfields, steel districts, chemical centers, electrical industries, and oil regions.
  6. Assess how railways, steamships, electric transit, telegraphs, telephones, and wireless signaling transformed transport and communication while coexisting with older systems.
  7. Distinguish mechanization, standardization, large-scale manufacture, and moving assembly-line production, and explain why the distinction matters.
  8. Explain how corporations, limited liability, banks, cartels, tariffs, and government policies shaped industrial investment and competition.
  9. Evaluate the effects of new industries on skilled and unskilled labor, consumption, urban services, public health, and the environment.
  10. Contextualize Europe's industrial development within global commodity chains and the growing military and political significance of industrial capacity before 1914.

Historical Context

The industrial transformation examined in Chapters 93 through 95 gave Britain, and then parts of Belgium, France, the German lands, and other regions, an economy based on coal, steam power, mechanized textile production, iron, and railways. By the middle of the nineteenth century, those technologies had changed landscapes, concentrated populations in cities, and created a new industrial working class. The Great Exhibition held in London in 1851 displayed this confidence, presenting British and foreign manufactures in a building of iron and glass that was itself an expression of industrial engineering. Later international exhibitions in Paris, Vienna, Philadelphia, Chicago, and elsewhere became arenas in which states and firms compared technological achievement.

Three developments in the middle decades of the century prepared the ground for the transformations of this chapter. First, railway and telegraph networks created integrated markets and the habit of treating industrial systems as networks. Second, universities and technical schools in several countries began to train chemists and engineers on a scale that earlier workshop apprenticeship could not match. Third, the political landscape changed. As Chapter 100 explained, the unification of Germany in 1871 created a large internal market and a state able to adopt national legislation on commerce, patents, and banking, while the unification of Italy and the reforms of the Habsburg and Russian empires altered the political conditions under which industries could develop. Industrial change was never separate from state formation.

The term Second Industrial Revolution is a historians' label, not a phrase used by most people at the time, and it has been debated. Some scholars emphasize the growing role of systematic scientific knowledge in industry, others the rise of large managerial enterprises, and others the importance of new energy sources and materials. Some economic historians stress that the British Industrial Revolution itself was gradual, so that a sharp contrast between a first and second revolution can mislead. This chapter uses the label as a convenient way of grouping overlapping developments that were most visible between about 1850 and 1914, and it does not treat 1870 as a precise technological break.

The economic environment was also unstable. The financial crisis that began in 1873 opened a period of falling prices and uneven growth, sometimes called the Long Depression or, in an older usage, the Great Depression, though historians now emphasize deflation and sectoral difficulty rather than continuous contraction. Industrial output in many branches continued to rise. These conditions encouraged firms to seek lower costs, larger scale, and cooperation with competitors, and governments to reconsider trade policy. They also coincided with the great wave of European emigration and the expansion of an integrated world economy.

Finally, the Second Industrial Revolution cannot be understood only in European terms. The United States produced important innovations and eventually surpassed every European producer in many industrial categories. European firms depended on cotton, copper, nitrates, rubber, petroleum, and foodstuffs from other continents, and on capital that flowed across borders. At the same time, Europe remained a region of sharp internal contrasts, from the dense industrial districts of Lancashire and the Ruhr to the largely agrarian economies of much of southern and eastern Europe. The chapter therefore proceeds at several scales, from the laboratory and the factory to the region, the state, and the world economy.

Section 101.1

From the First to the Second Industrial Revolution: Continuity, Transformation, and Scale

Historians usually apply the label Second Industrial Revolution to the period from the middle of the nineteenth century to the First World War, though the dates vary with the industry and the country. In the earlier phase, Britain and then parts of continental Europe built economies around coal, iron, steam engines, textile machinery, and railways. In the later phase, those industries were joined by cheap steel, organic and inorganic chemistry, electrical machinery, petroleum, precision engineering, telecommunications, and enterprises of unprecedented size. The term is useful because it points to a change in the kinds of knowledge, materials, and organizations that industry required. It becomes misleading if it suggests that a new technological age arrived suddenly around 1870 and swept away the old one.

Historical Vocabulary
Second Industrial Revolution

The Second Industrial Revolution is a modern scholarly label for the cluster of technological, organizational, and scientific developments that expanded rapidly between roughly 1850 and 1914, especially steel, industrial chemistry, electricity, petroleum and internal-combustion engines, telecommunications, and large corporations. Contemporaries did not use the phrase in this sense, and it gained currency in the twentieth century. It does not mean that the first industrial revolution ended: coal, steam power, and railways expanded alongside the newer industries. The term matters in this chapter as a way of grouping overlapping developments, and students should treat it as an analytical convenience rather than a precise period with fixed boundaries.

Continuity and the persistence of older technologies

The continuities were substantial. Coal remained the principal industrial fuel, and in Europe its consumption rose sharply throughout the period because steel furnaces, chemical works, railways, steamships, and even electric power stations depended on it. Steam engines did not give way to electricity in 1880; large steam turbines, which became important at the very end of the period, were themselves used to drive electrical generators. Railways, rather than being replaced, were extended and strengthened by steel rails and heavier locomotives. New technologies interacted with existing infrastructure, labor skills, and business habits, and they often succeeded because they could be integrated with what already existed. Electric motors, for example, spread in workshops in part because they could be fitted to existing buildings and used selectively, rather than requiring a factory to abandon its steam plant at once.

What was new

Several features distinguished the later phase. Productivity gains came increasingly from systematic improvement of materials and processes, such as the control of steel composition or the synthesis of compounds, rather than only from adding machines to established crafts. Energy conversion became more important, as engineers learned to transform heat, motion, and chemical energy into electrical power and back again. Standardization of parts, screw threads, electrical units, and chemical purity made it easier for products from different factories to work together. Many industries required large fixed investments, which gave advantages to firms capable of operating at scale, a pattern economists describe as economies of scale. And markets were increasingly integrated by rail, steamship, telegraph, and finance, so that a producer in the Ruhr or Liège competed with others across Europe and beyond.

Uneven timing

Industrial transformation occurred at different speeds in different industries and countries. Steel production changed rapidly in the 1860s and 1870s, while the diffusion of electric power through ordinary homes and workshops took decades and remained incomplete by 1914. Synthetic dyes quickly displaced some natural dyes, while the automobile remained a luxury product until the First World War. Countries that industrialized early had large installed investments in older technology, which could slow the adoption of new methods, while later industrializers sometimes adopted new systems more readily but lacked skilled labor or capital. The chapter therefore tracks inventions, public demonstrations, patents, commercial production, and mass adoption separately. A date for the first of these seldom tells us when an innovation began to matter to the economy.

This section establishes the central question: what made the later nineteenth-century industrial transformation distinctive, and what remained continuous with earlier industrialization? The answer, developed through the chapter, is that distinctiveness lay in the increasing integration of science, engineering, organization, and finance, and not in a single machine or fuel.

Section 101.2

Science, Engineering, Technical Education, and the Rise of Industrial Research

The early industrial revolution in Britain drew heavily on practical skill, workshop experience, and incremental improvement by mechanics and entrepreneurs, though contact with scientific societies and experimental culture also mattered. In the later nineteenth century, the relationship between scientific knowledge and manufacture became closer and more institutionalized, especially in chemistry and electrical engineering. This section examines the institutions through which that connection was made: laboratories, technical schools, professional societies, patent systems, and the firms that hired trained specialists.

Laboratories and technical schools

Chemistry offers the clearest early example. University laboratories, such as the teaching laboratory associated with Justus Liebig at Giessen, trained students in systematic experimental practice. August Wilhelm Hofmann, a German chemist who directed the Royal College of Chemistry in London from 1845, taught William Henry Perkin and other students before returning to Germany in 1865, a movement that illustrates the circulation of scientific expertise across borders. Technical education expanded in several countries: the polytechnic in Zurich (later the ETH) was founded in 1855, German states upgraded technical colleges into Technische Hochschulen, French engineers were trained in institutions such as the École Centrale and the École Polytechnique, and British technical education grew through institutions in cities such as Manchester and London, though it was often less coordinated by the state.

Professions, firms, and research

Industrial employers increasingly hired chemists, physicists, and engineers. German chemical firms were pioneers in building in-house laboratories where teams of trained chemists tested compounds, improved processes, and sought patentable products. Electrical manufacturers such as Siemens and AEG employed engineers to design generators, motors, and transmission systems and to test them under realistic conditions. The professionalization of engineering, through societies, journals, and credentials, created identifiable groups with shared standards. Governments also supported measurement science, as in the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt, established in Berlin in 1887 with significant support from Werner von Siemens, whose purpose included precision measurement relevant to industry.

Patent systems shaped the incentives for research. The German Empire's patent law of 1877 created a centralized examination system, and the British Patents Act of 1883 revised British practice. National rules differed, and these differences affected industrial strategy. Switzerland, for example, long restricted patents for chemical processes, which affected how its firms used competitors' research. The 1907 patent-law revision allowed greater protection for chemical processes, while the separate question of protection for chemical substances remained more restricted. Historians therefore must read patent records as evidence of claims and legal strategy, not as simple maps of invention.

Discovery, development, and commercialization

It is important to distinguish scientific discovery from engineering development and from commercial success. A laboratory finding might be correct but impractical to produce at scale, and a technically feasible process might fail commercially because of cost, raw-material supply, or competing technologies. Innovation was usually collaborative and cumulative. Skilled workers who understood furnaces and machinery, laboratory assistants, foremen, managers, investors, and customers all shaped the outcome. The image of the isolated inventor, though a persistent popular tradition, is a poor guide to how most of the technologies in this chapter developed.

Nor should national comparisons be simplified. British industry had deep traditions of practical engineering, skilled workshop culture, and applied improvisation, and British firms continued to lead in shipbuilding, many branches of mechanical engineering, textiles, and finance. German firms developed a more systematic relationship with universities and in-house research, especially in chemicals and electrical products. Neither system was universally superior: each had strengths in particular industries. The central question is why science, education, and industrial research became increasingly important to economic competition, and the answer lies in industries where processes could be improved by understanding materials and reactions rather than only by workshop experience.

Section 101.3

The Steel Revolution: Bessemer, Open-Hearth Production, and Modern Industrial Materials

Iron had been the basic structural metal of the early industrial era, but it had limitations. Cast iron is strong in compression but brittle, while wrought iron, produced slowly by labor-intensive puddling, is tougher but expensive and limited in the quantity and consistency in which it could be made. Steel, an alloy of iron with a controlled small proportion of carbon, offered a combination of strength and toughness, but before the mid-nineteenth century it was produced in small quantities by costly methods such as the crucible process, practiced in centers such as Sheffield. Producing large quantities of affordable steel of reliable quality was thus one of the most consequential technical problems of the period.

Bessemer's converter

In 1856 Henry Bessemer publicly announced a process in which air was blown through molten pig iron, oxidizing impurities and carbon and producing steel rapidly and in large batches. The announcement was an important moment, but it was not the end of the story. Early attempts by other producers to reproduce the process were disappointing, in part because of the impurities, especially phosphorus, in many ores and because of difficulties controlling the carbon content. Improvements, including the addition of manganese-bearing iron by Robert Mushet and the work of producers who understood which ores worked well, made commercial production more reliable. The Swedish ironmaster Göran Göransson, working with low-phosphorus Swedish ore, was among those who established early successful production. Science Museum Group documentation and other institutional sources identify 1856 as the date of Bessemer's presentation and emphasize the further development needed for dependable production.

Open-hearth and basic processes

The Siemens-Martin open-hearth process, developed in the 1860s from the regenerative furnace of the Siemens brothers and the work of the French metallurgist Pierre-Émile Martin, heated iron and steel scrap in a large furnace, allowing slower and more controllable refining. It offered greater control over composition and could use scrap, which made it attractive for producing structural steel and for applications requiring consistent quality. Over time it came to dominate steel output in many countries. The Thomas-Gilchrist process, developed in 1878 and 1879 by Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and his cousin Percy Gilchrist, used a basic converter lining together with added lime, forming a highly basic slag that removed oxidized phosphorus as phosphate compounds and allowing the phosphorus-rich ores common in much of continental Europe to be converted into usable steel. The process was especially significant for the iron ores of Lorraine, and its by-product, a slag rich in phosphate, became a useful agricultural fertilizer, an example of how the technologies of this period linked industry and agriculture.

Applications and regions

Steel found growing uses: rails, which lasted far longer than iron rails; locomotives and bridges; ship hulls and engines; industrial machinery; reinforcement and structural frames in construction; and artillery, armor, and other military equipment. Major steel regions included Sheffield and other British districts, the Ruhr and Upper Silesia in Germany, Lorraine and Luxembourg, the Sambre-Meuse region of Belgium, and later parts of northern Italy, Bohemia, and Ukraine. Firms such as Krupp in Essen and Schneider at Le Creusot became symbols of the connection between steel and armaments.

As with other technologies, the historian must distinguish production capacity, technical capability, and the speed with which users adopted steel products. Steel rails were adopted relatively quickly because of their durability, but steel did not instantly replace wrought iron in all uses, and the building of ships in steel proceeded unevenly. The central question is how new steelmaking technologies altered the scale and possibilities of European industrial production, and the answer lies in the cheaper and more reliable supply of a material that made heavier machinery, longer bridges, larger ships, and more powerful weapons possible.

Section 101.4

Industrial Geography and Shifting Economic Leadership: Britain, Germany, France, and the European Regions

In 1850 Britain was the world's pre-eminent industrial economy, producing a large proportion of the world's coal, iron, and cotton textiles and dominating shipping and finance. By 1914 the picture was more complicated. Britain remained a leading industrial and commercial power with an unmatched financial center in London, but Germany and the United States had surpassed it in the output of crude steel, and Germany had taken a leading role in chemicals and electrical engineering. Modern scholarly estimates, which are approximate, suggest that Britain's share of world manufacturing output declined substantially between 1880 and 1913, while Germany's rose. This was a change in relative shares. Britain's industries continued to grow in absolute terms, and relative decline did not mean contraction.

Britain, Germany, and France

Britain's strengths included its established coal and metal industries, shipbuilding, textile exports, engineering skills, and global commercial networks. Its weaknesses in some newer sectors, such as organic chemicals and certain branches of electrical manufacturing, have been much debated; explanations include differences in technical education, the structure of firms, existing investments, and the strong position of established industries, though historians disagree about how much weight to give each. Germany's expansion rested on large coal deposits in the Ruhr, Saar, and Silesia, iron ore from Lorraine and elsewhere, a growing rail network, a large internal market after unification, technical universities, and close relationships between banks and industry. Its steel, chemical, and electrical industries grew rapidly, though the German economy also retained a large agricultural sector and many small workshops. France had a more diversified industrial structure, with strong engineering firms, luxury and specialty manufacturing, regional textile centers, and increasingly important automobile and electrical enterprises. Smaller firms remained important, and the loss of much of Lorraine in 1871 affected French access to iron ore, though ore fields in French Lorraine around Briey were later developed.

Other European industrial regions

Belgium, an early industrializer, had dense coal, metal, and engineering districts in the Sambre-Meuse region, as well as the chemical firm Solvay. Switzerland, though without coal, developed precision engineering, electrical machinery, and a major chemical industry centered on Basel. Sweden combined high-quality iron ore with technical innovation in firms such as ASEA and Ericsson. Northern Italy, around Milan, Turin, and Genoa, developed engineering, textile, and later automobile and electrical industries, while the south remained far less industrial. In Austria-Hungary, Bohemia and parts of Austria were important industrial regions, with the Škoda works at Plzeň and the Ganz engineering works in Budapest, but large agrarian areas remained. In the Russian Empire, industrial growth accelerated in the 1890s with state support, foreign capital, and railway construction, in regions such as the Donets basin, St. Petersburg, Moscow, Russian Poland, and Baku, while most of the population remained rural.

  • Petroleum regions: Baku and Batum, the Galician fields around Boryslav, and the Romanian fields around Ploiești
  • Financial centers: London, Paris, Berlin, Brussels, and Amsterdam

Section 101.5

Synthetic Dyes, Coal-Tar Chemistry, and the German Chemical Industry

In 1856, the eighteen-year-old English chemist William Henry Perkin, working on a different problem in a home laboratory, obtained a purple dye from aniline, a compound derived from coal tar. He recognized its commercial potential, patented it, and with family financial backing opened a factory at Greenford Green near London in 1857. The dye, later called mauveine, was one of the first synthetic organic dyes to be manufactured on an industrial scale. Its importance lay less in the color itself, which was fashionable but short-lived as a commercial product, than in what it demonstrated: that laboratory chemistry could generate marketable products from industrial by-products. Coal tar, a residue of gas manufacture and coke production, became a raw material rather than waste.

From mauveine to a research industry

Further synthetic dyes followed in rapid succession, as chemists in Britain, France, Switzerland, and the German states explored the reactions of aniline and related compounds. Germany became the center of the industry. Firms including BASF (founded in 1865 at Mannheim and later Ludwigshafen), Bayer (1863) and Hoechst (1863) built chemical plants and increasingly employed trained chemists in company laboratories. A turning point came with the synthesis of alizarin, the red coloring matter of the madder plant, by Carl Graebe and Carl Liebermann in Berlin in 1868, with BASF developing production; Perkin independently patented a similar process in England in 1869. Synthetic alizarin devastated the cultivation of madder in regions such as southern France. Synthetic indigo, whose structure was worked out by Adolf von Baeyer and whose commercial production was achieved by BASF in the 1890s, had a comparable impact on natural indigo producers in India, illustrating the global reach of chemical innovation.

Why Germany?

Why did German companies eventually dominate an industry in which British and French chemists made early discoveries? Historians point to several overlapping factors: the abundant supply of university-trained chemists, a patent system that encouraged systematic research, close connections between academic chemistry and industry, large internal and export markets, sources of capital including banks, and organizational choices such as employing teams of researchers. Perkin sold his factory in 1874 and then pursued independent chemical research in his private home laboratory, while British firms faced obstacles including patent disputes and a smaller supply of trained chemists. These explanations are debated, and the outcome should not be treated as inevitable. Entrepreneurial choices and contingency mattered, as did the Swiss firms of Basel, which also became important. The central question is why industrial chemistry became a source of technological leadership and corporate competition, and the answer lies in the way research could repeatedly generate new products that could be protected by patents and sold across borders.

In a chemical factory, the transition from experiment to repeatable manufacture depended on controlling impurities and reproducing a consistent shade across batches. Dyers and textile printers needed colors that survived washing, light, and the chemical treatments used in finishing cloth. A laboratory compound therefore became commercially valuable only after manufacturers devised practical purification methods, secured raw materials, and persuaded textile businesses to alter established routines. These requirements help explain why firms with dedicated research staff, production engineers, and close relations with customers gained advantages that a patent alone could not guarantee.

Section 101.6

Fertilizers, Industrial Chemicals, Pharmaceuticals, and the New Chemical Economy

Chemistry extended far beyond dyes. Basic industrial chemicals underpinned glassmaking, soap, paper, textiles, and metallurgy. The older Leblanc process for making soda ash, which used salt and sulfuric acid, produced large quantities of noxious waste. The ammonia-soda process developed by the Belgian chemist Ernest Solvay in the 1860s offered a cleaner and more economical alternative, and Solvay's firm, along with British producers such as Brunner Mond, which began production in 1873, gradually displaced the Leblanc method. The change illustrates how process innovation reorganized an entire branch of the chemical economy, and also how environmental and economic considerations interacted. Sulfuric acid, the most widely used industrial chemical, was produced in rapidly growing quantities for fertilizers, metals, and many other products.

Fertilizers and agriculture

Agricultural chemistry also developed. Superphosphate, made by treating phosphate rock or bones with acid, had been commercialized in Britain from the 1840s, and potash salts, particularly from the Stassfurt deposits in Germany, became important. Chile supplied large quantities of natural nitrate, which linked European agriculture to a distant mining economy, and the phosphate-rich slag from basic steelmaking became a further fertilizer. Chemical fertilizers contributed to rising agricultural yields in parts of Europe, though their use varied greatly by region, and many farmers, especially in poorer areas, continued to rely on manure and traditional rotations.

Pharmaceuticals and controlled industrial processes

German firms such as Bayer and Hoechst expanded into pharmaceuticals, building on dye chemistry and research. Bayer introduced acetylsalicylic acid under the name Aspirin at the end of the 1890s, and Hoechst collaborated with Paul Ehrlich on an arsenic-based treatment for syphilis, marketed as Salvarsan from 1910. These developments depended on laboratory screening, animal testing, clinical trials of varying rigor, and marketing, and they also raised new issues of safety and regulation. Industrial chemistry increasingly required controlled temperatures and pressures, catalysts, and standardized production, which in turn demanded trained chemists and engineers.

Haber-Bosch and the separation of laboratory and factory

At the end of the period, one of the most significant developments was the synthesis of ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen. Fritz Haber and his collaborators demonstrated a catalytic laboratory process in 1909. Turning it into an industrial process required Carl Bosch and BASF engineers to solve formidable problems of materials, high pressure, and catalysts, and large-scale production began at BASF's Oppau plant in September 1913. The Royal Society of Chemistry documents this separation between the laboratory demonstration and the industrial plant. Industrial nitrogen fixation promised to end dependence on natural nitrate for fertilizer, and it also supplied a basis for explosives, a dual use that took on enormous importance during the First World War, discussed in Chapter 110. This chapter stops at the pre-war industrial foundation.

The central question is how industrial chemistry extended into agriculture, medicine, and the production of essential materials. The answer lies in the ability of chemists and engineers to design, scale up, and control processes, and in the large firms that could finance the investment required. It also lies in the unevenness of diffusion: much of European agriculture and medicine changed more slowly than the laboratories of Ludwigshafen or Basel.

Section 101.7

The Electrical Revolution: Dynamos, Generators, Motors, and Power Networks

Electricity had long been a subject of scientific investigation, and electrical telegraphy was already an established industry by the 1850s. The commercial electricity industry rested on the principle of electromagnetic induction, demonstrated by Michael Faraday in 1831, which showed that moving a conductor through a magnetic field could produce an electric current. Turning that principle into powerful, reliable machines required decades of engineering. Early generators used permanent magnets and were of limited power. A decisive advance was the dynamo-electric principle, in which the machine's own current energized its electromagnets, allowing much more powerful generators. Werner von Siemens developed this principle in 1866 and presented it publicly in early 1867, and the English physicist Charles Wheatstone and others arrived at similar ideas at about the same time, a typical instance of near-simultaneous invention in a field where knowledge circulated widely. Improved designs, such as the ring armature developed by Zénobe Gramme in the early 1870s, made practical generators available.

Historical Vocabulary
Electrification

Electrification refers to the process by which electric power was generated, distributed, and adopted for lighting, motors, transport, and industrial processes. It was not a single event but a series of interlocking developments: building generating stations, creating transmission and distribution systems, manufacturing lamps and motors, passing laws and franchises, and persuading customers to switch from older sources of light and power. In 1914 electrification was well advanced in some cities and industries and barely begun elsewhere. Students should avoid equating the first demonstration of an electric device with the electrification of society, which took decades and, in much of Europe, extended well beyond 1914.

Generation, transmission, distribution, and use

An electricity system includes distinct stages: generation, transmission, distribution, and consumption, each requiring different equipment and organization. Early lighting systems, such as those displayed at the Paris electrical exhibition of 1881, were often small and local. Power could be generated close to the point of use, and the technology of the time limited how far direct current could be carried economically. This led to the so-called battle of systems between direct current, which was simple and compatible with batteries and motors of the time, and alternating current, which could be transformed to high voltages for efficient long-distance transmission. Alternating current won out for large-scale transmission, though direct current remained in use in many cities and for specific applications for a long time. Inventions such as the transformer and polyphase motors, developed by engineers including Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky at AEG, made practical alternating-current systems possible.

Firms, cities, and finance

Electrical manufacturing became a major industry dominated by a few large firms. In Germany, Siemens & Halske and AEG, which grew from Emil Rathenau's Deutsche Edison-Gesellschaft organized in 1883 and adopted the name Allgemeine Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft in 1887, built generators, lamps, motors, and complete power plants. Swiss firms such as Maschinenfabrik Oerlikon and Brown, Boveri & Cie, Swedish ASEA, the Hungarian Ganz works, and British and French firms were also important. Building a power system involved engineers and investors, but also municipal authorities, who granted franchises and sometimes owned utilities, and banks, which financed power stations. Municipal ownership of gasworks, tramways, and electricity supply grew in many cities, though practice varied widely between countries.

The Lauffen to Frankfurt demonstration of 1891, with which this chapter began, showed that alternating current could be transmitted over a distance of around 175 kilometers with reported efficiency that impressed engineers, and it helped persuade skeptics. Documentation from Siemens and IEEE sources, among others, supports the key dates of 1866 and 1891. Yet the demonstration was an exhibition, and it should not be confused with a completed grid. Regional and national electricity networks developed gradually, often only after 1900, and in many areas only in the twentieth century. The central question is why the ability to generate and distribute electricity transformed industrial organization and urban infrastructure, and the answer lies in the flexibility of electric power: motors could be placed individually, lighting could be controlled, and transport could be powered without steam locomotives in city streets.

Section 101.8

Petroleum, Refining, and the Development of Internal-Combustion Power

Petroleum was known and used in small quantities for centuries, but its development as a major industrial commodity began in the middle of the nineteenth century, when distillation made it possible to produce kerosene for lamps. Kerosene lighting had developed in Europe in the 1850s, including in the Habsburg province of Galicia, where Ignacy Łukasiewicz and others developed lamps and a petroleum industry. The drilling of an oil well at Titusville, Pennsylvania in 1859 is conventionally treated as the beginning of the American oil industry, which became the largest in the world and is mentioned here as international context. In Europe, significant production developed in Romania, around Ploiești, in the Galician fields around Boryslav, and, most importantly, in the Baku region on the Caspian Sea, which belonged to the Russian Empire.

Baku and the organization of oil

Baku's oil industry expanded rapidly after the Russian state ended earlier restrictions and opened the fields to private exploitation in the 1870s. Local entrepreneurs, Russian and Caucasian merchants, and foreign investors participated. The Swedish brothers Ludvig and Robert Nobel built a leading enterprise, which developed refining and ordered the specialized oil tanker Zoroaster in 1877; the vessel entered service in 1878, using ship transport on the Caspian Sea and pipelines in the fields. The construction of a railway to the Black Sea port of Batum, in which the Rothschild banking family became involved, allowed oil to reach European markets. Recent research on imperial Baku emphasizes the combination of regional enterprise, foreign capital, transport infrastructure, rapid urban growth, and severe environmental consequences, as well as the labor of migrants from across the Caucasus and Persia, whose working conditions were often harsh.

Refining, markets, and engines

Petroleum was valuable only if it could be refined, stored, and transported, and the period saw the development of pipelines, rail tank cars, storage facilities, and eventually specialized tankers. For most of the nineteenth century, kerosene for illumination was the principal product, while gasoline was a by-product with limited use. Lubricating oils for machinery were also important. The development of the gas engine and the internal-combustion engine, discussed further in Section 101.11, created new demand for fuels, but the engine, the fuel, and the market developed separately and took time to converge. International distribution systems were increasingly organized by large firms, including the American Standard Oil, and, in the early twentieth century, by the Royal Dutch Shell group, formed by the combination of Royal Dutch and Shell Transport interests in 1907.

Petroleum did not immediately displace coal. Coal remained the dominant fuel for industry, railways, and shipping in Europe throughout the period, and most European countries had coal but little oil. Only toward the end of the period did navies and some shipping companies begin to consider oil-fired ships seriously, and the full strategic importance of oil emerged later. The central question is how petroleum became the foundation of new industries without immediately replacing established energy systems, and the answer is that its rise was gradual, depended on a series of linked technologies, and was concentrated in specific regions and uses before 1914.

Section 101.9

Railways, Steel Ships, Electric Transit, and the Expansion of Transport Networks

Railways were the signature technology of the first industrial revolution, and the later nineteenth century was a period of consolidation and extension rather than replacement. European railway networks continued to grow, reaching into Russia, the Balkans, Scandinavia, and the Mediterranean, and linking industrial regions to ports and to one another. Steel rails lasted much longer than iron rails and carried heavier locomotives and greater traffic. Steel bridges, such as those built in the later nineteenth century, and long tunnels through the Alps, including the Gotthard tunnel opened in 1882, extended the reach of the system. Signaling, braking, and timetabling improved capacity and safety, and the standardization of time, tied to railway operations, spread across the continent in the latter decades of the century.

Steamships and the ocean economy

At sea, iron and then steel hulls, compound and triple-expansion steam engines, and screw propellers made ships larger, faster, and more fuel-efficient. Steamships increasingly displaced sail on major routes, though sailing ships remained important for bulk cargoes for some decades. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 shortened the route between Europe and Asia and favored steamships. Refrigeration, first demonstrated successfully on a long voyage in the early 1880s with shipments of frozen meat from New Zealand to Britain, extended the range over which perishable food could be traded and affected agricultural producers in Europe and overseas.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Early Steam Railways to Steel Systems and Electric Urban Transit

What Changed

  • Rails, bridges, and locomotives moved from iron to steel, permitting heavier loads and greater durability
  • Networks grew from separate regional lines to dense national and international systems, with standardized timetables and signaling
  • Electric traction appeared, beginning with a Siemens demonstration railway in Berlin in 1879 and the electric tramway at Lichterfelde in 1881, and spread to urban tram systems and underground railways, such as the electric City and South London Railway opened in 1890
  • Steamships grew larger and more efficient, and refrigeration changed the trade in perishable goods

What Remained Continuous

  • Steam locomotives remained the dominant form of main-line railway traction through 1914
  • Coal powered most rail and sea transport
  • Horses remained important in cities and for local freight, and horse-drawn trams and omnibuses persisted alongside electric ones
  • Rail and water transport remained the backbone of bulk freight and industrial supply

Who experienced change most directly? Urban commuters in large cities, shippers of bulk goods, and railway employees experienced new technologies and work rhythms most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural communities distant from rail lines, and many small-town residents, continued to depend on horses, carts, and local roads. Why the pattern matters: The expansion of transport illustrates the layering of technologies: new systems were added to, rather than substituted for, the old.

Electric transit in cities

Siemens's company history confirms the 1879 demonstration and the subsequent expansion of electric transportation. Electric trams spread in European cities from the 1880s and especially the 1890s, supported by municipal authorities that often built or franchised them, and they allowed cities to extend further outward. Underground railways in London, Budapest, Paris, and other cities, powered increasingly by electricity, changed the geography of urban life, though only for those who could afford the fares and lived along the lines. Steam, electricity, horse traction, and walking coexisted. The central question is how new materials and power systems extended and transformed Europe's existing transportation networks.

The improvements on railways were cumulative rather than spectacular. Heavier locomotives required sturdier permanent ways, better bridges, more effective braking systems, and arrangements for maintaining increasingly busy junctions. A line's theoretical carrying capacity meant little if freight wagons spent days waiting to be unloaded or if incompatible administrative practices slowed movements across borders. Railway managers therefore invested in workshops, standardized operating procedures, timetables, and telegraph communication. These managerial changes transformed railways into coordinated industrial organizations, not simply collections of locomotives. They also enabled mines, blast furnaces, docks, and engineering plants to plan around more predictable deliveries of bulky raw materials.

In port cities the balance between ship, rail, and warehouse capacity determined whether long-distance commerce worked efficiently. The Suez Canal favored steamship routes because steam propulsion was less dependent on the winds of the Red Sea, but canal tolls and fuel costs still shaped companies' decisions. Refrigerated cargoes likewise required more than a cold compartment: stable machinery, appropriate handling at ports, and inland transport determined whether frozen meat reached buyers in usable condition. Transport innovation was therefore a chain of investments rather than an isolated improvement in vehicle design.

Section 101.10

Telegraphs, Telephones, Submarine Cables, and the Communications Revolution

The electrical telegraph, developed in the 1830s and 1840s, had by the 1850s spread along railway lines and between cities. In the following decades, networks grew denser, operators and clerks became a significant occupational group, and international agreements, including the International Telegraph Union established in 1865, set common standards. Submarine cables extended the system across seas. After a brief and failed transatlantic cable in 1858, a durable connection between Europe and North America was established in 1866, using the large steamship Great Eastern to lay cable. Cables soon linked Europe to India, East Asia, and elsewhere, and many of them were owned by British companies, which gave Britain an advantage in global communications.

Effects on markets, news, and government

Faster communication changed how markets, governments, and firms operated. Commodity and financial markets in different cities became more closely linked, so that price differences could be quickly reduced. Shipping firms could coordinate voyages, railway companies could manage traffic, and newspapers and news agencies, such as Reuters and Havas, could distribute information rapidly. Diplomats and military commanders could be instructed from the capital, which altered the balance between central authority and local discretion, with consequences for the diplomatic crises discussed in Chapter 107. Telegraph networks were also vulnerable and strategic, and the control of cables was a matter of state interest.

Telephones and wireless signaling

Alexander Graham Bell obtained a patent for the telephone in the United States in 1876, and demonstrations soon followed in Europe; the Science Museum Group preserves an early Bell telephone used in a demonstration for Queen Victoria in 1878. Telephone exchanges, which used human operators, many of whom were young women, and later automatic switching, built local networks, and companies, state administrations, or municipalities operated them. The diffusion of telephone service was extremely uneven: it was most widespread in cities, among businesses and affluent households, and in some countries, such as Sweden, relative to others, while most rural households and most working-class homes in Europe did not have telephones by 1914. In the 1880s Heinrich Hertz demonstrated electromagnetic waves, and in the 1890s Guglielmo Marconi developed wireless telegraphy, patenting his system in Britain in 1896. Early wireless communication was used for ship-to-shore signaling and transmitted Morse code messages, so it should be distinguished from voice broadcasting, which developed later.

Point-to-point telegraph communication, telephone conversation, and early wireless signaling were different technologies with different users, costs, and institutions. The central question is how faster communication transformed the coordination of enterprises, governments, and international markets. The answer is that it compressed the time needed to transmit information, enabling firms to operate over larger distances and governments to respond more quickly, while also making crises more difficult to contain.

The infrastructure behind a rapid message was expensive and labor-intensive. Cable manufacturers had to insulate conductors, survey routes, lay and repair undersea lines, and maintain landing stations whose failure could isolate distant markets. Telegraph offices required operators capable of handling coded messages, accountants to reconcile charges, and agreements governing traffic that crossed national borders. Companies could profit by controlling valuable routes, while governments worried that foreign-owned cables might be interrupted in a crisis. Speed thus changed the meaning of distance without eliminating geography: a cable landing point, a railway junction, or an exchange became a strategic location through which information and commercial power passed.

Section 101.11

Automobiles, Bicycles, Engines, and the Emergence of New Manufacturing Industries

The internal-combustion engine, in which fuel burns inside the cylinder rather than in a separate boiler, was developed in several forms in the later nineteenth century. Early gas engines were built in the 1860s, and Nikolaus Otto's four-stroke engine, developed with Eugen Langen and produced at the Deutz firm, entered practical development in the mid-1870s as a stationary power source for workshops. Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach, who had worked at Deutz, developed a lighter, high-speed engine in the 1880s, and Karl Benz built a three-wheeled motor vehicle in 1885, patenting it on January 29, 1886, as the National Motor Museum in Britain and other sources document. These engineers worked in a context of rapid improvement in metallurgy, machine tools, ignition systems, carburetion, and fuels.

Bicycles and the metalworking base

Bicycles played a surprisingly important role. The so-called safety bicycle, with equal-sized wheels and a chain drive, spread in the 1880s, and the pneumatic tire developed by John Boyd Dunlop in 1888 improved comfort. The bicycle industry gave manufacturers experience with precision parts, bearings, tubular frames, tires, and mass-marketed products. Many early automobile makers, such as Peugeot and others, came from bicycle or machinery manufacturing backgrounds. Bicycles were also important as consumer products and for personal mobility, particularly for middle-class and, increasingly, working-class users.

Automobiles and diesel engines

Automobile enterprises appeared in Germany and France in the 1890s, with firms such as Panhard and Levassor, Peugeot, Renault, Daimler, and Benz, and later in Italy, Britain, Belgium, and elsewhere. French manufacturers were particularly prominent in the early years, and France led European automobile production for some time. Even so, cars before 1914 remained expensive, handmade or produced in small batches, and unreliable by later standards. They were used mainly by wealthy individuals, doctors, and businesses, and horses, trams, bicycles, and railways carried most of Europe's passengers and goods. The automobile should not be portrayed as a common form of European transportation before the war. Rudolf Diesel patented his compression-ignition engine in the 1890s, and a practical experimental engine demonstrated in 1897 attracted the interest of industrial firms, though diesel engines took years to develop into reliable commercial products, initially for stationary and marine uses.

The central question is why new engines and manufacturing techniques created transportation industries whose full economic impact emerged only gradually. Part of the answer is that the vehicles, the road systems, the fuel supply, the repair infrastructure, and the consumer income needed for mass motoring all had to develop together, and in Europe this largely occurred after the First World War.

Commercial automobile manufacturing developed unevenly because early vehicles were not standardized household purchases. Their owners depended on mechanics who understood ignition, cooling, lubrication, transmission, and the frequent need to adjust individually fitted components. Road surfaces, traffic rules, registration practices, and access to petrol varied across countries and even between nearby districts. French manufacturers such as Panhard and Levassor helped establish the layout of engines and transmissions, while German workshops contributed strongly to engine design. Competitive racing and exhibitions advertised reliability, but victories did not guarantee a profitable mass market. The bicycle was more immediately accessible to a much wider public; its factories and repair shops also helped form networks of suppliers and mechanical knowledge later useful to motor manufacturers.

Section 101.12

Mechanization, Standardization, Mass Production, and the Reorganization of Factories

Larger and more complex industries required new ways of making and organizing things. Precision machine tools, such as lathes, milling machines, and grinders, made it possible to produce parts to consistent tolerances, and gauges and measuring instruments supported inspection. Standardization of components and measures, for example screw threads, electrical units, railway gauges, and later national engineering standards, facilitated interchangeability and trade. Manufacturers sought lower unit costs by increasing output and spreading fixed costs, the principle of economies of scale. The organization of the factory changed too: production was planned more systematically, workshops were specialized, cost accounting became more detailed, and engineers and managers took on new functions.

Different industries, different methods

These changes took different forms in different industries. In steelmaking and chemicals, continuous processes and large plants dominated. In electrical equipment, firms made both standardized products, such as lamps, and customized heavy machinery. In textiles and food processing, mechanization continued to advance, and firms in food and consumer goods, such as those producing canned or packaged products, adopted large-scale methods. In mechanical engineering, firms combined craft skill with machine tools. Capital-intensive machinery increased the need for engineers, technicians, maintenance workers, and skilled operatives, a point returned to in Section 101.17.

Precision in terminology

Historians distinguish several related but different developments. Mechanization is the use of machines to perform tasks. Standardization is the use of agreed specifications for parts, measures, or products. Large-scale manufacture involves producing in big factories and high volumes. Interchangeable parts mean components made precisely enough to be fitted without individual adjustment. The moving assembly line, in which the product moves past stationary workers who perform specialized tasks, is a specific system, introduced by Henry Ford in the United States in 1913 for automobile chassis assembly, and it should be treated as a comparative American development, not retrospectively attributed to European manufacturers throughout the nineteenth century. Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management, a set of ideas about time study and task organization published in the United States in 1911, became known in Europe only around the eve of the war, and its influence was largely a later development.

Small workshops and specialized craft production did not disappear. In many European countries, including Germany and France, small firms and artisanal production remained important, and some historians argue that flexible specialization in small firms was a viable alternative to large-scale mass production. The reorganization of factories was therefore partial and uneven.

The central question is how factories increased productive capacity, and why new machinery required new forms of industrial organization. The answer lies in the connections among capital-intensive equipment, continuous operation, technical expertise, and the managerial coordination needed to keep large plants supplied, staffed, and profitable.

The organization of production differed markedly between sectors. A steel mill depended on keeping costly furnaces and rolling equipment operating near capacity, so interruptions in coal, ore, and rail transport could be expensive. Chemical works needed careful sequencing of reactions and purification stages, as well as trained staff to monitor temperatures, pressure, and product quality. An electrical-engineering manufacturer might combine standardized lamps or small motors with individually designed generators and municipal installations. These businesses shared a need for reliable measurement and cost accounting but did not converge on one universal factory model. Their managers balanced economies of scale against the expenses of inventories, transportation, maintenance, and rapidly changing technology.

The place of human judgment did not vanish from the more standardized workplace. Inspectors checked gauges, machinists adjusted cutting tools, and foremen decided when worn equipment should be repaired. Standard parts reduced some dependence on individual craftsmanship but created demands for accuracy in measurement and for workers able to maintain sophisticated machines. These differences matter when comparing a large chemical plant with a machine-building workshop. Both could be highly productive, yet they organized labor, technical expertise, and quality control in quite different ways.

Corporations, Banking, Capital, and Industrial Competition

The institutional and financial systems that supported the Second Industrial Revolution, including corporations, banks, cartels, state policy, and technological competition among states.

Section 101.13

The Rise of Large Corporations: Joint-Stock Companies, Limited Liability, and Industrial Management

The capital requirements of the new industries were far larger than those of early textile mills. A steelworks with blast furnaces and rolling mills, a chemical complex, a power station and distribution network, or a shipping line required large sums, long periods before profits, and organization across multiple sites. Such needs encouraged firms to draw on many investors and to build administrative structures able to coordinate production, purchasing, research, and sales. Alfred D. Chandler Jr., whose work on industrial enterprise has strongly influenced this subject, argued that investment in large-scale production, distribution, and management gave some firms durable advantages. His emphasis on managerial organization has been supplemented and criticized by scholars who point to the persistence of family firms and varied national business systems in Europe.

Historical Vocabulary
Joint-Stock Companies and Limited Liability

A joint-stock company is a business whose capital is divided into shares that can be owned by many investors, allowing funds to be pooled. Limited liability means that shareholders can lose only what they invested and are not personally liable for all the company's debts. The two concepts are related but not identical: a joint-stock company could exist without limited liability, and legal systems introduced the features at different times and under different conditions. In Britain, the Joint Stock Companies Act of 1844 simplified incorporation, the Limited Liability Act of 1855 extended limited liability, and the Companies Act of 1862 consolidated the framework. In German states, commercial legislation of the 1860s and later reforms, including the 1892 law creating the limited liability company (GmbH), shaped corporate forms, and French law evolved through measures in the 1860s. The terms matter because legal structure influenced who could invest, how much risk they bore, and how firms were governed.

Forms of ownership and management

Partnerships and family-owned firms remained widespread. Others adopted the joint-stock form, which allowed them to raise capital from outside investors, and some combined family control with outside finance. Historians distinguish ownership (who holds the capital), management (who directs operations), and professional administration (salaried managers, engineers, accountants, and clerks). Large firms developed boards of directors, technical departments, accounting systems, and increasingly standardized financial reporting, though the degree of separation between owners and managers varied widely. German joint-stock companies, for instance, had supervisory boards that often included bankers, linking firms and financial institutions.

Comparing enterprises

Different enterprises illustrate different paths. Krupp, based in Essen, grew under the leadership of Alfred Krupp and his successors as a family-owned steel and armaments business, becoming a joint-stock company, Fried. Krupp AG, in 1903 while remaining under family control. Siemens & Halske, founded in 1847 as a telegraph firm, grew into a major electrical manufacturer with international operations, and the Siemens family long retained influence even as the enterprise adopted corporate forms. Schneider and Company at Le Creusot in France combined iron and steel, engineering, and armaments under a dynasty of industrialists who also exercised local social and political influence. The major German chemical firms, BASF, Bayer, and Hoechst, were joint-stock companies employing large numbers of trained chemists. These examples show variation in ownership, management, and relationship to the state.

Students should avoid imposing the twentieth-century multinational corporate model on all nineteenth-century enterprises. Many firms were nationally based, many were family-influenced, and professional management was still developing. The central question is why expanding industrial production encouraged the development of larger and more complex business organizations, and the answer lies in the combination of high fixed costs, technological complexity, market integration, and the legal innovations that allowed capital to be pooled and risks to be shared.

The legal form of the enterprise also affected who bore the costs of failure. Shareholders in a limited-liability company generally risked their investment rather than their entire personal wealth, although company law imposed different disclosure and governance requirements across countries. Share ownership was not the same as managerial control: a family might retain decisive voting influence, banks might hold seats on supervisory boards, and technical directors might direct operations day to day. Krupp remained closely associated with family ownership, while electrical firms more frequently relied on share capital and complex investor relationships. Comparing these arrangements makes it possible to distinguish the scale of an enterprise from the precise distribution of authority within it.

Section 101.14

Banking, Investment, and the Financing of Industrial Expansion

Banks and securities markets mobilized the capital that railways, steelworks, chemical factories, and power systems required. Commercial banks accepted deposits and made short-term loans, investment banks underwrote and sold securities, and joint-stock banks drew on many shareholders. Bond and share markets enabled firms and governments to borrow from investors across countries. Large, long-term investments, like electrical power stations that earned returns only after years of operation, depended on the willingness of financial institutions to take risks and to maintain relationships with industrial customers.

Different financial models

The French Crédit Mobilier, founded in 1852 by the brothers Émile and Isaac Pereire, was an influential early example of a bank intended to promote industrial and railway enterprises by investing in them and issuing securities. It was innovative and controversial, and it collapsed in the 1860s, a reminder that financial innovation could be risky. In Germany, universal banks developed that combined deposit-taking, lending, securities underwriting, and long-term involvement with industrial firms, often placing representatives on company supervisory boards. Deutsche Bank was established in Berlin in 1870, with an early emphasis on financing foreign trade, as the bank's own historical chronology confirms, and Dresdner Bank, Disconto-Gesellschaft, and other institutions also expanded. Over the following decades such banks financed railways, electrical firms, mining, and heavy industry, and promoted international lending.

Britain's financial system was different. London was the leading international financial center, specializing in trade finance, insurance, shipping, and the export of capital, and British investors placed enormous sums in railways and other ventures overseas. Domestic industrial firms in Britain often relied more on retained profits, local networks, and family capital than on large banks. Historians debate whether this contributed to weaker investment in some new industries or reflected rational responses to profitable opportunities abroad. Other countries had their own variants. In Russia, state-sponsored railway building and industrial policy drew on foreign loans and French and other foreign investment, and in Italy and Austria-Hungary banks played important roles in particular regions.

Founding documents and the justification of investment

A bank's founding statutes or a company prospectus can reveal how promoters presented opportunities to investors. Documents of this type typically described the activities and capital of the institution, the safeguards for shareholders, and the economic opportunities, such as trade or railway construction, that justified investment. Because such texts were promotional or legal documents, historians treat them as evidence of how institutions presented themselves rather than of their actual performance, and compare them with balance sheets, correspondence, and later outcomes.

Vulnerabilities

Finance also created new vulnerabilities. International lending, exchange rates, and the gold standard, adopted by many countries in the 1870s, tied economies together, so that crises spread. The collapse of the Vienna stock market in May 1873 and subsequent failures contributed to a financial crisis that spread internationally. Speculation, overextension, and interdependence of banks and industry could magnify shocks. The central question is how banking and financial markets made larger industrial investments possible, and what new vulnerabilities they created. The answer is that finance supplied the capital for large-scale enterprise while increasing exposure to panics, concentration of influence, and conflicts of interest.

The financial relationships of an industrial firm were often more intricate than a single loan. Entrepreneurs might finance a works with retained earnings, issue shares to new investors, borrow for machinery, seek short-term credit to purchase inventories, and insure shipments against loss. Banks evaluated not only a firm's assets but the prospects of its customers and the reliability of its suppliers. The risk was particularly pronounced in electricity supply: a utility had to erect generators and networks before sufficient paying customers might exist. Bank directors and corporate boards consequently shaped which technologies received capital, while successful firms could grow less dependent on external creditors once their profits rose.

Section 101.15

Cartels, Trusts, Tariffs, and the Political Economy of Industrial Competition

Industrial expansion created tension between competition and cooperation. Firms with heavy fixed investments in furnaces, plants, and equipment faced strong incentives to keep operating even when prices fell, which could lead to price wars and losses. Some turned to agreements to coordinate prices, output, or market shares. Terms for these arrangements must be used carefully. A cartel is an agreement among independent firms to regulate competition; a monopoly is control of a market by a single seller or small group; a trust, in the American usage of the period, was a legal device for combining control of multiple companies; and a merger is a full combination of firms. These were different forms of market organization.

German cartels

Germany became known for its cartels. The Rhenish-Westphalian Coal Syndicate, formed in 1893, controlled a large share of Ruhr coal sales. Steel producers formed associations, culminating in the Steelworks Association of 1904. In chemicals, the major dye firms formed alliances in 1904 and 1905. The German legal system was relatively tolerant: a decision of the Imperial Court in 1897 upheld the enforceability of cartel contracts. Supporters argued that cartels stabilized markets and protected investment, while critics argued that they raised prices and restricted competition. Britain and France had cartels and price agreements too, though legal and business traditions differed, and the United States responded with antitrust legislation, notably the Sherman Act of 1890.

The crisis of 1873 and the Long Depression

The financial crisis that began in 1873 opened a period often called the Long Depression, lasting in some accounts until the mid-1890s. Historians now emphasize that this was a period of persistent price deflation, falling profit margins in some sectors, and recurrent crises, while output, trade, and productivity in many industries continued to grow. It was not a continuous contraction. Agricultural prices fell as cheaper grain from the United States and Russia entered European markets, which affected landowners and farmers, and industrial producers faced intensified competition and lower prices.

Protectionism versus free trade

These pressures contributed to a return to protectionism in several states. In Germany, Bismarck broke with the free-trade policies of the earlier period and secured tariffs on iron and agricultural products in 1879, creating a coalition of heavy industry and large landowners, a political arrangement examined further in Chapter 106. France enacted higher tariffs in 1892, and other countries, including Russia and Italy, adopted protective measures. Britain, by contrast, largely maintained free trade, though debates over tariff reform intensified in the early twentieth century. Protectionism and free trade were competing responses to industrial competition, and each served particular interests: protection benefited some domestic producers but raised costs for consumers and for industries that used the protected goods.

Governments both encouraged large enterprises, for example through contracts and tariff protection, and worried about market concentration. The central question is why industrial competition produced both demands for freer markets and efforts to restrict competition. The answer lies in the contradictory incentives created by capital-intensive production, volatile prices, and the political influence of different economic groups.

Cartel agreements were difficult to sustain even when participants shared an interest in higher prices. Members could disagree over production quotas, receive different costs from their mines or furnaces, and find advantages in selling secretly outside the agreed arrangement. The Rhenish-Westphalian Coal Syndicate illustrates these tensions: its efforts to organize sales and output encountered the competing priorities of large integrated enterprises, smaller mines, and customers seeking inexpensive fuel. Cartels therefore did not abolish rivalry; rather, they shifted some of it into bargaining over quotas, enforcement, and market access. Students should distinguish the existence of an agreement from evidence that it successfully controlled every transaction.

Section 101.16

Governments, Technical Institutions, Patents, and the Competition for Industrial Power

Industrial development in this period was neither purely private enterprise nor entirely state-directed. Governments shaped it through many channels: patent and company law, technical education, transport and communications infrastructure, military procurement, public contracts, standards and measurement, tariffs, and the regulation or ownership of utilities. The nature of state involvement varied. In Germany, states and the Empire supported technical universities, the patent system, and institutions such as the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt. In France, the state had a strong tradition of engineering corps and public works. Switzerland relied on federal and cantonal support for technical education, and Britain depended more on private initiative and voluntary associations, though state involvement in areas such as the telegraph and navy was significant. In Russia, the state under finance ministers such as Sergei Witte promoted railways and heavy industry in the 1890s, relying on tariffs, state contracts, and foreign capital.

Military demand

Military modernization contributed to demand for steel, chemicals, explosives, ships, communications equipment, and engineering expertise. Armaments firms such as Krupp, Schneider, Armstrong, and Škoda grew with state orders. Alfred Nobel's invention of dynamite in 1867 and later smokeless powders transformed explosives. The naval competition among Britain, Germany, and others in the early twentieth century drove demand for armor plate, shipbuilding, and large guns. Industrial capacity became a component of strategic power, a theme returned to in Section 101.20 and in Chapter 107, which examines the diplomatic context.

Exhibitions, utilities, and public authority

Industrial exhibitions, from London in 1851 to the Paris exhibitions of 1855, 1867, 1878, 1889, and 1900, were venues where governments and firms displayed technological achievement, compared themselves with rivals, and educated the public. Their rhetoric of progress should be read critically, since exhibitions also presented selected images of national and imperial power. Public authorities shaped infrastructure: railway systems were built, regulated, or owned by states to different degrees; telephone systems were often run by state postal administrations or private companies under license; and electricity and tramway franchises were granted by municipalities that sometimes retained ownership.

State participation could stimulate innovation, as when governments funded education or placed large orders, but it could also reinforce uneven access, political privilege, and military competition. Contracts and tariffs created beneficiaries and opponents, and close links between officials and firms raised questions of influence. The central question is how government institutions and policies influenced the development of modern industrial economies. The answer is that they set the conditions within which firms, banks, and scientists operated, and that states with different institutions, priorities, and strategic concerns produced different mixes of private and public activity.

Public procurement offered an unusually clear example of interaction between states and industry. Naval departments required armor plate, engines, electrical systems, and artillery built to precise standards, creating dependable demand for some engineering enterprises. Technical institutes and national testing laboratories helped define measures and compare the performance of materials, while municipalities negotiated the prices and service areas of electric-lighting and tramway companies. Government decisions could also create new barriers: an exclusive utility franchise might stabilize investment but exclude competitors, and tariff protection could assist one sector while raising input prices for another. State policy was therefore part of the industrial system, not an external influence that could simply be added afterward.

Section 101.17

New Industrial Work: Engineers, Technicians, Skilled Labor, and the Changing Workforce

The new industries changed who worked in them and how. Alongside the factory operatives and miners familiar from earlier industrialization, there appeared growing numbers of professional engineers, industrial chemists, electricians, draftsmen, technicians, clerks, managers, and skilled machine operators. Large firms built technical departments and offices, and the share of salaried employees in the workforce of leading enterprises increased. Technical qualifications became a path to advancement for some, though access to technical education depended on family means, region, and gender.

Skill, danger, and repetition

Highly specialized technical occupations coexisted with demanding, dangerous, and repetitive labor. Steelworks involved extreme heat, heavy loads, and shift work, often twelve-hour shifts with long continuous operations, and accidents were common. Chemical factories exposed workers to toxic substances, and some occupational diseases became better recognized in this period, such as the necrosis of the jaw caused by white phosphorus in match factories, which led to international agreement to restrict its use in 1906. Electrical-equipment factories employed skilled assemblers, winders, and testers, while transport enterprises employed large workforces of railwaymen, tram workers, and seamen. Smaller workshops continued to employ artisans in traditional trades and sweated labor in some sectors. Working conditions therefore varied sharply by industry, region, and firm.

Women, clerks, and communications work

Women worked in textile and clothing industries, food processing, some chemical and electrical assembly tasks, and domestic service, and the period saw a significant expansion of clerical and communications occupations in which women were increasingly employed, such as typists, shop assistants, and telephone operators. These jobs offered new opportunities but also lower pay than men's, restricted promotion, and prevailing assumptions about gender roles. Machinery could increase productivity while reorganizing skills: some craftsmen found their expertise embodied in machines and their autonomy reduced by supervision, while others found new work as setters, maintenance fitters, or inspectors. Employment security and wages depended on economic cycles, and the instability of the period affected workers severely.

Multiple Perspectives

The Same Industrial Change, Seen from Different Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that historians have identified in company records, memoirs, factory inspection reports, and trade-union and employer publications. They are not quotations from any single named person, and the four groups did not hold equal power over the conditions of work or investment.

From the perspective of a skilled technician or machine fitter: New machinery created demand for those who could set, maintain, and repair it, and technical training offered a route to higher wages and status. At the same time, skills based on long craft experience could be reduced in value when machines or standardized methods embodied them, and technicians depended on firms' decisions about hiring and organization.
From the perspective of an unskilled or semi-skilled factory worker: Large plants offered steady wages when business was good, but the work could be heavy, hot, monotonous, and hazardous, with long shifts and strict supervision. Workers had little say in technological decisions, were exposed to layoffs in downturns, and often bore the health costs of dust, fumes, and accidents.
From the perspective of an industrial manager or engineer-director: Larger plants and costly equipment required careful planning, cost control, and reliable output. Managers sought to coordinate supply, labor, and sales, to reduce waste, and to attract skilled staff, and they often viewed labor discipline and technical efficiency as central to the firm's survival in competitive and unstable markets.
From the perspective of an investor or shareholder: Industrial enterprises offered the prospect of high returns, but also risks of crisis, technological obsolescence, and speculation. Investors, from wealthy families to a growing group of middle-class shareholders, relied on banks, company reports, and press commentary, and had little direct knowledge of conditions inside the works.

The consequences for labor organization, including the growth of trade unions and mass socialist parties, are examined in Chapter 102, and this chapter introduces only the technological and organizational conditions in which those movements developed. The central question is how new industries transformed the organization, skills, and distribution of industrial labor. The answer is that they expanded technical and administrative employment, reorganized skills, and intensified the contrast between secure, trained positions and exposed, physically demanding work.

Industrial specialization produced new hierarchies within workplaces. A trained electrical fitter might exercise considerable discretion while testing a machine, whereas a worker handling acids or operating a repetitive press could face tightly supervised routines and serious hazards. Chemical workers encountered corrosive substances and fumes; metalworkers confronted heat, noise, and heavy equipment; telegraph and telephone staff faced strict expectations about speed and accuracy. Employers sometimes offered apprenticeship or advancement to retain skilled personnel, but employers and workers did not share equal bargaining power. Women's growing presence in clerical and communications work created new opportunities while often assigning them lower wages and narrower career prospects than male colleagues. This varied occupational world helps explain why industrial progress could increase both technical expertise and insecurity.

Section 101.18

Electricity, Consumer Goods, Urban Services, and the Industrial Environment

New technologies reshaped urban life and consumption, but unevenly. Electric lighting appeared in theaters, department stores, cafés, public buildings, and affluent districts of major cities from the 1880s, and electric trams and telephones spread along with it. Gas lighting, the dominant urban illumination of the mid-century, was improved, as with the incandescent gas mantle in the 1890s, and remained widely used. Paraffin lamps, candles, and wood or coal stoves remained normal in most households, including in many cities. The public visibility of electrical innovation, in exhibitions and shop windows, should not lead us to overestimate how many people used it daily.

Consumption, retail, and advertising

Manufactured and standardized consumer products became more common: branded foods, packaged goods, ready-made clothing, soap, bicycles, and household items. Department stores, mail-order catalogues, chain retailers, and cooperative societies changed retailing, and advertising expanded in newspapers, posters, and exhibitions. Household technologies such as sewing machines, gas cookers, and, later, electric irons and vacuum cleaners entered some homes, but affordability and access to utilities limited them. Consumption differed profoundly between affluent urban households, working-class neighborhoods, and rural communities.

Everyday Life and Social History

An Electrified District and a Neighborhood Still Using Older Systems

Consider two kinds of place in a European industrial country around 1905. In a central district of a large city, the main streets may have electric street lighting and trams running along them, shops lit by electric lamps, a telephone exchange, and perhaps apartments of well-off families with electric light, a telephone, and piped water and gas. Cafés, offices, and department stores use electric lamps and sometimes lifts, and the district is served by an electricity company or municipal utility. Residents of such districts, mostly the middle and upper classes and their employees, experience time and the evening differently: shops stay open later, streets are better lit, and travel across the city is quicker.

In a working-class neighborhood on the edge of the same city, or in a village a few kilometers away, the experience may be quite different. Many homes would use paraffin lamps or candles, with gas in some, cook on coal or wood stoves, and draw water from a shared tap or pump. Electric trams may pass through the district but residents may not use them often because of the fare, and a telephone is a rare thing, perhaps available in a shop or inn. Smoke from factory chimneys and domestic fires is part of daily life, and children and adults live near workplaces. In a rural village, the main technological changes might be the railway station, the telegraph office, mass-produced goods bought at the market, and perhaps a creamery or a mill driven by water or steam. Historians draw on census data, municipal records, utility company archives, memoirs, and surveys to reconstruct these differences, while recognizing that surviving sources more often record the affluent and the urban.

Environmental and health consequences

Industrial growth also produced severe environmental and health problems. Coal smoke blackened cities and contributed to respiratory disease, and fog and smoke in cities such as London and in the Ruhr and other industrial regions were notorious. The Leblanc alkali process released hydrochloric acid, prompting the British Alkali Act of 1863, one of the early pieces of industrial pollution regulation. Chemical works, dye factories, and steelworks discharged waste into rivers, and in the Ruhr, the Emscher river became a heavily polluted channel, with a regional authority created at the end of the century to manage it. Mining caused subsidence and landscape change. Oil refining and drilling, notably in Baku, created pollution, fires, and hazardous living conditions around the fields.

Greater productivity coexisted with new public-health and environmental problems, and the costs fell disproportionately on workers and on neighborhoods near plants. Some public-health improvements, such as water supply and sewage systems, depended on the same engineering capacity, and municipal governments, engineers, and physicians gradually developed responses. The central question is how technological innovation transformed everyday life while producing new forms of inequality and environmental damage. The answer is that benefits were concentrated in particular districts and social groups, while costs, including pollution and occupational risk, were spread unequally.

Electricity was also a business relationship between infrastructure operators and customers. A city could install a generating station, yet its residents would not receive service unless lines reached their streets, connection fees were affordable, and suppliers could charge rates that covered equipment and maintenance. Early lighting was therefore concentrated in prestigious commercial districts, transport facilities, and comparatively prosperous buildings. The same contrast applied to sanitation and transport: visible civic improvements could stand close to overcrowded housing whose occupants remained exposed to smoke, polluted waterways, and hazardous workplaces. The distinction between public demonstration and ordinary access is essential to understanding why celebrated inventions did not yield simultaneous improvements in living conditions.

The distribution of industrial costs often followed the geography of political influence. Factory owners and municipal authorities could negotiate where tramlines, generating stations, or waste facilities would be placed, while poorer residents had fewer opportunities to redirect pollution away from their neighborhoods. Engineers developed methods for more efficient combustion and waste treatment, but technical possibility did not guarantee that firms or public authorities would pay for them. Industrial history must therefore examine public choices about service, regulation, and risk alongside the ingenuity of inventors.

Section 101.19

Global Commodity Chains, Industrial Resources, and Europe's Expanding Economic Reach

European industrial development depended on increasingly extensive international networks of resources, markets, and capital. Cotton came from the American South, Egypt, and India; copper from the United States, Chile, and Spain, where the British-owned Rio Tinto company began large-scale mining in the 1870s; nitrates from the Atacama region of Chile; rubber from the Amazon and, increasingly, from tropical Africa and Southeast Asia; petroleum from the Russian Empire, Romania, Galicia, and the United States; and grain, meat, and other foodstuffs from the Americas, Russia, and elsewhere. Industrial Europe also exported manufactured goods, machinery, capital, and engineers.

Infrastructure of the world economy

International shipping, cable communication, banking, commodity exchanges, and insurance supported these flows. London, in particular, served as a center for trade finance and shipping, while European capital financed railways and mines in the Americas, Asia, Africa, and elsewhere. The industrial regions of Europe were thus tied into a global system in which prices, supplies, and demand in distant places mattered. Baku offers a striking example within the Russian Empire: oil, financed by Russian and foreign capital and moved by rail, pipeline, and tanker, fueled the growth of a major city and an international industry, while its workers, many of them migrants, lived and labored under harsh conditions.

Effects beyond Europe

Demand for industrial materials affected regions beyond Europe. Mining and plantation economies in Latin America, Africa, and Asia expanded to supply raw materials, sometimes with forced or tightly controlled labor, and local producers of goods displaced by European manufactures or by synthetic substitutes, as in the case of Indian indigo, suffered. Chilean nitrate exporters, Egyptian cotton growers, and many others were integrated into dependent relationships with European markets, though local elites, merchants, and governments were active participants and sometimes beneficiaries. These economic relationships involved inequality and, in some places, imperial power.

Historians distinguish industrial demand from the broader political, strategic, military, and ideological motives that shaped imperial expansion. Economic interests were one factor among others, and the territorial conquests and colonial administrations of the period are the subject of Chapters 104 and 105, not of this chapter. What this section establishes is that industrial capitalism deepened Europe's integration with the world economy, and that this integration was unequal: it brought raw materials, food, and profit to Europe and capital, goods, and sometimes coercive pressure to other regions. The central question is how industrial development deepened Europe's integration with the world economy, and what inequalities accompanied those connections.

Resource chains also transmitted economic uncertainty across borders. A European manufacturer could depend on copper prices determined partly by mining output in Chile or the United States, while a dye plant's synthetic product might reduce demand for cultivated colorants elsewhere. Freight rates, credit conditions, railway connections, and political decisions shaped whether producers could respond to shifts in demand. These networks did not consist only of European financiers extracting passive supplies: regional governments, merchants, workers, and entrepreneurs negotiated contracts, organized transport, and sometimes challenged foreign control. Nevertheless, unequal bargaining positions and coercive labor arrangements could channel much of the benefit toward shipping companies, banks, and industrial purchasers. Such inequalities were important consequences of industrial integration even where no formal colonial annexation occurred.

Section 101.20

The Second Industrial Revolution and the Transformation of Europe before 1914

By 1914, steel, chemicals, electricity, petroleum, communications, new forms of transportation, and large enterprises had transformed European industry. The central argument of this chapter is that these developments increased productive capacity, encouraged larger business organizations, tightened connections among banks, manufacturers, and states, and altered the geography of industrial power. Scientific knowledge, investment capital, state policy, access to resources, and skilled labor became increasingly interconnected, and no single factor explains the outcomes.

Different national trajectories

Britain remained a major industrial and financial power, with strengths in shipbuilding, engineering, textiles, coal, and global commerce, but a relatively smaller share of world manufacturing than in 1850. Modern estimates, which are approximate, place German steel output in 1913 at more than twice the British figure, with French output smaller still. Germany led in chemicals and electrical engineering and had a dense network of cartels and universal banks. France had a diversified economy with significant engineering, automobile, and luxury industries and a high proportion of smaller firms. Belgium and Switzerland maintained specialized strengths, Sweden developed engineering firms, Italy and Austria-Hungary industrialized in specific regions, and the Russian Empire experienced rapid but uneven growth in industries supported by the state and foreign capital. Industrialization did not produce uniform prosperity or erase differences between countries and regions, and southern and eastern Europe remained mostly agrarian.

Gains and costs

The achievements of modern industrial systems were considerable: higher productivity, greater material output, faster communication and transport, new medicines and fertilizers, and, for many, rising living standards. Their social, environmental, and political costs were also considerable: dangerous work, pollution, economic instability, concentrated market power, and unequal access to new services. Whether technological change primarily expanded productive capacity, concentrated economic power, transformed daily life, or intensified international competition is a matter of emphasis: it did all four, in different places and for different groups.

Historical Debate

What Drove the Second Industrial Revolution: Science, Organization, Finance, Resources, or the State?

The question: Historians agree that Europe's industrial transformation between 1850 and 1914 was real and substantial. They disagree about the relative importance of the factors that explain it and that explain national differences, particularly Germany's rise in chemicals and electrical engineering relative to Britain.

Interpretation A: Knowledge and institutions of science and education were central. Scholars such as Joel Mokyr emphasize the growth and diffusion of useful knowledge and the institutions that connected science to production, including technical universities, laboratories, and professional engineering. In this view, countries that built stronger links between research and industry gained advantages in science-based industries.
Interpretation B: Organization, finance, and market structure were central. Alfred Chandler and others emphasize investment in large-scale production, distribution, and management, and the role of corporate organization. Others stress the role of banks, capital markets, and cartels, or of natural resource endowments, tariffs, and state policy. In this view, the form of enterprise and its financial and political environment shaped which technologies were developed and commercialized.

Evidence: The two interpretations draw on overlapping evidence: patent and educational records, company archives and annual reports, bank records, production statistics, and comparisons among countries. Each also faces difficulties: statistics are incomplete and not always comparable, company records are selective, and counterfactuals about what would have happened with different institutions cannot be tested directly. Many historians therefore combine the approaches rather than treat them as exclusive alternatives.

Why it matters: How one weighs these factors affects how one explains Britain's relative decline, Germany's rise, and the divergence of national paths, and it determines what lessons, if any, are drawn about industrial policy. It also cautions against single-cause or nationally triumphalist explanations.

Cause and Effect

Research, Investment, Productivity, Cities, Trade, and Geopolitical Competition

Long-term causes

The first industrial revolution's coal, iron, railways, and capital accumulation; growth of universities and technical education; expansion of scientific knowledge; integration of markets; and development of banking and company law.

Short-term causes

The price instability and competition after 1873; German unification and its large internal market; breakthroughs in steelmaking, chemistry, and electrical machinery; and state support for education, infrastructure, and armaments.

Event or transformation

Between about 1850 and 1914, new materials, energy systems, communications, and enterprise forms were developed, financed, and commercialized across Europe, in uneven sequence.

Immediate effects

Higher productive capacity; larger firms and cartels; closer bank and industry ties; new urban services and consumer goods; new occupational groups and hazards; and heavier demand for raw materials and shipping.

Long-term consequences

A shifted balance of industrial power within Europe; deeper integration with global resource and capital flows; the social conditions of mass politics; and industrial capacity as a central element of military power in 1914 and after.

Historical complexity

No single cause or actor explains these changes. Each technology depended on others, on institutions, and on contingency, and the outcomes differed by country, industry, and social group. Later outcomes, including industrialized war, were not inevitable products of industrial development.

Historical complexity: Because science, capital, and state policy reinforced one another, historians treat the process as a system. A change in any one element, such as a financial crisis or a tariff decision, could alter the whole.

Looking forward

The Second Industrial Revolution connects directly to the next chapters of Part XVI. It changed the size and concentration of the industrial workforce, which forms the background to the mass political movements, trade unions, and women's movements of Chapter 102. It supplied the materials, finance, and communications underlying the imperial systems examined in Chapters 104 and 105. And it increased the military and industrial capabilities available to governments, a theme that will be taken up in Chapter 107 on the road to war, and in Chapter 110 on total war and the European home fronts. These later developments were not predetermined by industrial growth, but they were made possible by it. The central question of the chapter, how the Second Industrial Revolution transformed Europe's economic power, social organization, and international position before 1914, has no single answer, but its component answers fit together: a more powerful, more interconnected, and more unequal industrial Europe.

The chapter's examples also show why the timing of technological change matters. A new process could be patented decades before it became widespread; an electric exhibition could demonstrate feasibility without paying customers; and a large steelworks could produce cheaper material without immediately transforming every building or railway. Different technologies advanced together only when energy supply, transport, finance, skilled labor, and legal arrangements could be coordinated. Consequently, national comparisons should assess both the industries a country led and those it continued to operate by older methods. Industrial modernity was not a single threshold that Europe crossed at once but a shifting set of capacities and constraints.

Europe's technological strengths also carried institutional vulnerabilities into the new century. Large factories required steady supplies of energy and imported materials; credit networks exposed firms to disruptions in distant financial markets; and states became increasingly interested in securing communications and industrial capacity for military use. None of these conditions made international conflict inevitable. They did mean, however, that the economic consequences of political crises could spread across much larger networks than before and that industrial infrastructure had acquired strategic value far beyond the factory gate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Second Industrial Revolution, the set of overlapping technological, scientific, organizational, and financial developments that transformed European industry between about 1850 and 1914. Its central argument is that industrialization in this period must be understood as a system. Steel, chemicals, electricity, petroleum, communications, new transport, and large enterprises depended on one another and on research, skilled labor, capital, legal institutions, and state policy, and no invention by itself explains the transformation.

The chapter distinguished the first and second industrial revolutions without separating them into two eras. Coal, steam, and railways remained essential and expanded, while new materials and energy systems were layered on top of them. What was distinctive about the later phase was the closer connection between scientific knowledge and production, the growing scale of enterprises, and the integration of markets by transport, communication, and finance.

In materials and chemistry, the Bessemer, open-hearth, and Thomas-Gilchrist processes made large quantities of affordable steel available, with consequences for railways, shipping, construction, and armaments. Perkin's mauveine, the German dye firms, synthetic alizarin and indigo, the Solvay process, fertilizers, pharmaceuticals, and the Haber-Bosch process showed how laboratories and factories could be linked. Each case required distinguishing discovery, patenting, commercial production, and widespread adoption, which were often separated by years.

Electricity and petroleum introduced new energy systems. Dynamos, generators, motors, and alternating-current transmission, culminating in the demonstration between Lauffen and Frankfurt in 1891, opened the way to electric lighting, tramways, and industrial power, though electrification was far from complete by 1914. Petroleum, developed in Galicia, Romania, and above all Baku, supplied kerosene and, later, motor fuels, but coal remained dominant. Internal-combustion engines, bicycles, and early automobiles generated new manufacturing industries whose broad economic impact came mostly after the First World War.

Transport and communication networks expanded and changed. Steel rails and ships, electric trams, and underground railways extended earlier systems, coexisting with steam, horses, and walking. Telegraphs, submarine cables, telephones, and early wireless signaling compressed the time needed to transmit information, affecting markets, diplomacy, and the coordination of firms. Mechanization, standardization, and large-scale manufacture changed factories, though the moving assembly line was an American development of 1913 and small workshops persisted in Europe.

Institutions made this expansion possible. Joint-stock companies and limited liability, which developed at different times and in different forms across Britain, France, and the German states, allowed pooling of capital. Banks such as the Crédit Mobilier and the German universal banks financed industry in different ways, and London served as the center of international finance. Cartels, trusts, and tariffs reflected the tension between competition and efforts to control markets, especially after the financial crisis of 1873 and the prolonged period of price instability sometimes called the Long Depression.

Governments shaped these developments through patents, technical education, infrastructure, procurement, standards, tariffs, and the regulation or ownership of utilities. Military demand for steel, explosives, ships, and communications linked industrial capacity to state power. Industrial exhibitions displayed technological achievement and national rivalry, and state participation both stimulated innovation and reinforced unequal access and strategic competition.

The social consequences were uneven. New industries created technical, managerial, and clerical occupations, expanded women's employment in clerical and communications work, and intensified the contrast between skilled and secure positions and demanding or dangerous labor. Electric lighting, consumer goods, and urban services transformed life in some districts while many households continued to use older systems. Industrial growth also produced coal smoke, chemical pollution, occupational disease, and other environmental costs borne unequally.

Regional and national differences were pronounced. Germany became a leader in chemicals and electrical engineering, Britain remained a major industrial and financial power with a declining relative share of world manufacturing, France retained a diversified economy with many smaller firms, and Belgium, Switzerland, Sweden, northern Italy, Bohemia, and parts of the Russian Empire followed their own paths. Large parts of southern and eastern Europe remained agrarian. Historians debate the relative importance of science, organization, finance, resources, and state policy, and most combine these factors.

Europe's industries also depended on global commodity chains for cotton, copper, nitrates, rubber, petroleum, and food, and on capital, shipping, and communication networks that deepened Europe's integration with the world economy, with unequal effects on producing regions. By 1914 these developments had changed the economic power, social organization, and international position of Europe, and had created material foundations for mass politics, imperial expansion, and industrialized warfare, though those outcomes were not predetermined. Chapter 102 turns to mass politics, labor, and women's movements.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Second Industrial Revolution
A modern scholarly label for overlapping technological and organizational changes, c. 1850 to 1914, involving steel, chemicals, electricity, petroleum, communications, and large firms; not a sharp break from earlier industrialization.
Bessemer process
A method announced in 1856 of converting molten pig iron to steel by blowing air through it; it became commercially reliable only after further improvements.
Open-hearth (Siemens-Martin) process
A slower steelmaking method, developed in the 1860s, using a regenerative furnace that allowed greater control of composition and the use of scrap.
Thomas-Gilchrist (basic) process
A late-1870s steelmaking method using a basic furnace lining that removed phosphorus, enabling use of phosphorus-rich ores such as those of Lorraine.
Coal-tar chemistry
The use of by-products of coal gas and coke production as raw materials for dyes, medicines, and other chemicals.
Mauveine
The purple dye discovered by Perkin in 1856 and produced commercially from 1857, an early synthetic organic dye.
Industrial research laboratory
A company facility in which trained scientists systematically investigated products and processes, pioneered by German chemical firms.
Technische Hochschule
A German-language technical university that trained engineers and applied scientists.
Patent
A legal grant of exclusive rights to an invention for a limited period, with a published specification; national rules differed.
Dynamo
A machine that converts mechanical energy into electrical current using electromagnetic induction; the dynamo-electric principle used the machine's own current to energize its magnets.
Alternating current (AC)
Electric current that periodically reverses direction, which can be transformed to high voltages for efficient long-distance transmission.
Electrification
The extended process of building generation and distribution systems and adopting electric power, which unfolded over decades.
Kerosene
A refined petroleum product used mainly for lighting before gasoline and diesel fuels became central.
Internal-combustion engine
An engine in which fuel burns inside the cylinder, including Otto four-stroke, Daimler-Maybach high-speed, and Diesel compression-ignition types.
Nitrogen fixation
The conversion of atmospheric nitrogen into compounds such as ammonia; achieved industrially by the Haber-Bosch process, whose plant opened at Oppau in 1913.
Solvay process
The ammonia-soda method of making soda ash developed in the 1860s, which displaced the polluting Leblanc process.
Submarine cable
An insulated telegraph cable laid on the sea floor; a durable transatlantic cable was established in 1866.
Wireless telegraphy
Transmission of coded signals by electromagnetic waves, developed in the 1890s; distinct from later voice broadcasting.
Standardization
Agreement on specifications for parts, measures, or products so that they can be made, combined, and traded consistently.
Economies of scale
Reductions in unit cost achieved by increasing the volume of production and spreading fixed costs.
Moving assembly line
A system in which the product moves past stationary workers performing specialized tasks; introduced by Ford in 1913 in the United States.
Joint-stock company
A business whose capital is divided into transferable shares held by many investors.
Limited liability
A legal principle limiting shareholders' losses to the amount invested; introduced at different times in different countries.
Universal bank
A bank, typical of Germany, combining deposit-taking, lending, securities underwriting, and long-term involvement with industrial firms.
Cartel
An agreement among independent firms to regulate prices, output, or markets.
Trust
In the American usage of the period, a legal device combining control of several companies; later a general term for large combinations.
Protectionism
Trade policy that uses tariffs or other measures to shield domestic producers from foreign competition.
Long Depression
A label for the period of price deflation and recurrent difficulty after 1873, which historians now characterize as uneven rather than a continuous contraction.
Commodity chain
The linked stages by which raw materials are extracted, transported, processed, and sold, often across continents.
Municipal utility
A service such as gas, water, electricity, or trams owned or franchised by a city government.
Dual-use technology
A technology, such as ammonia synthesis, with both civilian and military applications.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how industrial change in the later nineteenth century altered the material basis of European power. The new technologies were not simply inventions: they were embedded in institutions of education, finance, law, and government, and they depended on the labor of engineers, clerks, skilled workers, miners, and unskilled operatives. Understanding industrialization as a system helps explain both its achievements and its costs.

The chapter also revealed how uneven this transformation was. It connected the Ruhr, Lorraine, Basel, Baku, and Bohemia in networks of capital and trade while leaving large regions largely agrarian, and it deepened the integration of Europe with global sources of raw materials and markets. These patterns bear on the larger argument of Volume IV, which traces how Enlightenment ideas, revolution, industry, and empire combined to remake European states and societies, and they show industrial development as one of several interacting forces and not as a self-sufficient engine of history.

The developments examined here shaped later chapters in several ways. They enlarged and reorganized the industrial workforce, helping to create the conditions for the mass politics of Chapter 102. They supplied the materials, finance, and communications on which the imperial systems of Chapters 104 and 105 depended. And they increased the industrial and military capacities available to governments, a theme that becomes central in Chapters 107 and 110. None of these later outcomes was predetermined, and the chapter has tried to avoid reading 1914 into every earlier development.

Finally, the chapter leaves students with a methodological caution that will serve them throughout the textbook: dates of invention, demonstration, and adoption differ, and the visible symbols of change, such as an exhibition or a famous machine, can overstate how quickly ordinary lives changed. The history of technology is also a history of choices, institutions, and unequal consequences.
