The Northern Renaissance and Christian Humanism

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 1516, within the same year, two books left presses in Switzerland and the Low Countries that would be read for generations: a Greek and Latin New Testament prepared by a scholar from Rotterdam, and a short Latin fiction about an island with no private property, written by an English lawyer. Neither author was a prince, a cardinal, or a soldier. Both worked with printers, correspondents, and a few learned friends, and both believed that careful reading, linguistic skill, and moral seriousness could improve the societies in which they lived.

The New Testament was the work of Desiderius Erasmus, issued at Basel by the printer Johann Froben under the title Novum Instrumentum. The island fiction was Utopia, by Thomas More, printed at Louvain. Together with the altarpieces of Bruges and Ghent, the engravings of Nuremberg, and the schoolrooms of Deventer, Oxford, and Alcalá de Henares, they represent a cultural world that this chapter calls the Northern Renaissance. That label has a long history and real limitations, which the chapter examines in its first section. It is used here as a convenient name for a series of artistic, educational, scholarly, and religious developments in the Low Countries, the German lands, France, England, Iberia, and neighboring regions, roughly between 1400 and 1530.

Chapter 56 explained the commercial and political setting of the Italian city states, Chapter 57 traced the recovery and teaching of classical learning, and Chapter 58 examined the transformation of Italian art and architecture. A reader who moves from those chapters to this one might assume that the Renaissance was an Italian invention that was then exported across the Alps. This chapter argues that such a picture is too simple. Northern Europe already possessed wealthy cities, sophisticated courts, universities, cathedral schools, manuscript workshops, devotional movements, and traditions of religious criticism. Italian books, ideas, and works of art did cross the Alps, and northern scholars and artists traveled south. But the exchange moved in both directions, and what northern Europeans did with what they received was often distinctive. Netherlandish painters developed a language of luminous detail that Italian patrons admired and bought. German printmakers made images that circulated across the continent. Northern scholars joined classical learning to scriptural study with an intensity that Italian humanism, though also frequently Christian, rarely displayed in the same form.

This combination is usually called Christian humanism. Its participants studied Greek and Latin literature, learned Hebrew, edited the Church Fathers and the Bible, and promoted education as a way of improving character. They also criticized what they regarded as superstition, ignorance, clerical neglect, and scholastic quarrels that obscured the simple moral teaching of the Gospels. Most of them wished to reform Christian life within existing institutions. Erasmus, More, Colet, Lefèvre d'Étaples, Cardinal Cisneros, and many others differed substantially from one another, and only some of them later accepted the Protestant movement that began in 1517. Christian humanism therefore cannot be treated as Protestantism before Protestantism, and this chapter keeps the two carefully separate.

The evidence for this chapter is unusually varied. Surviving paintings, panels, prints, and illuminated books allow historians to study technique, patronage, and devotional use, and modern technical methods, including infrared imaging and tree ring dating of wooden panels, have refined attributions and chronologies. Printed books are themselves artifacts of the period, and the volumes produced by presses at Basel, Venice, Paris, Louvain, Antwerp, and Alcalá reveal what editors thought readers would buy. Erasmus left roughly three thousand surviving letters, written by him and to him, which make him one of the best documented intellectual figures of the age. Guild records, municipal accounts, inventories, contracts, school statutes, and wills show how artists, teachers, and printers actually earned their livings. Each type of source has limits. Letters were often written for publication and shaped by rhetorical convention. Surviving paintings represent what was preserved, not everything that was made. Official records say little about women, servants, apprentices, and ordinary readers, whose experience must often be reconstructed indirectly.

The chapter is organized in four movements. Sections 59.1 to 59.3 establish the geography, institutions, cities, and courts of northern Europe. Sections 59.4 to 59.8 examine northern art, from the oil paintings of Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden to the engravings of Albrecht Dürer and the portraits of Hans Holbein. Sections 59.9 to 59.14 turn to humanist scholarship, Christian humanism, Erasmus, biblical philology, satire, and Hebrew studies. Sections 59.15 to 59.18 follow humanism into England, France, and Iberia, analyze printing and education, and conclude by asking how this cultural world stood at the threshold of the Reformation.

This chapter belongs to Part IX, which examines Renaissance Europe, and to Volume III, which traces the movement from Renaissance and religious reform toward overseas empire and the competitive state system. Its particular task is to show how the Renaissance became a European rather than only an Italian phenomenon, and to explain the intellectual tensions that Chapter 60 and, in Part X, Chapters 61 and 62 will carry forward. It does not narrate the Reformation. Rather, it prepares students to understand why scholars who shared a love of learning and a desire for renewal could take sharply different paths when the unity of western Christendom began to break.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians use the term Northern Renaissance, and assess its strengths and limitations as a label for artistic and intellectual change in the Low Countries, German lands, France, England, and Iberia.
  2. Describe the medieval devotional, educational, and institutional foundations, including the Devotio Moderna, on which northern humanism and art developed.
  3. Analyze how urban commerce, Burgundian court patronage, guilds, and religious institutions shaped the production of northern art and learning.
  4. Interpret representative works by Jan van Eyck, Rogier van der Weyden, Hieronymus Bosch, and Albrecht Dürer with attention to date, patron, medium, use, and scholarly debate.
  5. Distinguish between refinement of oil painting techniques by northern artists and the incorrect claim that they invented oil painting.
  6. Compare Italian and northern developments in education, patronage, artistic media, scholarship, and religious concerns, identifying exchange as well as difference.
  7. Define Christian humanism and trace how it combined classical philology, biblical scholarship, moral education, and religious criticism.
  8. Trace Erasmus's career, his publications, and the chronology of the 1516 Greek and Latin New Testament in relation to the Complutensian Polyglot.
  9. Evaluate the relationship between Christian humanist Hebrew scholarship, Jewish learning, and anti-Jewish prejudice through the Reuchlin affair.
  10. Assess the contributions of More, Colet, Budé, Lefèvre d'Étaples, Nebrija, Cisneros, and Vives to regional variations in humanism.
  11. Explain how printing, schools, and readers circulated northern humanism, and identify who was included in or excluded from that circulation.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether Christian humanism prepared the Reformation, and explain why the relationship was complex rather than linear.

Historical Context

By 1400 northern Europe contained some of the most urbanized, commercially active, and politically complex regions of the continent. Flanders and Brabant, in the southern Low Countries, were among the densest zones of towns in Europe. Bruges, Ghent, Ypres, Brussels, Antwerp, and Leuven combined textile manufacture, international trade, banking, and powerful guild organizations. The German lands, under the loose political structure of the Holy Roman Empire examined in Chapter 46, contained hundreds of cities and territorial principalities, including the great trading and manufacturing centers of Nuremberg, Augsburg, Cologne, and Basel. France, England, and the Iberian kingdoms had developing royal governments, universities, and court cultures, and their relationships with one another had been shaped by the dynastic and fiscal pressures examined in Chapters 51 and 54.

The political landscape of the fifteenth century matters especially for the arts. After 1384 the Valois dukes of Burgundy gradually acquired Flanders, Brabant, Holland, Hainaut, and neighboring territories, forming a composite state that was not a kingdom but was among the richest and most ceremonially elaborate polities in Europe. Chapter 54 introduced Burgundy and its place among the European powers. Its dukes and the elites of its cities became principal patrons of painters, illuminators, musicians, tapestry weavers, and goldsmiths. After the death of Charles the Bold in 1477, Burgundian lands passed through marriage to the Habsburg dynasty, a transition that connected the Low Countries to the political systems discussed in Chapters 75 and 78.

Intellectual life in the north was already dynamic before Italian humanism arrived. The universities of Paris, Oxford, Cambridge, Leuven, Cologne, Heidelberg, Vienna, and Kraków trained clergy, lawyers, physicians, and officials in the methods of scholastic theology, canon and civil law, and Aristotelian philosophy, as Chapter 48 explained. Cathedral and municipal schools taught Latin to boys, and religious orders maintained libraries and scriptoria. Lay piety had flourished in the cities, with confraternities, endowed masses, pilgrimages, and devotional books forming a rich religious culture. Criticism of clerical wealth, ignorance, and misconduct was an old theme, and the troubles of the Avignon papacy and Great Schism, examined in Chapter 53, intensified debates about reform.

Printing by movable type, introduced in the mid fifteenth century and discussed in Chapter 55, supplied a new technology of communication. By the 1490s presses existed in hundreds of European towns, and printers in Venice, Basel, Paris, Nuremberg, and the Low Countries were publishing classical texts, devotional works, legal books, and illustrated pamphlets. Chapter 60 will examine the wider political consequences of printing; the present chapter focuses on how printers, humanists, and artists used the press to circulate scholarship, reform proposals, and images.

A word of caution about periodization is needed. The dates c. 1400 to 1530 mark the focus of this chapter, not the boundaries of a unified northern age. Developments occurred at different times in different regions. Netherlandish painting reached early maturity in the 1420s and 1430s, while German humanism flourished chiefly between about 1470 and 1520, and humanism in England and Spain gained momentum in the decades around 1500. The chapter also uses Italian examples only where they directly illuminate exchange. The Italian background is established in Chapters 56 through 58 and is not repeated here.

Section 59.1

The Northern Renaissance: Geography, Chronology, and Historical Meaning

The phrase "Northern Renaissance" is a modern scholarly convention. It was shaped by the nineteenth century idea, associated above all with the Swiss historian Jacob Burckhardt and his Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (1860), that the Renaissance began in Italy and expressed a new individualism and worldliness. Later historians, working north of the Alps, found that the fifteenth century in Flanders, Burgundy, and the Rhineland looked less like a break with the medieval past than like its culmination. The Dutch historian Johan Huizinga, in The Waning of the Middle Ages (1919), emphasized the intense religious symbolism, ritual, and court ceremony of the Burgundian world and presented it as an autumn of medieval culture rather than a spring of something new. Art historians such as Erwin Panofsky responded by showing how Netherlandish painters combined medieval devotional purposes with an unprecedented fidelity to the visible world. The result was a lasting scholarly debate: was the Northern Renaissance the same movement as the Italian Renaissance, a regional variant, or a separate set of developments with their own logic?

Most historians now take a middle position. Northern Europe participated in a Europe wide pattern of change that included growing urban wealth, expanding literacy, new technologies of reproduction, greater interest in classical antiquity, and increasing attention to individual experience. But the northern forms of these developments were shaped by local institutions and traditions. Italian humanism was a movement among urban elites, chancery secretaries, teachers, and court scholars, often concerned with civic life and rhetorical education, as Chapter 57 showed. Northern humanism tended to be taught in universities and cathedral chapters and to be closely tied to the study of Scripture, theology, and church reform. Italian painting and sculpture of the fifteenth century were often organized around linear perspective, anatomical study, and classical form, as Chapter 58 explained. Northern painters made their reputations through meticulous observation of surfaces, light, and domestic space, and through the luminous possibilities of oil glazes. Neither tradition was uniform, and the contrasts must not be overdrawn: many Italian humanists were devout Christians, and many northern artists studied Italian works.

Geographically, this chapter uses "northern" in a broad sense, meaning the regions beyond the Alps and Pyrenees whose Renaissance developments were not simply Italian in origin. Five major zones receive attention. The first is the Low Countries, especially Flanders and Brabant, whose cities produced the great school of Netherlandish painting and later became centers of printing and learning at Leuven and Antwerp. The second is the German lands and Switzerland, where Nuremberg, Augsburg, Basel, Strasbourg, Vienna, and a network of university towns hosted artists, printers, and scholars. The third is France, where Paris was the dominant university and publishing city and where the royal court gradually became more receptive to humanist and Italianate influences. The fourth is England, whose universities and London circles of the early sixteenth century produced a distinctive humanism, closely connected to the court. The fifth is the Iberian Peninsula, which belongs geographically to southern Europe but whose universities and scholarship resembled in many respects the northern pattern of biblical and philological work. Poland, Hungary, Bohemia, and Scandinavia also participated, as Section 59.16 notes, though space does not permit full treatment.

The chronology of the chapter spans approximately 1400 to 1530. This period has no single beginning. The introduction of oil painting at the highest level in Bruges and Tournai in the 1420s, the arrival of printing in the 1450s, the gradual spread of Greek learning in the late fifteenth century, and the religious controversies of the 1510s each mark different turning points. Likewise, there is no tidy ending. The opening of the Reformation after 1517 transformed the conditions under which humanists worked and forced many of them to choose between competing loyalties. This chapter treats 1517 and the following decade as a threshold, not as the end of Renaissance culture, which continued to evolve in Catholic, Protestant, and mixed environments throughout the sixteenth century.

One further caution concerns terminology. Words such as "Gothic," "medieval," and "Renaissance" were often applied to northern art by later critics who measured it against Italian classicism. Italian writers of the sixteenth century, Giorgio Vasari among them, admired the technique of some northern artists but often judged northern painting by the standards of Italian proportion and classical form. Such judgments reveal more about Italian taste than about northern achievement. The present chapter therefore tries to evaluate northern works in terms of their own purposes: devotion, donor commemoration, civic display, moral teaching, and the pleasures of looking carefully.

Section 59.2

Medieval Devotion, Education, and the Northern Intellectual Inheritance

Northern humanism did not appear in an intellectual vacuum. By the fifteenth century the north possessed an extensive system of education that ranged from parish and chapter schools to great universities. At the base, boys who could afford to attend Latin schools learned grammar from standard medieval textbooks, memorized passages, and practiced simple composition. Some continued to universities at Paris, Leuven (founded 1425), Cologne, Heidelberg, Vienna, Kraków, Oxford, and Cambridge, where they studied the liberal arts and then theology, law, or medicine under scholastic methods. Chapter 48 explained how these methods used logical analysis, commentary, and disputation to organize knowledge. Scholasticism was not static, and it was not hostile to learning. It was, however, the target of humanist criticism that scholastic Latin was barbarous, that its questions were too abstract, and that its emphasis on dialectic crowded out rhetoric, history, and the direct reading of ancient texts.

Beyond formal schooling, northern societies produced rich forms of lay and clerical devotion. Religious confraternities organized worship, burials, and charity. Guilds maintained altars and chaplaincies. Pilgrimage, relic veneration, and the endowment of masses for the dead were widespread, as Chapter 49 described. These practices were not mere superstition, as later polemic sometimes claimed. They answered real needs for community, consolation, and moral discipline, and they supported many painters, sculptors, and organists.

A particularly influential current of devotion emerged in the northern Low Countries in the late fourteenth century. Geert Groote (1340–1384), a former university man from Deventer who underwent a religious conversion and became a preacher, urged his listeners to cultivate inner devotion, humility, and imitation of Christ. His followers formed communities of lay and clerical men and women who lived together without taking the formal vows of the older religious orders. They became known as the Brothers and Sisters of the Common Life. Related to them were the Augustinian canons of the Windesheim Congregation, who adopted a stricter communal life. Together these groups are associated with the movement called the Devotio Moderna, or "modern devotion."

Historical Vocabulary
Devotio Moderna

Devotio Moderna ("modern devotion") was a late medieval spiritual movement that began in the Low Countries, especially around Deventer, Zwolle, and the valley of the IJssel. It emphasized interior piety, meditation on the life of Christ, moral self examination, manual work, and communal life among laypeople and clergy outside the traditional monastic orders. Its most famous product is the devotional book The Imitation of Christ, usually attributed to the canon Thomas à Kempis and composed in the early fifteenth century.

The word "modern" here did not mean "secular" or "Renaissance." It meant a new style of devotion within medieval Christianity. Historians debate how much the movement influenced humanism. It encouraged reading, copying, and education, and it formed many students who later became humanists, including Erasmus, who attended schools in Deventer and 's-Hertogenbosch connected to its milieu. Yet it was not itself a humanist program. Many of its leaders were suspicious of worldly learning, and Erasmus later wrote critically of some of the institutions in which he was educated. It is also a mistake to say that the Brothers of the Common Life simply ran an extensive school system; they more often provided lodging, supervision, and spiritual guidance for students attending existing town schools. The Devotio Moderna matters to this chapter because it supplies one of the northern roots of the belief that Christianity should be lived inwardly, read directly, and taught to ordinary people.

The movement's emphasis on reading, copying manuscripts, and simple moral instruction contributed to a literate lay piety. Many Brothers earned their livings by producing books, and when printing arrived some communities set up presses. The movement also fostered the use of the vernacular for devotional purposes and encouraged personal prayer based on Scripture and the life of Christ. These emphases overlapped with later humanist and reformist concerns, though they derived from monastic and mystical traditions as much as from classical learning.

Criticism of the church and its personnel was another northern inheritance. The Wycliffite movement in England and the Hussite movement in Bohemia, discussed in Chapter 53, had challenged ecclesiastical authority and promoted vernacular Scripture. Both were condemned, but concerns about clerical wealth, absenteeism, pluralism, ignorance, and the sale of spiritual benefits persisted across Europe. Sermons by popular preachers, satirical poems, and the writings of reforming councilors repeated such themes. Humanists inherited this critical vocabulary and gave it literary polish and philological authority. Chapter 61 will discuss the institutional history of reform and criticism in detail. Here it is enough to note that humanist criticism built on an older stream of northern complaint and was not invented by the Renaissance.

Section 59.3

Northern Cities, Burgundian Courts, and the Patronage of Learning and Art

The economic base of the Northern Renaissance was urban commerce. Bruges, in the fifteenth century, was one of the great trading hubs of Europe, a place where Hanseatic merchants from the Baltic, Italian firms from Genoa, Venice, Florence, and Lucca, Iberian traders, and English wool and cloth exporters met. The Medici bank maintained a branch there, and the foreign merchant communities built chapels and commissioned works of art. As the Zwin channel silted up in the later fifteenth century, commercial leadership gradually shifted to Antwerp, which by about 1500 had become a major center of finance and distribution on the Scheldt, a transition connected to the Atlantic and Asian trade networks examined in Part XI. Ghent and Ypres produced textiles, Brussels and Mechelen housed courts and administrative offices, and Leuven became the site of a university. Wealth, competition, and civic pride combined to support a high demand for luxury goods, from tapestries and goldsmiths' work to altarpieces and portraits.

The Burgundian dukes added a court dimension to urban patronage. Philip the Good founded the chivalric Order of the Golden Fleece in 1430, maintained a famous library of illuminated manuscripts, employed court painters, and used elaborate festivals and processions to project power over his scattered territories. Jan van Eyck served as his court painter and was also employed on diplomatic missions. Burgundian court culture favored costly manuscripts, polyphonic music, tapestries, and pageantry. Franco-Flemish composers such as Guillaume Dufay, Johannes Ockeghem, and later Josquin des Prez gained European reputations, a reminder that northern creativity extended well beyond painting. After the dynastic changes of 1477 and the marriage of Mary of Burgundy to Maximilian of Habsburg, the Low Countries became part of the wider Habsburg dynastic system, and Maximilian's grandson, the future Charles V, was raised in Mechelen under the supervision of his aunt, Margaret of Austria.

Margaret of Austria (1480–1530), who served as regent of the Netherlands from 1507 to 1515 and again from 1519 until her death, is one of the most important female patrons of the northern Renaissance. Her court at Mechelen assembled a notable library and collection of paintings, supported composers and chroniclers, and patronized artists such as Bernard van Orley and Jan Gossart. She also took responsibility for the education of her nephews and nieces, including Charles. Her example illustrates the significant, though socially restricted, role that elite women played as patrons and political actors. Women of lower rank participated in craft and commercial life through family workshops, but their access to guild privileges and formal training was far more limited.

Guilds were a crucial institution. In most northern cities painters, sculptors, illuminators, glass painters, and often printers belonged to guilds, commonly under the patronage of Saint Luke, the traditional painter of the Virgin. Guilds regulated apprenticeship, quality, prices, and admission to masterships, and they supported members in need. A young artist normally spent years in a master's workshop before presenting a masterpiece and paying fees to join. Workshop production involved assistants, copies, and standardized compositions, so that attribution is often difficult. Individual artistic personalities, though increasingly celebrated, worked within collective systems of production, and a "van Eyck" or "Rogier" painting could involve several hands.

Patrons varied. Churches, parish confraternities, and hospitals commissioned altarpieces for public worship. Wealthy merchants, officials, and nobles commissioned panels for private chapels, donor portraits, and domestic devotion. Foreign merchants in Bruges, including Italians, were important buyers, and Netherlandish paintings entered Italian, Spanish, and Portuguese collections in considerable numbers, affecting artistic practice abroad. Notably, patrons often appeared within the works they paid for, kneeling at the edge of a sacred scene. These donor portraits advertised piety and status while also being intended to secure divine favor. Religious institutions, meanwhile, were not only patrons but also schoolmasters, librarians, and employers, linking devotional life to the production and consumption of learning.

Geography Study

Routes of Artists, Scholars, Books, and Printing across Northern and Southern Europe

Using a historical atlas or a physical map of Europe, students should locate and trace the following, keeping in mind that the political boundaries of c. 1500 differ from modern ones and that the Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands, the Holy Roman Empire, and the kingdoms of France, England, Castile, and Aragon had very different structures:

  • The commercial and artistic cities of the Low Countries: Bruges, Ghent, Brussels, Mechelen, Leuven, Antwerp, and Tournai, and the waterways (Scheldt, Meuse, Zwin) that linked them to the North Sea
  • The Rhine corridor from Basel through Strasbourg and Cologne to the Low Countries, and its importance for the movement of books and scholars
  • Nuremberg, Augsburg, and Venice, and the trade route over the Brenner Pass that linked German merchants and artists to Italy (see Chapter 2)

Section 59.4

Robert Campin, Jan van Eyck, and the Transformation of Netherlandish Painting

In the 1420s and 1430s, a group of painters working in Tournai, Bruges, Ghent, and Dijon produced works whose realism, luminosity, and psychological presence had few precedents in European art. The innovators included Robert Campin (c. 1375–1444), a leading painter and civic figure of Tournai, and Jan van Eyck (c. 1390–1441), court painter to Philip the Good. Campin is often identified, though not unanimously, with the artist once called the Master of Flémalle, to whom the Mérode Altarpiece (c. 1427–1432) is attributed. This triptych places the Annunciation in a bourgeois interior furnished with a bench, a table, a candle, and a towel, while the donors kneel outside the open door and Joseph works in his carpenter's shop in the wing. Modern viewers often admire the apparent domesticity. But the painting is a devotional object, and its details contribute to a layered religious meaning that scholars continue to debate.

Jan van Eyck's career can be reconstructed from payment records and signed or dated works. He worked first at the court of John of Bavaria at The Hague, then entered the service of Philip the Good in 1425 and lived in Lille. In 1428 and 1429 he traveled with an embassy to Portugal, where he painted a portrait of the Portuguese princess Isabella in connection with her marriage to Philip, and in 1430 he moved to Bruges. His most famous work is the Ghent Altarpiece, a complex polyptych made for the chapel of Joos Vijd and his wife Lysbeth Borluut in the church of Saint John (now Saint Bavo's Cathedral). According to its inscription, the work was begun by Hubert van Eyck and completed by Jan, and was dedicated in 1432. The role of Hubert remains debated, and some scholars question how much he contributed. The open altarpiece displays the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb and a celestial court, with the Virgin, John the Baptist, singers, and nude figures of Adam and Eve in the upper and side panels. The closed wings show the Annunciation and portraits of the donors.

Van Eyck's portraiture is equally celebrated. The Arnolfini Portrait (1434, National Gallery, London) depicts a man and woman in a richly appointed room, with a convex mirror on the rear wall and an inscription that proclaims the artist's presence. The sitters are usually identified as members of an Italian merchant family resident in Bruges, but their exact identities and the meaning of the scene are contested. In the twentieth century, Panofsky argued that the painting recorded a marital contract and was filled with "disguised symbolism," so that the dog, the candle, the shoes, and the fruit all carried specific religious meanings. Later scholars have been more cautious, observing that some objects may simply have signaled wealth, domestic order, or fidelity, and that the viewer's interpretation, even in the fifteenth century, may not have been fixed. A responsible reading therefore notes both the density of potential meaning and the impossibility of recovering every intention.

The techniques of these painters deserve careful description. Oil as a painting medium was not invented by Van Eyck. Drying oils had long been used in northern Europe for decorative and protective purposes, and some earlier painters had experimented with them, as Chapter 58 explained. What the Netherlandish masters achieved was a refined method of building layers of finely ground pigment in oil, often over a carefully prepared, smooth, white ground on a wooden panel, using thin translucent glazes to produce depth, luminosity, and subtle gradations. The slow drying properties of oil allowed painters to blend and modify passages and to render the gleam of metal, the softness of fur, the transparency of glass, and the texture of skin with extraordinary subtlety. Vasari's later claim that Jan van Eyck invented oil painting is a legend that historians do not accept, although it shows how widely his achievement was admired.

These paintings had several functions. Altarpieces such as the Ghent polyptych were used in liturgy and displayed to the faithful at feast days. Portraits recorded the likeness of rulers, officials, wives, and merchants, sometimes for dynastic purposes or for marriage negotiations. Small devotional panels enabled private prayer. Their detailed naturalism may have helped viewers imagine sacred events as present, a purpose consistent with the Devotio Moderna emphasis on meditation. At the same time, their cost and refinement signaled the wealth and taste of those who commissioned them, and their export to Italian and Iberian patrons made Netherlandish art an important commodity as well as a spiritual aid.

Section 59.5

Rogier van der Weyden and the Development of Northern Devotional Art

Rogier van der Weyden (c. 1399/1400–1464) is generally identified with the Roger de le Pasture who was recorded in Tournai as an apprentice in Campin's workshop in 1427. He became a master in 1432 and moved to Brussels by 1435, where he was appointed city painter in 1436. In that role he painted for the municipality, but he is best known for religious works that conveyed grief, compassion, and pathos with unusual emotional force. His Descent from the Cross (c. 1435, now in the Prado, Madrid), painted for the crossbowmen's guild of Leuven, crowds the figures into a shallow shrine like space, and aligns the swooning body of the Virgin with the body of Christ, so that the viewer is invited to share a moment of shared sorrow. The polyptych of the Last Judgment (c. 1445–1450) in the hospital of Beaune, commissioned by the Burgundian chancellor Nicolas Rolin, shows how large scale painting served an institution of charity, reminding patients and visitors of divine judgment.

Rogier's reputation extended far beyond the Low Countries. Italian sources record that he traveled to Italy in the Jubilee year of 1450 and that he produced work for or was admired by several courts, including that of the Este at Ferrara. Whatever the details, his influence on Italian, German, and Spanish painters was considerable. His style, with its elegantly drawn figures and restrained gesture, was widely copied, and workshop replicas and followers extended his compositions into the later fifteenth century.

Subsequent Netherlandish painters adapted these emotional and observational resources in different ways. Hugo van der Goes (c. 1440–1482), a master in Ghent who later entered a monastery near Brussels as a lay brother, painted the monumental Portinari Altarpiece (c. 1475–1478) for Tommaso Portinari, a Florentine in the service of the Medici bank in Bruges. Installed in Florence in the 1480s, the triptych astonished local painters with its large scale naturalism and the individual faces of its shepherds, and it left a mark on Florentine art. This is a striking example of reverse influence: a northern work, financed by an Italian banker's profits from northern commerce, shaped Italian painters. Hans Memling (c. 1430–1494), who worked in Bruges and was probably trained in Rogier's circle, produced gentle, balanced devotional works and portraits for local patrons and foreign merchants. A Last Judgment triptych that he painted for a Florentine patron was captured at sea in 1473 by privateers from Danzig and remains in Gdańsk, showing how trade, piracy, and politics could redirect artworks across Europe.

Taken together, these artists show several features of northern devotional art. First, the viewer was often drawn into an intimate relationship with sacred figures, especially in small panels and diptychs for private prayer. Second, suffering and compassion received exceptional attention, consistent with late medieval meditations on the Passion. Third, patrons and donors were incorporated into the images. Fourth, the paintings circulated through trade networks and thus created connections between northern, Italian, and Iberian visual cultures. It is important to avoid the opposite error of treating northern art as merely medieval in outlook. These painters were deeply interested in portrait likeness, spatial coherence, and the study of human emotion, concerns that overlap with the artistic aims of the Italian Renaissance, though pursued by different methods.

Section 59.6

Hieronymus Bosch, Religious Imagination, and Moral Criticism

Hieronymus Bosch (c. 1450–1516) was born Jheronimus van Aken in 's-Hertogenbosch, a prosperous town in the duchy of Brabant, and took his professional name from it. He belonged to a family of painters and was a member of the Brotherhood of Our Lady, a prominent lay confraternity in the town. Documents record his marriage to a woman from a wealthy family, commissions for local religious institutions, and an altarpiece ordered by Philip the Fair in the first years of the sixteenth century. Beyond such facts, much of Bosch's life is obscure. Few of his paintings are signed, and scholars disagree about how many surviving works are autograph. A major research project, completed in 2016, used technical analysis and dendrochronology and revised some attributions while confirming others, but it did not resolve every dispute.

Bosch's subjects are among the most distinctive in European art. His panels depict the temptations of Saint Anthony, the carrying of the cross, the judgment of souls, and fantastic landscapes populated by hybrids, demons, and ordinary people. The Haywain triptych (Prado), the Ship of Fools (a fragment now in the Louvre), and above all the Garden of Earthly Delights (Prado; c. 1490–1510) present scenes in which human desire, folly, and sin are explored through vivid, often unsettling imagery. The Garden is a triptych. Its left wing shows the creation of Eve in a paradise of exotic animals, its central panel displays crowds of figures engaged in sensual play amid giant fruit and birds, and its right wing portrays a nocturnal hell of torment. When the wings are closed they reveal a grisaille depiction of the creation of the world.

Interpretation of such works is difficult. Older twentieth century readings found in Bosch evidence of heretical sects, alchemy, or secret esoteric doctrines. Most modern art historians reject such explanations as unsupported by the documentary evidence and note that Bosch worked within the mainstream of late medieval Christian culture, drawing on sermons, devotional treatises, proverbs, bestiaries, manuscript marginalia, and moral literature. His images of folly and temptation echo contemporary themes in northern literature, including the satirical Ship of Fools of Sebastian Brant (1494), though the connection between Brant's poem and Bosch's panel is a matter of thematic kinship, not proven dependence. The central panel of the Garden has been read as a warning about the transience of pleasure, as a vision of a lost or imagined innocence, or as an allegory of worldly vanity. None of these readings commands universal assent. A cautious approach acknowledges that the work plainly addresses sin, desire, and judgment but that the precise program and the intentions of its patron remain uncertain.

Bosch is historically significant for several reasons. His paintings illustrate how religious and moral anxieties found visual form in a prosperous urban culture. Their popularity among elite collectors, including later Spanish monarchs, reveals a taste for imaginative, intellectually challenging imagery. They also demonstrate that northern art could use fantasy for serious moral purposes. Comparisons with Erasmus's satire (Section 59.13), written within a generation, are tempting, since both explored folly, hypocrisy, and the gap between Christian ideals and daily practice. But the two cultures of criticism were different, one visual and devotional, the other literary and humanist, and there is no evidence that Bosch knew Erasmus's work or shared his program.

Section 59.7

Albrecht Dürer and the German Renaissance

Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528) is the most thoroughly documented northern artist of his generation. Born in Nuremberg, the son of a goldsmith who had immigrated from Hungary, he was trained first in his father's craft and then, from 1486, apprenticed to the painter and woodcut designer Michael Wolgemut, whose workshop produced altarpieces and illustrations for printed books. After his apprenticeship Dürer traveled as a journeyman in the Upper Rhine region, probably visiting Colmar, Basel, and Strasbourg, where he encountered the engravings of Martin Schongauer and the book illustration culture of Basel. He married Agnes Frey in 1494 and then, probably in the same year, traveled to northern Italy, a first exposure to the art of Venice and the Veneto. A second, longer stay in Venice from 1505 to 1507 allowed him to paint, among other works, the Feast of the Rosary for the church used by the German merchant community. His letters from Venice to his friend, the Nuremberg humanist and councilor Willibald Pirckheimer, survive and show a man conscious of the difference between the respect accorded to artists in Italy and their lower standing in his home city.

Dürer's most important contribution may have been the transformation of printmaking into a major art. In 1498 he published The Apocalypse, a series of fifteen large woodcuts illustrating the Book of Revelation, in both German and Latin editions, evidently at his own expense and risk. The images, including the celebrated Four Horsemen, are notable for their dramatic energy, their dense black and white contrasts, and the sophistication of their design. Their publication coincided with widespread anxiety about the approach of the year 1500 and with growing interest in apocalyptic themes, though historians caution against assuming that the work was simply a response to millenarian fears. A decade and a half later, between 1513 and 1514, Dürer produced three engravings often called his master prints: Knight, Death and the Devil, Saint Jerome in His Study, and Melencolia I. These works reveal both technical mastery and a deliberate engagement with intellectual themes, among them virtue, learning, and the melancholy associated with creative thought.

Historical Vocabulary
Woodcut and Engraving

A woodcut is a relief print. The designer draws on a block of wood, and a specialist cutter carves away the surfaces that are not to print, leaving the lines standing. The block is inked and pressed to paper, often in the same forme as type, so that woodcuts could be printed together with text in books. In the fifteenth century many woodcut designs were produced by workshops and cutters whose names are lost; Dürer's innovation was to treat the design as an individual artistic creation and to raise the quality of the cutting.

An engraving is an intaglio print. The engraver incises lines directly into a polished copper plate with a sharp tool called a burin. The plate is inked, wiped so that ink remains only in the incised lines, and pressed under heavy pressure onto damp paper. Engraving grew out of goldsmiths' techniques and allows very fine lines and subtle gradations of tone, but it must be printed separately from type. Both techniques made images reproducible and portable, and both depended on the commerce in paper that had grown alongside printing. Their significance for this chapter is that they allowed artists and publishers to circulate religious, moral, and decorative images across Europe more cheaply than painting and in greater numbers than any manuscript illustration.

Dürer's relationship to Italian art illustrates exchange rather than dependence. He studied Venetian color, the nude figure, and ideas of proportion, and he admired Italian painters such as Giovanni Bellini. He also remained deeply rooted in German craft and northern traditions of linear precision and detailed natural observation, as in his watercolors of landscapes, plants, and animals. His theoretical writings, including a treatise on measurement and geometry (1525) and the posthumously published Four Books on Human Proportion (1528), aimed to give artists a mathematical and rational basis for their work, in some ways paralleling Italian theory but written in German for a workshop audience. His self-portraits, including the frontal image of 1500 now in Munich, assert the dignity of the artist's vocation and the creative power of the painter, and have been read as evidence of a new confidence in individual artistic identity.

Dürer's career also shows how patrons from different social levels shaped German art. Nuremberg merchants and councilors, ecclesiastical patrons, and the Holy Roman Emperor Maximilian I all employed him. Maximilian commissioned monumental print projects, including a triumphal arch and processions, and a decorated prayer book, as part of an effort to project the emperor's reputation through print. In 1520 and 1521 Dürer traveled to the Netherlands, keeping a diary that records meetings with painters, scholars, and officials and his purchases of curiosities, including objects brought from the New World. This diary is one of the most revealing sources for the social life of a northern artist. It also records his strong interest in the early Reformation. Chapter 62 treats the religious controversy itself, and it is enough here to note that Dürer, like many artists and humanists, observed those events with intense concern, and that the religious conflicts of the 1520s complicated the market for religious imagery on which he and others depended.

Section 59.8

Northern Portraiture, German Artistic Traditions, and Cultural Exchange

Portraiture was one of the most characteristic northern achievements. The fifteenth century Netherlandish tradition of the half length, closely observed portrait, with sitters shown against plain or domestic backgrounds, influenced portrait practice across Europe, including Italy. In the early sixteenth century, German and Swiss painters elaborated this tradition for merchants, scholars, princes, and clerics. Hans Holbein the Younger (1497/98–1543), born in Augsburg and active in Basel from about 1515, painted and drew many of the leading humanists of the Rhine region, and designed title pages and illustrations for Basel printers, including Froben. His portrait of Erasmus (1523) has become the standard image of the scholar, showing him writing in a plain, concentrated pose. In 1526 Holbein traveled to England with an introduction from Erasmus and stayed in the household of Thomas More, where he drew and painted members of More's family and circle. He returned to Basel, then went to England again in 1532 and eventually entered the service of Henry VIII. His double portrait The Ambassadors (1533), with its array of scientific and musical instruments and its distorted skull, shows how portraiture could convey learning, diplomacy, and mortality in a single image.

Lucas Cranach the Elder (1472–1553) worked in Vienna in the early 1500s in a circle of humanists, then moved to Wittenberg in 1505 as court painter to Frederick the Wise, Elector of Saxony. His workshop produced portraits of princes and scholars, mythological and religious paintings, and woodcuts at large scale. Cranach later became a friend of Martin Luther and an important producer of Reformation imagery, a role discussed in Part X. Here he illustrates the dependence of German art on the patronage of territorial princes within the fragmented political structure of the Holy Roman Empire. Courts such as those of Saxony, Bavaria, and the Rhine Palatinate, and ecclesiastical princes such as the archbishops of Mainz, supplied commissions that no single national monarchy could have provided.

German art also cannot be reduced to portraits and prints. Matthias Grünewald's Isenheim Altarpiece (c. 1512–1516), made for the hospital preceptory of the Antonite order in Alsace, combines a harrowing Crucifixion with scenes of radiance and resurrection, and was probably meant to console patients suffering from painful diseases. Albrecht Altdorfer of Regensburg developed landscape painting of striking atmosphere in the so called Danube School. Sculptors such as Tilman Riemenschneider of Würzburg and Veit Stoss, who produced a monumental carved altarpiece for St Mary's Church in Kraków, show that carved and painted wood altarpieces were a major medium, and that German artists worked for patrons in Poland and elsewhere in central Europe.

The portrayal of scholars deserves special mention. In 1517 the Antwerp painter Quentin Massys painted a pair of portraits, one of Erasmus and one of his friend Pieter Gillis, the Antwerp humanist and town clerk, as a gift for Thomas More. The pair of images, exchanged between friends across the Channel, are a visible record of the friendships that held together the humanist network. Dürer's drawing of Erasmus (1520) and later engraving (1526) and Holbein's portraits further fixed the scholar's public image. These pictures presented learned men as serious, restrained, and devoted to books, an iconography of scholarly authority that painters elsewhere adopted.

Exchange with Italy continued to run in both directions. German and Netherlandish artists studied Italian composition, anatomy, and classical motifs; Italian painters and printmakers admired and copied northern engravings, which were highly portable. Italian workshops copied Dürer's prints so extensively that he is reported to have complained about the practice. The more important conclusion for historians is that artistic influence was dynamic and multi directional. Italian art did not simply transform northern art, and northern art was not a passive recipient.

Section 59.9

The Spread of Classical Humanism beyond Italy

The humanist movement examined in Chapter 57 crossed the Alps through several channels. Northern students, especially Germans, studied at Italian universities such as Bologna, Padua, Pavia, and Ferrara, and returned home with new tastes and book collections. Italian humanists traveled north as diplomats, papal officials, and teachers. The Council of Basel (1431–1449) and the imperial chancery of Frederick III brought scholars such as Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini, later Pope Pius II, into direct contact with German officials and clerics, and his writings about Germany stimulated German scholars to write about their own history. Italian printers, particularly Aldus Manutius of Venice, whose Aldine Press published Greek and Latin classics in compact, readable formats, supplied northern scholars with texts. Wealthy patrons, such as bishops and princely chancellors, brought humanists into their households. Maximilian I, who prized poets, historians, and artists who could glorify his dynasty, supported humanist circles in Vienna and elsewhere.

Early German humanists often worked as teachers, court officials, and wandering poets. Peter Luder lectured on classical authors at Heidelberg in the 1450s. Rudolf Agricola (1443/44–1485), born near Groningen in Frisia, studied in Italy, where he learned Greek and absorbed Italian rhetorical ideas, and spent part of his later career at Heidelberg under the patronage of Johann von Dalberg, later bishop of Worms. Agricola's treatise on dialectic, De inventione dialectica, written about 1479 and printed in 1515, reoriented logical instruction toward the techniques of argument and persuasion used in rhetoric. Its influence on northern schools and on figures such as Erasmus and Melanchthon was considerable. Alexander Hegius, who led the school at Deventer, became known for encouraging Greek and classical Latin. Conrad Celtis (1459–1508) was crowned poet laureate by Emperor Frederick III in 1487, founded literary sodalities in the Rhineland and at Vienna, and promoted study of the history and geography of Germany, a project that, like the work of other humanists, helped shape later national imaginations but should not be read as proof of a unified German nation in 1500. Alsace, Swabia, and Basel produced humanists such as Jakob Wimpfeling and Sebastian Brant, whose Ship of Fools (1494) was an illustrated satire that circulated widely in Latin and vernacular versions.

The northern adaptation of humanism was marked by several features. First, it was frequently connected to the training of clergy and the reform of existing schools and universities, rather than to chancery or republican politics. Second, Greek was initially rare. Few northern scholars had access to Greek manuscripts, grammars, or teachers until the early sixteenth century, and Hebrew was rarer still. Third, the philological techniques of comparing manuscripts, correcting texts, and studying style were increasingly applied to religious works. Fourth, humanism spread through close friendship networks and correspondence. Fifth, printing allowed texts to reach distant readers and established a market for humanist editions, a theme developed in Section 59.17.

Humanism also developed beyond the German lands and the Low Countries. In Hungary, King Matthias Corvinus (r. 1458–1490) assembled at Buda a famous library, and the bishop and poet Janus Pannonius was an important Latin author. In Kraków, the university and the circle of the Italian exile Filippo Buonaccorsi (Callimachus) supported classical learning. Bohemia produced Latin humanists, including Bohuslav Hasištejnský z Lobkovic, even amid the religious complexities of the Hussite aftermath. These examples show that the diffusion of humanism cannot be mapped only along a western axis from Italy to England and France.

Section 59.10

Christian Humanism, Learned Piety, and Religious Renewal

The term Christian humanism is a modern label for the characteristic northern combination of classical learning and religious purpose. It describes scholars who believed that the study of ancient languages, rhetoric, and history could clarify Scripture, deepen moral teaching, and improve the education of clergy and laity. Christian humanists did not oppose theology as such, and they did not abandon Christianity for pagan antiquity. They argued that Christian truth had been obscured by centuries of misleading commentary, incorrect texts, and quarrelsome disputation and that it could be recovered by careful scholarship.

Historical Vocabulary
Christian Humanism, Ad Fontes, and Philosophia Christi

Christian humanism refers to the use of humanist methods, especially philology, rhetoric, and historical study, in the service of Christian learning and moral renewal. It was never a formal institution or party, and its participants disagreed on many points.

Ad fontes, Latin for "to the sources," expressed the demand to return to original texts: the Greek New Testament and Septuagint, the Hebrew Old Testament, and the early Church Fathers, in place of reliance on medieval commentary and the Latin Vulgate alone. Philosophia Christi, "the philosophy of Christ," was Erasmus's phrase for the teaching of Christ understood as practical moral wisdom meant to shape daily conduct, not mainly as a subject of speculative debate. The modern phrase "Christian humanism" should not be confused with the secular "humanism" of the twentieth century, which has a different meaning, and it should not be read as meaning that Italian humanists were irreligious.

Several ideas gave the movement coherence. First was the principle of returning to the sources. Humanists compared manuscripts of the Bible and the Church Fathers, corrected scribal errors, and sought the original meaning of words in their linguistic and historical context. Lorenzo Valla's earlier critical annotations on the Latin New Testament, which Erasmus later published, showed what such analysis could achieve. Second was an emphasis on language. Because Scripture was composed in Greek and Hebrew, and because Latin translation inevitably shaped interpretation, knowledge of the original languages was seen as essential for theologians, not an optional ornament. Third was moral formation. Humanist pedagogy held that eloquence and virtue were connected and that reading well could educate character. Fourth was an affirmation of an interior, ethical Christianity that stressed love, humility, and peace more than ritual performance, an emphasis that overlapped with the Devotio Moderna. Fifth was a critical stance toward institutions. Christian humanists attacked abuses such as clerical ignorance, trivial disputation, the commercialization of relics and indulgences, and warfare among Christian rulers.

These concerns must be understood in context. Christian humanists did not usually reject sacraments, the priesthood, or the authority of the church. They differed on how far criticism should go, on whether Scripture should be freely available in the vernacular, and on the proper relationship between reason and grace. Some, like Erasmus, preferred gentle satire and appeals to enlightened rulers. Others, like Lefèvre d'Étaples, worked within circles of reforming bishops. Still others, such as Reuchlin, became entangled in institutional conflicts. Their ideas could be turned to different purposes after 1517, and this variability is central to the story of Section 59.18.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Medieval Devotion to Renaissance Christian Humanism

What Changed

  • Philology became a central method: scholars compared Greek and Hebrew manuscripts and corrected Latin texts rather than relying on inherited commentary alone
  • Printing multiplied copies of texts, enabling scholars to circulate editions and engage in public debate with distant colleagues
  • Rhetoric and classical literature became part of religious education, and eloquence gained new prestige as a way of promoting piety
  • Learned laypeople, such as More and Budé, increasingly participated in discussions once reserved to the clergy

What Stayed the Same

  • The ultimate aim remained the Christian life, and the Bible and the Church Fathers remained the authorities
  • The emphasis on inward devotion, humility, imitation of Christ, and moral self examination continued the Devotio Moderna and earlier monastic traditions
  • Reform was sought through existing institutions, including the church, universities, and princely courts
  • Criticism of clerical abuse and superstition drew on long established themes of complaint

Who experienced change most directly? University teachers, printers, court scholars, and patrons of learned colleges experienced the sharpest changes, since their work and careers depended on new methods and technologies. Who experienced greater continuity? Most parish clergy, peasants, and many townspeople continued to live within a ritual and devotional culture that humanist programs only gradually touched.

Why the pattern matters: The relationship between medieval devotion and Christian humanism was one of adaptation and partial overlap, not simple replacement. Recognizing this prevents both the claim that humanism was a sudden rupture with medieval religion and the opposite claim that it added nothing new.

Section 59.11

Erasmus of Rotterdam: Scholar, Traveler, Humanist

Desiderius Erasmus was born in Rotterdam or nearby on 27 or 28 October of a year that sources and scholars place variously between 1466 and 1469. He was the illegitimate son of a priest, a status that created legal and social difficulties and that he later handled discreetly. Orphaned as a teenager, he was educated at schools in Gouda, Deventer, and 's-Hertogenbosch, in an environment shaped by the Devotio Moderna and the early humanism of teachers such as Hegius. About 1487 he entered the Augustinian canons' house at Steyn, near Gouda, and was ordained a priest in 1492. Dissatisfied with monastic life, he obtained permission to serve the bishop of Cambrai, Henry of Bergen, and gradually established himself as an independent scholar. He studied theology at Paris about 1495, though he disliked the severity of the Collège de Montaigu and the scholastic curriculum.

His first visit to England in 1499 proved decisive. He met Thomas More, John Colet, William Grocyn, and Thomas Linacre, and he received encouragement from the noble Lord Mountjoy, who had been his pupil in Paris. Colet's lectures on the Pauline Epistles at Oxford and his insistence on reading Scripture in its historical and literary sense impressed Erasmus and encouraged him to turn his philological skills to biblical studies. Back on the continent he undertook the serious study of Greek, mostly on his own. In 1500 he published his first Adages, a collection of classical proverbs with commentary, which grew in successive editions. The expanded 1508 edition, printed at Venice by Aldus Manutius, contained several thousand proverbs and became a best seller. It popularized classical wisdom for readers beyond the learned elite and used some entries to criticize war, tyranny, and clerical vice. In 1503 he published the Handbook of the Christian Soldier (Enchiridion militis christiani), a guide to lay piety that emphasized prayer, Scripture, and moral struggle over ceremonial observance.

Between 1506 and 1509 Erasmus traveled to Italy, receiving a doctorate in theology at Turin, working with Aldus in Venice, and visiting Bologna and Rome. He then returned to England, where he wrote The Praise of Folly in More's house, and held a position teaching Greek and divinity at Cambridge between about 1511 and 1514. He was supported by patrons, among them William Warham, Archbishop of Canterbury, and Lord Mountjoy, and from 1516 by a salary as an honorary councilor to the young Charles of Burgundy, later Emperor Charles V. In his later career he lived chiefly at Basel, near the press of Johann Froben, with intervals in Leuven (1517 to 1521), Freiburg in Breisgau (1529 to 1535), and Basel again until his death on 12 July 1536.

Erasmus's career is important not only for what he wrote but for how he lived. He was among the first European scholars to support himself largely through writing, patronage, and publishing arrangements rather than by holding a permanent university or church post. He cultivated a vast circle of correspondents among scholars, bishops, princes, and printers, and he edited and published his own letters, deliberately shaping his public image. Through that network he helped create a European intellectual community whose members communicated in Latin across political and confessional boundaries, a precursor of the Republic of Letters examined in Chapter 82. Yet it is worth remembering that this community was elite, male, and clerical or learned, and that Erasmus often depended on the generosity of patrons and the commercial calculations of printers. His independence was relative, and he was vulnerable to criticism from theologians who regarded him as an amateur meddling in their field.

Historians also caution against reducing Erasmus to a heroic figure of tolerance and reason. He was a polemicist capable of sharp personal attacks, a man of some caution who avoided rupture with church authorities, and a scholar with strong convictions about the primacy of peace, moral reform, and education. His public persona was carefully constructed, and his letters, often composed with an eye to publication, must be read critically. These qualities make him a more interesting subject than the legend that has grown around him.

Section 59.12

Erasmus, Biblical Philology, and the Greek New Testament

Erasmus's most consequential scholarly project was his edition of the New Testament. Published by Froben at Basel in March 1516 under the title Novum Instrumentum, it printed the Greek text of the New Testament alongside a fresh Latin translation made by Erasmus, followed by annotations explaining his choices and discussing textual problems. It was dedicated to Pope Leo X and opened with prefatory essays, including the Paraclesis, an exhortation to the reading of Scripture (see the Primary Source Window below). Erasmus also produced an edition of the works of Saint Jerome, published in 1516, that supplied a model for editing the Church Fathers.

The edition rested on a limited manuscript base. Erasmus consulted a small number of Greek manuscripts available at Basel, most of them medieval Byzantine texts of relatively late date. For some passages, notably the last verses of the Book of Revelation, where his Greek manuscript was defective, he translated from the Latin Vulgate back into Greek. The haste with which the edition was produced, and the pressure of competition from other projects, contributed to numerous printing errors that Erasmus corrected in later editions. He revised the text in 1519, 1522, 1527, and 1535, and the second edition (1519) became the basis for Martin Luther's later German translation. A famous controversy surrounded the so called Johannine Comma, a Trinitarian verse in the Latin tradition that appears in few Greek manuscripts and was omitted from Erasmus's first two editions. Under pressure from critics, he included it in the third edition (1522), though he expressed doubts about its authenticity. The episode shows both the potential and the difficulty of humanist textual criticism: a scholar might follow the evidence of manuscripts, but institutions, theologians, and readers could press him to accommodate traditional readings.

The chronology of the first Greek New Testaments needs precision. The Complutensian Polyglot Bible, undertaken by Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros at the University of Alcalá de Henares, printed the Greek New Testament as part of its fifth volume in 1514. The complete Polyglot, in six volumes presenting Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and Aramaic texts in parallel, was finished in 1517, but it received papal authorization only in 1520 and circulated several years afterward. Erasmus's 1516 edition therefore reached readers first and is correctly described as the first published Greek New Testament, though not the first Greek New Testament to be printed. Whether his edition was rushed deliberately to beat the Complutensian project is a matter on which the surviving evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. What is clear is that both projects reflected the same scholarly impulse to establish the biblical text on a sounder philological basis.

Erasmus's Latin translation provoked sharp criticism because it altered familiar wording. In the Gospel of John, for example, he rendered the Greek logos as sermo ("speech" or "discourse") in the 1519 edition instead of the Vulgate's verbum ("word"), and his annotations often argued that the traditional Latin text contained errors. Opponents at Leuven, Paris, and Cambridge, and later at Rome and in Spain, accused him of undermining the authority of the Vulgate and of encouraging laypeople to dispute theological matters. Erasmus replied that he did not intend to replace the Vulgate, which he regarded as a respectable and widely used translation, but to illuminate the meaning of the original. He was not producing a "Protestant Bible." His edition, dedicated to a pope, was a Catholic scholarly project, and it was later used by Catholics and Protestants alike. The Council of Trent, discussed in Chapter 65, would eventually affirm the authority of the Vulgate for church use, showing how contested questions of biblical text and authority became.

It was addressed to a broad Latin reading audience of clergy, teachers, and educated laypeople, and it served a rhetorical purpose: to defend a controversial publication by presenting it as a service to Christian piety. It should be read together with the work's other prefaces and with the annotations.

Paraphrase of Erasmus, Paraclesis Erasmus urges his readers to take up what he calls the philosophy of Christ, which he describes as a teaching suited to ordinary people and not reserved to a learned elite. He criticizes the idea that only trained theologians should read Scripture and says he wishes that the Gospels and the letters of Paul could be read by all, including women, and be translated into the languages of all peoples. He imagines the farmer, the weaver, and the traveler drawing comfort and moral guidance from Scripture in the course of daily work. He presents Christ's teaching as the surest source of wisdom and contrasts its simplicity with elaborate scholastic argument. This is a summary of the general argument and not a translation of any passage.

Analysis: The Paraclesis is a concise statement of the educational and religious aims of Christian humanism. It expresses confidence that Scripture is accessible and morally transforming, links language study to religious life, and criticizes scholastic complexity. It also reveals tensions. Erasmus's wish for broad access did not mean that he supported an unregulated vernacular culture of interpretation; his own scholarship was in Latin and Greek, and he later grew uneasy as popular religious controversy expanded. The essay is an advertisement as well as a reflection, designed to win support from patrons and readers and to answer anticipated criticism. It also reflects the viewpoint of a male cleric and scholar addressing a Latin readership, so it tells us about the hopes of an educated minority more than about the religious experience of peasants, urban workers, or women, who are invoked as potential readers but are not themselves speaking.

Section 59.13

Satire, Education, and Religious Criticism in Erasmus's Writings

While the New Testament edition displayed Erasmus's scholarship, his satirical and educational writings reached the widest audience. The Praise of Folly (Moriae encomium), written in 1509 during a stay in More's house and first published in Paris in 1511, is a mock oration delivered by Folly herself, who praises her own power in human affairs. The Greek word moria also puns on More's name, and the work is dedicated to him. Folly mocks a long list of social types, among them scholars, grammarians, lawyers, merchants, princes, courtiers, and philosophers. She reserves some of her sharpest wit for theologians who pursue subtle disputes, monks whose observances replaced charity, and popes and bishops who wage wars and accumulate wealth. In the final part, the speech turns toward a paradoxical celebration of Christian "folly," drawing on Saint Paul's language about the foolishness of the cross. The text is deliberately ambiguous, and readers have long debated how far Erasmus meant Folly's criticisms as direct attack, how far as playful exaggeration, and how far as a serious statement of Christian humility.

Erasmus's Handbook of the Christian Soldier (1503) offers a more direct statement. It argued that Christians should rely on Scripture, prayer, and conscience and not mistake outward ritual for inner holiness. Its popularity increased after a new edition in 1518. Other writings addressed education and government. On the Method of Study and On Abundance of Words and Ideas, composed for the new school at St Paul's, set out a program of grammar, rhetoric, and reading of classical and Christian authors. The Education of a Christian Prince (1516), dedicated to the future Charles V, argued that rulers should be trained in virtue and the public good rather than glory in war. The Complaint of Peace (1517) and the adage "War is sweet to those who have not tried it" condemned conflict among Christians. His Colloquies, first published in 1518 and greatly expanded, were short dialogues originally designed to teach Latin conversation, which evolved into lively satires on pilgrimage, relics, marriage, monastic life, and ignorance. In one dialogue a learned woman politely rebukes an uneducated abbot, a sign of Erasmus's willingness to imagine women as readers and thinkers, even though his views on women's roles were far from modern.

Erasmus's criticisms targeted specific practices: the pursuit of indulgences in a mechanical spirit, trade in relics, pilgrimages undertaken without moral improvement, wealth and ostentation among bishops, monks who neglected study, and scholastic quarrels that he thought distracted from Christ's teaching. But he almost never attacked the sacraments, priesthood, or papacy as institutions, and he often protested that he wished to correct abuses, not overturn doctrine. His strategy was persuasion, not confrontation, and he relied on friendships with powerful patrons. This stance exposed him to attack from both sides. Conservative theologians at Leuven, Paris, and elsewhere accused him of fostering irreverence and undermining ecclesiastical authority. In 1515 the Leuven humanist Maarten van Dorp criticized The Praise of Folly and the plan to edit the New Testament, and Erasmus wrote a long reply defending satire and philology. Later the Paris faculty of theology censured propositions drawn from his works. Meanwhile, reformers who wanted more radical change found him evasive and cautious.

Multiple Perspectives

Contested Learning: Reactions to Humanist Scholarship and Satire, c. 1510–1520

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives that synthesize positions found in the surviving record. They are not quotations from any single named person. These groups did not possess equal power, and the aim is complexity, not equivalence.

From the perspective of Erasmus and like-minded humanists: Scholarship properly practiced would restore the original meaning of Scripture, expose superstition, and promote peace. Satire was a legitimate means of moral correction, and the study of languages was a service to the church and not a threat. Humanists depended on patrons and presses and sought to avoid open rupture with authority.
From the perspective of traditional university theologians: Theology was a demanding discipline, built on centuries of commentary, and altering the accepted Latin text of Scripture could unsettle doctrine and liturgical practice. Philologists, in this view, lacked training in the discipline and treated sacred matters with levity. Concerns about the authority of the Vulgate and the stability of teaching were not mere obstruction but reflected institutional responsibility for orthodoxy, even if some critics were also motivated by professional rivalry.
From the perspective of printers such as Johann Froben: Humanist editions were an expanding commercial opportunity but also a risky investment requiring capital, proofreaders, type, paper, and security from copying. A successful scholar like Erasmus could attract buyers across Europe, and controversy could stimulate demand. Printers had interests in scholarly quality, speed, and profit, which did not always coincide.
From the perspective of reform minded scholars such as Colet and Lefèvre d'Étaples: The renewal of the church depended on better preaching, improved clerical education, and direct engagement with Scripture and the Fathers. They tended to emphasize piety and pastoral reform, sometimes with less interest in satire than Erasmus, and they worked within the institutions of cathedral, college, and episcopal household.
From the perspective of lay readers in towns: Educated merchants, lawyers, officials, and some women found in humanist books, especially vernacular translations and school dialogues, material for self improvement and moral reflection. But access depended on literacy, money, and, for Latin works, years of schooling, and most townspeople and nearly all rural people remained outside this world.
Section 59.14

Hebrew Scholarship, Reuchlin, and the Limits of Humanist Toleration

The slogan "to the sources" implied the study of Hebrew as well as Greek, since the Old Testament was composed largely in Hebrew. Yet in the late fifteenth century very few Christians in northern Europe could read it. Jews, whose communities had lived in German towns, in the Low Countries, in Italy, and (until 1492) in the Iberian kingdoms, possessed deep traditions of scholarship, biblical interpretation, grammar, and law, sustained by their own schools, synagogues, and legal institutions. Christian interest in Hebrew therefore required contact with Jewish learning, a contact that took place against a background of hostility, restriction, and episodic violence. Jews were barred from many occupations and from universities, faced regulations and taxes, and were vulnerable to accusations of ritual murder and host desecration, charges that Chapter 49 introduced. Expulsions continued: Spain expelled its Jews in 1492, Portugal compelled conversion in 1496 and 1497, and imperial cities in the German lands, including Regensburg in 1519, drove out communities that had existed for centuries.

Johannes Reuchlin (1455–1522) was a jurist, diplomat, and scholar from Pforzheim in Swabia who served the court of Württemberg. He learned Greek and Latin, and he studied Hebrew under Jewish teachers, among them Jacob ben Jehiel Loans, physician to Emperor Frederick III, and, in Rome, Obadiah Sforno. In 1506 he published a Hebrew grammar and dictionary, a pioneering aid for Christian students. He also wrote on the Kabbalah, in De verbo mirifico (1494) and De arte cabalistica (1517), working in a tradition of Christian interest in Jewish mysticism that had earlier been explored by Giovanni Pico della Mirandola. Reuchlin thus represents an effort to bring Hebrew learning into the humanist program.

His position collided with a campaign against Jewish books. Johannes Pfefferkorn, a convert from Judaism who wrote polemical tracts, argued that Jewish books, especially the Talmud, were hostile to Christianity and should be confiscated or destroyed. He was supported by Dominicans of Cologne, including the inquisitor Jacob van Hoogstraten. In 1509 Emperor Maximilian authorized the confiscation of Jewish books, and in 1510 Reuchlin was asked for an expert opinion. He advised that most Jewish writings should be preserved, since they included works of law, medicine, grammar, and commentary, and that only openly blasphemous texts might be restricted. He also argued that Jews lived under the protection of imperial law. When Pfefferkorn attacked him in print, Reuchlin responded with Augenspiegel (1511), and the dispute expanded into a prolonged legal and literary conflict involving the theological faculties of Cologne, Leuven, Paris, and other universities, the bishop of Speyer's commission, and the papal court in Rome. A commission in 1514 acquitted Reuchlin, but the proceedings in Rome dragged on, and in 1520 a papal decision condemned the Augenspiegel and imposed silence on its author, a verdict that coincided with the first open rupture over Luther.

The affair also produced a famous satire. The Letters of Obscure Men (Epistolae obscurorum virorum), published anonymously in 1515 and 1517 by humanists associated with the circle of Ulrich von Hutten and Crotus Rubianus, imitated the barbarous Latin of fictional Cologne theologians in order to ridicule their ignorance and arrogance. The book was widely read and helped to define a humanist identity in opposition to scholastic theologians, though its comic cruelty also illustrates the mixture of motives involved.

Historians therefore emphasize several points. First, Reuchlin's defense of Jewish books did not amount to modern religious toleration. He wished to see Jews converted, he shared many conventional prejudices, and his arguments emphasized legal protection and scholarly value more than equality of religion. Second, the controversy illustrates that humanists were divided on the Jewish question. Many, including Erasmus, supported Reuchlin on grounds of academic freedom and opposition to the Dominicans, while Erasmus also, at times, used hostile stereotypes about Jews in his own writings. Third, Jewish scholars were not simply suppliers of Hebrew texts to Christian students. They possessed their own intellectual traditions, communal institutions, and purposes. Elijah Levita (1469–1549), a Hebrew grammarian who taught Christian students in Italy, including the cardinal Egidio da Viterbo, wrote for Jewish and Christian audiences. The Venetian printer Daniel Bomberg, a Christian from Antwerp, published the Talmud with papal permission from 1520 and produced a rabbinic Bible with the help of Jewish and convert editors. Later leaders such as Josel of Rosheim, who represented Jewish communities before imperial authorities, defended their rights in the Empire. A fair account must therefore emphasize both the genuine opening of Christian scholarship to Hebrew and the persistence of prejudice, as well as the agency of Jewish communities and scholars who negotiated, taught, defended, and sometimes suffered within this system.

Section 59.15

English Humanism: John Colet, Thomas More, and Utopia

England produced a distinctive and influential form of humanism at the turn of the sixteenth century. The universities of Oxford and Cambridge, the Inns of Court, the cathedral of Saint Paul's in London, and the household of the king and of great nobles formed a network of institutions in which new learning spread. Early English contact with Italian humanism occurred through scholars such as William Grocyn and Thomas Linacre, who studied Greek in Italy and taught in Oxford and London. John Colet (c. 1467–1519), the son of a wealthy London merchant, lectured at Oxford on the letters of Paul in the 1490s, emphasizing historical and moral readings of the text over scholastic commentary. Appointed Dean of Saint Paul's Cathedral in 1505, he used his inherited wealth to found Saint Paul's School in 1509, with the grammarian William Lily as first master and with Erasmus's educational writings providing guidance. The school taught Latin and Greek literature to boys of various backgrounds and became a model for the humanist grammar school, though its design should be seen alongside, not apart from, older grammar schools.

The royal succession of 1509, when Henry VIII became king, created a court receptive to scholars and writers. Bishops and noble patrons, such as Richard Fox, who founded Corpus Christi College at Oxford in 1517 with provisions for Greek and Latin lecturers, and Lady Margaret Beaufort and John Fisher at Cambridge, supported new colleges. Henry VIII himself, educated in the humanist style, wrote and patronized learned works. The environment was promising but also dependent on royal favor and subject to rapid change, as the careers of Wolsey, More, and Fisher would show.

Thomas More (1478–1535) was educated in London, at Oxford, and in law at Lincoln's Inn. A lawyer, then undersheriff of London and royal councilor, he moved easily between the world of the common law and the world of humanist letters. His friendship with Erasmus, begun in 1499, was close and lasting. In 1515 he traveled to Flanders on a diplomatic mission concerning English trade and began to write the work that became Utopia, completed in London and published in Latin at Leuven in 1516, with prefatory letters from Erasmus, Pieter Gillis, and others. Its full title advertised both usefulness and amusement.

Utopia is framed as a conversation between More, Gillis, and a fictional traveler, Raphael Hythloday, whose surname evokes Greek words for "dispenser of nonsense." The first book discusses problems in contemporary England: the harsh punishment of theft, the enclosure of arable land for sheep farming, which, as Hythloday notes, makes sheep seem to devour men, and the dilemma facing a philosopher who is asked to counsel a king. The second book describes the island of Utopia, a Greek pun meaning "no place" (and possibly "good place"), where there is no private property, work hours are limited, towns are planned, and citizens share meals. Gold and silver are despised, priests may marry, and a wide degree of religious diversity is tolerated, though atheists are excluded from office. At the same time, the Utopian system contains features that modern readers find troubling: bond slavery for criminals and war captives, strict regulation of travel and private life, the permissibility of euthanasia under some circumstances, and the willingness to wage war for economic reasons.

How should historians read this book? One reading takes Utopia as a serious blueprint for communal society. Another sees it as a satire, a critique of European greed, vanity, and war, using an imaginary society as a mirror. A third emphasizes irony and ambiguity, noting that More, a devout Christian and later a Lord Chancellor of England who defended church authority and was executed in 1535 (see Chapter 64), would have been unlikely to endorse every Utopian practice, and that the narrator More distances himself from Hythloday's conclusions at the end. Most scholars today avoid identifying the book with More's personal political program and instead stress its character as a humanist exercise in imaginative critique. It belongs to the literary tradition of Greek dialogue, especially Plato and Lucian, as translated and promoted by humanists, and it uses fiction to ask questions about property, justice, and the responsibilities of counsel.

English humanism also had a notable female dimension. More educated his daughters alongside his son in a household school, and his eldest daughter Margaret Roper became known for her Latin learning, producing an English translation of an Erasmian treatise on the Lord's Prayer, published in 1524. Queen Catherine of Aragon, herself educated by Spanish humanists, supported the education of women, including by commissioning the Valencian scholar Juan Luis Vives to write a treatise on the education of Christian women (1523). These examples show real opportunities for elite women. They should not be read as typical of English society, in which most girls received little or no formal Latin instruction and women remained excluded from universities and from most public offices.

Section 59.16

Humanism in France, Iberia, and Other European Centers

In France, humanism developed in the university and publishing city of Paris and among the royal officials and legal professionals who staffed the monarchy. Guillaume Budé (1467–1540), a lawyer, royal secretary, and scholar, became the leading French Hellenist. His Annotations on the Pandects and his treatise De Asse (1515), a study of ancient coinage and measures, applied philological methods to Roman law and to economic history, and his Commentaries on the Greek Language (1529) was a major lexicographical work. Budé helped persuade Francis I to appoint salaried royal lecturers in Greek, Hebrew, and mathematics in 1530, an institution that developed into the Collège de France. Parisian printers such as Josse Bade and, later, the Estienne family issued editions of classical, patristic, and legal texts. At the same time, the Paris faculty of theology remained a powerful and conservative authority, and its willingness to censure humanist works shaped the limits of reform.

Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples (c. 1455–1536) combined humanist philology with religious reform. He edited and commented on Aristotle in the 1490s and turned to Scripture in the following decade, publishing the Quincuplex Psalterium (1509), which presented the Psalms in several Latin versions, and a commentary on the Pauline Epistles (1512), which emphasized the primacy of Scripture and the gift of grace. He later served in the reforming circle gathered by Guillaume Briçonnet, bishop of Meaux, and published a French New Testament in 1523. He was the sort of scholar, like Erasmus, whom conservative theologians suspected, and who in turn suffered periods of threat and exile. Among his supporters was Marguerite de Navarre (1492–1549), sister of Francis I, a patron of reform-minded writers, and herself an author of poetry and devotional works, including the Mirror of the Sinful Soul (1531) and the collection of tales known as the Heptameron, published after her death. Her circle is a case in which a woman of the highest rank sustained an intellectual and religious network. It was a distinctive French counterpart to the humanist communities of England and the Low Countries, and its members held a range of views on Church reform that should not be assimilated to Protestantism.

The Iberian Peninsula contributed some of the most substantial philological projects of the period. Antonio de Nebrija (1444–1522), educated at Salamanca and Bologna, published a Latin grammar that reformed teaching, and in 1492 produced the Gramática de la lengua castellana, the first grammar of a modern European vernacular language, a work that combined humanist methods with the Castilian crown's interest in language and administration. He later worked on biblical scholarship and clashed with inquisitorial authorities. Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros (1436–1517), Franciscan, archbishop of Toledo, and for a time regent of Castile, founded the University of Alcalá de Henares, which opened in 1508 and offered instruction in theology, arts, and languages. Under his patronage a team of scholars produced the Complutensian Polyglot Bible, printed by Arnao Guillén de Brocar at Alcalá. Its editors included Hellenists and Hebraists, among them several conversos, Jewish converts to Christianity or their descendants, whose linguistic skills were indispensable, even though the Spanish monarchy had expelled unconverted Jews in 1492 and conversos faced persistent suspicion. The Polyglot demonstrates that Iberian humanism was not an imitation of northern models but a parallel development, influenced by Italian scholarship, by the crown's reforming program, and by the peninsula's multilingual inheritance of Latin, Hebrew, Arabic, and Romance languages.

Juan Luis Vives (1493–1540), born in Valencia to a converso family, studied at Paris, taught at Leuven and Oxford, and wrote on education, psychology, poor relief, and the training of women. His treatise on assisting the poor, composed for the magistrates of Bruges (1526), proposed civic organization of relief and has become a significant text in the history of social policy. Erasmus's works were widely read in Spain in the 1520s, with the Enchiridion appearing in Spanish in 1526, and Erasmians such as Alfonso de Valdés served at the court of Charles V. A theological conference at Valladolid in 1527 examined propositions from Erasmus's works without condemning them, but critics, including members of the mendicant orders and inquisitorial officials, grew increasingly suspicious, and the climate for Erasmian humanism in Spain became more difficult in the decades that followed.

Other regions contributed to the picture. In the Holy Roman Empire, Basel and Strasbourg were centers of humanist teaching and printing, and learned circles formed around figures such as Beatus Rhenanus, the scholar and editor who worked with Froben. In Poland-Lithuania, the University of Kraków and the courts of the Jagiellonian dynasty fostered humanist studies and wide cultural contacts, while in Hungary the court of Matthias Corvinus left a distinctive legacy of humanist patronage. Humanism in Scandinavia, Portugal, and the Baltic lands developed more slowly or on a smaller scale but nonetheless belonged to the same European conversation. These regional variations show why the label Northern Renaissance, though useful, cannot capture the full range of developments, and why no single region can be treated as the norm.

Section 59.17

Printing, Schools, Readers, and the Circulation of Northern Humanism

Without printing, Christian humanism could not have reached the audience it did. Chapter 55 introduced Gutenberg's innovation and the early spread of presses; the question here is how printers and scholars worked together to circulate northern humanism. Presses were commercial enterprises that required capital for type, paper, presses, and skilled labor, and they flourished in cities with merchant networks, universities, and church institutions that created markets. By 1500, catalogues of fifteenth century printing record on the order of thirty thousand editions, produced in hundreds of towns. Estimates of total copies run into the millions, perhaps as high as ten to twenty million, though the figures are uncertain and depend on assumptions about print runs, which were often a few hundred copies per edition.

Certain printers occupied central positions in the humanist world. Aldus Manutius established the Aldine Press in Venice in the mid 1490s, publishing editions of Greek authors, including Aristotle, and compact Latin and Italian texts in octavo format with an elegant italic type, making classical works portable and relatively affordable. Erasmus's expanded Adages was printed there in 1508. Johann Froben of Basel became the leading printer of Erasmus's works, issuing his New Testament, his edition of Jerome, and many other titles, and employing scholars such as Beatus Rhenanus as editors and correctors. Holbein designed title borders and illustrations for his books. In the Low Countries, Dirk Martens, who worked at Aalst, Antwerp, and Leuven, printed Utopia in 1516 and other humanist works. In Paris, Josse Bade and Henri Estienne combined scholarly publishing with a trade in school texts. Printers acted as editors, entrepreneurs, and intermediaries, and a scholar's relationship with a printer could determine whether a work was produced quickly, accurately, and cheaply. Printed books also circulated through fairs, particularly the Frankfurt fair, and through networks of booksellers and agents who carried texts across political frontiers.

Printed images multiplied this circulation. Dürer's prints, broadsheets, and illustrated books reached purchasers who might never see an altarpiece. Woodcut illustrations in books, such as those in the Ship of Fools, made texts accessible to people with limited reading skill, while portraits and emblems conveyed the reputations of scholars and princes. Maximilian I used print as a tool of dynastic self presentation, and humanists used title pages, dedications, and prefaces to identify patrons and audiences.

Schools and colleges were the institutions that created readers. Humanist educators advocated teaching Latin through classical and Christian authors, replacing medieval textbooks with improved grammars and encouraging the study of Greek. Erasmus's Colloquies and On Abundance were used in schools, and colleges such as Corpus Christi at Oxford, Saint John's at Cambridge, the Collegium Trilingue at Leuven (founded in 1517 with a bequest from Jerome de Busleyden to teach Latin, Greek, and Hebrew), and the University of Alcalá created institutional homes for the new learning. Erasmus and others criticized the harsh beating of pupils and urged teachers to attract students by encouragement, though corporal punishment remained widespread. Education was overwhelmingly a male, urban, and elite activity. Many boys left school early to enter apprenticeship or work, and most girls, other than those in wealthy households or convents, received little formal schooling.

Estimates of literacy are uncertain. For the German lands around 1500, historians often suggest that perhaps five to ten percent of the population could read, with much higher rates among urban men, clergy, and officials, and much lower rates in the countryside. Such figures vary widely by region and definition, since some people could read but not write, or read vernacular but not Latin. Many more people encountered printed material through being read to, through images, or through sermons that drew on books. Women's participation was limited but not absent. Elite women, such as Margaret of Austria, Marguerite de Navarre, Margaret Roper, and the learned abbess Caritas Pirckheimer of Nuremberg, who corresponded with humanists, read widely and sometimes wrote. Widows often continued printing businesses after their husbands' deaths. Women religious in convents copied and read devotional books. Nevertheless, universities and professional humanist careers remained closed to women, and humanist rhetoric about education often assumed male pupils.

Everyday Life and Social History

Inside a Printing Workshop and a Humanist Schoolroom

A sixteenth century printing shop was a crowded and noisy place of manual skill. Compositors selected individual pieces of metal type from cases and arranged them in a composing stick, line by line, building pages that were locked into frames. Pressmen inked the type with leather balls and pulled the bar of the wooden press, while others hung the damp printed sheets to dry. Correctors read proofs, and scholars such as Beatus Rhenanus sometimes worked in the shop alongside the printers, correcting texts. Apprentices learned the trade over several years. Paper, imported or produced by local mills, was a major expense. The proprietor needed credit, reliable suppliers, and favorable contacts at fairs, and a failed edition could ruin a business. Workers were sometimes in conflict with masters over pay and hours, and in a few cities labor disputes among printers are well documented.

A humanist schoolroom looked different from a university lecture hall. A boy of about eight to fourteen sat among classmates on benches, under a master who taught Latin grammar, read aloud passages from authors such as Terence, Cicero, or Virgil, and corrected written exercises. Pupils memorized, recited, and imitated models of style. The new curriculum placed more emphasis on literary reading and composition and less on the dense medieval grammars. Discipline remained strict, and parents' expectations usually concerned careers in the church, law, trade, or administration. For most boys of artisan or peasant background, such schooling was out of reach, and for girls it was available only in unusual circumstances. These small scale settings, not great debates, are where the humanist program actually became part of daily life.

Cause and Effect

Urban Wealth, Scholarship, Publishing, and Northern Cultural Change

Long-term causes

Commercial urbanization in the Low Countries and the German lands, a dense network of universities and cathedral schools, lay and clerical devotional movements, and a long tradition of criticism of church abuses.

Short-term causes

Burgundian and Habsburg court patronage, the arrival of printing, the circulation of Italian humanist texts, expanded Greek and Hebrew teaching, and the availability of merchant and princely capital for books and art.

Event or transformation

From the 1420s to the 1520s a distinctive set of artistic and intellectual changes occurred: Netherlandish oil painting, German printmaking, Christian humanist scholarship, biblical philology, and the founding of new colleges and schools.

Immediate effects

Wider circulation of images and learned texts, new educational institutions, increased scrutiny of the Vulgate and the church's practices, and intense controversy among theologians, humanists, and rulers.

Long-term consequences

Professionalization of philology and textual criticism, expansion of schooling and publishing, the cultural resources on which both Protestant and Catholic reformers drew, and a lasting European learned community, though one increasingly divided by confession.

Historical complexity

Wealth and printing did not by themselves produce Christian humanism. Specific patrons, individual scholars, institutional conflicts, and contingent events mattered, and similar commercial conditions elsewhere did not produce identical results.

Why this cannot be reduced to one cause: Technology, wealth, religious tradition, political fragmentation, and the choices of individual scholars and patrons all interacted. An explanation that stresses only the printing press, or only urban capitalism, or only the genius of Erasmus, omits crucial dimensions of the process.

Section 59.18

Northern Humanism at the Threshold of the Reformation

By 1516 northern humanism had achieved a remarkable range of results. Scholars had produced critical editions of Scripture and the Fathers, schools had begun to adopt classical and Christian literature, a Europe wide network of correspondence and publishing connected Basel, Leuven, Paris, London, Alcalá, and many smaller towns, and writers had developed satirical and imaginative forms to criticize contemporary society. Artists had produced paintings and prints that traveled across the continent and attained high status. Optimism ran high among the humanists. Erasmus wrote in letters of that period of hope for a Christian renewal under enlightened rulers and learned bishops, and More's circle shared a similar confidence, though not without anxiety about opposition.

The limitations were equally apparent. Humanism remained a minority culture of Latin readers. Its programs depended on patrons who might withdraw support, and its reform proposals rested on persuasion in an environment of entrenched institutional interests. Its attitudes toward Jews, women, the laity, and non-Europeans reflected the assumptions of a clerical and male learned elite. Its relationship with scholastic theology was a quarrel within the church as well as a struggle for professional position.

From 1517, a new controversy began in Wittenberg when Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar and professor of theology, challenged the theology and practice of indulgences. Chapter 62 explains how that dispute developed into a rupture. For this chapter, the central point is the position of Christian humanists as the crisis unfolded. Some were immediately attracted by Luther's criticisms of abuses, and a number of the younger generation, among them Philipp Melanchthon, a Greek scholar who became Luther's colleague at Wittenberg, and the Basel scholar Johannes Oecolampadius, joined the evangelical movement. Others, including More and Bishop John Fisher, defended the Catholic church and wrote against Luther. Erasmus, who had published the New Testament that Luther and others used, was pressed from both sides to declare himself. He declined to join Luther, argued for moderation, and in 1524 published a treatise on free will, to which Luther replied in 1525, a dispute whose theological content belongs to Chapter 62. Cisneros and the Spanish reformers, who died or retired before the Reformation, never faced that choice, and their followers developed within a Catholic framework. Lefèvre d'Étaples and Marguerite de Navarre's circle moved cautiously, sometimes close to evangelical ideas but without formal break. Thus the same intellectual resources, including philology, Scripture, the critique of abuses, and the use of print, supported divergent confessional outcomes.

The consequences for art and education were also varied. In Protestant territories the reduction or removal of religious images affected altarpiece painters and sculptors, while portraiture, printmaking, and book illustration, including Cranach's workshop's work for Wittenberg, remained active. Educational institutions, including humanist schools and colleges, were adapted by Protestant reformers, and Melanchthon's school reforms in German territories drew on humanist models. In Catholic regions, the Jesuit colleges and other institutions would also use humanist pedagogy, as Chapter 65 will show. Humanist habits of philology and rhetoric thus became part of the shared inheritance of rival confessions.

Historical Debate

Did Christian Humanism Prepare the Reformation?

The question: A saying often attributed to contemporaries claimed that Erasmus "laid the egg that Luther hatched." The attribution and exact wording are uncertain, but the idea captured a real historical question. To what extent did Christian humanism, with its biblical philology, criticism of clerical abuse, and use of print, help to create the conditions for the Protestant Reformation, and to what extent were the two movements distinct?

Interpretation A: Humanism contributed significantly to the Reformation's intellectual and practical preconditions. Supporters emphasize that humanism trained many future reformers in Greek, Hebrew, and rhetoric; that Erasmus's New Testament offered a new textual basis for scriptural argument; that humanist satire normalized public criticism of clergy and scholastic theology; and that networks of printers and scholars quickly became channels for evangelical ideas. Figures such as Melanchthon, Oecolampadius, Martin Bucer, and Huldrych Zwingli moved from humanist training into reform movements.
Interpretation B: The two movements had different roots and aims, and many humanists remained Catholic. Others argue that the Reformation arose primarily from late medieval theology, especially disputes about grace and justification, and from popular piety and political circumstances, not from humanism. They note that Erasmus, More, Fisher, and Cisneros's followers sought reform within the church; that Catholic reform drew heavily on humanist methods; and that Erasmus himself broke with Luther on free will. On this reading, the egg metaphor oversimplifies, since humanists disagreed profoundly about what the Reformation meant.
Interpretation C: Humanism provided shared tools and networks, but not a single direction. Many historians combine these views. Humanism supplied methods, personnel, and communication channels that both reformers and their opponents used, but it did not determine which side people chose. Choices depended on theology, personal relationships, local politics, and the pressures of the 1520s.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on the careers and writings of individual humanists, correspondence among scholars, printers' records, university statutes, the early Reformation's use of humanist editions, and the contrasting paths of figures who shared similar educations.

Why it matters: If humanism caused the Reformation, then the Reformation might seem a natural outgrowth of Renaissance learning. If the two were distinct, historians must explain the Reformation through other forces and recognize the tragic divisions among scholars who had once shared an ideal. The question also affects how historians describe Catholic and Protestant reform and how they treat Erasmus.

The northern Renaissance, then, was both a culmination and a beginning. It brought to maturity medieval traditions of devotion, urban artistry, and scholastic learning while introducing methods and publications that transformed European education and religion. It also left a set of unresolved tensions: between reform and rupture, between elite learning and popular piety, between openness to other traditions and entrenched prejudice, and between the unity of Latin scholarship and the diversity of vernacular culture. Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Northern Renaissance was not simply the Italian Renaissance moving north, but a set of artistic, educational, scholarly, and religious developments with their own institutional foundations. Northern Europe in 1400 already contained wealthy cities, powerful courts, universities, cathedral schools, manuscript workshops, and an active lay and clerical devotional culture. Italian books, ideas, and works of art entered this environment and were adapted to it, while northern paintings, prints, and scholarship traveled south and influenced Italian practice. Exchange ran in both directions, and the label Northern Renaissance remains useful only if it does not imply a derivative or secondary movement.

The medieval inheritance was crucial. The Devotio Moderna encouraged inward piety, reading, and moral discipline, and shaped the schooling of many future humanists, including Erasmus. Universities provided scholastic training that humanists criticized but also relied upon. Long traditions of complaint against clerical abuse supplied a vocabulary of reform. The economic and political context was equally important: the commercial cities of Flanders and Brabant, the court of the Burgundian dukes and later the Habsburgs, and the guilds, confraternities, hospitals, and merchant communities that commissioned art and books created demand for refined work.

In art, the achievement of Campin, Jan van Eyck, and Rogier van der Weyden established a Netherlandish tradition marked by refined oil technique, luminous surfaces, domestic interiors, convincing portraiture, and emotionally charged devotional imagery. Van Eyck did not invent oil painting, but he and his contemporaries brought its use to a new level. Hugo van der Goes and Hans Memling extended the tradition and, through Italian patrons, influenced painters abroad. Bosch used fantasy and satire to explore sin, folly, and judgment, though the meanings of his major works remain debated. Dürer combined Nuremberg craftsmanship with Italian study to raise the status of the artist and to transform woodcut and engraving into major art forms. Holbein, Cranach, Grünewald, Altdorfer, and sculptors such as Riemenschneider and Stoss show the diversity of German and Swiss art and its dependence on princely, civic, and ecclesiastical patrons.

Humanism spread to the north through student travel, diplomats, printers, and patrons. Agricola, Celtis, Wimpfeling, and others adapted classical learning to northern schools, courts, and universities, while humanism also flourished in Hungary, Poland, and Bohemia. The northern emphasis on applying philological methods to Scripture and the Church Fathers produced Christian humanism, defined by the demand to return to the sources, the ideal of the philosophy of Christ, and a commitment to moral education and reform within the church. It was not a formal party, and its adherents differed in method and ambition.

Erasmus became its most influential figure. Trained in the milieu of the Devotio Moderna and shaped by his experience in Paris, England, and Italy, he built a career through writing, patronage, and print. His Adages, Handbook of the Christian Soldier, Praise of Folly, Colloquies, and educational writings reached a wide readership, while his 1516 Greek and Latin New Testament, the first published Greek New Testament though not the first printed, brought textual criticism to the center of European religious debate. He was not the founder of Protestantism, and he never intended to produce a Protestant Bible. His reliance on persuasion and patronage left him exposed to attack from both conservative theologians and more radical reformers.

The affair of Johannes Reuchlin showed both possibilities and limits. Christian interest in Hebrew depended on Jewish teachers and scholarship, yet it unfolded in a context of persistent hostility, restriction, and expulsion. Reuchlin's defense of Jewish books helped protect important texts, but his motives and arguments did not amount to modern toleration, and many humanists, including Erasmus, held ambivalent or prejudiced views. Jewish scholars and leaders such as Jacob ben Jehiel Loans, Elijah Levita, and Josel of Rosheim were not passive suppliers of knowledge but actors with their own traditions and purposes.

Humanism took distinct forms in different regions. In England, Colet, More, Grocyn, Linacre, and others built on Oxford, Cambridge, and London connections, and More's Utopia (1516) used fictional form for social and political critique whose interpretation remains debated. In France, Budé advanced philology and secured royal lectureships, while Lefèvre d'Étaples and the circle of Marguerite de Navarre combined scholarship with evangelical reform. In Iberia, Nebrija, Cisneros, and the Alcalá scholars produced a grammar of Castilian, a new university, and the Complutensian Polyglot, with converso scholars playing an essential role, while Vives developed influential writing on education and poor relief.

Printing and schooling connected these regions. Presses at Venice, Basel, Paris, Leuven, Antwerp, and Alcalá supplied humanist editions, school texts, and illustrated books, and printers such as Aldus Manutius and Johann Froben became collaborators in scholarship. Printed images circulated art and religious ideas beyond the literate. Colleges and humanist schools created new readers, yet literacy remained limited, education was overwhelmingly male and urban, and women's participation, though visible among elites, was constrained. Humanist culture therefore reached a minority and did not represent the experience of most Europeans.

By 1516 and 1517 these developments had converged, and the outbreak of the Reformation transformed the setting. Humanist scholars chose different paths: some, including Melanchthon and Oecolampadius, joined the evangelical movement; others, including More, Fisher, and, in his own way, Erasmus, remained within the Catholic church. The historiographical debate over whether Christian humanism prepared the Reformation is best answered by recognizing that humanism supplied methods, networks, and personnel used by both sides but did not determine anyone's choice. The chapter's central lesson is that the intellectual resources of the Renaissance could support reform and rupture, tradition and innovation, and that historians must resist reading the later religious divisions back into the earlier moment of shared hope.

The next chapter, Chapter 60, examines politics, printing, and the Renaissance legacy more broadly. Part X, beginning with Chapter 61, takes up the late medieval church and the demand for reform, which this chapter has approached only as an intellectual background.

Key Terms

Northern Renaissance
A modern scholarly label for artistic and intellectual developments in the Low Countries, German lands, France, England, and neighboring regions c. 1400 to 1530, understood as interacting with, not derived from, Italian developments.
Devotio Moderna
A late medieval devotional movement from the Low Countries stressing inner piety, meditation, and communal life among laypeople and clergy.
Brothers and Sisters of the Common Life
Communities of laypeople and clergy, founded in the circle of Geert Groote, who lived together without formal monastic vows and often copied books and supervised pupils.
Windesheim Congregation
A reformed association of Augustinian canons connected to the Devotio Moderna.
Burgundian state
The composite territories of the Valois dukes of Burgundy, centered on Flanders and Brabant, a major patron of art and music until its absorption into Habsburg dynastic holdings after 1477.
Guild of Saint Luke
A common type of artists' guild, named after the patron saint of painters, regulating training, quality, and sale of works in northern cities.
Altarpiece
A painted or carved work placed on or behind an altar; forms include the triptych (three panels) and polyptych (several panels), often with hinged wings.
Oil painting
Painting with pigments bound in drying oil, which northern artists such as Van Eyck refined through layered glazes that created luminosity and detail; it was not invented by them.
Donor portrait
An image of the patron of a work, often kneeling beside sacred figures, advertising piety and status.
Disguised symbolism
Erwin Panofsky's term for the theory that ordinary objects in Netherlandish paintings carried hidden religious meanings, a theory later scholars apply with more caution.
Woodcut
A relief print made from a carved wooden block, printable together with type.
Engraving
An intaglio print made by incising lines into a copper plate with a burin, allowing fine detail.
Studia humanitatis
The humanist curriculum of grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy, developed in Italy and adapted in the north.
Philology
The study of texts through language, manuscript comparison, and historical context, central to humanist scholarship.
Christian humanism
The use of humanist learning, especially philology and rhetoric, for the study of Scripture, moral formation, and religious renewal.
Ad fontes
"To the sources," the humanist demand to return to original texts such as the Greek New Testament and the Church Fathers.
Philosophia Christi
Erasmus's phrase for the teaching of Christ as practical moral wisdom meant to shape daily conduct.
Vulgate
The Latin Bible, largely associated with Saint Jerome, which was the standard biblical text of western Christendom.
Novum Instrumentum
Erasmus's 1516 edition of the New Testament, printing Greek text, his Latin translation, and annotations.
Complutensian Polyglot
A multilingual Bible produced at Alcalá de Henares under Cardinal Cisneros, whose New Testament was printed in 1514 and whose complete set was finished in 1517, authorized in 1520, and circulated afterward.
Johannine Comma
A Trinitarian passage in the Latin text of 1 John absent from nearly all early Greek manuscripts; omitted by Erasmus in 1516 and 1519 and included in 1522.
Adages
Erasmus's expanding collection of classical proverbs with commentary, first published in 1500 and enlarged in later editions.
Praise of Folly
Erasmus's satirical oration spoken by Folly, written in 1509 and published in 1511.
Colloquies
Erasmus's dialogues, initially for teaching Latin, that became satirical commentary on religion and society.
Utopia
Thomas More's 1516 Latin work describing an imaginary island society and criticizing European conditions, its meaning and More's intentions being debated.
Augenspiegel
Johannes Reuchlin's 1511 pamphlet defending the preservation of Jewish books, central to the Reuchlin controversy.
Letters of Obscure Men
A satirical collection of fictional letters (1515, 1517) mocking opponents of Reuchlin and scholastic theologians.
Converso
A Jew who converted to Christianity in the Iberian kingdoms, or a descendant of such converts, often subject to suspicion.
Collegium Trilingue
The Leuven college founded in 1517 to teach Latin, Greek, and Hebrew.
Aldine Press
The Venetian printing house of Aldus Manutius, known for Greek and Latin editions and small, elegant formats.
Royal lecturers
Salaried teachers of Greek, Hebrew, and other subjects appointed by Francis I from 1530, forerunners of the Collège de France.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the Renaissance becoming a European phenomenon without losing its regional particularity. The achievements of Netherlandish and German artists, the scholarship of Erasmus and his colleagues, and the institutions founded from Leuven to Alcalá reveal how ideas, techniques, and tastes moved through networks of merchants, printers, courts, and universities. They also show that cultural exchange was reciprocal, and that no single region, whether Florence, Bruges, Nuremberg, or Basel, can stand for the whole.

The chapter also reveals a larger process: the growing importance of texts and images as instruments of public argument. Printing, philology, and satire allowed scholars to challenge inherited authorities with unprecedented speed and reach, and to appeal to readers beyond their own communities. That capacity would prove enormously consequential once religious dispute became open conflict, and Chapter 62 and Part X will show how the same tools served very different causes. At the same time, the chapter's emphasis on limits is important. Humanism reached a minority, depended on patrons, and often reproduced prejudices toward Jews, women, and the unlettered.

Several continuities survived beyond the chapter. Philological methods of textual criticism became permanent features of scholarship and theology. The humanist school curriculum, adapted by Protestant and Catholic educators alike, shaped European education for centuries. The northern portrait, the engraved print, and the illustrated book became established parts of European visual culture. The ideal of a learned community across political boundaries survived the confessional divisions and would reappear in the Republic of Letters examined in Chapter 82.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the argument of Volume III by showing that Renaissance culture and religious reform were intertwined but not identical. The scholars who hoped for renewal within a united Christendom had no way of knowing that the movement beginning in 1517 would divide western Europe into rival confessions, and the history of their hopes should not be told as if the outcome were inevitable. Understanding their position, as people who shared methods and ideals but made different choices under pressure, prepares students to approach the Reformation, the wars of religion, and the new states of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries with a sense of contingency.
