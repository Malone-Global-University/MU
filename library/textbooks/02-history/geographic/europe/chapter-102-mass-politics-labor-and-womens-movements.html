Mass Politics, Labor, and Women's Movements

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On May 1, 1890, working people in many European cities left their workplaces, or marched in their free time, to demand a legally limited working day of eight hours. The demonstrations combined political demands with public celebration in some cities, while confronting the risks of repression in others. They showed that workers, organized across borders, could present a common demand to governments that had often limited their influence.

The call for those demonstrations had been issued only months earlier, in July 1889, at a congress in Paris attended by delegates from socialist and labor organizations of many countries. The delegates resolved that a great international demonstration should be held on a single date, so that workers in every country would simultaneously present their governments with the same demand for an eight-hour day. May 1, 1890, was chosen. What happened that day differed from place to place. In some cities large processions passed peacefully through the streets. In others, employers threatened dismissal, police forces were mobilized, and workers who stayed away from work risked their livelihoods. In Britain, the main demonstration was held on the following Sunday, May 4, in London's Hyde Park, because many unions preferred not to risk the loss of a working day. In the Norwegian capital Kristiania, now Oslo, demonstrators assembled under banners calling for the eight-hour day, and the Norwegian Labour Movement Archives preserves a photograph of the occasion that historians can read as evidence of how an organized political crowd presented itself.

In the German Empire the situation was especially delicate. The Anti-Socialist Law of 1878 was still in force until the autumn of 1890, and the leaders of the Social Democratic movement, aware that a confrontation might provoke new repression, generally counseled caution about the form of the demonstrations. The event nevertheless marked how far organized labor had traveled in a single generation. Only two decades earlier, many workers across Europe remained excluded from elections, faced restrictions on union organization, or lacked enduring parties that represented their interests. The extent of these exclusions varied substantially, including in states that already had broad male suffrage. Within a few decades after 1890 the first of May became a recurring ritual of labor politics. A year later, on May 1, 1891, soldiers fired on a demonstration at Fourmies in northern France and killed nine people, a reminder that the claim to political presence could be met with violence.

This chapter studies the transformation that those demonstrations symbolized. Between about 1870 and 1914, European political life moved from a world dominated by monarchs, aristocratic networks, local notables, and narrow electorates toward one in which political parties enrolled mass memberships, trade unions bargained and struck on a national scale, socialist movements built newspapers, schools, and international congresses, and organized women's movements challenged the boundaries of citizenship itself. The central historical development is the growth of organized mass political participation. The chapter's guiding caution is that mass politics was not the same as democracy. Millions of men gained the vote while most women stayed excluded from national elections. Parliaments existed beside monarchs, chancellors, police forces, and colonial administrations that were not accountable to them. Workers organized, but they were divided by craft, religion, nationality, gender, and political strategy.

The chapter belongs to Part XVI, which examines Europe in an age of industrial capitalism, imperial expansion, and international rivalry. It builds on the intellectual origins of socialism in Chapter 96, on the constitutional reforms and revolutions examined in Chapters 98 and 99, on the national unifications of Chapter 100, and on the industrial and communications systems described in Chapter 101. Those chapters explained the ideas, institutions, and economic structures from which mass politics emerged. This chapter asks how large populations organized themselves to exercise, claim, or contest political power within those structures. It does not repeat the industrial conditions of Chapter 95 or the economic theories of Chapter 96, and it reserves the deeper analysis of racial nationalism and the Dreyfus Affair for Chapter 103, the imperial campaigns of Chapters 104 and 105, and the diplomatic road to war for Chapter 107.

Historians reconstruct this subject from a distinctive body of evidence. Election returns, parliamentary debates, and franchise statutes show who could vote and how. Party records, congress protocols, membership registers, and newspapers show how organizations functioned and argued. Trade union minute books, strike funds, and police reports document industrial conflict, although police and employers' sources represent an adversarial view. Memoirs, photographs, posters, songs, and banners record how participants wished to be seen. Women's journals, petitions, and the records of suffrage societies reveal a politics conducted largely by people excluded from the ballot. Each type of source reflects the perspective of its creator, and the chapter repeatedly asks whose voices are missing, particularly those of unorganized workers, rural populations, and colonial subjects.

The chapter is arranged in five divisions. The first examines why mass participation expanded and how suffrage grew unevenly. The second follows the formation of political parties in Britain, Germany, France, and the multinational and autocratic states of central, southern, northern, and eastern Europe. The third turns to trade unions, strikes, social insurance, and the wider world of cooperatives and mutual aid. The fourth traces socialist organization and the strategic disputes that divided it. The fifth examines women's movements and the uneven enfranchisement of women, and ends with an assessment of what mass politics achieved before 1914 and what it left unresolved.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why industrialization, urbanization, literacy, and new communication networks encouraged mass political participation after 1870.
  2. Distinguish among eligibility to vote, eligibility to stand for office, and the effective power of an elected assembly, using examples from at least three states.
  3. Compare the electoral systems of Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, Austria, Italy, and Finland, and explain why expanded suffrage was not synonymous with democracy.
  4. Analyze political parties as institutions with memberships, local organizations, finances, newspapers, and leaders, rather than only as bearers of programs.
  5. Trace the development of trade unions from craft societies to mass organizations, and explain how Christian, socialist, and syndicalist unions differed.
  6. Assess why governments combined repression, labor regulation, and social insurance in response to working-class organization.
  7. Describe the organizational development of German Social Democracy and compare it with British Laborism and French socialism.
  8. Evaluate the disagreements among revisionists, orthodox Marxists, and syndicalists over reform, revolution, and the role of unions.
  9. Interpret the aims and methods of women's movements, including constitutional suffragists, militant suffragettes, socialist women, and the early enfranchisement of women in Finland and Norway.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether pre-1914 Europe was becoming more democratic or developing new forms of organized political control.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Did the expansion of the electorate necessarily make European political systems more democratic?
  3. How did parties, newspapers, and associations create durable political constituencies, and what was lost or gained when politics became organized in this way?

  7. Why did women's political rights expand so unevenly across Europe, and how did suffrage campaigns interact with class politics?
  8. By 1914, how far had European political systems moved toward mass democracy, and what inequalities of class, gender, nationality, and imperial status remained?

Historical Context

By the 1860s the political vocabulary of nineteenth-century Europe had been established, but the political nation remained small. Chapter 98 examined the revolutions of 1820, 1830, and 1848, in which constitutional, national, and social demands were pressed on monarchies and then, in most cases, contained. Chapter 99 traced the more gradual reforms through which Britain, France, and other states altered their parliaments, monarchies, and citizenship rules. Chapter 100 showed how the unification of Italy and Germany created new states whose constitutions had to decide how far ordinary men would take part in public life. The decisive point for this chapter is that by about 1870, many European governments had accepted representative institutions in some form, while few accepted that the whole adult population should have an equal voice in them.

At the same time, society was changing in ways that made limited political participation harder to sustain. Chapters 93 to 95 described industrialization, the growth of cities, and the formation of an industrial working class, and Chapter 101 explained how steel, chemicals, electricity, railways, telegraphs, and large corporations enlarged both the scale of economic organization and the concentration of workers in factories, mines, ports, and railway systems. Literacy was rising across much of western, northern, and central Europe, assisted by state schooling, and cheap newspapers, pamphlets, and posters carried political information to far larger audiences than the pre-industrial press had reached. Railways and the postal and telegraph services allowed campaign organizers, union officials, and newspaper editors to coordinate activity over wide areas. These material conditions did not automatically create mass politics, but they supplied the means by which people already motivated by grievance, religion, nationality, or ideology could organize at scale.

The political thought examined in earlier chapters provided the languages in which organization occurred. Liberalism supplied arguments about constitutional government and individual rights, although liberal politicians were often wary of giving the vote to propertyless men. Conservatism defended monarchy, church, and social hierarchy, and some conservatives learned to mobilize popular support for those causes. Nationalism, discussed in Chapter 97, offered a shared political identity that cut across social classes, and socialism, discussed in Chapter 96, offered an analysis of class conflict and a program for social transformation. Catholic and Protestant churches organized their own political and social networks. The politics of the late nineteenth century was therefore contested among many mass mobilizations, not only between a working class and its opponents.

Historians have long debated the relationship between these social changes and political outcomes. An older tradition treated the growth of labor and socialist movements as a straightforward consequence of industrialization, but more recent scholarship, such as Geoff Eley's Forging Democracy, emphasizes that democratic and mass politics emerged through contested processes, shaped by religion, nationalism, state policy, and political choices, and that conservative and nationalist movements also mobilized masses. This chapter follows that more cautious approach. It treats social change as creating possibilities, and it asks how particular organizations, laws, leaders, and conflicts converted those possibilities into particular outcomes in different states.

Finally, students should recognize the limits of the evidence and of the geography of this chapter. Much of the best documented history concerns Britain, Germany, and France, where party archives, trade union records, and parliamentary debates have been extensively studied. This reflects the survival and accessibility of sources and the size of those movements, but it should not make those countries the European norm. The chapter therefore gives sustained attention to Belgium, Austria-Hungary, Italy, Scandinavia, Finland, and the Russian Empire, whose experiences show that mass politics took many forms. The chapter also stays close to the period before 1914. The Russian revolutions and the wartime transformations of European politics belong to later chapters, and the First World War is treated here only as the boundary that interrupted the pre-1914 development of mass movements.

The Emergence of Mass Politics and the Expansion of Political Participation

Why did mass political participation expand, how far did suffrage extend, and how unevenly did European states move toward wider participation?

Section 102.1

From Elite Politics to Mass Politics: European Political Society after 1870

In the middle of the nineteenth century, parliamentary politics in most of Europe was conducted by a small number of people. In Britain, local landowners, employers, and professional men dominated many constituencies, and election results often depended on personal influence, patronage, and the loyalty of dependents. In France, Italy, Belgium, and the German states, restricted electorates or indirect voting systems limited participation. Elections frequently depended on the standing of local notables, men whose wealth, office, or family connections gave them informal authority, rather than on disciplined organizations with programs and memberships. Politics outside parliament existed, in clubs, newspapers, and associations, but it reached comparatively few.

After 1870, several developments combined to transform this pattern. Urbanization brought large numbers of people into close contact in cities, workplaces, and neighborhoods, where they could share information and grievances. Population growth enlarged the pool of potential participants. Rising literacy, assisted by compulsory schooling laws in many states, enlarged the audience for political writing. Railways, the postal service, and the telegraph allowed organizers to coordinate meetings and campaigns across regions, and cheap printing allowed newspapers, leaflets, and posters to circulate widely. These changes did not by themselves cause mass politics, but they lowered the cost of organizing large groups and increased the number of people with the means and the motivation to take part.

The social groups drawn into politics were varied. Industrial workers in factories, mines, shipyards, and railways formed a growing constituency, but so did the salaried employees of large firms and the state, shopkeepers and small proprietors, artisans, and farmers whose economic position was affected by tariffs, credit, and market prices. Organized religious communities mobilized their members, notably Catholics in Germany, Belgium, and Austria, and Protestant and nonconformist congregations in Britain and the Netherlands. Nationalities within multinational states, such as Czechs, Poles, and Croats within the Habsburg monarchy, built their own political organizations. The politics of the period was a politics of many overlapping constituencies, not only of a conflict between capital and labor.

The relationship between class and political behavior was therefore complicated. Many industrial workers supported socialist parties, but many others voted for Liberals, Conservatives, or confessional parties, or did not vote at all, and many were not entitled to vote. Property owners were similarly divided. Some conservative politicians discovered that popular loyalties to church, monarchy, and nation could be organized in ways that strengthened existing authorities, while some liberal reformers feared that wider suffrage would empower groups hostile to property. Historians therefore avoid treating the working class as uniformly socialist or the propertied classes as uniformly conservative, and they ask instead which organizations succeeded in attaching particular groups to particular political causes.

Expanding participation could strengthen or threaten established governments. Rulers who feared revolution sometimes found that elections gave the population a legitimate channel for grievance and that parties could be negotiated with, bargained with, or manipulated. Others found that mass organizations, once established, could not easily be controlled. The same electoral systems could allow conservative parties to mobilize rural and religious voters against liberal reformers and allow socialists to mobilize urban workers against both. Governments responded in different ways, through reform, repression, concession, and attempts to shape public opinion directly. The nineteenth-century state increasingly used schools, military service, press policy, public ceremonies, and national symbols to cultivate loyalty, and parties and movements used similar techniques to cultivate their own followings.

Mass politics therefore involved both inclusion and control. Wider participation gave previously excluded people some leverage over public decisions, but it also created new methods by which organizations, governments, and press owners attempted to influence opinion. The historian must treat both aspects as real. A mass party could give a worker an education, a social network, and a political voice. It could also impose discipline on its members and employ propaganda techniques. The sections that follow examine how far, and in what ways, each of these possibilities was realized in different parts of Europe.

Central question: Why did mass political participation become increasingly important in Europe after 1870, and how did it transform relationships between governments and populations?
Section 102.2

Expanding Suffrage: Electoral Reform, Universal Male Suffrage, and the Limits of the Vote

The enlargement of parliamentary electorates in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was one of the most visible forms of political change, but it followed no single path. States differed in the groups they admitted, the rules by which they counted votes, and the powers of the assemblies that voters elected. Understanding this variety requires distinguishing several kinds of restriction. Property qualifications limited the vote to those who paid a specified amount of tax or rent. Literacy requirements excluded those who could not read. Residence rules disadvantaged mobile workers. Age limits varied, and some systems used indirect voting, in which voters chose electors who then chose representatives, or plural voting, in which some voters had more than one vote. Unequal constituency sizes meant that a vote in one district could weigh more than a vote in another. All of these mechanisms affected who held political power even when many men were nominally enfranchised.

Britain's Third Reform Act of 1884 and the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885 illustrate the point. Chapter 99 examined the earlier reforms of 1832 and 1867. The act of 1884 extended to the counties the household and lodger franchise that the 1867 act had already given to the boroughs, bringing many agricultural laborers and miners living in rural constituencies into the electorate and increasing the male electorate substantially, from roughly three million to roughly five million. The 1885 act then redrew constituency boundaries, replacing most multi-member constituencies with single-member districts of broadly similar size. Even so, a considerable proportion of adult men remained outside the electorate, because the system depended on registration, residence, and the definition of a household, and plural voting for property owners and university graduates continued. No woman could vote in parliamentary elections. The secret ballot, introduced in 1872, and the Corrupt and Illegal Practices Act of 1883, which limited campaign spending and bribery, reduced older forms of influence and made organized party campaigns more important.

The German Empire offers a contrasting example. The Reichstag was elected by equal, direct, and secret male suffrage for men over twenty-five, a franchise that was among the broadest in Europe in 1871. Yet the Reichstag did not choose the government. The chancellor was appointed by and responsible to the emperor, and the Bundesrat, a council of delegates of the federated states, shared legislative power. Moreover, the constituency boundaries drawn in 1871 were not revised as population shifted to the cities, so that urban districts became heavily underrepresented. The individual German states also retained their own franchises. Prussia, the largest, used a three-class voting system for its Landtag, which weighted votes according to tax payments, and this system lasted until 1918. The experience shows that a broad national franchise could coexist with institutions that limited the practical power of voters. Section 102.6 examines this combination in detail.

In France, universal male suffrage had existed since the revolution of 1848 and was a settled feature of the Third Republic, which was established after the fall of Napoleon III in 1870. Yet the practical meaning of that suffrage was shaped by the structure of the republic. The Chamber of Deputies was elected directly by male voters, but the Senate was elected indirectly, through colleges weighted toward rural communes, a design that gave conservative rural interests substantial influence. Women could not vote. In the French colonies, the status of the vote depended on legal categories. In Algeria, for example, the rights of settlers of European origin differed from those of the Muslim population. Chapters 104 and 105 treat the empires in detail, but the point for this chapter is that imperial status could place large populations outside the citizenship that metropolitan Europeans enjoyed.

These examples show why historians distinguish three questions. The first is eligibility to vote, which depended on the legal definition of the electorate. The second is eligibility to stand for office, which could be limited by property, residence, religion, or by the cost of campaigning in systems where members of parliament were unpaid, as was the case in Britain until 1911. The third is the effective power of the assembly itself. A parliament elected by broad suffrage might lack control over the executive, the army, foreign policy, or the budget. A narrow electorate might choose a powerful government, and a broad electorate might choose a weak one. Legal suffrage also did not erase social and economic barriers. Employers or landlords could still pressure dependents. Time away from work to vote or attend meetings was costly. Poverty, geographic mobility, and illiteracy could reduce practical participation even when the law allowed it.

The history of suffrage in this period also reveals that political actors had different goals in extending or restricting the vote. Some reformers believed that voters would become more responsible citizens. Some conservatives expected that rural, religious, or deferential voters would support established order. Some socialists saw the vote as a means of gaining power peacefully, while others treated it as a limited tool within a larger struggle. Governments sometimes extended the vote under pressure from protest, sometimes in order to forestall it, and sometimes in the expectation of political advantage. The history of electoral reform is thus a history of bargaining, calculation, and conflict, not of automatic expansion.

Historical Vocabulary
Universal Male Suffrage and Plural Voting

Universal male suffrage refers to the right of all adult men, or nearly all of them, to vote, usually subject to age and residence requirements. The word "universal" in the nineteenth century almost always excluded women, and it did not always mean that every man was included, since many systems excluded servants, paupers, soldiers, or those without a fixed residence. Plural voting is a system in which some voters are given more than one vote, typically on the basis of property, education, age, or family status. Belgium's system of 1893, which gave one vote to every eligible adult man but added additional votes for certain property owners and for men with specified educational qualifications, is the best known European example, and Britain also retained a form of plural voting.

These terms matter to this chapter because they show that "broad suffrage" and "equal suffrage" were different things. A state could enfranchise most men and still weight some votes more heavily than others, and a state with a nearly universal male franchise might exclude half of its adult population. Modern readers should therefore avoid assuming that the word "universal" implies the modern meaning of one person, one vote.

Central question: Did the expansion of the electorate necessarily make European political systems more democratic?
Section 102.3

Political Parties, Campaign Organizations, Newspapers, and the New Political Public

The modern political party, as an organization with a permanent structure, a membership, paid officials, local branches, and a recognizable program, was largely a product of the late nineteenth century. Earlier parliamentary groupings were often loose alliances of individual politicians whose electoral support depended on local standing. Larger electorates, secret ballots, and the need to reach many voters made individual notables less able to command victory unaided and encouraged the creation of standing organizations. In Britain, local Liberal and Conservative associations, linked to national federations, were built in the 1860s, 1870s, and 1880s. In Germany, parties established regional and local committees and developed disciplined party structures. Socialist parties and some confessional parties were often the most thoroughly organized, because they relied on dues-paying members and volunteers.

Parties used a repertoire of methods to reach voters. Election committees prepared lists of voters and arranged canvassing, the practice of visiting voters to learn and influence their intentions. Public meetings, in halls, taverns, and open spaces, gave candidates and speakers a direct audience. Leaflets, pamphlets, and posters were printed in large quantities, and illustrated posters could reach audiences with limited reading ability. Newspapers were central. Some were owned by wealthy individuals or syndicates, others by parties themselves. Socialist and confessional parties often maintained press systems that included daily newspapers, weekly journals, and local papers, giving them a means of communicating independent of commercial publishers. Political rallies, processions, and anniversary celebrations, such as May Day, served as visible public demonstrations.

The growth of national parties changed the relationship between local and national politics. Party leaders in the legislature increasingly coordinated the votes of their members, and in some systems, party discipline came to determine parliamentary behavior. National party conventions or congresses, where delegates from local branches debated programs and chose leaders, gave activists a voice and gave leaders a way to claim legitimacy. Parliamentary caucuses organized members' conduct, and national campaigns coordinated messages across many constituencies. Railways allowed leading speakers to tour the country, while the telegraph allowed news of speeches and results to be published rapidly. A local election could thus become part of a national contest, and a national leader could address audiences far from the capital.

Political participation extended beyond voting. Women, who were excluded from parliamentary elections everywhere in Europe in 1870, nevertheless took part in campaigns, petitions, and party auxiliary organizations. In Britain, the Conservative Primrose League, founded in 1883, admitted women as members and used them in canvassing and social events, and women were active in Liberal associations and in the work of nonconformist congregations. Young people who were below the voting age took part in socialist youth organizations and in demonstrations. Migrants and members of national or religious minorities sometimes lacked citizenship or voting rights but participated in labor organizations and community associations. These forms of participation show that the boundary between voters and non-voters did not coincide with the boundary between political actors and political spectators.

Political identities were also formed outside formal politics. Workplaces, churches, reading societies, neighborhood networks, choirs, gymnastic clubs, and voluntary associations offered sites of sociability in which political loyalties were created and sustained. In German Social Democracy and in Catholic movements, entire networks of associations accompanied the party, so that a member might belong to a party branch, a union, a cooperative store, a singing society, and a sports club, all connected to the same political milieu. The development of such milieus helps explain the durability of party loyalties, which could persist across generations and be relatively insulated from short-term political appeals.

The new political public raised questions about the quality of participation. Contemporaries and historians alike have distinguished informed engagement, in which citizens used newspapers and meetings to evaluate arguments, from persuasion and propaganda, in which organizers sought to create belief and loyalty. The commercial press, which relied on advertising and large circulations, sometimes cultivated sensationalism and partisan loyalty. Parties used slogans, symbols, and ritual to cultivate emotional attachment. Some observers, including early sociologists of the period, worried that mass politics encouraged demagoguery and crowd psychology, while others argued that organized participation educated citizens in public affairs. Both concerns reflected real features of the new politics, and neither can be dismissed. The challenge for historians is to assess particular cases rather than to apply a general judgment.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taking Part in a Late Nineteenth-Century Election Campaign or Mass Meeting

For most participants, political life was a matter of evenings, weekends, and neighborhoods, not of parliamentary debate. A campaign meeting might be held in a rented hall, a school, a tavern room, a church hall, or in the open air, and its organizers could be volunteers who had spent the day at work. Speakers addressed audiences that might include supporters, curious neighbors, and opponents who came to interrupt. Heckling was an expected part of many public meetings in Britain, and organizers sometimes employed stewards to keep order. Socialist and Catholic organizations in Germany and Austria often held meetings in rooms provided by sympathetic innkeepers, since the choice of a venue could itself be political, and police could attend to watch for illegal speech.

The experience differed by gender, age, and status. Women often could not vote but could attend meetings, distribute leaflets, collect subscriptions, and organize social events. In some places, laws restricted women's participation in political associations. In Prussia and elsewhere in the German states, women were barred by association laws from joining political organizations until a Reich law of 1908 changed this, so women's organizations sometimes had to describe themselves as social or educational. Workers who attended meetings or canvassed after a long working day paid a cost in time and energy, and those who took part in open political activity in a company town or under a hostile employer could risk dismissal. Election day itself might involve queues at polling stations, party workers offering transport and reminders, and, in places where the secret ballot was less secure, social pressure on voters. Those who could not read depended on friends, family, and speakers for political information, which illustrates how oral communication continued to matter alongside print.

Section 102.4

Uneven Democratization: Electoral Rights in Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, and Other States

A comparison of electoral reform across Europe shows that states moved toward wider participation at different speeds and for different reasons. Some of the broadest male franchises were established in states whose governments retained considerable independence from parliaments, while some states with strong parliamentary traditions kept relatively restrictive franchises. Britain, France, and Germany, discussed above, illustrate three combinations of franchise and executive power. Other states illustrate still further variation.

Belgium provides a vivid example of the difference between broad and equal suffrage. Before 1893, Belgium's franchise was based on property qualifications that confined voting to a small portion of adult men. Pressure from the Belgian Workers' Party and its allies, culminating in a general strike in April 1893, helped bring about a reform that gave a vote to nearly all men over twenty-five. The law also created plural voting. Each eligible man received one vote, but men who were property owners, or who held certain educational or professional qualifications, or who were fathers over a certain age, could receive additional votes, up to a maximum of three. This system broadened the electorate dramatically while preserving the political weight of the propertied and educated, and it endured, in modified form, until after the First World War. Belgium also adopted proportional representation for its legislative elections in 1899, which affected how votes were converted into seats.

In Austria, the franchise for the Reichsrat, the parliament for the Austrian half of the Habsburg monarchy, was organized into curiae, or voting classes, which gave very different weights to landowners, towns, chambers of commerce, and, from 1873, rural communes. A reform in 1896 added a further curia for the general male population while leaving the others in place. After sustained pressure from the Social Democrats, from popular demonstrations, and from the government's own calculation that wider suffrage might help manage competing nationalities, equal male suffrage for the House of Deputies was introduced in 1907. This reform excluded women and, at the parliamentary level, extinguished the limited property-based voting rights that a small number of women landowners had previously exercised. It also applied only to the Austrian half of the monarchy. In the Kingdom of Hungary, the other half, a restrictive franchise continued to limit the vote to a small minority of the population, which gave the Magyar landed elite and its allies dominant influence over a multinational kingdom.

Italy's electorate was enlarged in two stages. A law of 1882 lowered the age of voting and relaxed the tax and literacy qualifications, enlarging the electorate several times over, although it remained a minority of adult men. In 1912, a further reform gave the vote to all literate men aged twenty-one and over and to illiterate men aged thirty and over, as well as to younger men who had completed military service, which increased the electorate to well over eight million. The reform brought into politics many peasants and workers in the south, where illiteracy was highest. The consequences included opportunities for socialists and for the Catholic movement, which had been restrained from open participation in national elections by papal policy until the early twentieth century and began to cooperate with Liberal candidates in 1913.

Scandinavia and the Russian Empire show two further contrasts. In Norway, universal male suffrage was introduced in 1898, and Sweden moved to a broad male franchise and proportional representation for its lower chamber in 1907 to 1909, in the context of conflict between agrarian, liberal, and labor movements and a government that sought to avoid revolution. Denmark's reforms, too, proceeded through a mixture of parliamentary conflict and bargaining. In Russia, by contrast, there was no national representative assembly before 1905. The revolution of that year led Tsar Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, promising civil liberties and a legislative Duma, but the government then altered the electoral law in June 1907, giving greater weight to landowners and reducing the representation of peasants, workers, and non-Russian nationalities. The Duma therefore existed, but its powers were limited by the tsar and by the Fundamental Laws of 1906. The contrast with Finland, discussed in section 102.8 and 102.19, was striking.

These reforms were the outcome of several forces. Industrialization created new constituencies. Popular protest, including strikes and demonstrations, raised the cost of exclusion, as in Belgium and Austria. Political bargaining, in which parties exchanged support for reform, was often decisive, and governments sometimes extended the franchise in the hope of splitting opposition or stabilizing a political crisis. In multinational states, electoral reform was entangled with competition among national groups. There is no simple pattern whereby richer or more industrial states reformed earlier, and there is no basis for treating any single path as the model. The history of European suffrage is better understood as a series of contingent settlements.

For students, the key analytical point is the difference between breadth and equality. By 1914, most European states had enfranchised most adult men, in one form or another, and a few had enfranchised women. But the form of the franchise, the weight of each vote, the powers of the elected body, and the relationship between legislature and executive varied enormously. Comparisons that rank states only by the number of voters obscure those differences, and the Timeline Study below is intended to help students notice them.

Section 102.5

Britain: Conservatives, Liberals, and the Rise of Independent Labor Representation

British politics after 1870 was organized around two parties, the Conservatives and the Liberals, whose structures were reshaped by the enlarged electorate. The Liberal Party built its national organization on a network of local Liberal associations, many of them influenced by nonconformist chapels, trade unionists, and local business and professional men, and linked after 1877 through the National Liberal Federation, an organization that claimed to give ordinary members a voice in policy. The Conservatives created a comparable apparatus in the National Union of Conservative and Unionist Associations and in a central office, and they built mass popular support through organizations such as the Primrose League. Both parties relied on local volunteers, agents, and registration societies, because under British law, voters had to appear on a register, and much of the work of party organization consisted of identifying supporters and making sure they were registered.

The parties differed over several large questions. The Irish question divided the Liberals in 1886, when Gladstone's first Home Rule Bill, which proposed an Irish legislature, was defeated after a group of Liberal Unionists broke from the party. The Liberal Unionists later allied with the Conservatives, and the Unionist coalition governed Britain for most of the period from 1886 to 1905. Imperial policy, discussed in Chapters 104 and 105, divided opinion over the South African War of 1899 to 1902. Taxation, tariffs, and the proper scope of government were increasingly contested, and by the early twentieth century, debates over the cost of social reform and over tariff reform created new divisions. These conflicts mattered for labor politics, because they determined how far the major parties were willing to accommodate working-class candidates and demands.

For much of the 1870s and 1880s, labor representatives sought influence through the Liberal Party. A small number of trade unionists, such as the miners' leaders Alexander Macdonald and Thomas Burt, were elected to Parliament in the 1870s as Liberals, an arrangement often called Lib-Lab politics. Many union leaders preferred cooperation with the Liberals because it offered some chance of legislative gains and avoided the risk of splitting the progressive vote. Local Liberal associations, however, often preferred middle-class candidates, and working-class activists complained that they were offered few safe seats. Frustration was compounded by the legal environment, since unions relied on legislation that protected their funds and their right to organize and strike.

Alongside the Lib-Lab approach, socialist and independent labor organizations developed. The Social Democratic Federation, founded in 1881 under H. M. Hyndman, was an explicitly Marxist group. The Fabian Society, established in 1884, was a small society of intellectuals that favored gradual reform and research rather than revolution. The most important organizational step was the creation of the Independent Labour Party, which was founded at Bradford in 1893 under the leadership of Keir Hardie and others. Hardie, a former miner, had been elected to Parliament in 1892 as an independent labor member for West Ham South, and the ILP aimed to create a political party representing working-class interests independently of both Liberals and Conservatives. It was strongest in the industrial north of England and in Scotland, and it combined socialist propaganda with a moral and religious language that drew on nonconformist culture.

The step from small independent groups to a major party came in 1900. In February of that year, a conference in London, convened on the initiative of the Trades Union Congress and attended by representatives of trade unions and socialist societies including the ILP, the SDF, and the Fabian Society, established the Labour Representation Committee. The committee's purpose was to promote the election of working-class representatives to Parliament, under a loose federal structure. The Taff Vale judgment of 1901, in which the House of Lords held that a union could be sued for damages caused by a strike, threatened union funds and persuaded many union leaders that the unions needed direct parliamentary representation. Affiliations rose rapidly. In 1903, the committee's secretary, Ramsay MacDonald, concluded a secret electoral understanding with the Liberal chief whip, Herbert Gladstone, under which Liberals and Labour candidates avoided competing against each other in a limited number of constituencies. In the general election of 1906, a Liberal landslide, 29 candidates endorsed by the committee were elected, and the parliamentary group adopted the name Labour Party.

The new party brought together groups that did not agree. Trade unionists, who supplied most of its funding and membership, were largely concerned with legal protection for unions and with practical improvements. Socialists wanted more far-reaching change. Reformers and radical Liberals saw it as a vehicle for progressive reform. The party constitution of 1918, which adopted an explicit socialist objective, lay in the future, and before 1914 the party's program was loosely defined. The Liberal government then passed the Trade Disputes Act of 1906, which reversed the effect of the Taff Vale decision, but the House of Lords' Osborne judgment of 1909, which prevented unions from using funds for political purposes, was a setback until Parliament introduced payment of members in 1911 and later legislation restored the unions' political funds in 1913. In the two elections of 1910, Labour won around forty seats, but remained a junior partner in a Parliament dominated by the Liberals. Whether the party would replace the Liberals as the principal alternative to the Conservatives was not clear before 1914, and historians still debate how far its later rise was already under way.

The history of British Labour illustrates how an expanded electorate and a particular legal and institutional environment combined to produce a new party. It also shows that the party was not the inevitable political expression of the working class. The trade unions, which preceded the party and funded it, had their own interests, many workers continued to vote Liberal or Conservative, and the party did not represent women, who could not vote, or most agricultural laborers, who were weakly organized.

Section 102.6

Germany: The Imperial Reichstag, Confessional Parties, and Mass Electoral Organization

The constitution of the German Empire, adopted in 1871, divided power among the emperor, the Bundesrat, and the Reichstag. The emperor was the king of Prussia and held command of the army and the power to appoint and dismiss the imperial chancellor. The Bundesrat, composed of delegates instructed by the governments of the member states, held legislative authority and was dominated by Prussia. The Reichstag, elected by equal, direct, and secret male suffrage, voted on legislation and the budget, and it could reject laws and refuse funds. But the chancellor did not depend on its confidence. A chancellor could remain in office despite a hostile Reichstag if he retained the emperor's support, because his tenure did not depend on parliamentary confidence. Dissolving the Reichstag, however, required a Bundesrat resolution and the emperor's assent under Article 24 of the imperial constitution; the chancellor could not order dissolution independently. This combination makes imperial Germany an example of mass electoral politics without a fully parliamentary system.

The broad national franchise gave political parties incentives to organize widely. The main parties were the Conservatives, who drew support from Protestant landowners east of the Elbe and from the Prussian state apparatus, the National Liberals, who were supported by industrialists, professionals, and urban Protestants and who cooperated with Bismarck in the 1870s, the left liberals, the Catholic Centre Party, and the Social Democrats. Alongside these were parties representing regional and national minorities, including Poles, Danes, and the Alsatians and Lorrainers of the Reichsland of Alsace-Lorraine, which Germany annexed in 1871. Voting patterns were structured to a considerable degree by religion, region, and social milieu as well as by class.

Religion was particularly significant. In the 1870s, the Prussian and imperial governments, with the support of the National Liberals, conducted a campaign known as the Kulturkampf, or struggle over culture, against the influence of the Catholic Church in education, marriage law, and public life. The measures included state supervision of schools, civil marriage, restrictions on the clergy, and expulsion of the Jesuits. Catholics responded by organizing. The Centre Party, led in its early years by Ludwig Windthorst, became a disciplined defender of Catholic interests, supported by an extensive network of Catholic newspapers, associations, and local clergy. Its voters were drawn from peasants, artisans, workers, and the middle class, and its success showed how confessional identity could generate stable political loyalties across classes. By the 1880s, Bismarck had abandoned much of the Kulturkampf in order to obtain Centre support on other matters.

The relationship between the government and the Social Democrats was more hostile. After two attempts on the life of Emperor William I in 1878, Bismarck persuaded the Reichstag to pass the Anti-Socialist Law of October 1878, which banned socialist associations, meetings, and publications, and allowed local authorities to declare a state of minor siege in particular areas. The law did not abolish the party's right to contest elections, and socialist candidates continued to stand and to sit in the Reichstag as individuals. The party, which was driven underground in some respects, organized secretly, smuggled newspapers across the border from Switzerland, and developed networks of trusted individuals. The law also deepened the party's sense of identity as an excluded group. Its electoral support grew during the law's twelve years, rising from about 6 percent of the Reichstag vote in 1881 to nearly 20 percent in 1890. The law expired in September 1890, after Bismarck's dismissal by the new emperor, William II, in March of that year.

Repression failed to stop growth partly because it targeted organization more than votes, partly because it created sympathy, and partly because the party's appeal rested on genuine grievances and on practical services. After 1890, the Social Democrats built a formidable organization of local branches, trade-union links, cooperatives, and newspapers. Party membership rose to just over a million by 1914, and the party's press included dozens of daily newspapers. Other parties responded with their own organizations. The Conservatives organized the Agrarian League in 1893, a mass lobbying organization of landowners and farmers, and various nationalist associations developed. By 1912, the Social Democrats won more than a third of the vote and became the largest party in the Reichstag, but their ability to turn this into governing power was blocked by the constitution and by the unwillingness of other parties to cooperate with them. In 1913, the Reichstag passed a vote of no confidence in the government over a military scandal in the Alsatian town of Zabern, but the chancellor remained in office, which demonstrated the limits of parliamentary influence.

Germany therefore developed powerful mass parties without becoming a parliamentary democracy. Their sophistication, which involved professional organizers, newspapers, local associations, and campaign methods, was among the most advanced in Europe. Yet the Reichstag could influence legislation and budgets but could not decide who governed. The military retained a protected position, and the Prussian three-class franchise preserved conservative dominance in the largest state. Historians continue to debate how this combination should be interpreted, and the Continuity and Change feature below, along with the Historical Debate in section 102.20, examines the question.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Participation and Executive Authority in the German Empire, 1871 to 1914

What Changed?

  • Voter turnout in Reichstag elections rose from roughly half of eligible voters in 1871 to more than eighty percent in 1912, as parties mobilized more effectively.
  • Parties developed permanent local organizations, press systems, and associated social institutions, especially the Social Democrats and the Centre.
  • The Social Democrats grew from a small and persecuted movement to the largest Reichstag party by seats in 1912.
  • Secrecy of the ballot became more effective after reforms around 1903 introduced voting envelopes and screened booths.
  • Interest-group organizations, such as the Agrarian League and nationalist associations, entered politics as new forms of mass lobbying.

What Remained Continuous?

  • The chancellor remained responsible to the emperor rather than to the Reichstag.
  • The Bundesrat and the structure of federal power preserved Prussian influence.
  • The Prussian three-class franchise remained in place until 1918.
  • Reichstag constituency boundaries were not redrawn despite urbanization, leaving cities underrepresented.
  • The military retained a special constitutional position under the emperor's command.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban workers, Catholics, and members of national minorities experienced the growth of organized political life most directly, as did party officials. Who experienced greater continuity? Women, who remained excluded from the vote, and those in Prussia whose votes were weighted by tax class, saw much less change. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that rising participation and persistent executive authority could coexist, and that the expansion of mass politics did not by itself determine whether a state became parliamentary.

Section 102.7

France: Republican Parties, Radicalism, Socialism, and the Political Culture of the Third Republic

The French Third Republic, proclaimed in September 1870 after the capture of Napoleon III in the Franco-Prussian War, began without a settled constitution and with many monarchists in the National Assembly elected in 1871. The constitutional laws of 1875 created a republic with a president, a Chamber of Deputies elected by universal male suffrage, and a Senate chosen indirectly. The republic's survival was not certain. In May 1877, President MacMahon dismissed a republican prime minister and dissolved the Chamber, a crisis called the Sixteenth of May, but the republicans won the ensuing election, and thereafter presidents rarely exercised such powers. The crisis established the principle that governments should depend on the Chamber, which created a strongly parliamentary system with short-lived ministries and shifting coalitions.

French politics did not rest on two disciplined parties. It was organized around fluid groups in the Chamber, with party labels that corresponded loosely to organizations in the constituencies. The republican moderates, sometimes called Opportunists, governed in the 1880s. To their left, Radicals emphasized the defense of the republic, secularism, and social reform, and drew on the support of small proprietors, shopkeepers, and professionals, particularly in provincial towns. On the right were monarchists, Bonapartists, and conservative Catholics, who were divided among themselves. A distinctive feature was the importance of local republican committees, masonic lodges, newspapers, and civic institutions such as schools and veterans' associations, which created a republican political culture at the local level.

The relationship between the state and the Catholic Church was the most divisive issue. Republicans considered the church an ally of monarchism and an obstacle to civic education, and in the 1880s, Jules Ferry's education laws made primary schooling free, compulsory, and secular. Conflict intensified after the Dreyfus Affair, which began in 1894 and split the country over the conviction of a Jewish army officer for treason. The affair is treated in Chapter 103, but its political consequence was to unite republicans in defense of the republic. A coalition government under René Waldeck-Rousseau, formed in 1899, regulated religious congregations by the Law of Associations of 1901, and the Radicals, who organized as a national party in 1901, took the lead in anticlerical policy. This culminated in the law of 1905 separating the churches and the state. Catholics organized in response, through Catholic newspapers, associations, and political networks, though they remained a minority within French politics.

French socialism was divided into several rival groups through the 1890s. Jules Guesde led a Marxist party, the French Workers' Party. Others, including Paul Brousse's Possibilists, favored municipal and gradualist reform, and Edouard Vaillant's followers were influenced by the Blanquist revolutionary tradition. Jean Jaurès, a philosopher and politician who came to socialism through republicanism, became the leading figure of an independent socialist group and argued that socialism was the logical extension of republican principles. The question of whether socialists should participate in bourgeois governments split the movement in 1899, when the socialist Alexandre Millerand entered Waldeck-Rousseau's cabinet alongside the general who had suppressed the Paris Commune. The Second International's congress at Amsterdam in 1904 condemned such participation and urged unity, which led to the formation in 1905 of the unified French Section of the Workers' International, known as the SFIO. Jaurès became its best-known leader, though Guesde's influence on the party's doctrine was strong.

The SFIO's electoral position grew steadily, winning around a hundred seats in 1914, but it operated in a political culture in which Radicals competed for the same voters and in which governing coalitions could be assembled on a variety of combinations. The party's official doctrine rejected cooperation with bourgeois parties, yet in practice, socialist deputies often supported Radical-led governments on specific issues, a tension that echoed debates elsewhere in Europe. France also had a strong tradition of revolutionary syndicalism, discussed in section 102.16, which regarded parliamentary politics with suspicion.

The French republic accommodated many competing mass organizations, including Radical committees, socialist federations, Catholic leagues, and nationalist movements. This accommodation was assisted by universal male suffrage and by the central role of the Chamber, but it left some groups outside, especially women, and left unresolved tensions over the relationship between the republic and its colonies. The French case shows that a parliamentary political culture could be both stable in its institutions and unstable in its governments.

Section 102.8

Political Parties and Mass Participation in Austria-Hungary, Italy, Scandinavia, and Russia

In the Habsburg monarchy, mass politics developed within a multinational state. The Austrian half of the monarchy, often called Cisleithania, contained Germans, Czechs, Poles, Ukrainians (then often called Ruthenians), Slovenes, Italians, and others, while the Hungarian half had its own constitution and parliament. In Austria, two parties that organized on a mass basis from the 1880s and 1890s were the Social Democrats, led by Victor Adler, who succeeded in uniting the movement in 1889 and who emphasized equal suffrage, and the Christian Social movement, led by Karl Lueger, who became mayor of Vienna in 1897. The Christian Socials combined Catholic institutions with an appeal to artisans, shopkeepers, and lower-middle-class voters, and Lueger used anti-liberal and, at times, antisemitic rhetoric, a subject that Chapter 103 examines. German nationalists formed a further current.

National politics was equally important. Czech parties built networks of associations, newspapers, and gymnastic societies, and competed for influence in Bohemia and Moravia, where language rights in schools and administration were contested. Poles in Galicia dominated provincial government and held considerable influence in Vienna. Croats, Slovenes, and other groups organized their own parties. The Social Democrats attempted to organize across nationalities, and reorganized in 1897 as a federation of national parties, but disputes between national sections eventually weakened the unity of the movement. Austrian politics thus combined class, religious, and national organization, and the government found it difficult to assemble stable parliamentary majorities. The reform of 1907 broadened the franchise, but it did not resolve those national conflicts.

In Italy, the political system after unification was dominated by Liberal parliamentarians who built shifting coalitions around notables and patronage. The practice, associated with Agostino Depretis, of absorbing opponents into governing majorities was sometimes called transformism. Outside the Liberal establishment, the Italian Socialist Party was founded in 1892 at Genoa, bringing together socialist and labor groups, and its strength lay among industrial workers in the north and landless laborers in parts of the Po valley. Catholics were constrained by the papal prohibition on participation in national elections, the non expedit, which was relaxed in practice in the early twentieth century and allowed Catholic voters to support Liberal candidates under the Gentiloni Pact of 1913. Giovanni Giolitti, who dominated government in the first decade of the twentieth century, tried to integrate moderate socialists and trade unions while retaining Liberal control. The 1912 franchise expansion brought a much larger and more rural electorate into politics.

In Scandinavia, parties and labor movements developed in a largely agrarian society with strong peasant institutions. Danish and Swedish farmers' parties, cooperative movements, and popular high schools of the Danish folk high school tradition gave rural populations organizational capacity. Social Democratic parties were established in Denmark in 1871, Sweden in 1889, and Norway in 1887, and alliances with liberal and agrarian parties helped win parliamentary reforms. The Scandinavian monarchies became increasingly parliamentary, with Norway, which was in a union with Sweden until 1905, moving toward parliamentary government in the 1880s. These developments show that labor movements could grow in relatively small, agrarian economies and that rural populations were political actors in their own right.

Russia's political environment was more restrictive. Until 1905, the empire had no national representative assembly, political parties were illegal, and the police suppressed strikes and organizations. The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party was founded in 1898 but held its meetings abroad or in secret, and its second congress in 1903 produced the division between Bolsheviks and Mensheviks, discussed in section 102.14. The Socialist Revolutionaries, who emphasized the peasantry, and various liberal groups also formed. After the strikes and uprisings of 1905, the October Manifesto promised civil liberties and a Duma, and political parties and trade unions were permitted to operate legally within limits. The Fundamental Laws of 1906 retained the tsar's authority over ministers, the military, and foreign policy, and the revised electoral law of 1907 favored landowners. Trade unions were legalized in 1906 but subject to police supervision and frequent closure. The period after 1905 saw real if constrained legal political activity, and also continuing repression.

The Grand Duchy of Finland, which belonged to the Russian Empire but had its own constitutional institutions, presented a striking contrast. Finland had been governed through a Diet of four estates, a body that represented nobles, clergy, burghers, and peasants. After the general strike of 1905, the Diet and the tsar accepted a Parliament Act in 1906, which replaced the estates with a unicameral Parliament of 200 members elected by universal and equal suffrage for men and women over the age of twenty-four, who could also stand for election. This was the most radical franchise reform in Europe, and it applied in an autonomous territory whose institutions were distinct from those of the wider empire. Russian policy would soon restrict Finnish autonomy, but the electoral reform and its consequences for women are examined in section 102.19.

The comparison among these states demonstrates that political organization developed differently in parliamentary systems, multinational empires, and autocracies. In the Habsburg lands, nationality and class intersected. In Italy, patronage and a restricted electorate shaped the early system, and later reform changed its social base. In Scandinavia, agrarian society and small states produced powerful reform coalitions. In Russia, an autocratic framework limited what organizations could do, and revolution forced partial concessions. Students should resist treating any one of these as a deviation from a Western norm, and should instead ask how each system's institutions framed the possibilities of mass participation.

  • Norway, with women's parliamentary suffrage by 1913, and Hungary, with its restrictive franchise within the Habsburg monarchy.
  • Major industrial and mining regions that were centers of union and party organization, including the Ruhr, Saxony, and Berlin in Germany, the north of England, South Wales, and Scotland in Britain, the Nord and Pas-de-Calais coalfield in France, Wallonia and Brussels in Belgium, Vienna and Bohemia in the Habsburg lands, and the industrial north of Italy.
  • Rural and agrarian regions where Catholic, peasant, or cooperative organization was strong, such as the Rhineland and Bavaria, Galicia, the Po valley, and Denmark.

Trade Unions, Industrial Conflict, and the Social Politics of Labor

How did workers organize collectively, confront employers and governments, and influence labor law and social insurance?

Section 102.9

Trade Unions and Collective Organization: From Skilled Trades to Mass Membership

Trade unions were older than mass political parties, and in many countries they developed independently of socialism. The earliest unions were organizations of skilled craftsmen, such as printers, engineers, carpenters, and cotton spinners, who controlled access to a trade through apprenticeship and who used their organization to maintain wages and conditions and to provide benefits to members in times of sickness, unemployment, or death. In Britain, these craft unions, sometimes called the "new model" unions of the 1850s and 1860s, collected substantial regular dues and employed full-time officials. They preferred negotiation to confrontation and kept strike action as a last resort. Their stability and funds made them durable institutions, but their membership was limited to relatively well-paid and skilled men.

Miners, railway workers, dockers, and textile workers developed their own organizations. Mining unions, such as the miners' associations of Durham, Northumberland, and South Wales, organized large numbers of men who shared a single employer and a common dependence on the colliery, and they became politically influential, in part because mining communities were relatively homogeneous and solidaristic. Railway workers formed unions that confronted large corporations, and transport workers in ports organized to deal with casual hiring. In Germany, the Free Trade Unions, linked to Social Democracy, were federated under the General Commission of the Trade Unions of Germany, led by Carl Legien from 1890. Rival unions were organized by liberal reformers, known as the Hirsch-Duncker unions, and by Christians, as discussed in section 102.12. In France, the law of 1884 legalized trade unions, and local labor exchanges, the bourses du travail, provided a framework for local union federations before the formation of the national CGT in 1895.

The British Trades Union Congress, which first met in Manchester in 1868, was a forum for unions across trades and regions to discuss common concerns and coordinate parliamentary pressure. Its early work focused on the legal position of unions, since the decade also saw legislation that determined whether union funds were protected and whether peaceful picketing was lawful. The Trade Union Act of 1871 gave unions legal recognition and protected their funds, while the Criminal Law Amendment Act of the same year restricted picketing, and the Conspiracy and Protection of Property Act of 1875 enlarged the legal space for strikes. In other European countries, the legal path was different. In Germany, unions were permitted but subject to police oversight and restrictions on political activity, and in Austria and Russia, they faced limits and repression for much of the period. The distinction between legal recognition and effective ability to organize is crucial, because unions could be legal and yet face dismissal of activists, blacklists, and employer hostility.

The late 1880s saw the rise of what contemporaries called the New Unionism in Britain, in which less-skilled and general workers formed large unions open to anyone in an industry or in no particular trade. The Gasworkers' and General Labourers' Union, led by Will Thorne, was founded in 1889, and the dockers' union emerged after the London dock strike of that year. These unions charged lower dues than craft unions, offered fewer benefits, and relied more on strikes and on political action. They were often led by socialists, such as Tom Mann, Ben Tillett, and John Burns, though many of their members were not socialists. Similar developments occurred elsewhere as unions extended to transport, general labor, and the new mass-production industries described in Chapter 101. By 1914, the Free Trade Unions in Germany claimed membership of roughly two and a half million, and British unions together claimed around four million members, though these figures are union claims and must be treated as approximate.

Unions faced barriers. Employers could refuse to recognize them, dismiss activists, or employ replacement workers. Governments sometimes treated unions as threats to public order. Occupational divisions separated skilled workers from the unskilled, and differences of religion and nationality divided workers within the same industry, as in Belgium, where Catholic and socialist unions competed, or in the Habsburg lands, where unions often divided along linguistic lines. Gender also shaped access. Many unions excluded women or confined them to separate sections, on the argument that women's lower wages threatened men's pay or that women's proper place was in the home. Even so, women organized in textile industries, in the clothing trades, and in some general unions, as the strikes discussed in the next section show. Migrant workers, such as Italian laborers in France and Germany or Polish workers in the Ruhr, often faced additional barriers of language and prejudice.

Unions served several functions. They bargained for wages, hours, and conditions. They offered mutual insurance through benefit funds for unemployment, sickness, and burial. They provided a forum for education and leadership training, and they served as a source of identity. Strike funds, accumulated through dues, allowed members to survive disputes. Full-time officials gave unions continuity and expertise, though some critics argued that officials became cautious and bureaucratic. The relation of unions to political parties varied, a point that returns in sections 102.14 and 102.16. In Britain, unions created the Labour Party, in Germany unions and the Social Democratic Party were closely allied but formally separate, and in France the CGT insisted on independence from parties.

Historical Vocabulary
Trade Unions and Collective Bargaining

A trade union is a permanent association of workers formed to protect and improve their conditions of employment. Collective bargaining is the process by which representatives of a group of workers negotiate wages, hours, and conditions with an employer or an employers' association, in contrast to individual workers negotiating separately. The term reflects the principle that a worker acting alone had little power against an employer, while workers acting together could, under the right conditions, negotiate on more equal terms.

These terms matter to this chapter because the legal recognition of unions and the practice of collective bargaining were gradual and contested, and they varied greatly from one country and industry to another. In many workplaces before 1914, employers refused to bargain with unions at all, and the word "union" did not always imply a formally recognized negotiating partner. A modern reader should not assume that a union in 1900 had the legal standing or bargaining rights associated with unions in the later twentieth century.

Central question: How did collective organization change the balance of power between individual workers and employers?
Section 102.10

Strikes, Labor Disputes, and the Struggle over Working Conditions

The strike, the collective withdrawal of labor, was the unions' principal means of exerting economic pressure, though most unions used it sparingly. Strikes arose from disputes about wages, working hours, dangerous conditions, harsh discipline, dismissals, and the recognition of unions. Some were brief and local. Others involved entire industries or regions. A distinction was drawn by contemporaries between economic strikes, aimed at employers, and political demonstrations, aimed at governments, though the line was often blurred, as when a strike for the eight-hour day sought to compel legislation. A general strike, in which workers in many industries stopped work at once, was attempted rarely and was the subject of heated debate, as section 102.16 shows.

Several disputes became symbols. In July 1888, women and girls who worked at the Bryant and May match factory in London's East End went on strike after the dismissal of a worker. The conditions of the work, including low pay, fines, and the health effects of phosphorus, had been publicized by the social reformer Annie Besant. The strikers won concessions and formed a union, and the dispute demonstrated that unskilled women could organize successfully. The London dock strike of August and September 1889 was larger. Dockers, who were hired by the day or even by the hour, demanded a wage of sixpence an hour, the "dockers' tanner," and an end to the casual system. The strike was led by Ben Tillett, John Burns, and Tom Mann, and it succeeded in part because of public sympathy, financial support that included contributions from Australian dockers, and the discipline of well-organized processions. It led to the formation of a durable dockers' union and prompted similar organization elsewhere.

In France, a dispute at Carmaux in the south in 1892 began when a mining company dismissed a miner, Jean-Baptiste Calvignac, who had been elected mayor of the town and who took time away from work for municipal duties. The miners struck in support, the government sent troops, and the case became a national question. Jean Jaurès, who represented the area as a deputy, took up the miners' cause, and the dispute helped establish his reputation as a defender of labor. The strike ended with an arbitration that went partly in the miners' favor. A very different event was the Courrières mine disaster of March 1906 in northern France, in which more than a thousand miners died, which set off a wave of strikes in the coalfield and in other industries, and prompted a government response that combined negotiation with the dispatch of troops.

The years from about 1911 to 1914, sometimes called the period of the labor unrest, saw a surge of industrial conflict in several countries. In Britain, strikes by seamen, dockers, railwaymen, and miners in 1911 and 1912 paralyzed parts of the economy and brought troops into some cities, and in 1913, the Dublin lockout saw employers in the Irish capital attempt to prevent their workers from joining the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union, led by James Larkin, with severe hardship for working-class families. Strike waves occurred in Germany, particularly in mining and shipbuilding, in France, in Italy, and in Russia, where strikes rose sharply after 1912. These disputes reflected rising prices, the growth of mass unions, and the confidence of organized labor, but they also revealed the limits of union power.

Employers responded to strikes in several ways. They dismissed strikers, imposed lockouts in which they closed workplaces to break union resistance, hired replacement workers, formed employers' associations to coordinate resistance and share information, and used blacklists. In some countries, private guards or police were employed, and governments sometimes deployed troops. In other cases, employers negotiated, or accepted arbitration, particularly when disputes threatened large industrial or public interests. The state's role varied, from relative neutrality in some British disputes to forceful intervention in parts of the continent. The Fourmies shooting of May 1891, mentioned in the chapter opening, shows how demonstration and repression could escalate into violence.

For strikers, the costs were considerable. Wages stopped, and households depended on savings, credit from local shopkeepers, strike pay from union funds, help from relatives, and charity. Community support could be decisive, because local traders, landlords, and neighbors could prolong or shorten a dispute. Women ran households under strain, and they took part in picketing and relief activities. Children could be affected by hunger. A defeated strike could lead to blacklisting, loss of homes owned by employers, and the collapse of a union. Some disputes succeeded, while many did not, and the outcome depended on conditions that included the state of the labor market, the unity of the workforce, the financial reserves of unions and employers, public opinion, and government attitudes. The historian should therefore avoid the assumption that collective action invariably produced improvements, and should note that even defeated strikes could leave organizations, memories, and solidarities that shaped later conflicts.

Central question: Under what conditions could organized workers successfully challenge employers, and what risks did industrial conflict involve?
Section 102.11

Labor Legislation, Social Insurance, and the State's Response to Working-Class Pressure

European states in the nineteenth century had intervened in industrial employment before 1870, notably by limiting the hours of children and women in factories, as earlier chapters have shown. In the late nineteenth century, intervention grew in scope and ambition. Governments passed laws on factory inspection, workplace safety, hours of work, and, increasingly, on insurance against the risks that threatened working-class households: sickness, accident, disability, old age, and unemployment. The motives were mixed. Some officials were moved by humanitarian concern or by the findings of social investigators. Others were concerned about the health of military recruits, the loss of productivity, or the political threat of discontent. The history of social insurance is therefore inseparable from the history of labor politics.

Germany led the way. In an imperial message of November 1881, Emperor William I, in a text prepared by Bismarck, said that the remedy for social disorder lay not only in repression of socialist excesses but also in positive measures for the welfare of workers. The Reichstag passed three major laws. The Sickness Insurance Law of 1883 required most industrial workers to belong to insurance funds, financed by contributions of workers, who paid two thirds, and employers, who paid one third. The Accident Insurance Law of 1884 was financed by employers through trade associations. The Old Age and Disability Insurance Law of 1889, which took effect in 1891, provided pensions funded by equal contributions from employers and workers and by a state subsidy, with old-age pensions payable from the age of seventy. Benefits were modest, and in the early years many workers did not live to receive an old-age pension or received small amounts. Later legislation consolidated the system in the Imperial Insurance Code of 1911.

Bismarck's intentions were political as well as social. He hoped to bind workers to the state, to reduce the appeal of Social Democracy, and to establish state insurance rather than leaving provision to private or voluntary bodies. He combined these laws with the Anti-Socialist Law, a policy often described as carrot and stick. The results did not match his expectations. The Social Democrats continued to gain votes, and workers who benefited from insurance did not necessarily abandon socialism. At the same time, the insurance system created a large administrative apparatus, including local sickness funds in which workers participated, and many socialists and trade unionists later took part in running these funds, which gave them administrative experience and organizational resources.

Britain moved later and along a different path. Before 1906, British support for the poor rested largely on the Poor Law, which was stigmatized, and on voluntary organizations such as friendly societies. After the Liberal landslide of 1906, the government of Herbert Asquith, with David Lloyd George as Chancellor of the Exchequer, introduced the Old Age Pensions Act of 1908, which gave a weekly pension to people over seventy, subject to a means test and to character requirements. It was funded from general taxation, not from contributions. The National Insurance Act of 1911 had two parts. One provided health insurance for workers in a range of occupations, financed by contributions from workers, employers, and the state, and the other provided unemployment insurance in particular trades. The health scheme was administered through approved societies, including friendly societies and trade unions, which gave those organizations a stake in the system. Doctors, insurance companies, and some workers opposed various features, and the scheme excluded many dependents, including most married women who were not in paid employment.

France and other states developed their own provisions. France passed an accident law in 1898, limited the hours of work of women and children, introduced a weekly rest law in 1906, and in 1910 adopted a retirement pensions law for workers and peasants that relied on contributions and was only partly effective because many workers did not participate. Belgium, Austria, Italy, and Scandinavian states introduced various insurance laws before 1914. The patterns differed, but in each case, governments were responding to a mix of pressure from labor movements, the examples of neighboring states, and calculations about order and national strength.

The simultaneous use of repression, surveillance, labor regulation, and welfare provision shows that states did not choose between conciliation and coercion. Police continued to monitor unions and socialist organizations while governments legislated insurance. Insurance schemes imposed contributions and administrative conditions that many workers resented, and benefits were often less generous than advocates claimed. Yet insurance also established the principle that the state bore some responsibility for economic security, and it created institutions that outlasted the regimes that built them. The interpretation of the purpose of social insurance remains debated, between those who stress its role in reducing political threat and those who emphasize its response to genuine needs and to democratic pressure, and the evidence suggests that both motives operated.

Section 102.12

Cooperatives, Mutual Aid, Religious Labor Movements, and the Diversity of Working-Class Organization

Trade unions and parties were only part of a wider world of working-class association. Friendly societies, which collected small regular payments to provide benefits in sickness and death, had existed for generations and were among the largest voluntary organizations in Britain. Mutual aid societies in France, Belgium, and the German states served similar functions. These organizations provided security, social contact, and a measure of self-respect, and they embodied a tradition of self-help that differed from both socialism and state provision. They also had a different relationship to politics. Many were officially nonpolitical, and their members held a range of political views.

Consumer cooperatives, in which members purchased goods from a store they collectively owned and shared the profits, expanded greatly. The model of the Rochdale Pioneers of 1844 had spread across Britain, where the Co-operative Wholesale Society, founded in 1863, supplied local societies and became a major commercial enterprise. Cooperative stores helped working-class households by providing reliable goods at fair prices and by paying a dividend based on purchases, which acted as a form of savings. Cooperatives were widespread in Belgium, where socialist cooperatives such as the Vooruit in Ghent and the Maison du Peuple in Brussels financed party activities, in Germany, in Denmark, and in many other countries. Producer cooperatives, in which workers collectively owned their workplace, were less common, and credit cooperatives, associated with Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen and Hermann Schulze-Delitzsch in Germany, were important for farmers and artisans. Cooperatives thus connected the household economy with collective organization, though they were not always aligned with socialism.

Religion shaped working-class organization profoundly. The Catholic Church's response to industrial society was articulated in the encyclical Rerum Novarum, issued by Pope Leo XIII in May 1891. The encyclical defended private property and criticized socialism, but it also asserted that workers had a right to just wages and decent conditions, that employers had duties toward their workers, and that workers might form associations to protect themselves. It encouraged Catholic social action as an alternative to socialist organization. Its influence was long lasting, and it provided a framework for Catholic labor movements, which developed in Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, France, Italy, Austria, and elsewhere. In Germany, Christian trade unions were established in the 1890s, bringing together Catholic and some Protestant workers and competing with the Free Trade Unions. In Belgium, Christian unions grew within a dense Catholic network of parishes, mutual aid societies, and banks. Protestant labor movements and the nonconformist traditions that influenced British labor represent related, though different, religious currents.

The Catholic labor movement should not be treated merely as a defensive reaction to socialism. It offered its members organization, education, and a voice, and its priorities, including family welfare, moral standards, and protection of religion, were not identical to those of the socialists. Catholic workers could be strongly committed to both their church and to better pay, and their choice of union often reflected local religious and social ties rather than a calculated political decision. The competition between Christian and socialist unions sometimes weakened workers' bargaining power, but it also expanded the number of workers organized.

The influence of occupation, nationality, gender, and locality on organizational loyalty was strong. A miner in Wales, a textile worker in Lancashire, a Polish worker in the Ruhr, a Catholic railway worker in Bavaria, and a Czech factory worker in Prague had different networks and priorities. Nationality divided workers in the Habsburg monarchy and in border regions, and in some places, workers' organizations adopted nationalist positions. Women workers often organized in separate associations or auxiliary sections. Local conditions, such as the dominance of one employer in a company town, shaped what organization was possible.

Workers' cultural life offered another dimension. Working-class clubs, choirs, gymnastic societies, cycling clubs, libraries, and educational societies sustained collective identity and provided recreation and instruction. In Germany, the Social Democratic milieu included the Free Choral Societies, workers' gymnastic and cycling associations, and educational programs, and the movement's libraries and lectures encouraged self-education. In Britain, working men's clubs, brass bands, and cooperative guilds served similar purposes, and in Belgium, Houses of the People combined cafes, meeting rooms, and cooperatives. These institutions allowed members to live a large part of their lives within a particular milieu, but they also reinforced divisions among milieus, which has led historians to speak of subcultures that were relatively separate from one another.

For these reasons, the working class cannot be treated as a politically homogeneous group. Workers differed in occupation, religion, nationality, gender, skill, and locality, and they created multiple kinds of organizations to serve different needs. These organizations sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed, and their variety was a source of strength, giving workers many forms of collective experience, as well as a source of weakness, preventing the creation of a single unified working-class movement. This diversity should be kept in mind in the discussion of socialist organization that follows, since socialist parties claimed to speak for the working class as a whole while actually representing particular parts of it.

Central question: Why did European workers create multiple kinds of organizations rather than uniting behind one political movement?

Section 102.13

German Social Democracy: From the Gotha Program to the Erfurt Program

German socialism began as two separate organizations. In 1863, Ferdinand Lassalle, a lawyer and publicist, founded the General German Workers' Association in Leipzig. Lassalle argued that workers should organize as an independent political party, win universal suffrage, and use the state to establish producers' cooperatives funded by state credit. He was killed in a duel in 1864, but the association continued. In 1869, at Eisenach, August Bebel, a master turner, and Wilhelm Liebknecht, a journalist who had known Marx and Engels, founded the Social Democratic Workers' Party, which was closer to Marxism and to the international workers' movement. The two groups competed, differed over attitudes toward Prussia and the newly founded empire, and were divided over the role of the state. These disagreements were connected to deeper strategic questions that the movement would repeatedly face.

In May 1875, the two organizations met at Gotha and merged to form the Socialist Workers' Party of Germany. The compromise program that they adopted, which the Primary Source Window below examines, combined Lassallean demands for state-aided producers' cooperatives with a general statement about the right of workers to the full product of their labor, and a list of practical demands for democratic rights and social reform. Karl Marx, in a private critique circulated among a few colleagues, attacked its theoretical weakness and its concessions to Lassallean ideas, although his critique was not published until 1891. The incident shows that the leading theorist of socialism did not control the movement and that the party's leaders, who were practically concerned with unity, were willing to accept programmatic compromise.

The party grew in the later 1870s, but its position changed abruptly in October 1878 with the Anti-Socialist Law, discussed in section 102.6. The law banned party organizations, meetings, and publications, and authorities expelled hundreds of socialists from their homes in cities declared to be under a state of minor siege. The party leadership dispersed, some of it in exile in Zurich and London, and the party's newspaper was produced abroad and smuggled into Germany. At home, socialists maintained contact through informal associations, social clubs, and trusted individuals, and they continued to contest Reichstag elections. The party's organizational method during this period, with its emphasis on discipline, secrecy, and trusted local intermediaries, left a durable imprint. Its vote rose over the twelve years of the law, and the experience gave the movement a collective memory of persecution and a conviction that history was on its side.

After the law expired in 1890, the party reorganized openly. At a congress at Halle in 1890, it adopted the name Social Democratic Party of Germany. At Erfurt in October 1891, it adopted a new program that replaced the Gotha Program. The first, theoretical part of the Erfurt Program, drafted by Karl Kautsky, set out a Marxist analysis of capitalist development, the concentration of capital, the proletarianization of small producers, and the inevitability of class struggle leading to socialism. The second part, largely the work of Eduard Bernstein, listed immediate practical demands, including universal, equal, direct suffrage for men and women over twenty, the secret ballot, an eight-hour working day, the prohibition of child labor, protection for workers, and the right of association. The program thus combined a long-term revolutionary perspective with a program for reform within existing society.

This combination was characteristic of German Social Democracy and was the source of both its strength and its tensions. The party's theory predicted a coming transformation, while its daily activity involved electoral campaigns, parliamentary debate, trade unions, cooperatives, newspapers, and educational and cultural associations. Historians have long debated how far the party's practice had become reformist even while its doctrine remained revolutionary. By 1914 the party had more than a million members, a press with dozens of daily newspapers, a large paid staff, and the support of the Free Trade Unions, whose leaders were often more concerned with practical gains than with theory. The party's size created its own interests, including the preservation of its organization, and some critics, notably the sociologist Robert Michels, argued in 1911 that the party's leadership had become oligarchic, a point returned to in section 102.20.

The German Social Democrats also commanded international prestige. Because they were the largest socialist party and had survived repression, they became a model for other movements, and the Erfurt Program was widely imitated. Their leading figures, Bebel and Kautsky, had great influence in the Second International. Yet the party remained excluded from government and, in the Reichstag, was usually isolated, a situation that fostered internal debate about whether to participate in coalition governments, support budgets, and cooperate with bourgeois parties. The next sections examine how these debates shaped socialism across Europe.

Section 102.14

Socialist Parties across Europe: British Laborism, French Socialism, and Continental Organization

The German model influenced socialists elsewhere, but it was never simply copied. Socialist and labor parties emerged in nearly every industrializing state, and each reflected its country's political institutions, religious divisions, and labor traditions. A useful contrast is between the German Social Democrats, who adopted an explicitly Marxist program, and the British Labour Party, whose original constitution contained no socialist objective and whose strength derived from trade union affiliation. British Laborism was shaped by nonconformist religion, by the tradition of cooperative and friendly society self-help, and by a political system in which an independent party needed to win seats in single-member constituencies. It focused on the legal position of unions, on social reform, and on parliamentary representation, and it drew only partly on Marxist theory.

Within Britain, the Independent Labour Party of Keir Hardie, the Fabian Society, and the Social Democratic Federation represented different socialist currents. The ILP combined ethical and religious appeals with electoral activity and was the most important organizational base for socialists in the Labour Representation Committee. The Fabians, who included Sidney and Beatrice Webb and George Bernard Shaw, believed that socialism could be advanced through research, administrative reform, and permeation of existing parties. The SDF followed a more doctrinaire Marxism, but its influence was limited, and it eventually left the Labour alliance. The result was that British socialism was a minority current within a broader labor movement whose priorities were set largely by the unions.

French socialism, as section 102.7 showed, was divided among rival schools until the unification of 1905. Jules Guesde championed a disciplined Marxist party, and Jean Jaurès, a former Radical, argued that socialists should work with republicans to defend and extend the republic while holding to the goal of a socialist society. The Millerand affair of 1899, in which a socialist joined a bourgeois cabinet, intensified the division over ministerialism. The Amsterdam congress of the Second International in 1904 resolved that socialists should not participate in bourgeois governments and urged unity, which pushed French socialists toward the SFIO, formed at the Paris congress of April 1905. The SFIO's attitude toward the Radicals remained ambiguous, and its position was complicated by the strength of a union movement that was suspicious of parties.

Socialist parties emerged in Belgium, where the Belgian Workers' Party was founded in 1885 and linked to cooperatives and mutual societies. In Austria, Victor Adler's Social Democrats built a party that combined a Marxist program with practical campaigns for suffrage, and in Italy, the Socialist Party, founded in 1892, wrestled with differences between reformists and revolutionaries and between the industrial north and the agrarian south. In Scandinavia, parties were established in the 1870s and 1880s and developed close ties with unions and, at times, with liberal and agrarian parties. In the Netherlands, Spain, and elsewhere, further parties and unions developed, with Spain also notable for a strong anarchist movement. The variety shows that socialism was adapted to different national circumstances, and that it was not a single doctrine applied uniformly.

The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party was founded in March 1898 at a small congress in Minsk, though most of its leaders were arrested soon after. The party's second congress in 1903, held first in Brussels and then in London, took up questions of organization. Vladimir Lenin argued for a tightly disciplined party of professional revolutionaries, with strict criteria of membership, while Julius Martov favored a broader definition of membership. In a vote on the party's leadership, Lenin's supporters won a majority and were called Bolsheviks, from the Russian word for majority, and their opponents became Mensheviks, from the word for minority. The names stuck even though the balance of support shifted. This chapter does not trace the later development of these factions, which belong to Chapter 111, but it is important to note that in 1914 the division was an internal quarrel within a clandestine party operating under autocracy, not yet the foundation of a ruling regime.

Despite their differences, socialist parties shared methods. They contested elections, campaigned for suffrage, published newspapers and periodicals, ran educational programs, held congresses at which delegates debated policy, and built local associations that provided social life, support, and a sense of belonging. They also shared a language of class conflict, solidarity, and progress, though different parties interpreted these words differently. They aimed at representing the working class, but they were often led by intellectuals, journalists, and professional politicians as well as by workers, and the composition of their memberships and electorates varied.

The variety of organizations answers the central question. Socialist movements developed different structures and strategies because they operated under different electoral systems, religious divisions, legal frameworks, and labor traditions. Where unions were strong and parliamentary representation promised results, as in Britain, labor politics took a pragmatic form. Where parties were persecuted yet allowed to contest elections, as in Germany, they built disciplined organizations and embraced a doctrine of historical inevitability. Where the republic gave parties openings but the Radicals competed for the same voters, as in France, socialists were drawn toward both cooperation and independence. Where autocracy prohibited open politics, as in Russia, parties operated underground and gave priority to conspiratorial organization.

Central question: Why did European socialist movements develop different political organizations and strategies despite their common concern with class inequality?
Section 102.15

The Second International, May Day, and the Possibilities of International Working-Class Solidarity

The First International, founded in London in 1864 with Marx's participation, collapsed in the 1870s amid conflicts between Marxists and anarchists and after the repression that followed the Paris Commune. By the late 1880s, national socialist parties and trade unions had grown strong enough to attempt a new international organization. In July 1889, to coincide with the centenary of the French Revolution, rival congresses met in Paris, one organized by the Possibilists and another, which was larger and more influential, supported by the German Social Democrats and the Marxist socialists. The Marxist congress is considered the founding congress of the Second International. It was a loose association of national parties and unions, with no binding authority, which held congresses every few years, and from 1900, maintained an International Socialist Bureau in Brussels to coordinate communication.

The congress of 1889 passed a resolution calling for an international demonstration, to take place on a single day in all countries, demanding that governments introduce a legal eight-hour working day. The date chosen was May 1, 1890. The choice reflected an American precedent, since American labor organizations had agitated for an eight-hour day and had called for action on May 1, 1886, in a campaign that included the Haymarket events in Chicago. The demonstrations of 1890 varied by country, as the chapter opening showed. Labor organizations in some countries held marches or rallies on the day, while elsewhere the demonstration was moved to the following Sunday, or took the form of a work stoppage. Subsequent congresses debated whether May Day should be a day of work stoppage, which was the aim of French and some other delegates, or a demonstration outside working hours, which German and British trade unionists preferred because of the risk of dismissal. The result was a compromise that left decisions to national organizations. May Day became an annual occasion on which workers marched, wore symbols, listened to speeches, and sang songs, and it created a shared calendar of labor politics across Europe.

International congresses, held at Brussels in 1891, Zurich in 1893, London in 1896, Paris in 1900, Amsterdam in 1904, Stuttgart in 1907, and Copenhagen in 1910, debated resolutions on suffrage, labor legislation, the general strike, colonial policy, militarism, and the unity of national parties. The 1896 London congress excluded the anarchists, which settled that the Second International would be a body for parliamentary and trade-union socialists. Delegates also developed cross-border relations among unions, socialist newspapers, and parties, exchanged information, and supported strikes through financial contributions. The International provided a forum in which leaders of small and persecuted parties could meet leaders of large parties, and it gave socialists a sense of belonging to a worldwide movement.

The International was divided over several questions. On colonialism, the Stuttgart congress of 1907 saw a majority narrowly reject a resolution that would have accepted a socialist colonial policy, and the debate showed that some socialists, particularly reformists in the larger parties, did not reject empire as such, while others condemned colonial exploitation. On women's political participation, the International supported women's suffrage as part of a demand for universal suffrage, though practical support varied. On the parliamentary question, the congress of 1904 condemned participation in bourgeois governments. On militarism, the Stuttgart resolution of 1907, which was amended following the proposals of Rosa Luxemburg, Lenin, and Martov, stated that socialists should work to prevent war and, if war broke out, to use the crisis to bring about the end of capitalist rule. The wording was broad enough for all sides to accept and left the question of what exactly to do unresolved.

International cooperation was limited by language, by the independent interests of national parties, and by differences in legal systems. Congresses relied on translation and on delegates from different traditions who did not always understand each other's context. The International had no power to compel its member parties, and each party responded primarily to its own electorate and legal environment. The German Social Democrats, whose delegation was the largest and whose resources were greatest, were often decisive, which led some other parties to resent German dominance. Language barriers and the cost of travel meant that many rank-and-file workers had little direct contact with the International, which was largely led by officials and intellectuals.

Rising international tension in the early twentieth century tested the proclaimed solidarity. The International debated what to do in the face of imperial rivalries, arms races, and the Balkan wars of 1912 and 1913, and an extraordinary congress in Basel in November 1912 issued a manifesto that denounced the danger of war and pledged the workers' parties to oppose it. Chapter 107 examines the diplomatic crises themselves. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that socialist internationalism coexisted with national loyalties. Leaders of large parties, who represented millions of voters and administered large organizations, were aware that their members were also citizens of states and, in many cases, believed their countries faced external threats. The responses of the socialist parties to the outbreak of war in 1914 revealed the limits of the International, but those events lie beyond this chapter.

Central question: How did international socialist cooperation develop, and why did national differences continue to limit working-class solidarity?
Section 102.16

Reform or Revolution? Revisionism, Syndicalism, and the Strategic Divisions of European Socialism

The strategic question that divided socialists was whether the goal of socialism should be pursued through participation in existing institutions or through the overthrow of those institutions. The practical experience of large parties, which were winning votes, building organizations, and obtaining reforms, raised the question with increasing urgency. If the existing political system permitted workers to organize and to elect representatives, and if reforms brought improvements, did the prediction of a revolutionary collapse of capitalism remain credible? The question was posed most sharply in Germany, where the party's doctrine was most explicitly revolutionary and its practice most clearly parliamentary.

Eduard Bernstein, who had co-written the practical part of the Erfurt Program and had spent years in exile in London where he encountered British labor politics and the Fabians, published a series of articles in the 1890s and in 1899 a book usually known in English as Evolutionary Socialism. He argued that capitalism was not collapsing in the way that Marx's predictions had suggested, that the middle classes were not disappearing, that the working class was gaining from reforms, and that the party's actual practice was reformist. He concluded that the party should recognize this and concentrate on democratic reform, trade unions, and cooperatives, and that the final goal mattered less than the movement. His arguments came to be called revisionism.

The party leadership rejected revisionism formally. Karl Kautsky, the party's leading theorist, defended the Marxist analysis, arguing that capitalism's contradictions would deepen and that the party should prepare for the moment when it would take power, without provoking premature confrontation. Rosa Luxemburg, a Polish-born socialist active in the German and Polish movements, published a critique of Bernstein in 1899, entitled Reform or Revolution, in which she argued that reforms could not by themselves abolish capitalism, and that the party's purpose was to use reforms to build the strength of the working class for a revolutionary transformation. Party congresses at Hanover in 1899 and Dresden in 1903 condemned revisionist positions. Yet in practice, many party leaders and trade unionists, notably in the South German state parliaments and the trade unions, pursued reformist policies, and the party's behavior toward the budget and cooperation with other parties was often cautious. The gap between doctrine and practice persisted.

A related debate concerned the mass strike. After the Russian revolution of 1905, in which strikes played a central role, Luxemburg and others argued that the mass strike could be a means of political struggle, while many trade-union leaders, who feared the risk to union funds and organization, opposed it. The 1905 party congress at Jena accepted the mass strike in principle as a defensive weapon, and the following year, an agreement at Mannheim between party and union leaders recognized the independence of the unions in trade matters and the necessity of consultation on political action. The episode confirmed the growing influence of the union leadership within the movement.

In France, a different current emerged. Revolutionary syndicalism, which was influenced by the thinking of Fernand Pelloutier, who developed the bourses du travail, and by intellectuals including Georges Sorel, held that unions, not parties, were the vehicles of working-class emancipation. The CGT, formed at Limoges in 1895, brought together local labor exchanges and trade federations. The syndicalists favored direct action, including strikes, boycotts, and sabotage, and regarded the general strike as the means by which workers would take over the economy. They viewed parliamentary politics as a distraction that divided workers and corrupted leaders. At the CGT congress of Amiens in 1906, delegates adopted a statement, the Charter of Amiens, that asserted the union's role in both the daily defense of workers' interests and the preparation for the complete emancipation of workers through expropriation of capitalists, and that declared the independence of the union from parties and sects. Individual members were free to take part in political life outside the union, but the union itself was not to be tied to any party.

In practice, syndicalism had a smaller membership than moderate unions, and the CGT's influence in the labor movement and in strikes fluctuated. Syndicalist ideas also appeared in Italy, Spain, and elsewhere, and in Britain, syndicalist influences were visible in the labor unrest of 1911 to 1914. Anarchists, who rejected the state and parliamentary politics entirely, were a separate current. Some used violence, as in a series of assassinations in the 1890s, including that of the French president Sadi Carnot in 1894, and their actions were used by governments to justify repression, but most anarchists in unions and communities were not advocates of terrorism. Anarchism was particularly strong in Spain and parts of Italy, and the International's exclusion of anarchists in 1896 defined mainstream socialism as electoral and trade-unionist.

The different strategies rested on different assumptions about political power and social transformation. Reformists assumed that democratic institutions could be used to achieve social change, and that the state was not simply an instrument of the propertied classes. Orthodox Marxists assumed that capitalism's contradictions would produce a crisis that would permit workers to seize power, and that parties should prepare for that moment. Revolutionary socialists such as Luxemburg gave more weight to the mobilization of masses. Syndicalists believed that the workplace, not parliament, was the decisive arena. Each position had empirical support, since parliaments did deliver reforms, since capitalism did experience crises, since mass strikes did exert pressure, and since many workers distrusted politicians. Each also faced difficulties. Reformists risked losing a critical stance, orthodox Marxists risked inaction, and syndicalists faced the limits of union organization. The strategic divisions would intensify after 1914, but the issues were already clearly defined.

Central question: Could socialism be achieved through parliamentary reform, or did its supporters need to transform existing institutions more fundamentally?

Section 102.17

Women's Movements: Education, Employment, Legal Rights, and Organized Feminism

Women's political organization in the late nineteenth century did not begin with the demand for the vote. It grew out of a range of campaigns about education, employment, property, marriage law, and public morality, which together challenged the assumption that women's place was exclusively in the household. The word "feminism" came into use in French and then in other European languages in the 1890s, though women had long organized under other labels. Historians such as Karen Offen have emphasized that European feminisms were plural, shaped by different national laws, religious cultures, and understandings of equality, and that they should not be reduced to a single template derived from Britain or the United States.

Education was a primary demand. Secondary and higher education for girls was limited in 1870, and campaigners argued that women needed access to schools and universities both for their own development and to qualify for professions. In Britain, Girton College, Cambridge, was founded in 1869, and by the end of the century women attended universities in many countries, though often without being allowed to receive degrees. In France, the Camille Sée law of 1880 established state secondary schools for girls. Women gained admission to universities in Switzerland and the Netherlands relatively early, and women from the Russian Empire, who were barred at home, studied in Zurich and elsewhere. Women's access to professions such as medicine, teaching, and, much later, law, was opposed by male professional bodies, and each opening required persistent campaigning.

Legal and property rights were another focus. In many states, married women had limited control over their property and earnings and were subject to their husbands' legal authority. In Britain, Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 gave married women rights over their earnings and property. In France, the Napoleonic Civil Code kept married women in a position of legal incapacity, and reforms came slowly, including a law of 1907 that gave married women control of their own earnings. In Germany, the civil code that took effect in 1900 left husbands with considerable authority over wives, a source of protest from the organized women's movement. Campaigns also addressed custody of children, divorce, and the sexual double standard. In Britain, the campaign led by Josephine Butler against the Contagious Diseases Acts, which subjected women suspected of prostitution to compulsory examination, led to their repeal in 1886 and was an early example of women's mass organization on a moral and legal question.

Women's organizations took many forms. In Germany, Louise Otto-Peters and others founded the General German Women's Association in 1865, and in 1894, a federation of women's associations, the Federation of German Women's Associations, was established, led by moderate middle-class women such as Helene Lange and Gertrud Bäumer. Their emphasis was on education, employment, and social work, and the federation avoided the suffrage question until the early twentieth century. A more radical wing, including Anita Augspurg and Lida Gustava Heymann, demanded the vote. In France, Hubertine Auclert campaigned for women's suffrage from the 1870s, while the National Council of French Women, formed in 1901, brought together moderate societies. In Scandinavia, associations such as the Danish Women's Society of 1871 and the Fredrika Bremer Association in Sweden, founded in 1884, combined reform of women's legal status with philanthropy. International bodies appeared, including the International Council of Women in 1888 and, in 1904, the International Woman Suffrage Alliance, founded at Berlin.

The women involved held a variety of goals. Middle-class feminists often stressed equal rights in education, employment, and law, while some argued that women's special qualities as mothers made them suitable for public roles in welfare and education. Working-class women, who in most countries already worked for wages, often prioritized pay, conditions, protective legislation, and the cost of living, and in some cases found that middle-class reformers did not understand their concerns. Socialist women, such as Clara Zetkin in Germany, argued that women's emancipation depended on the overthrow of capitalism and criticized bourgeois feminism for neglecting class. Religious reformers, including Protestant and Catholic women's groups, combined concern for social welfare with moral campaigns, and advocates of maternal welfare, such as the Swedish writer Ellen Key, emphasized the protection of motherhood. These approaches sometimes complemented one another and sometimes conflicted.

Disagreements centered on marriage, motherhood, and employment. Some activists argued that married women should have the right to work, and others that mothers should be supported to remain at home, and unions and many male socialists feared that women's wage labor would depress men's wages. Disagreements over whether women should demand the same rights as men or special protections that recognized their different circumstances recurred in debates over protective labor laws. The relation of women's interests to class politics was contested, as socialist parties formally supported women's equality yet often neglected it in practice, and women's movements that were led by middle-class women sometimes distanced themselves from socialism.

Women participated in politics well before they could vote. They campaigned in elections, circulated petitions, ran newspapers, organized strikes, and sat on school boards and poor law boards where the law allowed. In some countries, women could vote in local elections earlier than in national elections, as in Britain, where some women had municipal voting rights from 1869. But in many states, laws restricted women's participation in political associations. In Prussia and several other German states, and in Austria, women were banned from joining political organizations until reforms in 1908 and 1918 respectively, which obliged many groups to adopt non-political labels. The organized women's movement was therefore broader than the suffrage movement: voting rights were central to its demands, but women also sought education, legal autonomy, economic opportunity, and a public voice.

Central question: Why did women establish independent political and social organizations, and how did different understandings of equality shape their objectives?
Section 102.18

The British Women's Suffrage Movement: Suffragists, Suffragettes, and Political Militancy

The British campaign for women's suffrage began in earnest in the 1860s. In 1866, a petition for women's votes was presented to Parliament, and in 1867, John Stuart Mill's amendment to the Second Reform Bill to give votes to women was defeated, though it attracted a significant minority. Local suffrage societies were formed, and for decades, campaigners presented petitions, lobbied members of Parliament, and drafted bills that were repeatedly introduced and defeated, sometimes after receiving a majority on a second reading. The movement was divided and often small, and many politicians who sympathized in principle did not give the question priority. During the 1890s, there was growth in activity, and in 1897, the various constitutional societies united in the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies (NUWSS) under the leadership of Millicent Garrett Fawcett.

The NUWSS pursued suffrage by constitutional means. It organized petitions, public meetings, educational campaigns, publications, and lobbying of MPs and candidates, and, in February 1907, it held a large procession in London, known as the Mud March because of the weather, to demonstrate support. Its membership was largely middle class, but it drew in women from many backgrounds, and in Lancashire, activists including Selina Cooper and Esther Roper worked with women cotton workers and with trade unions. The NUWSS argued that women were already taxpayers, workers, and citizens, and that the vote would permit them to protect their own interests and to contribute to public life. In 1912, the NUWSS formed an alliance with the Labour Party through an election fighting fund, supporting Labour candidates because the party endorsed women's suffrage.

In October 1903, in Manchester, Emmeline Pankhurst, a widow with a background in the Independent Labour Party, and her daughters Christabel and Sylvia founded the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU). The organization's motto, "Deeds, not words," expressed frustration with the slow progress of constitutional methods. Its early tactics included interrupting meetings of Liberal politicians, and in 1905, Christabel Pankhurst and Annie Kenney were arrested after disrupting a meeting at Manchester's Free Trade Hall at which Sir Edward Grey was speaking, an event often seen as the beginning of the militant campaign. In 1906, a journalist at the Daily Mail used the word "suffragettes" to describe them, intending to belittle, but the WSPU adopted the term. The WSPU moved its headquarters to London, held large demonstrations such as Women's Sunday in Hyde Park in June 1908, and developed a distinctive visual culture with its colors of purple, white, and green, banners, badges, and newspapers. Its supporters included many working women as well as the wealthy.

Militant tactics escalated over time. Suffragettes were arrested for breaking windows, obstructing the police, and refusing to pay taxes. From 1909, imprisoned suffragettes went on hunger strike to demand treatment as political prisoners, and the authorities responded by forcible feeding, a painful and dangerous procedure that attracted public protest. In April 1913, the government passed the Prisoners (Temporary Discharge for Ill Health) Act, known as the Cat and Mouse Act, under which weakened hunger strikers were released and rearrested after recovery. From 1912, the WSPU turned to arson and the destruction of property, including attacks on empty buildings and pillar boxes. In June 1913, Emily Wilding Davison, who had been imprisoned several times, died after being struck by the king's horse at the Epsom Derby, and her funeral was a major demonstration. Historians continue to argue over the intentions of Davison's action, which cannot be established with certainty from the surviving evidence.

The movement was not defined only by the Pankhursts. Annie Kenney, a former cotton mill worker, became a leading organizer, and many women in local WSPU branches carried out the day-to-day work. Differences within the WSPU led to a split in 1907, when Charlotte Despard, Teresa Billington-Greig, and others left to found the Women's Freedom League, which favored militant but nonviolent methods and advocated a more democratic internal structure. Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst focused increasingly on a campaign against the Liberal government and on direct militant action, and in 1913 and 1914, Sylvia Pankhurst, who disagreed with this direction and was committed to working-class politics, led the East London Federation of Suffragettes, which organized working-class women in the East End. Other groups included the Men's League for Women's Suffrage and numerous specialized societies of professional women and women in various religions and regions.

The effect of militancy was contested then and is contested now. Supporters argued that militant action brought the issue to public attention and forced politicians to respond, while critics, including many constitutional suffragists, argued that violence alienated potential allies and gave opponents an excuse to delay. The government alternated between negotiation and repression. In 1910 and 1911, parliamentary committees of MPs from different parties drafted Conciliation Bills to grant a limited female franchise, but these failed, in part because of differences over the franchise they would create and because of the government's reluctance. By 1914, the WSPU's campaign was at its height of violence, and the NUWSS was pursuing a parallel constitutional strategy. The outbreak of war in August 1914 led both organizations to suspend their campaigns. The vote was granted in 1918, to women over thirty who met property qualifications, a development outside the scope of this chapter, and historians continue to discuss how far suffragist agitation, wartime service, and parliamentary politics each contributed.

The relations among women's suffrage, labor, and the Liberal Party were complex. Many Liberals supported women's suffrage in principle but disagreed about the terms, because some feared that a limited franchise based on property would favor Conservatives. The Labour Party formally supported women's suffrage but worried about a franchise that would exclude working-class women. Labour's relations with the suffrage movement were uneven, and some Labour leaders were enthusiastic while others were hesitant. The campaign shows that women's suffrage was entangled in party politics, and that suffragists had to navigate between parties whose interests did not align with theirs.

Historical Vocabulary
Suffragists and Suffragettes

In British usage, suffragist generally refers to a supporter of women's right to vote who used constitutional and peaceful methods, such as petitions, lobbying, and public meetings, and it is most closely associated with the NUWSS. Suffragette was a term coined in 1906 by a newspaper to describe the more militant members of the Women's Social and Political Union, and the WSPU adopted it. It is generally used for those who took direct action, including civil disobedience and property damage.

These terms matter because the distinction between them is useful but not perfectly clean. Many women belonged to organizations that overlapped, and individual views changed. In continental Europe, activists used other terms, and the British division does not map neatly onto movements in France, Germany, or Scandinavia. Students should also avoid assuming that every suffrage campaigner was either a constitutionalist or a militant, since many supported different methods at different times.

Central question: Why did British suffrage campaigners adopt different strategies, and how did those differences influence the political struggle for women's voting rights?
Section 102.19

Women's Suffrage across Europe: Finland, Norway, Socialist Women, and Uneven Political Inclusion

The British campaign, though prominent, was only one of many. Suffrage movements existed across Europe, and in the period before 1914, the earliest breakthroughs in the enfranchisement of women occurred in the northern periphery, not in the countries often regarded as political leaders. The Finnish reform of 1906 was the first in Europe to give women full parliamentary voting rights and the right to stand for election, followed by Norway, where women's rights expanded in stages culminating in 1913. In other states, including Britain, France, Germany, and the Habsburg lands, campaigns continued, and women did not obtain national voting rights until 1918 or later.

Finland was an autonomous grand duchy within the Russian Empire, with its own laws, institutions, and a Diet of four estates that excluded most of the population. Women's organizations, including the Finnish Women's Association, founded in 1884, and the Unioni League of Finnish Feminists, formed in 1892, campaigned for women's rights, and Finnish women's participation in the nationalist and labor movements was extensive. The general strike of 1905, which was part of the wider revolutionary crisis in the Russian Empire, forced the tsar to suspend the measures of russification that had been imposed on Finland, and it opened the way to parliamentary reform. The Parliament Act of 1906, which Nicholas II confirmed in July, replaced the Diet with a unicameral Parliament of 200 members elected by universal and equal suffrage, including women, and by proportional representation. The age of voting was twenty-four, and women could stand for office. In March 1907, in the first elections, 19 women were elected, among them Miina Sillanpää, a Social Democrat who had worked as a servant and trade union organizer. The reform was a result of a specific combination of national autonomy, mass mobilization, and imperial crisis, and it did not reflect a general Russian development, since in the empire as a whole, women had no vote.

In Norway, women's suffrage was achieved through a gradual extension. The Norwegian Association for Women's Suffrage was founded in 1885, with Gina Krog as a leading figure, and a rival association was established in 1898. After the introduction of universal male suffrage in 1898, in 1901, women who paid taxes or were married to men who paid taxes were granted the right to vote in local elections. In 1907, women who met certain income or tax requirements gained the right to vote in parliamentary elections, which enfranchised a minority of women. In 1913, after campaigning by women's organizations and with support from the Labour Party and parts of the Liberal and Conservative parties, the Storting extended equal parliamentary voting rights to women and men, with the final decision dated June 11, 1913. Norway's experience shows the use of incremental franchise reform, in which property-based qualifications were first extended to women before being eliminated.

Elsewhere in Europe, progress was slower. In Denmark, the 1915 constitutional change granted women the parliamentary franchise, first exercised at a general election in 1918. In the Netherlands, legislation enfranchised women for national elections in 1919. In Sweden, parliamentary decisions in 1919 were followed by completion of the constitutional change in 1921, when women first voted in a national parliamentary election. In France, though women were active in the movement, led by figures such as Hubertine Auclert and the later organizations of the 1900s, the Chamber of Deputies considered proposals and a committee report in 1909 supported limited reform, but the Senate and Radical politicians, who feared that women would vote under clerical influence, blocked progress. In Germany and Austria, where women could not join political parties until 1908 and 1918 respectively, suffrage organizations grew in the early twentieth century, and Social Democrats supported women's suffrage in their program, but the governing systems did not permit it. In the Russian Empire outside Finland, women's equal rights organizations were formed in 1905 and campaigned during the revolution, but the Duma system did not enfranchise women.

Socialist women were important in suffrage politics. Clara Zetkin, who edited the socialist women's newspaper Die Gleichheit from 1892, was the leading figure. Socialist women argued that the vote was necessary but that class liberation was the larger aim, and they were often critical of middle-class suffrage organizations that accepted a franchise limited by property, which would give votes to propertied women but exclude working-class women. At the International Socialist Women's Conference at Stuttgart in 1907, which met alongside the congress of the Second International, delegates affirmed support for universal suffrage including women. The second conference, which met in Copenhagen in August 1910 before the International's congress there, passed a resolution proposed by Zetkin and others to hold an annual Women's Day to promote women's suffrage and socialism. The first such days were observed in March 1911 in several countries, including Germany, Austria, Denmark, and Switzerland. The date and the later international custom of observing International Women's Day on March 8 evolved after these first observances.

Why did women obtain parliamentary rights earlier in some European societies than in others? No single cause suffices. In Finland, a revolutionary crisis, national mobilization against imperial authority, and a labor movement that supported equality combined. In Norway, sustained women's organizing, liberal and labor support, and a tradition of gradual franchise reform made wider suffrage politically feasible. The relatively small population should not be confused with ethnic homogeneity, since Sámi and Kven communities also lived under state policies of assimilation. In larger and more heavily armed states, such as Germany, concern about military and imperial authority, conservative opposition, and the reluctance of parties that feared the electoral effects of women's votes were obstacles. Religious divisions, such as Catholic influence in France and Belgium, shaped the calculations of anticlerical parties. In Britain, a parliamentary system that gave government control over the legislative agenda allowed ministers to delay. Differences in national political systems, party competition, women's organizational strength, and international example all mattered.

International cooperation among women's movements was extensive. The International Woman Suffrage Alliance held congresses in cities including Amsterdam in 1908 and London in 1909, enabling delegates to share strategies. Socialist women's international contacts paralleled these, though the two networks were mostly separate. These exchanges show that women's suffrage was not simply a national question, but also an international one, in which the results in Finland and Norway were used as evidence in other countries that women's voting was possible and not disruptive.

Central question: Why did women's political rights expand unevenly across Europe, and how did suffrage campaigns interact with labor and socialist movements?
Section 102.20

The Achievements and Limits of Mass Politics before 1914

By 1914, the scale of European political participation was transformed. In the 1870s, most states had restricted electorates, few had permanent mass parties, and trade unions were weak or illegal in many places. By 1914, most adult men in western and central Europe could vote in some form, and millions belonged to parties, unions, cooperatives, confessional associations, and nationalist leagues. Parties and unions were permanent institutions with salaried staffs, buildings, newspapers, and funds, and they could influence legislation, administration, and public opinion. Participation was unequal, however, between countries and within them. Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Austria, Italy, and Scandinavian states each had distinct systems, Russia and Hungary had very restricted ones, and women could vote nationally only in Finland and Norway.

Labor's pressure had practical effects. Governments passed laws on union rights, factory regulation, social insurance, and pensions, partly in response to organized labor and partly to head off its more radical forms. Labor parties sat in parliaments and, in a few cases, supported governments. Political representation of workers, which had hardly existed in 1870, had become routine in many states. At the same time, the gains were partial. Benefits were modest, unions could be sued or restricted, strikes could be defeated, and large categories of workers, including agricultural laborers, domestic servants, and many women, remained outside effective organization.

The relationships among parliamentary reform, protest, strikes, and direct action were close. Franchise reform in Belgium and Austria followed strikes and demonstrations, and social legislation often followed periods of labor unrest. Yet protest could also provoke repression, and governments learned to combine concession and coercion. Socialist parties did not unite workers behind a single strategy. They were divided over revolution and reform, over the relation of unions to parties, and over the place of religion and nationality in working-class politics. Many workers belonged to Catholic or other confessional organizations, voted for liberal or conservative parties, or were unorganized.

Women's movements exposed contradictions in the claim that representative systems spoke for the whole people. Women were taxpayers, workers, and mothers, and in many countries they were increasingly educated and employed, yet they were excluded from national elections in all but two states. Their campaigns, which ranged from lobbying to militant action to international coordination, widened the definition of citizenship and political participation, and the results in Finland and Norway showed that women's voting was practicable. Yet the movement was also divided by class, religion, nation, and strategy, and the exclusion persisted in most of Europe.

Persistent limitations included class and property, as in Prussia's three-class system, Belgian plural voting, and the Austrian and Hungarian restrictions, and executive power, as in the German chancellor's independence from the Reichstag and the Russian tsar's retained authority. Imperial status was a further limit. Colonial populations were governed without representation or with limited rights, as later chapters on imperialism show, and the political rights of metropolitan citizens coexisted with empire. Religious and national identities shaped political organization, producing confessional parties and nationalist movements that sometimes divided workers and sometimes mobilized people across classes. Tensions between international labor solidarity and national loyalties grew in the early twentieth century, as international crises, arms races, and nationalism intensified, a development examined in Chapter 107.

The conclusion for this chapter is that mass politics transformed European societies without guaranteeing equal citizenship, parliamentary democracy, or political stability. It changed how people took part in public life, created institutions that would shape the twentieth century, and extended participation to groups previously excluded. It also created new forms of organization, discipline, and mobilization that could serve different purposes, including nationalist and, later, authoritarian ones. This duality is the subject of the Historical Debate below. The war that began in 1914 would test every one of these institutions, and the later chapters of Volume V examine what became of them.

Historical Debate

Was Europe Becoming More Democratic, or Developing New Forms of Organized Political Control?

The question: Did the growth of mass parties, unions, and wider suffrage before 1914 represent progress toward democratic self-government, or did it create new, organized ways of managing, directing, and containing popular participation?

Interpretation A: Participation from below drove democratization. Historians such as Geoff Eley, in Forging Democracy, argue that democratic politics in Europe was won through popular struggle, including socialist and labor organization, women's campaigns, and the pressure of strikes and demonstrations, and was not a gift from liberal elites. From this perspective, the expansion of parties, unions, and suffrage opened real channels of influence, forced governments to change, and created the institutions of later democracy. Eley's work also emphasizes that this process was contested and uneven, and that democratization was not a straight path.
Interpretation B: Organization also brought new forms of control. Other analyses stress that mass organizations tended to concentrate power in leaders and apparatuses. Robert Michels, in a study of German Social Democracy published in 1911, argued that large organizations tended toward rule by a small leadership, an argument often summarized as the iron law of oligarchy. Eric Hobsbawm, in discussing the period, observed that states and movements deliberately created public rituals, symbols, and ceremonies to secure loyalty, and that governments and parties alike learned to manage mass opinion. On this view, mass politics could discipline participation as much as it empowered it, and it could serve nationalist and conservative mobilization as well as democratic reform.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same developments: party structures, membership records, election returns, franchise laws, union histories, press systems, the records of congresses, and the political rituals of May Day and national celebrations. Interpretation A points to franchise reforms, social legislation, and the strength of movements from below. Interpretation B points to leadership dominance within parties, the persistence of unequal systems, state use of propaganda and public ceremony, and the growth of nationalist and conservative mass movements. The two views are not mutually exclusive, and much recent scholarship holds that both processes occurred together.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these interpretations affects how they understand the political crises after 1914 and the later fate of European democracies. If mass politics primarily advanced democracy, the later collapse of parliamentary systems needs explanation as an interruption. If mass organization also contained the capacity for control and exclusion, the continuities between pre-war and later developments appear different.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Mass Politics in Europe, c. 1870 to 1914

Long-term causes

Industrialization, urbanization, population growth, and rising literacy, along with the earlier spread of constitutionalism, liberal and socialist ideas, and religious organization, created large populations capable of political organization.

Short-term causes

Franchise reforms of the 1860s to 1880s, the creation of national communication networks, economic depressions and labor disputes, the Kulturkampf and other religious conflicts, and the formation of new parties and unions provided specific opportunities and grievances.

Event or transformation

Between 1870 and 1914, mass parties, trade unions, socialist organizations, confessional movements, and women's associations became permanent institutions, and electorates expanded in most European states.

Immediate effects

Governments reformed electoral systems, passed labor and social insurance laws, and used repression and negotiation. Labor parties entered parliaments, and women gained parliamentary suffrage in Finland and Norway.

Long-term consequences

Mass organization shaped the party systems, welfare states, and labor movements of the twentieth century, and created institutions and techniques that nationalist, democratic, and authoritarian movements would each use after 1914.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains these developments. Reform came from protest, elite calculation, party bargaining, and international example, and it varied by state. Mass politics did not make Europe uniformly democratic, and the same organizations that expanded participation could also discipline it.

Historical complexity: Industrialization created the possibility of mass politics, but outcomes depended on political institutions, religious divisions, state policy, and the choices of organizers. Britain, Germany, Belgium, Finland, and Russia, all industrializing or experiencing social change, ended up with very different political systems.

Central question: By 1914, how far had European political systems moved toward mass democracy, and what important political inequalities and conflicts remained unresolved?

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how European political life was transformed between about 1870 and 1914 by the growth of organized mass participation. Industrialization, urbanization, rising literacy, and new communication networks made it possible for large populations to organize, and political parties, trade unions, socialist movements, confessional associations, and women's organizations became permanent institutions. The central argument is that mass politics greatly enlarged the scale of political participation without establishing a uniform model of democracy or removing the inequalities that structured European society.

Suffrage expanded unevenly. Britain's reforms of 1884 and 1885 broadened the male electorate and reorganized constituencies, yet plural voting persisted and women remained excluded. The German Empire combined equal male suffrage for the Reichstag with a chancellor responsible to the emperor, a Prussian three-class franchise, and unrevised constituencies. France had universal male suffrage within a parliamentary republic, but a Senate chosen indirectly. Belgium established broad but plural voting in 1893, Austria introduced equal male suffrage in 1907 while Hungary kept a restrictive franchise, Italy expanded its male electorate in 1882 and 1912, and Russia's Duma system was narrowed in 1907. The chapter distinguished eligibility to vote, eligibility to stand for office, and the effective power of an elected body, and showed that breadth of suffrage did not equal democracy.

Parties became institutions with memberships, local branches, paid officials, press systems, and campaign techniques. In Britain, the Liberals and Conservatives built national organizations, and trade unionists, socialists, and reformers created the Labour Representation Committee in 1900 and the Labour Party in 1906 after the Independent Labour Party had pioneered independent representation. In Germany, the Catholic Centre Party and the Social Democrats built dense organizational milieus, and the Social Democrats grew despite the Anti-Socialist Law of 1878 to 1890, becoming the largest Reichstag party in 1912 without gaining access to government. In France, a parliamentary republic accommodated Radicals, socialists, conservatives, and Catholics, and socialists united in the SFIO in 1905. In the Habsburg lands, Italy, Scandinavia, and the Russian Empire, class, religion, and nationality shaped different patterns.

Trade unions developed from craft societies to mass organizations, and the New Unionism of the late 1880s brought less-skilled workers into large unions. Strikes such as those of the London match workers in 1888, the London dockers in 1889, and the miners at Carmaux in 1892 demonstrated both the potential and the risks of collective action, and the labor unrest of 1911 to 1914 showed rising conflict. Employers responded with dismissals, lockouts, replacement workers, and associations of their own, and governments sometimes deployed police or troops. Success depended on the labor market, solidarity, funds, public opinion, and state attitudes, and many strikes failed.

Governments responded to labor pressure with a mixture of repression, regulation, and welfare. Bismarck's insurance laws of 1883, 1884, and 1889 pioneered state social insurance, while Britain adopted old-age pensions in 1908 and national insurance in 1911, and France and other states passed their own measures. The motives combined humanitarian concern, the desire to stabilize society and weaken socialism, and responses to democratic pressure, and the results were limited benefits alongside an expanded state role. Workers also built cooperatives, friendly societies, Christian unions, and cultural associations, and the encyclical Rerum Novarum of 1891 offered a Catholic framework for social action. This diversity meant that the working class never formed a single political movement.

Socialist organization followed national paths. German Social Democracy evolved from the Lassallean and Eisenach groups through the Gotha unification of 1875 and the Erfurt Program of 1891 into the largest socialist party in Europe. British Laborism rested on trade unions and pragmatic reform, French socialists were divided until 1905, and socialist parties in other states reflected local conditions. The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party, formed in 1898, divided into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks in 1903 in a clandestine setting. The Second International, founded in 1889, linked these parties and coordinated May Day demonstrations, but it could not bind its members and was limited by language, national interests, and legal differences.

Strategic divisions ran through the movement. Bernstein's revisionism argued for gradual reform, Kautsky defended Marxist orthodoxy, and Luxemburg criticized revisionism and favored mass action. French syndicalists, organized in the CGT, held that unions rather than parties were the instruments of emancipation, and the Charter of Amiens of 1906 asserted union independence. Anarchists rejected parliamentary politics entirely. These positions rested on different assumptions about power and change, and each had empirical support and practical difficulties.

Women's movements campaigned for education, professional access, legal rights, and political participation. They were diverse, ranging from moderate middle-class associations to socialist women's organizations, and they disagreed over equality, motherhood, employment, and class. In Britain, the NUWSS pursued constitutional methods from 1897, and the WSPU, founded in 1903, adopted militant tactics that included hunger strikes and, later, arson, while the Women's Freedom League and the East London Federation represented other directions. Finland enacted universal and equal suffrage in 1906 and elected 19 women in March 1907, and Norway established equal parliamentary suffrage in 1913, while most other European states continued to exclude women from national elections.

Interpretations of the period differ. Some historians emphasize that popular organization and struggle forced democratic change, while others stress that mass organization also created leadership oligarchies and techniques of control and mobilization that nationalist and conservative movements used as well. The evidence supports elements of both, and the chapter's analysis rejects any assumption of inevitable progress toward democracy.

By 1914, mass politics had become a defining feature of European public life, but it had not produced equal citizenship, universal parliamentary democracy, or political stability. Executive power, property qualifications, gender exclusion, imperial status, and national and religious divisions persisted. Internationalism coexisted with growing national loyalties, and the outbreak of war in August 1914 would test every institution described in this chapter. The later chapters of Volume V trace how the war, revolution, and the interwar crisis reshaped these movements and the political systems in which they operated.

Key Terms

Mass politics
Political life organized around large populations mobilized through parties, unions, associations, and campaigns, in place of politics dominated by notables and narrow electorates.
Franchise
The legal right to vote, defined by qualifications that could include property, age, residence, literacy, and gender.
Universal male suffrage
The right of nearly all adult men to vote, usually excluding women and sometimes other groups.
Plural voting
A system giving some voters more than one vote on grounds such as property, education, or age, as in Belgium after 1893.
Redistribution of seats
The redrawing of parliamentary constituencies to change their number, boundaries, or population balance, as in Britain in 1885.
Curial system
A voting arrangement dividing voters into classes, or curiae, with different weights, used in Austria before 1907.
Three-class franchise
The Prussian system, used for the Landtag until 1918, which weighted votes according to tax payments.
Political party
A permanent organization with members, local branches, officials, funds, and a program, aimed at winning elections and influencing government.
Canvassing
The practice of visiting voters to learn and influence their intentions and to ensure that supporters were registered and voted.
Confessional party
A party organized around religious identity and institutions, such as the German Catholic Centre Party.
Kulturkampf
The conflict of the 1870s between the German government and the Catholic Church over education, marriage, and clerical influence, which strengthened Catholic political organization.
Anti-Socialist Law
The German law of 1878, in force until 1890, that banned socialist organizations, meetings, and publications while allowing candidates to stand for the Reichstag.
Trade union
A permanent association of workers formed to protect and improve wages, hours, and conditions.
New Unionism
The British movement of the late 1880s that organized less-skilled and general workers into large unions with low dues.
Collective bargaining
Negotiation of terms of employment between employers and representatives of a group of workers.
Lockout
An employer's closure of a workplace, or exclusion of workers, to resist union demands.
General strike
A coordinated stoppage by workers across many industries, used as a political weapon, as in Belgium in 1893.
Social insurance
Compulsory or state-organized insurance against sickness, accident, disability, old age, or unemployment, financed by contributions or taxes.
Friendly society
A mutual association whose members paid small regular sums to receive benefits in sickness or death.
Cooperative
An enterprise owned by its members, such as a consumer store distributing profits according to purchases.
Rerum Novarum
The 1891 encyclical of Pope Leo XIII that defended private property, criticized socialism, and affirmed workers' rights to fair wages and association.
Social democracy
In this period, a label for Marxist-influenced socialist parties, especially the German SPD, combining a socialist goal with electoral and reform activity.
Revisionism
The argument, associated with Bernstein, that socialism should be pursued through gradual reform because capitalism was not collapsing as Marx predicted.
Revolutionary syndicalism
A doctrine, strong in France, holding that unions and the general strike, not parties and parliaments, would bring about workers' emancipation.
Second International
The association of socialist and labor parties founded in Paris in 1889, which held congresses and coordinated May Day.
Direct action
Action taken without parliamentary mediation, such as strikes, boycotts, or, for some suffragettes, civil disobedience and property damage.
Suffragist and suffragette
Respectively, a supporter of women's suffrage by constitutional methods and, in British usage, a member of the militant WSPU.
Citizenship
Legal membership of a political community, which in this period carried unequal rights depending on gender, property, and, in empires, imperial status.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how ordinary people became participants in political life on a scale without precedent in European history, and how their participation was organized, limited, and contested. The parties, unions, cooperatives, and women's associations described here were not simply products of industrial society. They were created by the decisions of organizers, the pressures of members, the calculations of governments, and the legal frameworks of each state. In revealing that process, the chapter connects the industrial and economic changes of Chapter 101 with the political and imperial history of the chapters that follow.

The chapter also reveals how varied European political experience was. A reader who follows only Britain, Germany, and France would miss the Belgian general strike, the Austrian electoral reform, the Finnish parliament of 1907, the Norwegian suffrage campaign, and the restricted politics of Russia and Hungary. Observing these cases together shows that no single path led from industrialization to democracy, and that regions that were sometimes treated as peripheral made some of the most significant electoral advances.

Several developments of this period would shape what followed. Mass parties and unions became the foundations of the political systems of the twentieth century, and the welfare institutions begun before 1914 would expand. The tensions between reform and revolution, between international solidarity and national loyalty, and between formal rights and effective power would shape the crises of the First World War and the interwar period, which Volume V examines. At the same time, historians must resist reading the later breakdown of democracies back into the earlier period, because the outcomes of the 1920s and 1930s were not determined by the institutions of 1914.

For the argument of Volume IV, the chapter shows how industrial and imperial Europe produced its own political counterforces. The same age that built steel mills, railways, and overseas empires also built mass organizations that demanded a share of power, and the claims of workers and women about who belonged to the political community challenged assumptions that governments and propertied classes had long taken for granted. Chapter 103 turns to another dimension of the politics of belonging, in the rise of racial nationalism, antisemitism, and the social thought that shaped them.
