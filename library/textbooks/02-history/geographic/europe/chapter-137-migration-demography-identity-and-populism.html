Migration, Demography, Identity, and Populism

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Europe's twentieth-century wars and its postwar institutions are often told as stories of states and borders. This chapter asks a different question: who lived inside those borders, how did that population change, and who was recognized as belonging to it?

On 30 October 1961, representatives of the Federal Republic of Germany and Turkey signed a labor recruitment agreement in Bonn. The document was an administrative instrument, concerned with medical examinations, placement through official offices, and the regulation of temporary employment. Its authors imagined a short-term arrangement that would supply workers to a booming West German economy and relieve pressure on Turkish labor markets. They did not imagine that, within two generations, their descendants would be among the largest minority populations in the Federal Republic, shaping its cities, schools, mosques, football clubs, literature, and electoral politics. The gap between what the signatories intended and what followed is one of the central themes of this chapter.

Between 1945 and 2026, Europe underwent three overlapping transformations. First, it shifted from a continent that exported people, to the Americas, Australasia, and colonial territories, into one that received them, through labor recruitment, decolonization, refugee movement, family reunification, and, after 1989, the opening of the former communist world. Second, its populations aged to a degree without precedent in recorded history, as fertility fell and life expectancy lengthened. Third, its societies became religiously, linguistically, and culturally more varied, and the question of what it meant to belong to a nation became a subject of intense public argument. In 2025, according to Eurostat, the European Union had a population of about 451 million, a median age of 44.9 years, and a total fertility rate, measured for 2024, of 1.34 births per woman.

These developments were connected, but they were not interchangeable. Population aging did not produce a single immigration policy. Immigration did not produce a single political reaction. National identity did not mean the same thing in Paris, Berlin, Stockholm, Warsaw, and Sofia. This chapter therefore avoids two common simplifications: the assumption that immigration automatically produces populism, and the assumption that populist movements are identical across countries. It treats migrants, the descendants of migrants, established populations, governments, parties, courts, employers, and voters as historical actors whose choices interacted over decades.

The chapter also distinguishes terms that political debate frequently blurs. A foreign-born resident is not necessarily a foreign national, because many migrants naturalize. A refugee is not the same as an asylum seeker, a beneficiary of temporary protection, or a labor migrant. A citizen of one European Union member state living in another exercises free movement rights under a legal framework quite different from the one that governs a Turkish or Nigerian national seeking residence. These categories are legal and historical constructions, and much of the political history of the period consists of disputes over how they should be drawn.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. It includes bilateral agreements and treaties, national census and register data, Eurostat and national statistical series, parliamentary debates, court judgments, party manifestos, election returns, opinion surveys, newspapers, television, social media, oral histories, memoirs, novels, and the records of civil society organizations. Each has limits. Official statistics define categories differently across countries and change definitions over time. Surveys depend on sampling. Political speeches seek to persuade rather than to describe. Historians must therefore separate demonstrable events from claims about them, and must ask whose experience each source records and whose it omits.

Within Part XX, this chapter is the social and political counterpart to the institutional history in Chapter 136. That chapter showed how European states entered new arrangements of enlargement and integration. This chapter examines what was happening inside their societies while those arrangements developed. It prepares the ground for Chapter 138, which examines the financial crisis, sovereign debt, and Brexit, and for Chapter 139, which treats contemporary European security. Where it touches those subjects, notably the displacement caused by Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, it concentrates on migration and legal protection rather than on military or diplomatic history.

Within Volume VI, the chapter extends a theme that began with postwar reconstruction in Chapter 126 and decolonization in Chapter 128: the discovery that Europe's postwar prosperity, empire, and later enlargement had produced a population more diverse than most of its governments had planned for. Its cutoff is 18 November 2026. Developments after the 2024 European Parliament elections, the entry into application of the Pact on Migration and Asylum on 12 June 2026, and the national elections of 2026 are recent, and their ultimate significance cannot yet be known. They are narrated here with exact dates and are distinguished from settled historical scholarship.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace Europe's shift from a continent of emigration to a major destination of immigration after 1945, comparing West Germany, France, Britain, Belgium, and the Netherlands.
  2. Distinguish voluntary migration, family reunification, forced displacement, return migration, and free movement within the European Union.
  3. Explain fertility decline, longer life expectancy, and population aging, and assess the limits of migration as a response to them.
  4. Compare growing metropolitan regions with shrinking rural and peripheral regions, and analyze the political consequences of that divergence.
  5. Distinguish the legal categories of citizen, EU citizen, permanent resident, refugee, asylum seeker, and beneficiary of temporary protection.
  6. Analyze the refugee movements of 2015 to 2016 and the displacement from Ukraine after 2022, and evaluate the development of the Pact on Migration and Asylum.
  7. Compare French, German, British, Dutch, and Swedish approaches to citizenship, integration, and religious pluralism, recognizing change over time.
  8. Interpret the long presence of Islam in southeastern Europe and the diversity of Muslim communities in western and northern Europe.
  9. Assess competing explanations for the rise of right-wing populist parties without treating any single factor as sufficient.
  10. Evaluate mainstream parties' responses to demographic and cultural change and the debate over how democratic institutions should respond to illiberal movements.
  11. Interpret demographic projections as conditional models rather than predictions.
  12. Connect migration, identity, and democracy into a historically grounded account of contemporary European belonging.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did changing populations and conceptions of belonging transform European societies, and why did these changes produce both new forms of integration and intensified political conflict?
  2. Why did temporary labor recruitment so often become permanent settlement, and what does that outcome reveal about the gap between policy intention and social reality?
  3. In what ways do the legal categories used to classify people shape the political debate about them?
  4. Why did countries with similar demographic profiles adopt different immigration policies, and why did countries with different profiles sometimes reach similar ones?
  5. What evidence allows historians to distinguish demonstrable events from political claims, stereotypes, and perceptions in debates about migration?
  6. Why have populist parties succeeded in some countries and periods but not in others, and why do national outcomes differ?
  7. How have immigrants and their descendants acted as historical agents, rather than simply as objects of policy?
  8. To what extent can demographic projections guide public policy, and what are their limits as historical evidence?

Historical Context

Before 1945, Europe's migratory history ran largely outward. Between the early nineteenth century and the First World War, tens of millions of Europeans left for the Americas, Australasia, southern Africa, and colonial territories, driven by population growth, agrarian crisis, and the attractions of cheaper land and industrial employment overseas, as the Volume IV chapters on industrialization and empire explain. Within Europe, there was also substantial movement from countryside to city and across borders, including Polish workers in German industry and Italian laborers in France, Switzerland, and Argentina. Antisemitism, imperial collapse, and war then produced large forced movements: the Armenian and Greek catastrophes of 1915 to 1923, the exchange of populations between Greece and Turkey in 1923, the flight of Russian émigrés, and the persecution and expulsion of Jews examined in Chapter 121.

The Second World War and its aftermath produced the largest forced displacement in modern European history. Chapter 123 examined the millions of expellees, displaced persons, and returning prisoners and the population transfers that redrew Europe's ethnic map, notably the expulsion of Germans from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and elsewhere. These movements left much of central and eastern Europe more homogeneous in language and nationality than it had been in 1939, an outcome that was a product of mass violence and state policy rather than of long-term development. Western Europe absorbed expellees, displaced persons, and, in some cases, former colonial settlers, without regarding itself as a region of immigration.

The postwar economic boom described in Chapter 126 altered that assumption. Reconstruction and rapid growth created labor shortages, first met by internal reserves such as refugees and rural workers, and then by recruitment abroad. Decolonization, examined in Chapter 128, brought migrants and settlers from Algeria, the Caribbean, South Asia, Indonesia, Suriname, and Africa, many of whom held some form of citizenship or subject status. By the 1970s the oil shock and recession had led most western European governments to end formal recruitment, yet settlement continued through family reunification, and migrants' children were born as residents of the countries to which their parents had come. Chapter 127 outlined the national frameworks within which these changes occurred.

Meanwhile, Europe's demographic regime had changed. The first demographic transition, in which falling mortality was followed by falling fertility, had unfolded across the continent over roughly a century and a half. After the postwar baby boom, fertility declined again, in many countries below the level of about 2.1 births per woman needed to replace a population in the long run without migration. The 1989 revolutions, discussed in Chapter 132, then opened borders in the east, while the Yugoslav wars of Chapter 135 produced the largest refugee movements in Europe since 1945. The enlargement process of Chapter 136 made citizens of new member states into holders of free movement rights. The result was a continent in which immigration, aging, and the meaning of citizenship had become political questions.

The chapter draws on categories used by demographers, lawyers, and statisticians, and it is important to know their limits. Eurostat publishes harmonized data, but national definitions of foreign-born, foreign national, and migrant differ, and the European Union's boundaries changed in 2004, 2007, 2013, and, with the United Kingdom's departure, in 2020. Asylum statistics count applications and decisions, which differ from the number of persons. Surveys of attitudes measure stated opinion, which is shaped by question wording. Election results record votes cast, not the reasons behind them. Throughout this chapter, the reporting year, the country coverage, and the type of measure are identified wherever numbers are used.

Population Transformation and Migration in Modern Europe

From recruitment and decolonization to post-communist movement, aging, and regional divergence.

Section 137.1

From Emigration to Immigration: Europe's Postwar Population Transformation

In the first postwar decade, no western European government described its country as a destination for immigrants. Reconstruction absorbed millions of expellees and displaced persons, and policymakers expected that Europe's surplus labor would continue to emigrate, as it had for a century. Italy, Spain, Portugal, Ireland, and Greece remained major exporters of people. Yet by the late 1950s, rapid growth in West Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and Britain had exhausted domestic reserves. The arrival of East German refugees ended abruptly in 1961 with the construction of the Berlin Wall, examined in Chapter 124, and West German employers turned to foreign recruitment on a large scale.

The West German system, which began with an agreement with Italy in 1955 and expanded to Spain, Greece, Turkey, Morocco, Portugal, Tunisia, and Yugoslavia, was designed around rotation. Workers were to be recruited by German labor offices, employed for a limited time, and replaced by others. In practice employers preferred to retain trained workers, and workers often preferred to remain and bring their families. By the time the federal government halted recruitment in November 1973, in response to the oil shock and recession, more than a million foreign workers lived in the Federal Republic. The halt did not reverse the process. It made departure risky, since a worker who left might not be readmitted, and it channeled new arrivals toward family reunification. The Swiss writer Max Frisch is widely credited with the observation, made in the 1960s, that a labor force had been summoned and human beings had arrived. Whether or not its precise wording is accurately transmitted, the remark captures what policymakers had overlooked.

France, Belgium, and the Netherlands followed related but distinct paths. France recruited through its National Immigration Office, drawing on Italians, Spaniards, and Portuguese, and, increasingly, on Algerians, Moroccans, and Tunisians, many of whom already held a legal status shaped by empire. Belgium had signed an agreement with Italy in 1946 to supply miners, a policy whose human costs became evident when 262 men, among them 136 Italians, died in the Marcinelle mining disaster of 1956. It later concluded agreements with Morocco and Turkey in 1964. The Netherlands received repatriates from Indonesia after 1949, then recruited Mediterranean workers, and received large numbers of Surinamese migrants around the time of Suriname's independence in 1975. In each case, economic recruitment intersected with decolonization, examined in Chapter 128, and the border between labor migration and postcolonial migration was never sharp.

Britain was a distinctive case. The British Nationality Act of 1948 confirmed that citizens of the United Kingdom and Colonies and of Commonwealth countries shared a common status, and migrants from the Caribbean, India, Pakistan, and later East Africa could settle without a recruitment scheme, though some employers and public services, including transport and the National Health Service, actively recruited. As arrivals grew, Parliament restricted entry through the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act of 1971, while the Race Relations Acts of 1965 and 1976 created legal protections against discrimination. The pattern of restriction on entry combined with regulation of discrimination within the country contrasted with the German emphasis on temporary status and the French emphasis on a republican model of equal citizenship.

Across these cases, three features recur. Governments initially treated migration as a labor-market instrument. Migrants and employers treated it as the beginning of a life. And the children of migrants, educated in local schools and socialized in local languages, became a new population whose legal and cultural status was unsettled. Southern Europe changed direction during these decades. As Chapter 131 notes, Spain, Portugal, Greece, and Italy shifted from exporters of labor to importers by the 1980s and 1990s, recruiting workers from North Africa, Latin America, and, later, eastern Europe. By the end of the twentieth century, Europe as a whole had become a continent of net immigration. Its postwar boom, its imperial past, and its demographic structure had combined to produce societies that were more diverse than most of their governments had planned.

Primary Source Window

The German–Turkish Labor Recruitment Agreement, Bonn, 30 October 1961

Creators: Governments of the Federal Republic of Germany and the Republic of Turkey · Date: 30 October 1961 · Type: Bilateral administrative agreement, preserved in German and Turkish official records and reproduced in published documentary collections

Source document: 1961 recruitment agreement, original German text (Federal Agency for Civic Education).

Context: The agreement was concluded after the Berlin Wall ended the flow of East German workers and while West German industry faced labor shortages. Turkey's government sought employment for workers and remittances, and its western allies had security as well as economic interests in the relationship. It followed earlier agreements with Italy, Spain, and Greece and was revised in 1964.

Paraphrase of the agreement's principal provisions The agreement provided for the recruitment of Turkish workers through official placement offices, medical screening, employment contracts equivalent to those of German workers, and a limited initial duration of stay. The original text anticipated rotation, though the 1964 revision relaxed limits on length of residence. This is a paraphrase of the structure of the agreement, not a translation of its text. Students should consult a published translation for exact wording.

Analysis: The agreement reveals the logic of a labor-market instrument. Its categories were employment, medical fitness, and contract. It contains no provisions for citizenship, language instruction, or family settlement, because its drafters did not expect them to become issues. Its significance lies in the contrast between its narrow administrative purpose and the long-term social transformation it initiated. The document speaks for governments, not for the workers themselves.

Limitations: The agreement cannot show how migrants experienced recruitment, how many intended to stay, or how their families and communities responded. Oral histories, letters, employer records, and migrant organizations are needed for that. It also cannot explain why Turkish migrants, among all the recruited groups, became the largest minority population of Germany, which depended on later family reunification, refugee migration, and demographic growth.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the agreement say or imply about the expected duration and purpose of migration?
  2. Who created it, and what interests did each government have in doing so?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the assumptions behind guest-worker policy?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone about migrants' own experience and intentions?
Section 137.2

Migration after the Cold War: New Routes and New Communities

The end of communist rule in 1989 to 1991 opened Europe's eastern borders and removed restrictions on emigration, as described in Chapter 132. The immediate result was a surge in movement, much of it by people who had been unable to leave for decades. Germany admitted large numbers of ethnic Germans from Poland, Romania, and the Soviet Union and its successor states under its constitutional and statutory provisions for persons of German descent. These Aussiedler and later Spätaussiedler numbered, over the period from the late 1980s, in the low millions, though many spoke little German and encountered difficulties of integration. Other states operated comparable policies of ethnic preference: Greece for persons of Greek descent from the former Soviet Union and Albania, Finland for Ingrian Finns, Hungary for Hungarians in neighboring states, and Spain and Italy through privileged naturalization routes for descendants of earlier emigrants. Return migration therefore blurred the line between immigration and the reabsorption of people regarded as co-nationals.

The Yugoslav wars of 1991 to 1999, examined in Chapter 135, produced Europe's largest refugee movements since 1945. Millions were displaced within and beyond the former Yugoslavia, many of them Bosnians, Croats, Serbs, and, later, Kosovo Albanians. Germany received several hundred thousand Bosnian refugees, who were generally given temporary protection rather than refugee status, and Austria, Sweden, Switzerland, and others also took in large numbers. Many returned after the Dayton Agreement of 1995, though others stayed, and some who returned did so to places where they were now in a minority. The wars helped establish concepts of temporary and subsidiary protection that later became central to European asylum law, and they demonstrated that mass displacement could occur within Europe.

Free movement within an enlarging Union created a different kind of migration. Following the accessions of 2004 and 2007, discussed in Chapter 136, millions of citizens of Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Lithuania, and other new members moved westward in search of work. The United Kingdom, Ireland, and Sweden opened their labor markets immediately in 2004, while Germany and Austria retained transitional restrictions until 2011. Poles became one of the largest foreign-born groups in the United Kingdom, and Romanians and Bulgarians became numerous in Italy, Spain, Germany, and Britain. This movement differed from earlier migration in that it was legally unconstrained by immigration controls, often circular, and typically undertaken by citizens of the same Union. It also produced strong demographic effects on sending countries, where emigration of younger workers accelerated population decline, a subject returned to in Section 137.4.

Migration from outside Europe changed in composition as well. The old pattern of recruitment from the Mediterranean periphery gave way to more varied origins: South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, Latin America, and East Asia. Southern European states, notably Italy and Spain, became major destinations, with workers in agriculture, construction, domestic service, and care. Spain's immigrant population grew rapidly after 1998, drawing heavily on Latin America and Morocco. Conflicts in Iraq, Afghanistan, Somalia, Eritrea, and above all Syria generated flows of refugees, while family reunification, student migration, and skilled labor migration expanded alongside them. By the 2010s the typical migrant to a western European country could be a Polish nurse, a Syrian refugee, a Brazilian student, an Indian software engineer, or the Moroccan spouse of a long-resident citizen, and each belonged to a different legal category with different rights.

It is therefore essential to distinguish forms of movement. Voluntary labor migration responds to employment opportunities and is regulated by visa, work permit, or free movement rules. Family reunification and family formation, protected in European human rights law and Union law, now account for a large share of non-EU immigration in many countries. Forced displacement involves persons fleeing violence or persecution, and it is governed by the protection regimes examined in Sections 137.6 to 137.8. Return migration includes the movement of ethnic co-nationals and also of migrants going back to their countries of origin, which official statistics often capture poorly. Combining these into a single number of immigrants, a common feature of political debate, obscures the different legal frameworks, motives, and social consequences involved.

Section 137.3

Aging Europe: Fertility, Longevity, and Population Change

Population aging is the most predictable of Europe's demographic transformations, because it results from long-term and well-measured changes in two variables. The first is fertility. The total fertility rate is the average number of children a woman would have if she experienced the age-specific birth rates of a given year throughout her reproductive life. A rate of about 2.1 is conventionally regarded as replacement level in low-mortality societies. Eurostat reported that the EU's rate was 1.34 births per woman in 2024, down from 1.46 in 2004. The second variable is longevity. Life expectancy at birth has risen in most European countries across the whole postwar period, although rises were interrupted by the post-communist mortality crisis in parts of eastern Europe in the 1990s and by the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020.

These trends transform age structures. Eurostat's 2026 edition of Demography of Europe reports that the EU's median age, the age at which half the population is older and half younger, rose from 39.6 years in 2005 to 44.9 years in 2025, and that persons aged 65 and over accounted for about 22 percent of the population. Italy had the highest median age in 2025, at 49.1 years, followed by Bulgaria and Portugal at 47.3 and Greece at 47.2, while Ireland had the lowest, at 39.6. The share of people under 19 declined in every member state over the two decades. These are observed figures, not projections. Eurostat publishes harmonized EU-series aggregates, so students must check the country coverage used before comparing rates or populations across enlargement periods.

Several causes lie behind fertility decline, and historians and demographers weigh them differently. Education and employment for women, the availability of contraception, the rising cost of housing and child-rearing, the length of education, uncertain employment, and changing preferences about family size and partnership all play a role. Parenthood has been postponed: in the latest Eurostat data the youngest first-time mothers in the EU were found in Bulgaria, Romania, and Slovakia, with average ages of about 27, while in much of western and southern Europe the average is considerably higher. Household structures have changed too, with smaller households, more single-person households, later marriage, and higher rates of cohabitation and childbearing outside marriage. Fertility is not uniformly low: in 2024 Bulgaria recorded the EU's highest rate at 1.72, and France, Ireland, and Cyprus recorded the highest crude birth rates. The variation shows that no single national trajectory exists, and that family policy, labor markets, and cultural expectations shape outcomes.

Natural population change is the difference between births and deaths. In many eastern and southern European countries deaths now exceed births, so that population stability depends on migration. Eurostat reported that between 2005 and 2025 the EU's population grew by about 4 percent, but that eight member states recorded declines, most of them in central and eastern Europe. The EU as a whole has grown in recent years chiefly because of immigration, which offsets a surplus of deaths over births. The dependency ratio relates the number of persons outside working age to those within it. In its old-age form it measures how many persons aged 65 and over there are per hundred of working age, and its increase places pressure on pension systems, health care, and long-term care, even though measures such as raising retirement ages and increasing labor force participation may moderate that pressure.

Migration alone cannot reverse aging, and this point is often misunderstood in both directions. Immigrants tend to be younger than the resident population, so immigration slows the rise in median age and increases the working-age population in the short run. But immigrants age too, and their fertility typically converges toward that of the host population over time. Eurostat's long-term projections, discussed in Section 137.19, indicate that net migration flows as currently modeled are not sufficient to offset aging, even though they matter greatly for population size. Demographic change is therefore best understood not as a problem that migration solves or causes, but as a structural condition within which migration, family policy, retirement ages, productivity, and labor participation interact.

Section 137.4

Demographic Geography: Growing Cities and Declining Regions

National averages conceal sharp spatial divergence. Within most European countries, population has concentrated in metropolitan regions and a limited number of dynamic urban centers, while rural districts, small industrial towns, and peripheral regions have lost population. The pattern reflects the movement of young people toward education and employment, the concentration of services and high-skilled work in large cities, and the effects of aging in places left behind. Large cities such as London, Paris, Madrid, Berlin, Stockholm, Warsaw, and Vienna have grown through domestic migration, international migration, and, in some cases, relatively higher birth rates among younger residents. Many immigrants settle in cities, where networks of kin and compatriots, rental housing, and employment in services and construction offer access to livelihoods.

The demographic experience of eastern Europe differs from that of the west. Since 1989, many eastern European states have lost population through a combination of low fertility, emigration, and, in some cases, elevated mortality. Bulgaria, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, Croatia, and parts of Poland and eastern Germany lost a significant share of their populations, with the loss concentrated among young adults. The former East Germany provides a documented case: its population fell steeply after reunification as workers moved west and birth rates plunged, and the share of older residents increased in many districts. Saxony-Anhalt, for instance, had lost about a quarter of its population since 1990, according to reporting on the state's demographic history. Southern Europe presents another pattern of low fertility and rural depopulation, visible in inland Spain, southern Italy, and Greece, where small villages have emptied.

Population change alters infrastructure and public services in ways that become political. Growing cities face pressure on housing, transport, schools, and health services. Shrinking regions face the closure of schools, clinics, post offices, banks, and public transport, a decline in the tax base, and difficulty in recruiting doctors and teachers. In both settings, population change interacts with housing markets and public investment. A town losing young people may also lose the services that might attract them to stay, in a self-reinforcing cycle. Governments have responded with regional development funds, relocation of public institutions, incentives for rural doctors, and, in some cases, active recruitment of migrants to settle in declining areas, as in some Spanish and Italian municipalities and in programs for refugee settlement in rural districts of Germany and Sweden.

These patterns matter for political history because the experience of demographic change is unequal. A person in a growing metropolitan district may encounter immigration as part of daily life and ordinary employment, while a person in a depopulating district may encounter chiefly closures and emigration. Studies of electoral behavior in several countries have found that some of the strongest support for anti-immigration parties has come from places with relatively few immigrants but with experiences of economic decline, aging, and a loss of local institutions, an argument returned to in Division IV. Such findings do not establish that decline causes populism, since patterns vary by country and by election. They do show that the geography of demographic change must be part of any adequate explanation, and that the people who sit at the receiving end of migration policy are not always those who respond to it politically.

The contrast between immigrant destinations and sending regions also deserves emphasis. Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, and the Baltic states have, since 2004 and 2007, been sending countries within the EU, with consequences for local labor markets, remittances, and family structures, including the phenomenon of children raised by relatives while parents work abroad. After 2022 Poland and the Czech Republic became major hosts for displaced Ukrainians, reversing the east-to-west flow in a new form. The same state could therefore be, simultaneously, a country of emigration, a destination of immigration, and a country of transit. Categories such as sending and receiving states are useful but provisional, and they should be applied to specific periods, never to nations as permanent characteristics.

Section 137.5

Free Movement, Citizenship, and Immigration Policy

Public debate frequently speaks of "migration" as though it were one phenomenon governed by one set of rules. In the European Union it is governed by at least two. The first is the law of free movement, which applies to citizens of member states. The second is the law of immigration by nationals of non-member countries, which remains, to a large extent, a matter for national governments, subject to Union directives and to human rights law. The distinction explains why a Romanian citizen working in Germany and a Nigerian student in Germany have different legal rights, and why a decision to close the Union's external borders would not affect the former in the way it would affect the latter.

Free movement of workers was included in the Treaty of Rome of 1957, examined in Chapter 133, as a feature of the common market, and it was extended by later measures to students, retirees, and the economically inactive who have sufficient resources. The Maastricht Treaty of 1992, discussed in Chapter 134, created citizenship of the Union, which is held by every national of a member state in addition to, and not instead of, national citizenship. Directive 2004/38/EC consolidated the rights of Union citizens and their families to move and reside within the Union. Citizens may reside for up to three months with a valid identity document, may stay longer if they are workers, self-employed, students, or have sufficient resources and health insurance, and acquire a right of permanent residence after five years of lawful residence. The 2004 and 2007 enlargements gave these rights to citizens of the new members, subject to transitional restrictions that some older members applied for up to seven years.

The Schengen system is a related but separate arrangement. Beginning with an agreement among five states in 1985 and a convention in 1990, and entering into operation in 1995, it abolished systematic checks at internal borders for participating states and created common rules for external borders and short-stay visas. Schengen membership does not coincide with EU membership. Ireland and Cyprus, for example, have not fully joined, while Iceland, Norway, Switzerland, and Liechtenstein participate without being members of the Union. Bulgaria and Romania joined for air and sea borders in March 2024 and for land borders from 1 January 2025, after Croatia had joined on 1 January 2023. Internal controls have been temporarily reintroduced by several states at various times, for reasons including terrorism, the 2015 to 2016 refugee movements, the COVID-19 pandemic, and irregular migration, a practice that the Schengen Borders Code allows under conditions.

Immigration by non-EU nationals follows different logic. The Union has adopted directives on family reunification (2003), long-term residents (2003), students and researchers, highly qualified workers (the Blue Card, first adopted in 2009 and revised in 2021), and single permits. But Article 79 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union preserves the right of member states to determine the volumes of admission of third-country nationals seeking work. Governments therefore design their own labor migration systems, and they differ widely: some rely on employer sponsorship, some on points systems, some on shortage lists, and some on bilateral agreements with countries of origin. Family reunification, protected as part of the right to family life under the European Convention on Human Rights, has been a major channel of immigration, and governments have attempted to regulate it through income, housing, age, and language requirements that courts have sometimes reviewed.

Citizenship is a further and distinct stage. Residence, permanent residence, and citizenship convey different bundles of rights, and acquisition of nationality remains a national competence. The Court of Justice has held that member states determine who their nationals are, but must exercise that power with due regard to Union law, since national citizenship brings with it citizenship of the Union. Naturalization requirements vary in length of residence, language tests, civic knowledge, income, criminal record, and rules on dual nationality, as Section 137.9 shows. The United Kingdom's withdrawal from the Union, completed on 31 December 2020 and examined in Chapter 138, ended free movement between Britain and the Union, and it turned millions of citizens on each side into persons whose residence required documentation, an illustration of how contingent the rights of mobile citizens can be.

Historical Vocabulary
Free Movement, Schengen, and Union Citizenship

Free movement refers to the right of citizens of EU member states to move, reside, and work in another member state. The Schengen Area refers to the group of European states that have abolished systematic internal border checks. Union citizenship is a status derived from, and additional to, national citizenship. They overlap, but each has a different membership, a different legal basis, and a different history. Treating them as synonyms leads to errors, for example in attributing to "the EU" decisions that member states make about third-country nationals. They matter to this chapter because the political debate often assumes that free movement of EU citizens and irregular migration from outside Europe are aspects of the same policy, when in law and in fact they are not.

Section 137.6

Refugees, Asylum, and International Protection

The modern refugee regime is a product of the Second World War and the early Cold War. The Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees, adopted at Geneva on 28 July 1951, defined a refugee as a person who, owing to a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, is outside the country of nationality and unable or unwilling to return. The original definition applied to events occurring before 1 January 1951 and allowed states to limit it to events in Europe, a reflection of the postwar displacement of Europeans. The 1967 Protocol removed these temporal and geographical limits and made the convention universal. Almost all states, including all members of the European Union, are parties.

The convention's central safeguard is Article 33, the principle of non-refoulement. It prohibits a state from expelling or returning a refugee in any manner whatsoever to territories where life or freedom would be threatened on one of the protected grounds. The principle is also found in human rights law: the European Court of Human Rights has interpreted Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights, the prohibition of torture and inhuman or degrading treatment, as barring removal to a country where a person faces a real risk of such treatment. The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union guarantees the right to asylum. In important judgments the Strasbourg court held that Greece's reception and asylum conditions made transfers there under EU rules unlawful (M.S.S. v. Belgium and Greece, 2011) and that Italy had violated the convention by returning migrants intercepted at sea to Libya (Hirsi Jamaa and Others v. Italy, 2012).

Asylum is the procedure by which a state decides whether a person is entitled to protection. An asylum seeker is a person who has applied and awaits a decision. A refugee is a person recognized as meeting the convention definition, whether or not recognized formally. Many European states also grant subsidiary protection to people who do not meet the convention definition but who face serious harm, such as indiscriminate violence in armed conflict. The EU developed a Common European Asylum System from 1999, harmonizing standards on qualification, procedures, and reception, though implementation differed greatly among states. The Dublin system, which originated in a convention of 1990 and was revised in 2003 and 2013, assigns responsibility for examining an application, in most cases to the state of first entry. Its effect was to place heavy burdens on states at the Union's external borders and to encourage secondary movement onward.

Other categories are frequently confused with refugees. A migrant, in common usage, is a person who moves across a border, but international law has no single binding definition of the term, and the distinctions between voluntary and forced movement are sometimes unclear. An irregular migrant has entered or remains without authorization. An internally displaced person has fled home but remains within the borders of the state, and so does not count as a refugee under the convention. A beneficiary of temporary protection holds status under the Temporary Protection Directive of 2001, designed for situations of mass influx and first activated in March 2022. Each category carries different rights and obligations. Political and media debate often uses them interchangeably, and scholarly analysis must keep them apart.

Debate over asylum rests on competing principles. Humanitarian obligations, rooted in postwar experience and in European and international law, are binding on states, but the cost of reception, the difficulty of distinguishing protection claims from other motives for migration, the public's perception of fairness, and pressures on reception systems have led governments to seek to limit access. Critics of restriction point to the principle that protection cannot be conditioned on avoiding irregular routes, because for many refugees no legal route exists. Critics of the existing system argue that unrestricted access to asylum, combined with slow procedures and low rates of return after rejection, undermines public support. Historians need not resolve this argument, but they must record its terms accurately and identify who is making each claim.

Historical Vocabulary
Refugee, Asylum Seeker, Migrant, and Beneficiary of Temporary Protection

A refugee is a person who meets the definition in the 1951 Convention as amended by the 1967 Protocol. An asylum seeker has applied for protection but not yet received a final decision. A beneficiary of temporary protection holds a status granted collectively under the 2001 directive without individual examination. A migrant is a general term with no single legal definition. These are not stages of one progression: a person may move from one to another, as when a temporary protection beneficiary applies for asylum, but each category has its own rules. The terms matter because statistics counting them differently produce very different totals, and because political rhetoric can create the impression that all arrivals are asylum seekers, or that all asylum seekers are refugees.

Section 137.7

The Mediterranean and the Refugee Crisis of 2015 to 2016

The refugee movements of 2015 and 2016 were the result of converging developments. The Syrian civil war, which began in 2011, had displaced millions inside Syria and into Lebanon, Jordan, and Turkey, where reception capacity and international assistance deteriorated. Conflict and repression in Iraq, Afghanistan, Eritrea, and elsewhere added to the flows. In 2013 the drowning of several hundred migrants near Lampedusa had already drawn attention to the central Mediterranean route from Libya to Italy and Malta, and Italy had launched the naval Operation Mare Nostrum, followed by the more limited EU operations Triton and Sophia. In 2015 the principal change was the scale of crossings from Turkey to the Greek islands and onward through the Western Balkans to Austria, Germany, and Sweden.

Eurostat recorded 1,216,860 first-time asylum applicants in the EU in 2015, the highest figure in the series that begins in 2014. Syrians, Afghans, and Iraqis were the largest groups. Germany received the largest number of applications, and Sweden recorded, in proportion to its population, one of the highest rates. Greece and Italy were the principal points of arrival, and Hungary, which saw large transits, built a border fence along its frontier with Serbia and later Croatia in September and October 2015. Germany's chancellor, Angela Merkel, decided in late August 2015 to suspend Dublin transfers to Syrians and said on 31 August that the country would cope. The decision was greeted by volunteers and many ordinary citizens as an act of solidarity, and condemned by critics as an uncontrolled opening of borders, and both reactions are part of the historical record. Reception systems, designed for far smaller numbers, were overwhelmed in the short term, and municipalities, civil society groups, and churches improvised accommodation, language classes, and legal help.

Responses by governments diverged. The EU adopted a plan in September 2015 to relocate 120,000 asylum seekers from Greece and Italy to other member states, adopted by qualified majority over the opposition of Hungary, Czechia, Slovakia, and Romania. Implementation fell far short of the targets, and the Court of Justice later found that Hungary, Poland, and Czechia had failed to meet their obligations. The disagreement created a lasting division over responsibility sharing between states of first arrival, states preferring relocation quotas, and states rejecting mandatory distribution. The EU-Turkey statement of 18 March 2016 provided that migrants arriving irregularly in Greece could be returned to Turkey, in exchange for resettlement of Syrians from Turkey and financial assistance. Crossings in the Aegean dropped sharply, and the Western Balkan route was effectively closed by border closures in the same months. The statement has been praised as a pragmatic solution and criticized for making the protection of refugees depend on a third state and for the conditions on the Greek islands, where reception centers such as Moria on Lesbos became notoriously overcrowded before they were destroyed by fire in September 2020.

The central Mediterranean route continued to be dangerous. Estimates of deaths and disappearances at sea, recorded by international organizations, run to many thousands in single years during the peak period, and the figures are uncertain because many deaths go unrecorded. Search-and-rescue became a political issue: nongovernmental organizations operated rescue vessels, and Italian governments from 2017 onward adopted codes of conduct, restricted port access, and supported the Libyan coast guard, while critics alleged that such measures increased the danger to migrants and facilitated abuse in Libyan detention. Supporters of these policies argued that rescue operations encouraged departures and that cooperation with Libya reduced deaths by deterring crossings. The evidence on such causal claims is contested, and students should treat them as arguments to be tested rather than as facts.

Political and security concerns accompanied the humanitarian dimension. The attacks in Paris in November 2015 were followed by public debate about whether terrorists were entering Europe among refugees. Investigators found that a small number of the attackers had traveled through Greece using false documents, but that most of those identified were citizens of France and Belgium. The assaults on women in Cologne and other German cities on New Year's Eve 2015 to 2016 led to a national controversy over the conduct of some men, many of North African origin, as well as over police and media responses. These episodes were used in political arguments across Europe, and they illustrate why historical analysis must separate the demonstrable events from the claims made about them.

Those who lived through the crisis experienced it differently. Refugees faced dangerous journeys, smugglers, detention, uncertain status, long waits, and, in many cases, family separation. Residents of receiving communities included volunteers who organized welcome, local officials who coped with sudden demands, and citizens who felt anxious about resources, security, and cultural change. Communities on the Greek islands, among the poorest in the Union, bore unexpected burdens for years. Hungarian, Polish, and other governments argued that the crisis showed the limits of the Union's ability to protect its borders, and Germany's government argued that it demonstrated the need for common responsibility. No single perspective captures the whole.

Section 137.8

Displacement and European Migration Policy, 2020 to 2026

The early 2020s tested European migration governance in new ways. The COVID-19 pandemic temporarily closed borders and suspended or slowed asylum procedures in 2020. In 2021 Belarus, in a confrontation with the Union, facilitated the movement of migrants from the Middle East to its borders with Lithuania, Latvia, and Poland, and the EU described this as an instrumentalization of migration. Poland erected a barrier and imposed restrictions along the frontier, and nongovernmental organizations and humanitarian bodies reported deaths and the denial of access to asylum. The episode showed that migration could be used as a means of political pressure, and it influenced later legislation on crisis situations.

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 produced the largest movement of people into the Union since 1945. On 4 March 2022 the Council of the European Union activated the Temporary Protection Directive of 2001 for the first time, granting Ukrainian nationals and certain others fleeing the war immediate residence, work, education, and access to accommodation and social assistance without the need to apply for asylum. Reception differed from that of 2015 in several respects: it was rapid, it was based on a collective legal status, it was supported across political lines in states that had resisted relocation quotas, and the arriving population was predominantly women, children, and older people, since men of military age were generally not permitted to leave Ukraine. Poland, Czechia, Germany, Moldova, and Romania, and many households and municipalities, hosted and assisted large numbers. Observers noted the contrast with the treatment of some other refugees and drew comparisons that were widely debated.

The status has proved durable. The Council extended it successively, including through Implementing Decision (EU) 2025/1460 of 15 July 2025 until 4 March 2027. It then adopted Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1912 on 30 July 2026, published on 4 August, extending temporary protection until 4 March 2028. Article 2 of that decision requires new beneficiaries, where applicable, to demonstrate compliance with Ukrainian military obligations; the requirement does not apply to persons who held temporary protection by 30 July 2026 and retain it continuously in the same member state. The requirement is not restricted by the EU legal text to men of a particular age. Eurostat recorded 4.43 million beneficiaries on 31 July 2026, and its latest release available by this chapter's cutoff counted 4.37 million on 30 September 2026; both are stocks, not new arrivals. Some states have narrowed access and benefits as the war lengthened, and debate has centered on how to transition from collective protection to other statuses, and on how to balance integration of those likely to remain with the expectation of return. This chapter treats only the displacement and legal protection. The war's military, diplomatic, and security history belongs to Chapter 139.

Documentary verification: Council Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1912, Articles 1–3; Eurostat, 10 November 2026 release reporting September stocks. These confirm the extension, its limited applicability to previously registered beneficiaries, and the latest count available at the cutoff.

Other migratory pressures continued. The central Mediterranean remained the principal sea route, with fluctuating numbers, while the Atlantic route to the Canary Islands and routes into the Balkans and across the eastern land border also saw shifting traffic. The EU concluded partnership agreements with countries of transit and origin, including Tunisia in 2023 and Egypt in 2024, linking financial support to migration cooperation, and Italy concluded a protocol with Albania in 2023 for offshore processing, which faced legal and practical difficulties. The fall of the Assad government in Syria in December 2024 raised questions about the status of Syrian refugees and led several states to pause some decisions and discuss return. These arrangements drew praise from governments that regard them as effective border management and criticism from rights organizations that argue they transfer responsibility and risk to places with weaker protections.

The legislative centerpiece was the Pact on Migration and Asylum. After the failure of earlier attempts to reform the Dublin system, the Commission proposed the Pact in September 2020, and the Parliament and Council agreed on ten legal acts, adopted by the Council on 14 May 2024. They include a Screening Regulation, an expanded Eurodac database, an Asylum Procedures Regulation with a mandatory border procedure for certain applicants, a Qualification Regulation, a revised Reception Conditions Directive, an Asylum and Migration Management Regulation that replaces Dublin, and a Crisis and Force Majeure Regulation. The central compromise is a system of "mandatory solidarity but flexible contributions": member states must contribute to an annual solidarity pool through relocation, financial payments, or operational support, while states of first entry retain the principal responsibility for screening and processing. The Pact entered into application on 12 June 2026 after a two-year transition, and the Commission described it as a robust and balanced framework while acknowledging that implementation would continue. On 29 January 2026 it presented the first European Asylum and Migration Management Strategy for 2026 to 2031, and the proposal for a Return Regulation remained under negotiation.

As of the chapter's cutoff, the Pact has been in operation for only five months, and its effects cannot be assessed historically. What can be said is that it expresses competing objectives. Border management aims to know who enters and to process rapidly those with low prospects of protection. Humanitarian protection requires access to fair procedures and safeguards for vulnerable people. State responsibility reflects governments' claim to control admission and to maintain public confidence. European solidarity asks states to share burdens that fall unevenly. Supporters regard the Pact as the first workable compromise among these aims, while critics, including many humanitarian organizations, argue that the border procedures risk detention-like conditions and weaken protection, and some governments object to the solidarity provisions as an infringement of national discretion. Its success will depend on implementation, on courts, and on conditions beyond Europe's control.

Section 137.9

Citizenship, National Identity, and the Meaning of Integration

Scholars often contrast a civic tradition of nationality, in which membership rests on residence, adherence to political principles, and participation, with an ethnic tradition, in which it rests on descent and shared culture. The contrast is a useful starting point but should not be treated as a permanent national character. France is the classic example of the civic model, shaped by the Revolution of 1789 examined in Chapter 88, and Germany of the ethnic model, shaped by nineteenth-century nationalism and a law of 1913 that based citizenship on descent. Both countries, however, have changed their laws repeatedly, and each has contained elements of the other. The historian's task is to follow the changes rather than assign each country a fixed type.

France combined a long-standing tradition of birthright citizenship, the principle of jus soli, with a republican ideal that distinguishes the citizen from particular origins, and officially collects little data on ethnicity or religion. Its integration policy has emphasized assimilation into a common civic culture and the secular school. Conflicts over its limits have recurred, from the legal restrictions on nationality acquisition in the 1990s to the urban riots of 2005 and 2023 and the law of 2021 on respect for the principles of the Republic. Germany remained for decades officially a country that was not a country of immigration. Its reform of 2000 granted citizenship at birth to children of long-resident foreign parents, introduced the principle of jus soli in a limited form, and reduced the residence needed for naturalization. A further reform, in force from 27 June 2024, generally permitted dual nationality and shortened the standard period of residence to five years. The coalition that took office in 2025 moved to end the accelerated three-year route introduced in 2024, which shows that nationality law remains subject to electoral alternation.

Britain developed what was often called a multicultural approach, defined less by a theory than by anti-discrimination law, accommodations for religious and cultural practice, and support for community organizations. After disturbances in several northern English towns in 2001, an official review described communities living "parallel lives," and policy turned toward community cohesion, citizenship tests, and language requirements. The Life in the UK test and a citizenship ceremony were introduced in the 2000s. The London bombings of July 2005 and subsequent debates intensified the shift, and in 2011 Prime Minister David Cameron declared that state multiculturalism had failed. Britain's Commonwealth heritage also meant that many migrants arrived with citizenship, and later restrictions and the Windrush scandal of 2018, in which long-resident Caribbean-origin people were wrongly treated as unlawfully present, showed how fragile that status could be.

The Netherlands moved from a policy of supporting minority institutions, which grew out of its tradition of religious and social "pillars," to an emphasis on civic integration. The murder of the politician Pim Fortuyn in 2002 and the film-maker Theo van Gogh in 2004 brought sharp public debate. Civic integration examinations, including one taken abroad before arrival, were introduced from 2006. Sweden adopted in 1975 a policy built on equality, freedom of choice, and partnership, an explicit multicultural commitment. After the large intake of asylum seekers in 2015, which was among the highest in Europe relative to its population, and persistent problems of employment and segregation, policy tightened considerably, and the governing agreement of 2022 between the center-right parties and the Sweden Democrats emphasized stricter rules, language, and employment. These cases show that every model is historical: each country has shifted between policies of accommodation, expectation of assimilation, and restriction.

Terminology matters. Integration is used to mean different things: participation in employment, education, and public life; acquisition of language; acceptance of legal norms; or cultural conformity. Assimilation implies the disappearance of difference. Multiculturalism may describe a social fact of diversity or a set of policies that recognize group identities, and its critics and defenders often argue past each other. Interculturalism, preferred by the Council of Europe and some cities, emphasizes interaction. The same policy may be described by one speaker as respect for diversity and by another as abdication. Scholars generally agree that outcomes in education, employment, and political participation depend on structural factors such as labor markets, schooling systems, residential patterns, and discrimination, as well as on policy labels.

Everyday Life and Social History

Becoming a Citizen: Documents, Tests, and Ceremonies

For individuals, naturalization is a bureaucratic process lasting years. Applicants typically assemble proof of continuous residence, employment or income, language ability, a clean criminal record, and, in some countries, knowledge of history and institutions, then pay fees, wait for decisions, and, in many states, attend a ceremony and swear an oath. Documentary requirements may be demanding for those who have lost papers in flight or whose countries of origin do not issue them easily. Language examinations can be a barrier for older applicants and those with limited schooling.

Citizenship changes lives in concrete ways. It confers the vote in national elections, access to public-sector employment, protection against expulsion, and, for citizens of EU states, the rights of Union citizenship and a passport that allows travel and settlement. Many applicants have emphasized in surveys and oral histories mixed feelings: pride, relief, and sometimes grief at renouncing an earlier nationality where dual nationality was not permitted. Established residents also experience the process, as neighbors, employers, teachers, and sometimes as officials. Rules on dual nationality, which differ widely, are one reason why naturalization rates vary across countries with comparable immigrant populations.

Section 137.10

Islam in Modern Europe: History, Diversity, and Community Life

Islam is not a recent arrival in European history. As earlier chapters have shown, Muslim rule in Iberia and Sicily shaped the medieval Mediterranean, and Chapters 41 and 79 examined the Ottoman Empire as a major power in southeastern Europe. Following Ottoman expansion in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, substantial Muslim populations developed in the Balkans, in what are now Bosnia and Herzegovina, Albania, Kosovo, North Macedonia, and Bulgaria. Albania has a Muslim-majority population, and Bosnia's population includes a large Bosniak Muslim community. Tatar Muslim communities have lived in Lithuania and Poland since the late Middle Ages, and the Habsburg monarchy gave legal recognition to Islam in 1912 after annexing Bosnia. Muslims of the Balkans have experienced empire, nation-building, communist secularization, and the violence of the 1990s, including the genocide at Srebrenica in July 1995, discussed in Chapter 135.

The Muslim populations of western and northern Europe have mostly origins in migration since the 1950s. Turks and Kurds in Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, and Belgium; Algerians, Moroccans, and Tunisians in France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Spain; Pakistanis, Bangladeshis, and others in Britain; and later Somalis, Iraqis, Syrians, and Afghans in Scandinavia, Germany, and elsewhere form distinct communities. They are divided by country of origin, language, ethnicity, class, generation, region, and religious tradition. They include Sunni and Shia Muslims, Alevis, Ahmadis, Sufi orders, devout believers, cultural Muslims, and nonbelievers of Muslim background. To speak of "European Muslims" as a single population is therefore misleading, and so is the assumption that religious identity is the main characteristic of people who share it.

Religious institutions developed gradually. First-generation migrants often prayed in improvised spaces, while later purpose-built mosques and associations were organized along national and linguistic lines, sometimes with links to the religious authorities or governments of countries of origin, such as Turkey's directorate of religious affairs. States sought interlocutors, creating bodies such as the French Council of the Muslim Faith in 2003 and the German Islam Conference from 2006. Governments and communities debated the training of imams in Europe, religious instruction in public schools, halal slaughter, burial rules, and the funding of mosques. Educational and social participation varied by country and group. Many second- and third-generation Europeans of Muslim background have moved into professions, politics, media, and the arts, while others face disadvantages in education and employment that surveys link to discrimination, residence, and family circumstances.

Generational differences are significant. Many children of migrants combine national identities with religious ones, and relations between the generations over language, marriage, religious practice, and gender have been sources of both tension and creative adaptation. A few young people have been drawn into violent extremism, and attacks in the 2000s and 2010s, including Madrid in 2004, London in 2005, Paris and Brussels in 2015 and 2016, caused deaths and fear. The overwhelming majority of Muslims have never been associated with such violence, and many have been among the victims. Historians should record both the reality of terrorism and the distortion that arises when a violent minority is used to characterize a whole population.

Section 137.11

Secularism, Religious Freedom, and the Public Sphere

European states have very different arrangements between religion and the state, which developed through the confessional conflicts examined in Part X and the revolutions of Part XIV. In France, the law of 1905 separated church and state, and laïcité became a principle of state neutrality and a civic ideal, especially in public schools. In Germany, the Basic Law guarantees freedom of religion while treating recognized religious communities as public-law bodies entitled in many cases to collect church tax and to provide religious education in state schools, a model sometimes called cooperative. In England, the Church of England remains established, while the law accommodates many faiths. In Scandinavia, former state churches were disestablished or reformed only recently, as in Sweden in 2000. In Greece, Orthodoxy has a constitutional position, and the status of the Muslim minority of Thrace was defined by the Treaty of Lausanne.

When Muslim communities became large, these arrangements met new demands. In France, the 1989 dispute over three girls wearing headscarves at a school in Creil became a national controversy, and in 2004 a law prohibited conspicuous religious symbols in public schools, followed in 2010 by a law prohibiting covering the face in public places. The European Court of Human Rights upheld the face-covering ban in 2014 in S.A.S. v. France, accepting the argument of "living together" while critics argued that it was too vague. In Germany, states passed laws on teachers' headscarves, and the Federal Constitutional Court held in 2015 that a general ban required a concrete danger. In 2009 Swiss voters approved a constitutional ban on building minarets. Denmark, Austria, Belgium, and the Netherlands have adopted restrictions on face coverings in public places. In several cases the Court of Justice of the EU has ruled that private employers may enforce neutrality policies under conditions of consistency and necessity.

The debate concerns different principles. Supporters of strict state neutrality argue that it protects equal citizenship, freedom of conscience, and women's autonomy by keeping religion out of public institutions and guarding against pressure from religious groups. Critics respond that neutrality can function as a majority culture's standard in disguise, that bans fall disproportionately on Muslim women, and that they restrict the freedom of religion that European human rights law protects. Defenders of cooperative or pluralist models argue that recognizing religious communities as partners integrates them into public life. Others emphasize that Christian traditions continue to shape public holidays, calendars, and funding, so that neutrality is rarely complete. The debate has also touched Jewish and Sikh practices, circumcision, ritual slaughter, and the place of Christian symbols, which shows that it is not solely about Islam.

Public attitudes to religion within Europe are themselves changing. Church attendance and formal affiliation have declined in most western and northern European countries, as Chapter 140 will examine in a long-term perspective, while religious identification remains strong in Poland, Romania, Greece, and parts of southeastern Europe. Many Europeans who are not religious regard religion as a private matter, and some perceive visible religiosity, particularly among minorities, as unusual. The result is that arguments about migration and diversity frequently take the form of arguments about secularism, though the underlying concerns may involve class, security, gender, and national identity.

Historical Debate

Does State Neutrality Protect Freedom or Restrict It?

The question: Do restrictions on religious dress and symbols in public institutions, justified in the name of secularism or neutrality, protect equal citizenship and individual freedom, or do they discriminate against religious minorities and limit freedom of religion?

Interpretation A: Neutrality protects a shared civic space. Defenders, particularly in the French republican tradition, argue that a public sphere without religious markers allows citizens of all beliefs to participate as equals, shields pupils and employees from pressure, and prevents the state from favoring any faith. They point to the historical struggle in France against clerical control of education and to the principle that religion is a matter of conscience, not of public status.
Interpretation B: Restrictions burden minorities and narrow liberty. Critics argue that rules framed neutrally have unequal effects, reflect suspicion of Muslims in particular, and undermine the autonomy of women who choose to dress as they wish. They cite the guarantees of religious freedom in European human rights law, the more accommodating practices of Britain and Germany, and evidence that restrictions have reduced participation in education or employment for some women.

Evidence: Both sides draw on court judgments, legislative debates, the experiences of those affected, surveys, and comparisons across countries. The evidence on outcomes, such as the effect of the 2004 French law on school attendance or integration, is contested and often limited by the available data. Historians must also distinguish stated purposes from effects and from the motives of different supporters, who included feminists, secularists, and those hostile to Islam.

Why it matters: How the question is answered shapes interpretations of whether European secularism is a neutral framework or a particular national tradition, and whether integration is better understood as equal participation within common rules or as accommodation of difference.

Section 137.12

Multiculturalism, Discrimination, and the Debate over Belonging

Diversity has been accompanied by discrimination, and its forms deserve precise description. Racism in Europe has older roots in the imperial and colonial systems examined in Chapters 103 to 105, and it affects people of African, Caribbean, South Asian, Middle Eastern, and Roma background. In Britain, the murder of Stephen Lawrence in 1993 and the inquiry that followed produced the finding in 1999 that the Metropolitan Police was institutionally racist, a landmark in public understanding. In Germany, the murders committed by the National Socialist Underground between 2000 and 2007, mostly of people of Turkish origin, were for years misattributed by investigators to criminal motives in migrant communities, and an attack in Hanau in February 2020 killed nine people with migrant backgrounds. The Racial Equality Directive of 2000 required member states to prohibit discrimination on grounds of racial or ethnic origin in employment, education, and access to goods and services.

Antisemitism persisted and changed form. Postwar Europe had committed to confronting the legacy of the Holocaust, examined in Chapter 121, and Jewish communities rebuilt, but antisemitic incidents, including attacks on synagogues, schools, and individuals, continued, from far-right and Islamist sources and from conspiracy thinking across the political spectrum. Following the attacks of 7 October 2023 in Israel and the war in Gaza, Jewish organizations and national authorities in several countries reported marked increases in incidents. Anti-Muslim prejudice similarly appeared in street harassment, discrimination in hiring and housing, attacks on mosques, and political rhetoric. Roma, who number in the millions across Europe, continued to face particular exclusion in housing, education, and employment, and the language of "anti-Gypsyism" was adopted by European institutions to name it. Historians should treat these forms of prejudice as distinct, with different histories and sources, while recognizing that they sometimes coexist and that political debate may pit communities against each other.

Immigrants and their descendants were never merely objects of policy. In the 1970s, Turkish workers at the Ford factory in Cologne struck in 1973, and South Asian women at the Grunwick film-processing plant in London led a long dispute in 1976 to 1978 over union recognition. In France, the March for Equality and Against Racism in 1983, sometimes called the Beur march, and the organization SOS Racisme from 1984, put the claims of the children of migrants on the national agenda. Trade unions, mosques, churches, migrant associations, student groups, and local councils became channels of participation. Writers, filmmakers, musicians, athletes, and chefs reshaped national cultures, as in the work of Fatih Akin in Germany, Zadie Smith in Britain, or the footballers who became national symbols. In politics, descendants of immigrants entered parliaments and held high office, including Sadiq Khan as Mayor of London from 2016 and Rishi Sunak as British Prime Minister from 2022 to 2024. Their presence changed institutions and debates over who counts as a member of the nation.

Integration outcomes can be assessed through indicators of language, education, employment, housing, health, political participation, and intermarriage, and the evidence shows variation among countries, groups, and generations. In many places the second generation has achieved higher education and occupational mobility relative to their parents, while in some countries, groups of migrant background remain disadvantaged in school tracking, hiring, or neighborhood quality. Public attitudes also changed, and surveys across Europe show increasingly diverse views, including large shares supportive of diversity in some countries and substantial concern in others, with strong differences by age, education, and region. Historians should be cautious about treating survey responses as stable beliefs, since they respond to events and to political framing.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Belongs? Perspectives on Citizenship and National Identity

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical syntheses of patterns documented in surveys, oral histories, and public debate. They are not quotations from named individuals, and no group is homogeneous.

From the perspective of a third-generation citizen of Turkish descent in Germany: Born in the country and educated in its schools, she regards herself as German and is frequently asked where she is "really" from. Her citizenship is secure in law, but her sense of belonging depends on whether others treat it as secure.
From the perspective of a long-established resident of a shrinking town: He sees the closing of the local school, clinic, and bus route and feels that national politics ignores his region. He may have little direct contact with immigrants, yet may regard immigration as a symbol of a wider sense that distant elites make decisions about his community.
From the perspective of a recent refugee awaiting a decision: Her immediate concerns are legal status, housing, language, and the safety of relatives. She often has less power than any other actor in the debate, and her fate depends on rules and decisions made by governments and courts.
From the perspective of a member of a Jewish or Muslim community: Each community has experienced heightened vigilance and rising prejudice, and members are often asked to speak for a diverse population. Many want to be recognized as citizens whose religion is one dimension of their lives, and many emphasize solidarity across communities while others feel it is under strain.
From the perspective of a municipal official or teacher: Responsible for implementing policy in schools, housing offices, and clinics, such a person deals with practical questions of language, resources, and trust and may be skeptical of both slogans and statistics from national debate.

Note on balance: These perspectives do not have equal power, and they do not carry equal responsibility for outcomes. The purpose is to show that the same development, demographic change, was experienced very differently according to legal status, place, and resources.

Nationalism, Populism, and European Political Realignment

The postwar far right, the contemporary populist parties, political communication, and the responses of the mainstream.

Section 137.13

The Historical Roots of Postwar Nationalism and the Far Right

After 1945, open fascism was discredited by the defeat of Italy and Germany and by the revelations of the Holocaust, as Chapters 115 and 121 explain. Far-right politics did not disappear, but it was marginalized and reorganized. In Italy, former fascists formed the Italian Social Movement in 1946, which remained a pariah in national politics for decades. In West Germany, the Socialist Reich Party was banned in 1952 as a successor to the Nazi Party, and the National Democratic Party, founded in 1964, won seats in some state parliaments in the late 1960s without breaking through nationally. In Britain, the National Front emerged in 1967, and in 1968 the Conservative politician Enoch Powell delivered a speech opposing immigration that led to his dismissal from the shadow cabinet while winning wide public sympathy. In France, the Poujadist movement of the 1950s had mobilized shopkeepers and small proprietors in protest against taxation and the modernizing state, and the Algerian War, examined in Chapter 128, left a legacy of bitterness among some veterans and settlers.

From the 1970s and 1980s a different kind of party appeared. The National Front in France, founded in 1972 under Jean-Marie Le Pen, achieved its first electoral breakthroughs in local elections in 1983 and in the European elections of 1984, with a program centered on immigration, law and order, and national identity. The Flemish Vlaams Blok in Belgium, founded in 1978, became prominent in Antwerp. The Freedom Party of Austria, founded in 1956 with a membership that included former Nazis, was transformed after 1986 under Jörg Haider into a national-populist party, and in 2000 it entered government, which provoked bilateral diplomatic measures by fourteen other member states against Austria. Scandinavian progress parties began in the 1970s as tax protests and moved toward immigration as a central issue. In 2002 Jean-Marie Le Pen reached the second round of the French presidential election, where he was defeated overwhelmingly by Jacques Chirac in a broad republican front, an event that remained a reference point in later debates.

These developments call for careful distinctions. Neo-fascism describes movements that explicitly claim continuity with interwar fascism, glorify violence or authoritarian rule, reject liberal democracy, and often harbor open racism or antisemitism. Such groups have existed but have rarely won broad electoral support. The radical right or national populist parties that gained votes from the 1980s generally accept elections and multiparty competition in form, while combining nativism, the belief that the nation should be composed of natives and that outsiders threaten it, with hostility to established elites and a stress on law and order. Conventional conservatism accepts the liberal-democratic framework and emphasizes tradition, order, and the market, although it may also support restrictions on immigration. The categories overlap, parties change, and individuals move between them, and a party may include members of each type. Scholars debate how to classify specific parties, and the terminology itself, including "far right," "populist," and "extreme," is politically contested.

After 1989, new nationalist parties arose in postcommunist states, often drawing on grievances about national minorities, border changes, or economic transition. They included movements in Slovakia, Romania, Serbia, and Croatia in the 1990s, the last two discussed in connection with Chapter 135, and, later, Hungary's Jobbik. Their experience differed from the western pattern, because the debate in central and eastern Europe concerned national sovereignty, memory of Soviet domination, minorities, and, from 2015, the refusal of relocation quotas, more than immigration from former colonies. The continuities across the whole postwar period are therefore real but partial, and a unitary "far right" explanation obscures national histories.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Postwar Marginality to Electoral Competition, 1945 to 2026

What Changed

  • Parties once treated as pariahs entered national governments in Austria, Italy, Hungary, Finland, the Netherlands, and elsewhere, and became the largest party in Austria in 2024
  • Explicit appeals to racial hierarchy were replaced in most major parties by claims about culture, identity, security, and sovereignty
  • The principal target shifted from European Jews and colonial migrants to Muslims and asylum seekers, while antisemitism persisted in other forms
  • Attitudes to the European project shifted from rejection toward efforts to change it from within, though with exceptions

What Stayed the Same

  • The core themes of national belonging, hostility to established elites, and the call to restore order and sovereignty recurred over decades
  • Support remained concentrated in particular social groups and regions, though the geography has shifted
  • Mainstream parties continued to disagree on whether to isolate or engage such parties

Who experienced change most directly? Minority communities, who were the objects of these parties' policies, and the established parties whose electorates were eroded. Who experienced greater continuity? Voters in regions of long-term economic and demographic decline, whose grievances predated the arrival of the new parties. Why the pattern matters: It shows that contemporary populism is neither a simple revival of fascism nor an entirely new phenomenon.

Section 137.14

The Rise of Right-Wing Populism in Contemporary Europe

The contemporary parties vary widely, and comparison shows more about the differences than the similarities. France's National Rally, renamed in 2018 from the National Front, was led from 2011 by Marine Le Pen, who pursued what was called "de-demonization," distancing the party from the antisemitic and openly extremist image of its founder, whom she expelled in 2015. She reached the second round of the presidential election in 2017 and 2022, winning about 34 percent and 41.5 percent against Emmanuel Macron. The party finished first in France in the 2024 European election with 31.37 percent. In the June–July 2024 snap National Assembly election, the New Popular Front left alliance won the most seats (approximately 182 under a commonly used grouping), followed by the centrist Ensemble alliance (about 168) and the National Rally with its allies (about 143). RN remained the largest individual party, but its alliance was not the largest bloc, and estimates vary slightly with party classifications. In March 2025 a Paris criminal court convicted Marine Le Pen of misuse of European Parliament funds and imposed an immediately operative ineligibility sanction. On 7 July 2026 the Paris Court of Appeal upheld the conviction but reduced the sanction, including a 45-month disqualification of which 30 months were suspended; the already-served 15-month period left her eligible to run. Le Pen appealed further to the Cour de Cassation, so the legal process was not finally concluded as of this chapter's cutoff.

The Freedom Party of Austria, led from 2017 by Herbert Kickl, finished first in the Austrian parliamentary election of September 2024 with about 29 percent, but the other major parties formed a government without it in early 2025. In Germany, the Alternative for Germany was founded in 2013 around opposition to eurozone bailouts, and it shifted toward immigration and national identity after 2015. It entered the Bundestag in 2017 with 12.6 percent and in the federal election of February 2025 won about 20.8 percent, becoming the second-largest party. Germany's domestic intelligence service classified parts of the party, and in 2025 the whole party, as extremist, with the latter designation suspended pending court review. The party's strength is concentrated in the east: on 6 September 2026 it won the Saxony-Anhalt state election with 43.8 percent, its best result in any state election, though it fell short of a majority, and on 20 September it also finished first in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania with 38.2 percent. The same weekend, in Berlin, the Left party finished first and the AfD did much less well, which illustrates how sharply outcomes differ within a single country.

In Italy, the League, which began as a northern regionalist party, was transformed under Matteo Salvini into a national party opposed to immigration and to European fiscal rules. Brothers of Italy, descended from the post-fascist tradition, won about 26 percent in September 2022, and its leader Giorgia Meloni became prime minister in October 2022. Her government combined restrictive migration policies, support for Ukraine, and membership in NATO and the Union, which differed markedly from expectations of confrontation with Brussels. In Sweden, the Sweden Democrats, whose origins lay in the 1980s extremist milieu, expelled openly extremist members in the 2000s and entered parliament in 2010. They won about 20 percent in 2022 and became the second-largest party, and under the 2022 Tidö agreement they supported a center-right government without sitting in cabinet. In the election of 13 September 2026 their support fell to about 17.5 percent, their first decline since entering parliament, and the centre-left bloc won a narrow majority. In the Netherlands, Geert Wilders's Party for Freedom became the largest party in 2023 and led a coalition government in 2024, but the coalition collapsed in 2025 and the party lost seats in the election of October 2025.

Hungary and Poland present a different pattern, because their governing parties combined national-conservative cultural policy with attacks on institutions. Fidesz under Viktor Orbán governed Hungary from 2010, adopting a self-described "illiberal" model, and it campaigned against immigration and against the European Union's relocation scheme. In the parliamentary election of 12 April 2026, however, Orbán's rule ended: the opposition Tisza party under Péter Magyar won 141 of 199 seats with about 53 percent of the list vote, and Fidesz won 52 seats with about 39 percent. Law and Justice governed Poland from 2015 to 2023 and conflicted with the Commission over judicial independence, was replaced by a coalition under Donald Tusk, and retained significant influence through the presidency, won in 2025 by Karol Nawrocki. The Hungarian result in particular shows that national-conservative governments can be voted out, and it warns against narratives of an uninterrupted populist advance.

The parties differ in ideology, electoral strategy, relations with Europe, and conduct in institutions. Some, such as the National Rally, have embraced welfare protection for natives, while others, such as the early AfD, favored market liberalism. Most no longer demand withdrawal from the Union or the euro, preferring to reform it from within. Their conduct in office also varies: Meloni's government has largely operated within EU rules, whereas Orbán's government was repeatedly in conflict with the Union over the rule of law. Their positions on Russia and Ukraine have divided them, since some have been sympathetic to Moscow and others strongly critical, a subject for Chapter 139. These parties cannot be understood as one movement. They share some themes, notably nativism and anti-elitism, but they pursue different goals within different institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Populism

Populism is a contested term. Many political scientists define it as a thin ideology that divides society into two antagonistic camps, a virtuous "people" and a corrupt "elite," and claims that politics should express the will of the people. It can be combined with left-wing or right-wing programs, and several left-wing populist parties have also existed in Europe. The term is often used as a political insult and so has lost precision. Right-wing populism typically adds nativism and an emphasis on national identity. Historical analysis should specify the party, its program, and its conduct, not rely on the label. Populism matters in this chapter because it is often assumed to be a direct response to immigration, whereas the evidence shows that it also draws on distrust of institutions, regional inequality, and economic insecurity.

Section 137.15

Media, Political Communication, and the Politics of Fear

Political arguments about migration take place in a media environment, and that environment has changed profoundly. In the postwar decades, national newspapers, public service broadcasters, and, in some countries, partisan press dominated public information. Commercial television and tabloid newspapers expanded in the 1980s and 1990s, and from the 2000s the internet, and later social media platforms, allowed parties and individuals to reach audiences without editorial gatekeeping. Platforms' algorithms, designed to maximize engagement, tend to favor emotionally arousing content. Parties of all kinds have adopted these tools, and populist parties have often used them skillfully, including, in 2024, short-video platforms to reach younger voters.

Electoral framing of immigration has linked it to several themes: terrorism, crime, welfare costs, housing and wage competition, cultural change, and national sovereignty. Such framing has taken many forms. Hungarian government billboard campaigns in 2015 targeted migrants and the Union. The British "Breaking Point" poster during the Brexit referendum campaign of 2016, showing a column of refugees, was condemned by many and reported to the police. Left and liberal parties also frame immigration, emphasizing humanitarian duty, economic contribution, or demographic need, and mainstream parties of the center-right and center-left have adopted language and policies once associated with the radical right. All these are political claims, designed to persuade, and historians should read them as evidence of political strategy and not as accounts of reality.

The relation between perception and fact deserves particular attention. Surveys in several countries have found that publics tend to overestimate the proportion of immigrants in the population and, in some cases, the proportion of Muslims. Perceptions of crime and security are shaped by media coverage and individual experience as much as by statistics, and official crime data are themselves complicated by differences in reporting, in how nationality is recorded, and in the age and social profile of groups being compared. This chapter therefore does not rely on single statistics about crime and immigration, and students should be cautious about any such figures without a clear source, definition, and comparison group. The distinction between a demonstrable event, a political claim about it, and a public perception of it is central to historical method in this field.

Disinformation has become an explicit concern. The disorder across England and Northern Ireland in the summer of 2024 followed the murder of three children in Southport on 29 July 2024 and the rapid online spread of false claims about the identity of the attacker, who was born in Britain, and it included attacks on mosques and on accommodation housing asylum seekers. In Romania, the Constitutional Court annulled the first round of the presidential election on 6 December 2024 amid allegations of coordinated online manipulation and irregular campaign financing in favor of a candidate who had performed unexpectedly well, a decision that was criticized by some as an infringement of democratic choice and supported by others as necessary to protect the election. The European Union adopted the Digital Services Act in 2022 to impose obligations on large platforms. Such episodes demonstrate the capacity of online communication to mobilize anger, but also the difficulty of regulating speech in democratic societies.

The politics of fear can work in more than one direction: fear of migrants, but also fear of the far right, fear of economic decline, fear of war, and fear of terrorism. Historians should avoid explaining political behavior by attributing it solely to manipulation, as this treats voters as passive. They should equally avoid treating political claims as neutral descriptions. Evidence from voter surveys suggests that people respond to a combination of material circumstances, local experience, trust in institutions, partisan identity, and media exposure, and that the relative weight of these factors differs by country and by individual.

Section 137.16

Mainstream Parties, Democratic Institutions, and Political Responses

Explaining the support for populist parties requires attention to several kinds of cause. Economic dislocation, including deindustrialization, job insecurity, and the consequences of the financial crisis examined in Chapter 138, has affected regions and social groups unevenly. Distrust of established parties and institutions has grown where voters see little difference among governing parties or feel unrepresented. Regional inequalities between prosperous metropolitan centers and declining peripheries have created grievances that are geographic as well as social. Cultural concerns, including anxiety about rapid social change, national identity, and the pace of immigration, matter to many voters. Research finds that none of these explanations is sufficient on its own, and that their weight varies with country, period, and the voters concerned. The correlation between local immigrant populations and support for anti-immigration parties is, notably, often weak or negative, since some of the strongest support has come from areas with few immigrants.

Mainstream parties responded in different ways. Christian democrats and conservatives faced pressure to compete for voters on migration. Germany's Christian Democrats under Angela Merkel opened the borders in 2015 and then tightened controls, and under Friedrich Merz, chancellor from May 2025, pursued stricter border enforcement and the repeal of fast-track naturalization. A parliamentary vote of January 2025 in which a Christian Democratic motion on migration passed with the votes of the AfD provoked a major controversy over the exclusion of that party from cooperation. In Britain, Conservative-led governments from 2012 promoted a "hostile environment" for irregular migration and in 2022 agreed a policy of sending asylum seekers to Rwanda, abandoned by the Labour government elected in July 2024, while Reform UK rose in the polls. Social democrats were divided. The Danish Social Democrats under Mette Frederiksen, in office from 2019, adopted a restrictive asylum policy while maintaining a generous welfare state, and the Swedish Social Democrats shifted after 2015. Elsewhere, social democrats, liberals, greens, and left parties emphasized human rights, integration, and economic inequality, while arguing that voters should not be led to blame migrants for the consequences of economic policy.

Coalition politics shaped outcomes. In Belgium, parties agreed from the 1990s on a cordon sanitaire, refusing to cooperate with the Vlaams Blok after it was convicted of racism in 2004, and Germany maintained what is called the Brandmauer, or firewall, against the AfD at federal level. In other states, parties of the right entered coalitions with populist partners: Austria in 2000 and 2017, Italy in 2018 and 2022, Finland in 2023, and the Netherlands in 2024. The consequences varied. Participation in government sometimes led to moderation, sometimes to loss of support through the pressures of office, and sometimes to the adoption of the party's agenda by the larger partner. Debates in the Union over Hungary and Poland led to the activation of Article 7 procedures in 2017 and 2018 and to a mechanism, adopted in 2020, linking funds to respect for the rule of law, with significant funds withheld from Hungary.

Democratic constitutions provide tools for the defense of democracy, and their use is disputed. The German Basic Law allows the Federal Constitutional Court to ban parties that seek to undermine the free democratic order, a response to the experience of 1933 examined in Chapter 115. The Court declined to ban the National Democratic Party in 2017 because it lacked the capacity to achieve its aims, though it later allowed exclusion from state funding. Proposals for a ban on the AfD were debated in 2025 and remained unresolved at the chapter's cutoff. Other states rely on criminal law against incitement, constitutional courts, and independent institutions. Disagreement concerns the proper balance between protecting democracy against parties that threaten its principles and respecting the rights of voters and parties to participate.

Historical Debate

Should Democracies Exclude or Engage Radical-Right Parties?

The question: When parties that critics regard as illiberal win substantial support, is it better for democratic institutions and mainstream parties to isolate them, through cordons, bans, and legal action, or to include them in the democratic process, including in coalitions?

Interpretation A: Exclusion protects democratic norms. Advocates argue that parties that hold extremist positions or undermine institutions should not be given legitimacy, that cooperation normalizes ideas that threaten minority rights, and that the German and Belgian experiences show that firewalls can stabilize the system. They point to the history of the 1930s as a warning of what can happen when conservatives enter alliances with extremist movements.
Interpretation B: Inclusion allows accountability and moderation. Advocates argue that excluding parties supported by large numbers of voters deepens distrust of the system and confirms the parties' claim that elites ignore the people, that governing exposes populists to the constraints of office, and that the Italian and Austrian experiences show that parties can moderate in practice. They also argue that mainstream parties should address the grievances that drive support, not simply isolate the party.

Evidence: The evidence is mixed and depends on the case. Hungary and Poland show that governing parties can erode institutions, but Hungary also shows that a hegemonic party can lose. Italy's government has largely stayed within European rules. Sweden's cooperation with the Sweden Democrats coincided with a decline in their vote in 2026, though many factors may have contributed. The comparative record does not support a single conclusion, and comparisons are complicated because countries differ in electoral systems, institutions, and party histories.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the rise of populist parties is understood, as a threat to be contained or as a part of democratic politics to be engaged, and what role historical analogy to the interwar period should play.

Section 137.17

Everyday Life in a Changing Europe: Work, Family, School, and Community

Most of the history of migration is lived not in parliaments or courts but in workplaces, kitchens, classrooms, and streets. Employment is the arena in which migrants and established populations most frequently meet. Migrants have been prominent in sectors with persistent labor demand: construction, agriculture and food processing, hospitality, logistics, cleaning, domestic work, and health and long-term care. Many have worked below their qualifications, because credentials earned abroad are not always recognized, and employment rates and earnings of migrants from outside the Union have tended to lag behind those of natives in many countries, though gaps vary with origin, gender, language, and the time since arrival. Mobile EU citizens from eastern Europe have often combined high rates of employment with jobs below their level of education. Informal and precarious work, including seasonal agriculture in southern Europe, has exposed some workers to exploitation, and trade unions, labor inspectorates, and nongovernmental organizations have campaigned against it.

Family life has crossed borders. Many households are transnational, with parents working abroad and children in the care of grandparents, or with spouses separated for years by visa requirements. Remittances to families in countries of origin have been a major source of income for sending regions, in the Balkans, Romania, Morocco, Turkey, and the Philippines, among others. Family reunification, marriage migration, and the birth of children have changed the demographic profile of immigrant communities. Marriage across national and religious lines has increased, and so has the number of households with several languages. Care for older relatives raises different questions in different communities, and a growing number of older people with migrant backgrounds, the first guest-worker generations, are now retired, with distinctive needs for health care and housing.

Schools are central sites of integration and of dispute. Children of migrants must learn the language of instruction, often alongside a home language, and school systems differ in how they handle this. Systems that divide pupils into academic and vocational tracks at an early age, as in Germany and Austria, have been criticized for channeling children of migrants into less academic routes, while comprehensive systems face challenges of resources and concentration in particular schools. International assessments have consistently found that, on average, students with a migrant background score lower than native-born peers in many countries, though the gaps differ in size, narrow after controlling for socioeconomic status in many cases, and in some countries are small or reversed for particular groups. Teachers, parents, and local authorities have experimented with language support, mother-tongue instruction, parental involvement, and desegregation policies, with mixed evidence of success.

Religious practice and cultural exchange form part of daily life. Mosques, Orthodox churches, Pentecostal congregations, Hindu temples, and gurdwaras joined older churches and synagogues in cities and towns. Food, music, sport, and language changed: kebab and curry became national dishes in some countries, Afro-Caribbean, Turkish, Maghrebi, and South Asian music influenced popular culture, and national football teams drew increasingly on the descendants of migrants. These exchanges have often occurred without political fanfare. At the same time, established residents have had varied experiences, from friendship and cooperation to unease, loss of familiar surroundings, or competition for scarce housing and services. Older residents in changing neighborhoods have described feeling that the places they knew were disappearing, a sentiment that may be sincere without being a judgment on any group. Historians should record the experiences of both.

Everyday Life and Social History

Work, School, and Neighborhood: Two Kinds of Ordinary Day

Consider two documented patterns of life, described here in general terms drawn from oral histories, surveys, and ethnographic studies, not from named individuals. A care worker recruited from outside the Union may spend days in a private home or care facility, working shifts, sending money to relatives, and attending language classes in the evening, while dealing with a residence permit that depends on her employer. Her children translate documents for her and attend a school where several languages are spoken. She may know her neighbors only slightly, and may belong to a congregation or a regional association that provides practical help.

A retired factory worker in a town that has lost its main employer may have lived in the same house for decades. The closure of a shop, the decline of public transport, and the arrival of new neighbors may all form part of what he experiences as the change of the place. He may be hospitable to individual newcomers while distrusting politicians in the capital. Both lives are shaped by the same forces, economic restructuring, demographic change, and migration, but their experiences are not symmetrical, since their legal status, resources, and security differ.

Section 137.18

Cities, Neighborhoods, Housing, and Social Integration

Cities are where diversity is most visible and where integration is worked out in practice. Four cases illustrate the range. London, whose 2021 census recorded roughly two-fifths of its residents as born outside the United Kingdom, has been shaped by successive waves from the Caribbean, South Asia, Africa, eastern Europe, and elsewhere. It is characterized by high diversity within neighborhoods, many of which have no single majority group, and by severe housing costs that affect both migrants and natives. Berlin, divided until 1990, has long-established Turkish and Kurdish communities in Kreuzberg and Neukölln, later arrivals from eastern Europe and the Middle East, including many Syrians after 2015, and an influx of young Europeans. Rapid increases in rents after reunification, and the failed attempt in 2020 to cap rents by law, which the Federal Constitutional Court struck down in 2021, made housing a central issue.

In Paris, immigrants from North and West Africa and Southeast Asia, and earlier from southern Europe, settled in older working-class districts and, from the 1950s and 1960s, in the large public housing estates built on the periphery. Deindustrialization and unemployment hit these areas, and the riots of October and November 2005 and of June and July 2023, the latter following the police shooting of a seventeen-year-old, were explosions of anger linked to policing, discrimination, and neglect. A law of 2000 requires municipalities to maintain a minimum share of social housing, and the Grand Paris Express transport project aims to connect the periphery to the center. Malmö in southern Sweden lost its shipbuilding industry in the 1980s and 1990s, reorienting toward services and knowledge industries, especially after the opening of the bridge to Copenhagen in 2000. It received large numbers of migrants, including from the former Yugoslavia, Iraq, and Somalia, and has neighborhoods with high concentrations of foreign-born residents and low employment, such as Rosengård, alongside intensive municipal programs. Gang violence in several Swedish cities in the 2010s and 2020s became a major political issue.

Segregation can be measured by income, ethnicity, tenure, or education, and these are not identical. Concentrations of immigrant households result from several causes: the location of affordable or public housing, discrimination by landlords, networks that lead newcomers to join relatives, and the preferences of residents. Some concentration brings benefits, such as shops, services, religious institutions, and mutual aid, and can be a stage in settlement. Other forms are associated with poor schools, limited networks to employment, and stigma. Research does not support the assumption that residential concentration is automatically equivalent to failed integration. It does show that very high concentration of disadvantage, in schools and neighborhoods, is associated with poorer outcomes, and that policies of dispersal, such as Sweden's allocation rules for refugees or Denmark's designation of "ghetto" areas in 2018, have produced both claimed gains and sharp criticism.

Municipal services and grassroots institutions mediate community relations. Local councils manage housing allocation, schools, policing, and social care. Neighborhood associations, mosques, churches, youth clubs, sports teams, and trade unions provide spaces for contact, and studies of urban life generally find that regular interaction across groups in schools, workplaces, and local institutions reduces prejudice, although contact alone does not overcome structural inequalities. Cities have often taken more pragmatic and welcoming positions than national governments, forming networks of "welcoming" or "solidarity" cities. Housing markets, which are far more strongly shaped by planning, finance, and national policy than by immigration, remain a decisive factor in whether newcomers and established residents experience competition or cooperation.

Section 137.19

Europe's Demographic Future: Labor, Aging, Migration, and Public Policy

Demographic projections are widely used in policy debate, and their use requires care. A projection is a calculation of how a population would develop under stated assumptions about fertility, mortality, and migration. It is not a prediction, since the assumptions may prove wrong, and the further ahead the projection runs, the less it can say about reality. Eurostat publishes a baseline projection and sensitivity scenarios, for example one with higher net migration and one with lower fertility. Such scenarios are valuable for showing how sensitive outcomes are to assumptions. As with all such models, they say what would follow if the assumptions held, and historians should treat them as conditional models and not as statements about the future.

The likely policy pressures are clear in outline. A shrinking working-age population, reported in Eurostat's long-term projections, puts pressure on labor supply in health care, care for older people, construction, transport, and skilled trades, and many employers in these sectors already report shortages. Pension systems that depend on current workers' contributions to pay current retirees face fiscal strain, and governments have raised retirement ages, as France did in 2023 despite mass protest, adjusted indexation, and encouraged longer working lives. The demand for long-term care for older people, which relies heavily on family members and, increasingly, on migrant workers, is growing faster than public provision. These pressures interact with productivity growth, which could reduce the need for labor, and with technology, which may change which skills are needed.

Family policy is a second area. Governments have tried to raise or sustain fertility through parental leave, child allowances, childcare, tax benefits, and housing support. The Nordic countries and France developed extensive support, and Hungary introduced generous incentives from the 2010s, but studies indicate that such policies may affect timing and the ease of combining work and family more than the number of children, and that their effect on completed fertility is limited and uncertain. Hungary's fertility rate, despite large spending, did not recover to replacement level, which suggests the limits of policy. Immigration policy is a third area. The Union and its members have pursued skilled migration through the Blue Card, national schemes such as Germany's Skilled Immigration Act of 2020 and its points-based "opportunity card" introduced in 2024, and quota decrees for seasonal and other workers in Italy, while seeking at the same time to limit asylum and irregular migration. The tension between the need for labor and political resistance to immigration is a central feature of the present.

National responses differ. Germany combines active recruitment of skilled workers with tighter border controls and changes in naturalization rules. Italy, whose median age is the highest in the Union, has relied on quota decrees to admit workers while governing parties campaign against irregular arrivals. Hungary has emphasized family policy and has relied on guest-worker permits for labor rather than permanent immigration. Poland, which was for years a country of emigration, became home to large numbers of Ukrainian workers and refugees, and its government later restricted some benefits. Sweden has tightened rules for labor migration and citizenship and, as already noted, faces a new government after the election of September 2026. Spain, whose economy has relied heavily on immigrant labor, has generally been more open. These differences confirm that demographic similarities do not dictate a single policy, and that political context shapes the response.

Integration investment, in language courses, recognition of qualifications, education, and early labor-market access, has been shown by many studies to affect long-term outcomes, and the costs of delay can be high. Yet such investment is politically difficult when public attention focuses on borders and costs. The relation between immigration and long-term demographic change is therefore not simply arithmetical. Immigration can slow aging and fill labor shortages but cannot by itself reverse a structural shift, and its social effects depend on how well newcomers are integrated, and on the political acceptance of those policies.

Section 137.20

Historical Synthesis: Migration, Identity, Democracy, and European Belonging

The chapter's central argument is that population change, migration, cultural diversity, and political conflict over identity were interconnected but not interchangeable. Europe's postwar transformation from a region of emigration to a region of immigration came from specific decisions: recruitment during the boom, the legacies of empire, the opening of the east, free movement within an enlarging Union, and the protection of refugees under international law. Population aging came from long-term shifts in fertility and mortality that predate and are largely independent of immigration. The expansion of populist parties drew on a range of grievances, among them economic insecurity, regional decline, distrust of institutions, and concern about national identity, and migration was one theme among these and not their single cause.

Citizenship and national identity proved to be historically variable. France, Germany, Britain, the Netherlands, and Sweden each changed their approaches, moving between accommodation, assimilationist expectation, and restriction, and none of them corresponded permanently to a stereotype. Religious pluralism, above all the presence of Muslim communities in both southeastern and western Europe, tested arrangements between states and religions built in earlier centuries, and the debates over dress, schooling, and public neutrality were disputes over the meaning of freedom as much as of religion. Discrimination, racism, antisemitism, and anti-Muslim prejudice persisted, while immigrants and their descendants became participants in the economy, culture, and politics of the societies in which they lived.

Democratic institutions were tested in several ways. Mainstream parties struggled to respond to electorates that felt unrepresented. Courts and the Union's institutions defined limits on what governments could do to asylum seekers and on how they treated the rule of law. The 2026 results show that outcomes are not uniform or predetermined: the AfD achieved its best results in eastern German states in September, the Sweden Democrats lost support in the same month, Hungary's long-governing national-conservative party was removed by voters in April, and Berlin chose differently from its eastern neighbors. A historical account must hold such differences together without reducing them to a trend.

For these reasons, Europe's future cannot be understood through population statistics, migration policy, or electoral politics in isolation. Demography sets constraints and creates needs, but the policies that respond to them result from political choices and the balance of social interests. Migration policy shapes who arrives and with what rights, but it operates in societies with their own memories and divisions. Elections register choices of voters, but the meanings of those choices lie in local experience and national histories. This chapter's subject differs from that of Chapter 138, which treats the financial crisis, sovereign debt, and Brexit as problems of economic governance and sovereignty, though they overlapped with the political developments described here. It also prepares for Chapter 139, in which the contemporary security order, including Russia's war against Ukraine, forms the principal subject, and where displacement, energy, and the future of Europe's institutions are analyzed from the standpoint of security and geopolitics.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how migration, demographic change, religious and cultural diversity, and competing ideas of national identity reshaped European societies and political systems between 1945 and 18 November 2026. Its central argument is that these developments were interconnected but not interchangeable. Population aging did not produce identical immigration policies, immigration did not produce identical political reactions, and national identity did not have a single meaning across the continent.

Division I traced Europe's transformation from a continent of emigration to one of immigration. Postwar reconstruction created labor shortages that West Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Switzerland met through recruitment, while Britain, France, and the Netherlands received migrants from former empires. Governments expected temporary stays, but settlement, family reunification, and the birth of a second generation turned labor migration into permanent diversity. After 1989, the opening of the east, ethnic return migration, the Yugoslav wars, and enlargement created new movements, including large intra-EU mobility, while migration from Africa, Asia, and the Middle East diversified origins.

Demographic change was a separate, parallel development. Eurostat's 2026 edition reported an EU population of about 451 million in 2025, a median age of 44.9 years, up from 39.6 in 2005, and a fertility rate of 1.34 in 2024. Aging and low fertility were widespread but uneven, with Italy, Bulgaria, Portugal, and Greece at the older end and Ireland at the younger. Immigration slows aging but cannot reverse it, and demographic geography divided growing metropolitan regions from shrinking rural and eastern districts, with political consequences.

Division II explained the legal and political framework. Free movement of EU citizens is governed by Union law, while immigration by non-EU nationals remains largely national, and Schengen, Union citizenship, naturalization, and asylum are distinct. The 1951 Convention and its 1967 Protocol, non-refoulement, and European human rights law created binding protection obligations, while the Dublin system placed heavy burdens on states of first arrival. The refugee movements of 2015 to 2016, with 1,216,860 first-time applicants in 2015, led to deep disagreement about responsibility sharing and to externalized agreements such as the EU-Turkey statement.

After 2020, Europe faced the instrumentalization of migration at the Belarusian border and the displacement caused by Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, handled through the Temporary Protection Directive. Eurostat counted 4.43 million beneficiaries in July 2026 and 4.37 million in September; these are successive stocks rather than cumulative arrivals. The Pact on Migration and Asylum, adopted on 14 May 2024, entered into application on 12 June 2026, and it combines screening, border procedures, new responsibility rules, and a solidarity mechanism. Its effects cannot yet be assessed, and it expresses competing aims of border management, humanitarian protection, state responsibility, and solidarity.

Division III turned to identity and belonging. France, Germany, Britain, the Netherlands, and Sweden followed distinct and changing models of citizenship and integration, so that no single country can be treated as a fixed type. Islam has a long history in southeastern Europe, and the Muslim communities of western and northern Europe, formed by migration, are diverse in origin, tradition, and generation. Disputes over secularism, religious dress, and public neutrality revealed different state-religion arrangements and competing conceptions of freedom. Racism, antisemitism, anti-Muslim prejudice, and exclusion of Roma persisted, while immigrants and their descendants shaped institutions, culture, and politics.

Division IV analyzed nationalism and populism. After 1945 the far right was marginalized and reorganized, and new radical-right parties emerged from the 1970s and 1980s. Contemporary parties, among them the National Rally, the Freedom Party, the AfD, the League, Brothers of Italy, the Sweden Democrats, the Party for Freedom, Fidesz, and Law and Justice, differ in ideology, strategy, and conduct. The 2024 European Parliament elections showed gains for right-wing groups without giving them control, and the national results of 2026 diverged: the AfD won in two eastern German states in September, the Sweden Democrats lost support, and Fidesz lost power in Hungary.

Explanations for populist support must include economic dislocation, distrust of established parties, regional inequality, representation, and cultural concern, and none of these is sufficient alone. Media and political communication shaped the framing of migration, and episodes such as the Southport disorder of 2024 showed the role of disinformation. Mainstream parties responded by competing, tightening policy, isolating, or entering coalitions, and the debate over whether democratic institutions should exclude or engage illiberal movements produced no consensus among scholars.

Division V examined daily life, cities, and the future. Work, family, school, and community were the settings in which integration occurred, and the experiences of migrants and established residents differed with legal status, place, and resources. London, Berlin, Paris, and Malmö showed how housing markets, public services, and history shaped neighborhoods, and residential concentration was not equivalent to failed integration. Demographic projections were treated as conditional models, and national policy responses to labor shortages, pensions, care, and family policy differed.

The historical significance of these developments lies in the revision of earlier assumptions. Europe's nation-states discovered that their populations were more varied than their legal and cultural conceptions of nationhood had allowed. The legal order of the Union, the human rights jurisprudence of European courts, and national politics all had to respond. The outcomes were contingent: neither the integration of immigrants nor the success of populist parties was inevitable, and the 2026 results show that political trajectories can change quickly.

The chapter has also emphasized method. Historians must distinguish stocks from flows, applicants from decisions, foreign-born residents from foreign nationals, and demonstrable events from political claims about them. They must treat projections as conditional and recent events as provisional. The subject continues into Chapter 138, which examines the financial crisis, sovereign debt, and Brexit, and Chapter 139, which turns to security, energy, and Europe's changing global position.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Guest worker (Gastarbeiter)
A foreign laborer recruited under a temporary scheme, especially in West Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, whose stay often became permanent.
Family reunification
The admission of relatives of a resident migrant, a major channel of immigration after recruitment ended and a right protected in European law.
Foreign-born
A person born outside the country of residence, which includes naturalized citizens.
Foreign national
A person who does not hold the citizenship of the country of residence, which includes people born there.
Naturalization
The legal process by which a foreign national acquires citizenship, with conditions that vary by state.
Jus soli
The principle that citizenship follows birth within a state's territory, in pure or qualified form.
Jus sanguinis
The principle that citizenship follows descent from a citizen parent.
Free movement
The right of EU citizens to move, reside, and work in other member states under conditions set by Union law.
Schengen Area
The group of European states that have abolished systematic internal border checks, whose membership differs from that of the EU.
Union citizenship
A status created at Maastricht in 1992, held by every national of a member state in addition to national citizenship.
Refugee
A person who meets the definition of the 1951 Convention as amended by the 1967 Protocol, outside their country owing to a well-founded fear of persecution on protected grounds.
Asylum seeker
A person who has applied for protection and awaits a final decision.
Non-refoulement
The principle that a person must not be returned to a place where life or freedom, or protection from torture or inhuman treatment, would be at risk.
Subsidiary protection
A status in EU law for persons who do not meet the refugee definition but face serious harm if returned.
Temporary protection
A collective status under the 2001 directive, activated on 4 March 2022 for persons fleeing the war in Ukraine.
Dublin system
The rules assigning responsibility for an asylum application, usually to the state of first entry, replaced for most purposes by the Asylum and Migration Management Regulation of 2024.
Pact on Migration and Asylum
A package of ten legal acts adopted on 14 May 2024 and applicable from 12 June 2026, covering screening, procedures, responsibility, solidarity, and crisis situations.
Total fertility rate
The average number of children a woman would have if she experienced the age-specific birth rates of a given year; replacement level is about 2.1.
Median age
The age at which half of a population is older and half younger.
Dependency ratio
The ratio of persons outside working age, typically aged 65 and over in its old-age form, to those of working age.
Natural population change
The difference between births and deaths, excluding migration.
Conditional projection
A calculation of how a population would develop under stated assumptions, which is not a prediction.
Integration
A contested term for the participation of migrants and their descendants in economic, social, and political life; its meaning differs among states and speakers.
Assimilation
The process or policy by which minority groups adopt the culture of the majority, to the point of disappearance of difference.
Multiculturalism
Either the fact of cultural diversity or a set of policies recognizing group identities and accommodating cultural and religious practices.
Laïcité
The French principle of state secularism and neutrality, based on the 1905 law separating churches and state.
Nativism
The view that a state should be composed of members of the native group and that non-natives threaten it.
Populism
A contested term, often defined as an ideology that divides society into a virtuous people and a corrupt elite.
Radical right
A scholarly label for parties that combine nativism with authoritarian or populist themes while generally accepting electoral competition.
Cordon sanitaire / Brandmauer
An agreement among parties not to cooperate with a specified party, used in Belgium and Germany.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that Europe's postwar history was not only a history of states, alliances, and institutions but also of people moving, aging, settling, and arguing over who belonged. The migrations of the 1950s to 1970s, the movements after 1989, and the displacements of 2015 and 2022 changed the composition of every society they touched, and they posed questions about citizenship, religion, and equality that earlier chapters had treated mainly at the level of law and diplomacy.

It also reveals larger processes. Empire, industrial growth, demographic transition, and the integration of the Union all contributed to the contemporary pattern, and none of them could be understood without the others. The long history of Islam in southeastern Europe, of Roma and Jewish communities, and of earlier migrations reminds us that European diversity is not new, even when its forms and politics are. The chapter shows how a continent that had defined itself through nation-states revised, unevenly, its understanding of nationhood.

The developments examined here continue into the following chapters. The financial crisis and Brexit in Chapter 138 intersected with debates over sovereignty and migration, and Chapter 139 will place displacement, energy, and political divisions within a changed security environment. Chapter 142 will return to empire, migration, and diaspora over the long term, and Chapter 140 to religious pluralism and secularization.

For Volume VI, the chapter contributes the argument that contemporary Europe is a historically contingent political and social order. Neither integration nor populism, neither diversity nor restriction, was predetermined, and the events of 2026 show that outcomes can shift in months. Understanding Europe now requires, as this chapter has tried to show, holding together demography, law, identity, and politics while resisting the temptation to explain one by the other.
