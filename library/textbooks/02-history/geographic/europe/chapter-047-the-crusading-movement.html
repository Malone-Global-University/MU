The Crusading Movement

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 1096, in towns and monasteries across France, Flanders, and Lotharingia, landholders appeared before the monks of local abbeys to sell or pledge property for ready money. Many of the surviving charters record, in a few formulaic lines, that the seller was departing for Jerusalem. These small, dry documents are among the best evidence for the largest and most distinctive religious movement of the High Middle Ages. They show that crusading was not only a matter of sermons and battles, but of families, debts, estates, and decisions made in countless households.

The documents belong to the first wave of a movement that lasted for centuries and that touched almost every region of Latin Christian Europe. Its origin is conventionally dated to November 1095, when Pope Urban II called at Clermont for an armed expedition to assist the Christians of the East and to liberate Jerusalem. Thousands responded. Their expedition, which reached Jerusalem in 1099, established Latin Christian lordships on the eastern Mediterranean coast, and it set a pattern that popes, kings, and nobles would repeat, adapt, and extend across the next two hundred years. The people involved did not at first use a single name for what they were doing. They spoke of pilgrimage, of the way to Jerusalem, of taking the cross, and of the service of Christ. The word crusade, and the tidy numbered sequence of "the Crusades" that students inherit, belong to later usage.

This chapter is therefore about a movement, not a series of campaigns. It asks how a late eleventh century expedition authorized for Jerusalem developed into a durable religious, political, and military institution that Latin Christians applied across the eastern Mediterranean, Iberia, the Baltic, southern France, and eventually against the pope's own enemies inside Latin Christendom. Following that question requires attention to penitential religion, papal authority, aristocratic family strategy, finance, maritime transport, and frontier expansion. It also requires attention to the societies that experienced crusading from the other side: Muslim rulers and populations in Syria, Egypt, Iberia, and North Africa; Eastern Christians of many traditions; Jewish communities in the Rhineland and the Levant; and the pagan peoples of the Baltic.

The chapter builds on Chapter 44, which examined the reforming papacy whose authority made the crusading appeal possible, and on Chapter 46, which showed how the emperors and the papacy contended over Italy. It draws on Chapter 36 through Chapter 41 for the Byzantine setting, and it leaves the longer consequences of the Fourth Crusade for Byzantium to Chapter 41. It also recalls Chapter 35 on the Christianization of east central Europe and Chapter 43 on Mediterranean commerce, since crusading depended on the ships and credit of Italian cities. Part VII's larger argument, that the High Middle Ages combined expansion, institutional growth, and cultural confidence, finds its most dramatic and most troubling expression here.

Students should be prepared for a subject that continues to inspire strong feeling. The crusades have been used for centuries as a source of heroic legend, of anti-Christian polemic, of national and imperial myth, and of modern political rhetoric. This chapter treats them as a historical phenomenon to be explained from evidence. That means distinguishing the aims and language of medieval participants from later interpretations, recognizing the violence that crusading produced, including against Jewish communities and Eastern Christians, and recognizing equally that relations among Latin Christians, Muslims, Jews, and Eastern Christians included diplomacy, commerce, cooperation, and coexistence as well as war.

The sources are varied and each has limits. Latin chronicles of the First Crusade, including the anonymous Gesta Francorum, Fulcher of Chartres, Raymond of Aguilers, and Albert of Aachen, were written by participants or by authors who relied on them, and they interpret events in providential terms. Anna Komnene's Alexiad gives a Byzantine view, written decades later. Muslim chroniclers such as Ibn al-Qalanisi in Damascus and, later, Ibn al-Athir record how the arrival of the Franks appeared to Syrian and Egyptian observers. Hebrew narratives written after 1096 describe the attacks on Rhineland Jews. Papal letters, charters, tax records, ship contracts, and the architecture of castles supply further evidence. No single source gives a neutral account, and historians must compare them.

For Volume II, the crusading movement shows Latin Christendom at its most expansive and most self-confident, but also reveals the tensions within it: between papal ambition and royal power, between religious ideals and worldly motives, between Latin and Orthodox Christians, and between the aim of defending Christendom and the reality of attacking its neighbors. Understanding the movement is essential for the chapters that follow on commerce, learning, medieval society, and the political transformations of the late Middle Ages.

Historical Context

Latin Christian religious culture in the eleventh century placed great value on pilgrimage, on penance, and on the veneration of holy places and relics. Pilgrims traveled to Rome, to Santiago de Compostela in Galicia, and, in smaller but significant numbers, to Jerusalem, where the Church of the Holy Sepulchre marked the site of Christ's burial and resurrection. Pilgrimage was understood as a form of penance, and some pilgrims traveled in armed groups. The destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in 1009 on the orders of the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim shocked western observers, but Christian pilgrimage resumed and the church was later rebuilt with Byzantine support. Jerusalem was therefore both a real destination and a powerful symbol.

Aristocratic society was warlike, and the Church had long sought to limit and channel its violence. The Peace of God and Truce of God movements, which began in southern France in the late tenth century and spread widely, sought to protect clergy, peasants, and sacred places and to forbid fighting on certain days. Chapter 34 described the lordship and military culture of the knightly class. Churchmen also developed ideas of legitimate war, building on Augustine's reflections on just warfare and on the reform papacy's willingness to call for armed defense of the Church, examined in Chapter 44. The crusade combined these elements: it offered warriors a way to use their profession in an act of penitential devotion, and it placed the authority to authorize such war in the pope.

The eastern Mediterranean was politically fragmented. The Byzantine Empire, examined in Chapter 36 and Chapter 37, had lost much of Anatolia after the defeat by the Seljuk Turks at Manzikert in 1071, and its emperor Alexios I Komnenos, who came to power in 1081, worked to recover territory and to recruit soldiers. The Seljuk sultanate, which had overrun much of Syria and Anatolia, fragmented after the death of Sultan Malik-Shah in 1092 into rival principalities. Fatimid Egypt, a Shi'ite caliphate rival to the Sunni Abbasid caliphs of Baghdad, controlled Jerusalem at times and recaptured it from the Seljuks in 1098 during the crusaders' march. Italian maritime cities, including Venice, Genoa, and Pisa, were expanding their commerce, as Chapter 43 explained. The political divisions of the Muslim world were an essential condition for the success of the First Crusade.

Latin Christians were already fighting frontier wars against Muslim rulers in Sicily and Iberia. Norman adventurers conquered Sicily from its Muslim rulers between 1061 and 1091, and the Christian kingdoms of northern Iberia were pushing south, taking Toledo in 1085. In the later eleventh century the papacy sponsored some of these efforts with spiritual rewards. Urban II himself encouraged Christians in Catalonia to remain and help restore Tarragona, rather than go east. This background shows that the call of 1095 was not entirely novel, though its scale, its goal, and its offering of a general spiritual reward were new.

Finally, the relationship between Latin and Greek Christians shaped the setting. Chapter 40 examined the growing separation, symbolized by the mutual excommunications of 1054, between the Roman and Constantinopolitan churches. The break was not total in the eleventh century, and Alexios I sought western military assistance in the 1090s, including at the Council of Piacenza in March 1095, where his envoys appealed for help. What he expected was a force of mercenaries under his control. What he received, as this chapter explains, was something very different.

Section 47.1

What Was a Crusade?

Historians still disagree about how to define a crusade, and the disagreement is instructive. Medieval people did not originally have a single word for the phenomenon. Chroniclers of the First Crusade spoke of the journey to Jerusalem, of pilgrimage, of the army of the Lord, and of those who took the cross. The Latin term crucesignatus, one signed with the cross, and later vernacular words such as the French croisade developed during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The numbered series, First Crusade, Second Crusade, and so on, is a modern convenience that begins to appear much later and that obscures the fact that expeditions came in many sizes and that the movement had no clear points of division.

Historians have proposed several definitions. Traditionalists have generally restricted crusading primarily to expeditions directed toward the Holy Land. Pluralists emphasize the institutional form of crusading, including papal authorization, the vow, the sign of the cross, indulgences, and associated privileges, and therefore include campaigns in Iberia, the Baltic, and against heretics or political enemies. Generalists use a still broader category of Christian holy war. These labels are useful but imperfect, and individual historians do not always fit neatly within them. This chapter adopts a broadly pluralist working definition: a crusade was a penitential war, proclaimed under papal authority, in which participants took a vow, wore the sign of the cross, and received spiritual and legal privileges, including an indulgence, a spiritual privilege that in Urban II's Clermont legislation allowed the devotional journey to Jerusalem to take the place of imposed penance, and whose theology and legal form developed further during later crusades. The definition helps explain why campaigns in Iberia, the Baltic, and southern France belong to the same story as expeditions to Jerusalem.

Each element of this definition mattered. The vow made participation a solemn religious obligation and gave the Church legal authority over the participant. The cross, sewn onto clothing, publicly marked the person as a crusader and entitled him or her to protections, such as exemption from some debts and legal proceedings. The indulgence connected military service to the system of penance that structured Latin Christian religious life, as Chapter 49 will describe. And papal authorization distinguished a crusade from the ordinary wars of lords and kings, because it claimed that the war was an act of religious devotion and not simply a political or dynastic enterprise.

Students should also avoid two common errors. The first is to treat crusading as indistinguishable from ordinary medieval warfare, which was frequent and which also used religious language. What made crusading distinctive was the combination of penance, vow, and papal authorization. The second is to equate Christian holy war with the Islamic concept of jihad, as if the crusades were a Christian version of an Islamic institution or vice versa. Jihad has its own history, vocabulary, and legal tradition, and its meanings varied across time and circumstances, as discussed in Section 47.8. The two traditions interacted but were not mirror images.

Historical Vocabulary
Crusade and Crucesignatus

Crusade is the modern English term for a penitential war proclaimed under papal authority and entered by a vow, with spiritual privileges for participants. A participant was a crucesignatus, one who had taken the cross, and the term refers to the cloth cross worn as a badge. The word "crusade" itself is a late development and was not used by the participants of 1095 and 1099, who described their enterprise as a pilgrimage or journey. The term is useful but should not be read back as if medieval people shared our idea of a single historical series. The term also does not imply that all participants were motivated by the same beliefs or that the movement was unified in its goals.

Section 47.2

Reform Christianity, Pilgrimage, Warfare, and the Road to Clermont

The religious culture from which crusading emerged combined several elements. Pilgrimage was central. By the eleventh century, large groups traveled to Jerusalem, sometimes in the thousands, and the journey was understood as an act of penance that might substitute for other penalties. A famous German pilgrimage of 1064 and 1065 under the archbishop of Mainz and other bishops is documented to have included thousands of participants and to have suffered attacks along the way. Such experiences made the security of pilgrims a matter of concern, and they helped create an association between pilgrimage and armed protection.

Aristocratic violence and the Church's response formed a second element. The Peace and Truce of God movements sought to limit fighting, while the reforming papacy of the later eleventh century, as Chapter 44 described, increasingly asserted the right to call on lay warriors to defend the Church. Pope Gregory VII had considered leading an expedition to the East in the 1070s, though nothing came of it. Urban II, elected in 1088, continued the reform program and sought to strengthen papal leadership of Latin Christendom at a moment when his rival, an imperial antipope, held Rome. A successful appeal for a great expedition would demonstrate papal authority in a new way. Chapter 46 showed how contested that authority was in the Empire.

The Byzantine appeal formed a third element. After Manzikert, the Byzantines had lost the Anatolian plateau, and Alexios I, who had defended his throne against Norman invaders and other enemies, sought military help from the West. At Piacenza in March 1095 his envoys addressed Urban's council. The sources do not preserve their exact request, and historians debate how far Alexios sought a large army rather than modest contingents of mercenaries. The Byzantine emperor had a long tradition of employing western soldiers, including Normans, and he understood the West as a source of manpower. Urban, however, had a more ambitious vision. He connected the request for aid to the liberation of the Eastern Christians and to the sacred city of Jerusalem, and he presented the expedition as a spiritual undertaking under papal leadership.

The result was a convergence of interests that did not imply a convergence of understandings. Urban saw an opportunity to demonstrate papal leadership and, perhaps, to heal the breach with the Greek Church, discussed in Chapter 40. Alexios hoped for troops. Western knights and clerics, moved by religious feeling and by the prospect of spiritual reward, understood the enterprise as a pilgrimage in arms to the holy places. These differing expectations help explain the tensions that arose when the armies reached Constantinople.

Section 47.3

Urban II, Clermont, and the Mobilization of 1095 to 1096

The Council of Clermont met in November 1095 in central France, and the pope delivered his appeal to a gathering of clergy and laity on 27 November. What he actually said cannot be recovered. The principal accounts of the speech, by Fulcher of Chartres, Robert the Monk, Baldric of Bourgueil, and Guibert of Nogent, were composed after the capture of Jerusalem in 1099, and each shapes the speech in light of subsequent events. The accounts agree that Urban called for aid to the Eastern Christians and for the liberation of Jerusalem and that he promised spiritual rewards, but they differ on emphasis, and the vivid descriptions of eastern atrocities in some versions probably reflect later embellishment. Contemporary canons of the council, which survive in different forms, state that a journey to Jerusalem undertaken from devotion alone, and not for honor or money, would count as a complete penance.

Urban supplemented Clermont with preaching tours and letters, and he appointed Adhémar, bishop of Le Puy, as his representative to the expedition. He set the departure for August 1096. The pope had evidently expected an organized army of knights, but the response was much wider. Preachers, notably the charismatic Peter the Hermit, spread the message across northern France and the Rhineland, and many people who were not fighting men took the cross, including women, the elderly, and the poor. Clergy were officially meant to seek permission from their superiors, and the Church attempted to discourage those unfit to fight, with limited success.

Mobilization was a social and financial process. Taking the cross was a vow, but fulfilling it required money, equipment, transport, and arrangements for family and property. Knights and nobles often sold or mortgaged land to monasteries, as the charters mentioned in the chapter opening show, and they sought the permission of their lords and relatives, whose consent was needed for alienation of property. Contemporary estimates and modern studies suggest that the cost of the journey was several times a lord's annual income. Many participants also made arrangements for the protection of their families and estates, sometimes placing them under church protection. Participants came disproportionately from northern and southern France, Lotharingia, and southern Italy, the regions where preaching was most intense and where the reform papacy had close ties. There were comparatively few from England, Germany, and Spain.

The motives of participants were mixed, and the evidence does not allow confident generalization. Charters and chronicles express religious devotion, the desire for penance, loyalty to lords and kin, and the hope of gaining land and fame. Some leaders, notably Bohemond of Taranto, had clear political ambitions. Historians have debated the balance, a question taken up in the Historical Debate below. The central point for this section is that crusading did not simply happen once the pope spoke. It was a mobilization, depending on preachers, on networks of lordship and kinship, on the willingness of monasteries to supply cash, and on a religious culture that made the appeal intelligible.

Section 47.4

Popular Mobilization and the Rhineland Massacres

The expeditions commonly grouped as the People's Crusade left earlier than the main armies. Peter the Hermit led a large company, drawn from northern France and Germany, that reached Constantinople in the summer of 1096, and Walter Sans Avoir led a smaller advance group. Other bands, under figures such as the priest Gottschalk, were destroyed in Hungary after disorder and violence. These expeditions were not simply bands of poor peasants. They included knights and other combatants as well as noncombatants, and some of their leaders were of noble origin. Peter's force crossed the Bosporus in August and was largely destroyed by the Turks at Civetot in October 1096, an outcome that the leaders of the main armies, who were already marching, would soon recognize as a warning.

Alongside these expeditions, groups of crusaders in the Rhineland attacked Jewish communities in the spring of 1096. The violence, which affected Speyer, Worms, Mainz, Cologne, and other towns, is known from Latin chronicles and, especially, from Hebrew narratives written by members of the communities and their descendants. At Speyer in early May, the bishop sheltered Jews and limited the killing. At Worms and Mainz in late May, crusaders led or joined by local townspeople, among them a band associated with Count Emicho of Flonheim, broke into episcopal palaces where Jews had sought protection and killed many of them. Many Jews chose death by their own hands or those of their families rather than forced baptism, an act understood in the Hebrew accounts as martyrdom, kiddush ha-Shem, the sanctification of God's name. Some were forcibly baptized, and Emperor Henry IV, who was in Italy at the time, later permitted those who wished to return to Judaism to do so, though the Church objected. No secure total can be established, but the dead across the Rhineland numbered in the thousands.

Why did this happen? Contemporary Christian writers offered their own explanations. Some Hebrew narratives report that crusaders asked why they should travel far to fight the enemies of Christ when Christ's enemies were close at hand, and that the Jews were also seen as sources of wealth. Historians stress the interaction of religious hostility toward Jews, particularly the accusation that Jews bore responsibility for the crucifixion, local rivalries, the loosening of social restraint among large and poorly disciplined bands, and the desire to seize property. The violence was not authorized by Urban II, and Christian bishops and some secular authorities tried to protect Jewish communities, with varying success. The events nevertheless reveal something important: crusading enthusiasm, once mobilized, was difficult to control and could be turned against groups the leaders had not intended to target.

The consequences for Jewish communities were profound. The Rhineland communities, among the most significant in Latin Europe, recovered and rebuilt, but the memory of 1096 shaped Jewish liturgy and historical writing for centuries, and the later crusades brought further attacks. Chapter 49 will examine Jewish life in medieval Europe more broadly, and the present chapter notes only that crusading's relationship to Jewish history cannot be reduced to episodes of persecution. In 1146, during the preaching of the Second Crusade, Bernard of Clairvaux intervened to oppose attacks on Jews, a reminder that the Church's response was not uniform.

Primary Source Window

Two Accounts of 1095 and 1096: Latin Reports of Clermont and a Hebrew Narrative of Mainz

Creators: Latin chroniclers of the First Crusade, including Fulcher of Chartres and Robert the Monk (written after 1099); Jewish authors of the Hebrew narratives of 1096, written in the twelfth century · Type: Narrative chronicles in Latin and Hebrew · Note: The excerpts below are paraphrases, not translations, and they synthesize themes across the surviving versions.

Context: No eyewitness transcript of Urban II's speech at Clermont survives. The accounts that describe it were written after the success of the First Crusade, by authors who knew the outcome. The Hebrew narratives of the attacks on the Rhineland communities were composed by survivors or by writers who drew on survivors' memories, some decades after the events, and they were intended to commemorate the dead and to teach later generations.

Paraphrase of the Latin accounts of Urban II's appeal at Clermont The chroniclers represent the pope as urging the knights of Latin Christendom to cease fighting one another and to turn their arms against those who oppressed Christians in the East. They describe suffering pilgrims and desecrated holy places, they present Jerusalem as the center of the world and the heritage of Christ, and they promise that those who die on the journey will be freed from the penalty of their sins. The versions differ in their details and in the vividness of their language.
Paraphrase of the Hebrew narrative of the attack at Mainz The narrative describes how the Jews of Mainz sought protection from the archbishop and the town authorities, how armed crusaders and townspeople broke into the places of refuge, and how many Jews, rather than accept baptism, killed their children and themselves or were killed. The text treats these deaths as acts of devotion and sanctification, and it presents the attackers as acting against God's will and against Christian teaching on protecting the Jews.

Analysis: The Latin accounts show how the appeal of 1095 was remembered and shaped as a founding moment, and they reveal the images, of suffering Christians and holy places, that gave the appeal power. They cannot show precisely what Urban said or how his audience understood it. The Hebrew narrative gives a different vantage on the same year, that of a community targeted by crusaders, and it shows how the movement's violence was experienced by those who were not its stated objects. Both types of source are shaped by the religious outlook of their authors and by the passage of time between events and writing. Neither tells us much about the motives of ordinary participants, or the view of Muslim and Eastern Christian populations in the East.

Section 47.5

The First Crusade: From Constantinople to Jerusalem

The main armies set out in the autumn of 1096 and assembled at Constantinople between late 1096 and the spring of 1097. They were not a single army under unified command but several contingents under separate leaders, including Godfrey of Bouillon, duke of Lower Lotharingia, with his brother Baldwin; Raymond IV, count of Toulouse; Bohemond of Taranto, a Norman from southern Italy, and his nephew Tancred; and Robert Curthose of Normandy, Robert of Flanders, and Stephen of Blois. The papal legate Adhémar of Le Puy gave a degree of spiritual leadership, but no one commanded the whole. The sizes of the armies are uncertain. Modern estimates for the combined force that crossed Anatolia typically range from tens of thousands, including noncombatants, with fighting men a fraction of that total, and the contemporary figures in chronicles are not reliable.

At Constantinople, Alexios I pressed the leaders to swear oaths promising to return to the empire any former Byzantine territory they might recover. Most of them did, with varying reluctance, and Raymond of Toulouse gave only a qualified pledge. The relationship between the crusaders and the Byzantines was marked from the beginning by suspicion. The leaders saw the emperor's demands as a constraint, and Alexios feared the unruly armies and the ambitions of men like Bohemond, who had fought Byzantium in the 1080s. Chapter 36 and Chapter 37 describe the empire that Alexios was trying to rebuild. The crusaders nevertheless benefited from Byzantine guidance, supplies, and, at Nicaea in June 1097, from imperial cooperation in the city's surrender.

The march across Anatolia in the summer of 1097 was perilous. After the victory at Dorylaeum on 1 July, the crusaders crossed a harsh landscape, suffering from heat, thirst, and hunger. The leaders divided, and Baldwin of Boulogne broke away to take Edessa, where he became ruler in 1098, creating the first crusader state. The main army besieged Antioch from October 1097. The city, one of the strongest in the region, fell in June 1098 when Bohemond persuaded an Armenian commander within the walls to open a tower. The crusaders themselves were then besieged by a relief army under Kerbogha, the atabeg of Mosul, and they won an improbable victory on 28 June. Bohemond claimed Antioch for himself, defying the oath to Alexios, and the dispute with the Byzantines hardened. Disease, famine, and desertions reduced the army.

The march to Jerusalem resumed in early 1099, and the army reached the city in June. The Fatimid governor, who had retaken Jerusalem from the Seljuk emirs the previous year, had prepared its defenses and expelled many Christians. The crusaders attacked with assault towers built from timber supplied by Genoese sailors and local sources. On 15 July 1099 they broke into the city. The conquest was accompanied by massacre. Latin, Hebrew, and Arabic sources all report the killing of large numbers of Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, and while the figures given are unreliable, the violence was severe and was recorded without apology by Latin authors who saw it as divine judgment. A Fatimid relief army was defeated at Ascalon in August. Godfrey of Bouillon became ruler of Jerusalem, refusing the title of king, and his brother Baldwin succeeded him in 1100 as the first king.

Cause and Effect

Why the First Crusade Succeeded

Long-term causes

Latin Christian pilgrimage culture, papal reform, an aristocratic warrior class seeking religious outlets, the weakening of Byzantine power after Manzikert, and the fragmentation of the Seljuk world after 1092 created the conditions for a large and effective expedition.

Short-term causes

Urban II's appeal and an effective preaching campaign, Alexios I's request for aid, the support of Genoese and Pisan shipping, and rivalries among Muslim rulers in Syria, Anatolia, and Egypt who did not at first coordinate against the invaders.

Immediate effects

The capture of Antioch and Jerusalem, the foundation of Edessa, Antioch, Jerusalem, and soon Tripoli, and a break between the crusader leaders and the Byzantine emperor over the oath and over Antioch.

Long-term consequences

The success established crusading as a model of religious warfare, set up the Latin East that later expeditions sought to defend, and encouraged papal use of the crusade in other theaters.

Historical complexity: Medieval writers saw the outcome as proof of divine favor, and later writers sometimes attributed it to Western military superiority. Neither is persuasive. The expedition survived by narrow margins, it succeeded in large part because of Muslim political division and Byzantine assistance, and it nearly collapsed several times. The success was contingent, and the later failure to repeat it shows how dependent it was on particular conditions.

Multiple Perspectives

The First Crusade as Seen by Different Groups

A Latin knight who took the cross: He understands the journey as a pilgrimage and penance, undertaken at great cost and in obedience to the pope. He has sold or pledged land, left his family in the care of kin and a monastery, and hopes for spiritual reward and, if fortunate, a share of conquered territory.
The Byzantine emperor: Alexios welcomes military help but fears the independent ambitions of the Latin leaders. He seeks to bind them by oaths, to recover lost territory, and to keep control of the process, and he sees the capture of Antioch by Bohemond as a breach of agreement.
A Muslim ruler in Syria: Divided from neighboring rulers by rivalry, he initially views the Franks as one more invading force, possibly useful against his rivals, and only gradually comes to see them as a lasting presence. Some Muslim writers later interpret the loss of cities as punishment for disunity.
A Jewish community in the Rhineland: The community is threatened by neighbors and passing crusaders, seeks protection from bishops and nobles, and in some places suffers devastating loss. Its response, in both resistance and martyrdom, becomes central to Jewish memory.
An Eastern Christian in Syria or Armenia: Under Muslim rule but not unprotected, the local Christian may welcome or fear the crusaders, who are Latin and often unfamiliar with Eastern liturgy and custom. Armenians in Cilicia and Edessa often cooperate with the newcomers, while many Greek and Syrian Christians find that new Latin authorities treat them as subordinates.
Section 47.6

The Latin East: Kingdoms, Lordships, and Frontier Societies

The states founded by the First Crusade are conventionally called the crusader states or the Latin East, and their territory is often called Outremer, the French for overseas. They comprised the county of Edessa, the principality of Antioch, the kingdom of Jerusalem, and, from 1109, the county of Tripoli. Their rulers faced the problem of governing a region in which they were a small minority. The Latin population, mostly of French and Italian origin, was concentrated in cities and castles, while the countryside and many towns remained populated by Muslims, Greek, Syrian, and Armenian Christians, and Jews. Estimates of the size of the Frankish population are very uncertain, but it was likely a small proportion of the whole.

The states developed institutions that combined western and local elements. The kingdom of Jerusalem had a royal court, a feudal hierarchy of lords who owed military service, high courts that dealt with disputes among the nobility, and the Latin Church, with a patriarch of Jerusalem and Latin bishops. Local administrative practices, particularly in taxation and in the management of rural populations by native headmen, often continued from earlier Muslim or Byzantine rule. The Italian maritime cities of Venice, Genoa, and Pisa received commercial privileges and quarters in coastal towns in return for naval aid, and these communities became important actors in the Levant. Their commerce linked the Latin East to the Mediterranean trade examined in Chapter 43.

Settlement was limited, but it did occur. Castles such as Belvoir and Krak des Chevaliers, held after 1142 by the Hospitallers, anchored defense and provided centers of lordship. Some Latin settlers established agricultural villages, and the kings tried to attract colonists, with moderate success. Interaction with local communities was complex. There is evidence of cooperation, intermarriage, and cultural borrowing, including in architecture, cuisine, and administrative practice, and also of segregation and discrimination. Muslims were generally excluded from Jerusalem and subjected to Latin lordship in the countryside. Greek and other Eastern Christians generally retained their churches but found their hierarchies subordinated to Latin prelates. Jews were present in small numbers in some cities. The term crusader states should not suggest that these were culturally sealed European colonies, nor that they were simply integrated into the region.

The states also had enduring weaknesses. Their manpower was limited, and they depended on a trickle of pilgrims and soldiers from Europe and on the military orders. Their rulers often divided by dynastic disputes, especially when kings died leaving minors or when succession passed through women. Their survival depended heavily on Muslim disunity, which made them valuable as buffers or as allies of convenience to some Muslim rulers. Latin lords and Muslim rulers sometimes made truces and alliances across religious lines, and trade continued through periods of conflict. The first century of their existence was thus a story of precarious success.

Historical Vocabulary
Outremer and the Latin East

Outremer, French for "overseas," was the contemporary term for the lands in the Levant ruled by Latin Christians after 1099. Latin East is the modern scholarly term for the same region and its states, in which the ruling class belonged to the Latin Church. The older label "crusader states" remains common, though it can imply that these were purely military enterprises. They were, in fact, societies containing Latin settlers, Eastern Christians, Muslims, and Jews. The term "Franks" was used by Muslims and by Eastern Christians for all western Europeans, whatever their origins, and it appears in Arabic sources as Ifranj. Students should use these terms with awareness that they are conventions, and that the mixed character of the societies they describe was central to their history.

Section 47.7

Military Orders, Pilgrimage, and the Institutions of Crusading

The military orders were among the most original institutions created by the crusading movement. They combined the vows of monks, poverty, chastity, and obedience, with the duty to fight. The Order of the Hospital of St. John began as a hospice for pilgrims in Jerusalem and received papal recognition in 1113. Over the twelfth century it took on military responsibilities, defending pilgrims and later holding major castles. The Order of the Temple, the Templars, was founded around 1119 by a small group of knights under Hugh de Payns to protect pilgrims on the roads to Jerusalem, and it received formal recognition at the Council of Troyes in 1129. Bernard of Clairvaux wrote in its defense, presenting the Templar as a new kind of knight whose violence served Christ. Later, the Teutonic Order, which began as a hospital community at Acre in 1190 and became a military order, and the orders of Calatrava and Santiago in Iberia, followed the same pattern.

The orders addressed a persistent problem of the Latin East: the shortage of permanent, disciplined military manpower. Crusaders typically came for a limited time and returned home, while the orders provided continuous garrisons, castle building, and expertise. They became major landholders in the East and, through donations, throughout Europe, where they held commanderies that supplied money, supplies, and recruits. The Templars also developed financial services, holding deposits and arranging transfers of money for pilgrims and kings, which made them important to the growth of medieval banking, a subject related to Chapter 43. The orders were answerable to the pope rather than to local bishops, which gave them considerable independence and also provoked resentment.

The idea of professed religious who fought was contested. Some contemporaries, including some churchmen, objected that killing was incompatible with monastic life, and the orders were sometimes accused of avarice and pride. Their exemptions from local church control and their wealth provoked rivalry with bishops and with other orders, and the Templars and Hospitallers occasionally clashed with each other and with the kings of Jerusalem over policy. Nevertheless, the orders became symbols of the militant devotion associated with crusading and they were essential to its continuation.

Beyond the orders, other institutions made crusading durable. A system of preaching, in which bishops and special preachers proclaimed expeditions, became more organized, and by the late twelfth century the papacy issued detailed instructions for recruiting. Indulgences were extended to those who contributed money without going, which opened participation to women, the elderly, and the poor and created a regular fiscal basis. Taxation of the clergy and, later, of the laity for crusading, as in the Saladin tithe of 1188, was the origin of some of the earliest general taxes in medieval Europe. Legal privileges, including protection of crusaders' property and exemption from certain debts, gave the movement a legal framework. Together these developments transformed crusading from a one-time response to Urban's call into a permanent institution.

Everyday Life and Social History

Paying for a Crusade: Families, Property, and Participation

For most participants, crusading was a household decision with large financial consequences. A knight needed equipment, horses, provisions, and often a retinue, and the journey was expensive. Many raised money by selling or pledging land, often to monasteries that could pay cash in return for property and for spiritual benefits, and they usually needed the consent of their wife, children, and lord. Charters often record these transactions and sometimes mention the wife's consent, which shows the role of women in family property. Others borrowed from lenders, including Jewish and Christian moneylenders, and the crusader's privileges could complicate repayment. Some participants never returned, and families had to manage inheritance and widowhood.

Women took part in the movement in several ways. Some traveled with the armies as wives, servants, pilgrims, and, rarely, as combatants. Others stayed behind to manage estates while husbands were away, and they sometimes held lordship in their own right. Eleanor of Aquitaine accompanied her husband Louis VII on the Second Crusade, an episode that later writers embellished with legend. The growth of indulgences for financial contributions allowed women and others unfit to fight to take part by giving money. Ordinary people also experienced crusading through preaching, processions, prayers for the success of expeditions, and taxation. In this sense, the movement reached much further into daily life than the numbers of those who actually traveled to the East would suggest.

Section 47.8

Muslim Politics and the Growth of Coordinated Resistance

The Muslim world that the crusaders encountered was not a unified opponent. Sunni Seljuk rulers controlled much of Syria and Anatolia in the late eleventh century, but after 1092 their power was divided among rival emirs and atabegs. In Egypt, the Shi'ite Fatimid caliphate had its own interests and was a rival of the Seljuks. The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad was a religious figurehead with limited political power. Local rulers in Damascus, Aleppo, Mosul, and other cities competed with one another and sometimes allied with the Franks against Muslim rivals. The first Frankish successes owed much to this fragmentation.

Some Muslim writers and religious scholars called for a response. In 1105, Ali ibn Tahir al-Sulami in Damascus wrote a treatise on jihad that urged Muslims to unite against the Franks and criticized rulers for their disunity. The response in practice was slow and uneven. Over the twelfth century, however, a series of rulers consolidated territory and made jihad against the Franks a source of legitimacy. Imad al-Din Zengi, the atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo, captured Edessa in December 1144. His son Nur al-Din united much of Syria, including Damascus in 1154, and promoted jihad through the building of madrasas and the patronage of religious scholars. Nur al-Din also sent forces to Egypt, where Fatimid weakness created an opportunity.

Saladin, Salah al-Din Yusuf, a Kurdish officer in Nur al-Din's service, became vizier of Egypt in 1169, abolished the Fatimid caliphate in 1171, and after Nur al-Din's death in 1174 extended his rule over Damascus and, by 1183, Aleppo. He founded the Ayyubid dynasty. His success in uniting Egypt and Syria placed the Latin kingdom under pressure from both sides. His propaganda, and that of his secretaries and biographers, presented him as the champion of jihad and the liberator of Jerusalem, but historians note that he spent much of his career fighting other Muslims and that his authority depended on balancing many interests. His victory at Hattin in July 1187, where he destroyed the field army of the kingdom of Jerusalem, and his recovery of Jerusalem in October 1187, were achievements of a determined coalition builder.

Muslim writers interpreted the Franks in varied ways. Early accounts often treat them as strange and barbarous, though some, such as Usama ibn Munqidh, a Syrian noble who wrote a memoir in the twelfth century, describe personal acquaintances among the Franks with a mixture of contempt, curiosity, and sometimes respect. Others emphasize their military prowess and religious zeal. Treaties, trade, and diplomatic exchanges also continued. The term jihad, as the vocabulary box explains, had a range of meanings, and its use against the Franks was one element in a broad political and religious context.

Historical Vocabulary
Jihad

Jihad is an Arabic word meaning striving or struggle. In Islamic thought it has multiple meanings, including inner moral and spiritual effort and, in legal discussions, armed struggle conducted under specified rules. Medieval Muslim jurists developed a body of doctrine on armed jihad, concerning who might wage it, against whom, and on what conditions. In the twelfth century, rulers such as Nur al-Din and Saladin, and writers such as al-Sulami, invoked jihad against the Franks, and the concept was used to mobilize support and to confer legitimacy. It is not equivalent to the Latin Christian concept of crusade, which was defined by the vow, the indulgence, and papal authority, and it should not be translated as "holy war" without qualification. Both traditions justified certain wars by religious language, but their institutions, histories, and meanings differ.

Section 47.9

The Second Crusade and the Widening Geography of Crusading

The fall of Edessa to Zengi in December 1144 prompted Pope Eugenius III to issue the bull Quantum praedecessores in 1145, reissued in 1146, calling for a new expedition. Bernard of Clairvaux, the most influential churchman of the age, preached the crusade at Vézelay in March 1146 and across France and the Rhineland. King Louis VII of France took the cross, and so, after hesitation, did Conrad III, the German king, at Speyer in December 1146. Both monarchs led armies east, in the first crusade to be led by kings. Chapter 46 explained the political circumstances of Conrad's reign, which were complicated by the rivalry with the Welfs.

The Second Crusade was a military failure. Conrad's army was heavily defeated by the Seljuk Turks near Dorylaeum in October 1147, and Louis's forces suffered in the crossing of Anatolia. After they reached Jerusalem, the leaders and the Latin barons decided in 1148 to attack Damascus, the one Muslim power that had been friendly to the kingdom of Jerusalem. The siege collapsed after only a few days. The expedition thus accomplished nothing of lasting value in the East, and its failure produced bitter recriminations. Some contemporaries blamed sin, others blamed the treachery of Byzantines or of Eastern Christians, and the shock contributed to the later search for explanation.

Yet the Second Crusade is crucial for the history of the movement because it extended the geography of crusading. In 1147, Eugenius III authorized campaigns in Iberia and against the pagan Wends on the Baltic frontier, offering the same spiritual privileges as for the Holy Land. In Iberia, a fleet of crusaders from England, Flanders, and the Rhineland, traveling by sea to the East, assisted King Afonso Henriques of Portugal in capturing Lisbon in October 1147, and other forces assisted in the conquest of Almería and Tortosa. Against the Wends, a campaign of Saxon, Danish, and other forces proved largely unsuccessful in its stated aim of conversion, though it advanced the interests of the participating princes. These were the first clear examples of crusading beyond the eastern Mediterranean as part of a single proclaimed effort.

This expansion shows that by the mid twelfth century, crusading was understood as a flexible instrument available to the papacy and to rulers. Students should note that it was not simply a result of the failure in the East but of a recognition that crusading could be applied wherever Christians faced non-Christian enemies. The implications for the people on the receiving end, Muslims in Iberia and pagans in the Baltic, were profound, as the later sections of this chapter show.

Section 47.10

Saladin and the Third Crusade

The defeat at Hattin on 4 July 1187 was a catastrophe for the kingdom of Jerusalem. Saladin's army, after drawing the Latin field forces into a waterless march in the summer heat, destroyed them and captured King Guy of Lusignan and many leading nobles, including the master of the Templars. The Latin kingdom, stripped of its defenders, lost city after city, and Jerusalem surrendered to Saladin on 2 October 1187. In contrast to the capture of 1099, the surrender was negotiated, and the inhabitants were allowed to ransom themselves, though not all could afford to do so. Saladin permitted Eastern Christians to remain and Jews to return to the city. The Dome of the Rock and the al-Aqsa mosque, which had been turned to Christian use, were restored to Muslim worship.

Pope Gregory VIII's bull Audita tremendi, issued in October 1187, called for a new expedition and interpreted the disaster as divine punishment for Christian sins. The response was massive. Frederick Barbarossa, the emperor examined in Chapter 46, took the cross in 1188 and led a large army overland in 1189. Henry II of England and Philip II of France agreed to take the cross and imposed the Saladin tithe, a tax on revenues and movable goods, on their subjects. After Henry's death in 1189, his son Richard I of England and Philip led their forces by sea. Barbarossa drowned in a river in Cilicia in June 1190, and much of his army, deprived of its leader, returned home or dispersed.

The central campaign was the siege of Acre, which the Latins had begun in 1189 under Guy of Lusignan and which lasted nearly two years. Richard and Philip arrived in 1191, and the city surrendered in July. Philip then returned to France to pursue his own political interests. Richard, a skilled commander, defeated Saladin at Arsuf in September 1191 and captured Jaffa, but he decided not to attack Jerusalem, which he could not hold without control of supply lines and the cooperation of the squabbling Latin leaders. During the Acre campaign, he ordered the execution of a large number of Muslim prisoners when negotiations over their ransom failed. Richard also conquered Cyprus in 1191, which later became a Latin kingdom.

Richard and Saladin negotiated a settlement in September 1192. Jerusalem remained in Muslim hands, the Latins retained a strip of coast from Tyre to Jaffa, and Christian pilgrims were guaranteed access to the holy places. The expedition thus accomplished the partial recovery of the Latin kingdom but not its original capital. The campaign illustrated the logistical complexity of crusading, the importance of sea power, the limits of coalition warfare, and the possibility of negotiated agreement. Both sides were divided: the Latin leaders by rivalry and the succession to the kingdom of Jerusalem, and Saladin's coalition by the diverse interests of his emirs and relatives. Saladin died in 1193.

Section 47.11

The Fourth Crusade and the Sack of Constantinople

Pope Innocent III proclaimed a new crusade in 1198, aimed at Egypt, seen as the key to controlling Jerusalem. The leaders, mostly French nobles, contracted with Venice in 1201 for a fleet to carry the army. The contract, negotiated with the doge Enrico Dandolo, was based on estimates of numbers that proved too high, because far fewer crusaders arrived at Venice than had been expected. The crusaders could not pay the agreed price, and the Venetians, who had committed enormous resources to building the fleet, demanded payment. As a way of reducing the debt, the crusaders agreed to help Venice capture the Adriatic city of Zara, a Christian city under the king of Hungary, in November 1202. Innocent III condemned the attack and excommunicated the participants, though he later lifted the penalty from the crusaders but not from the Venetians.

The next diversion came with an offer from Alexios Angelos, the son of the deposed Byzantine emperor Isaac II, who promised money, troops, and submission of the Greek Church to Rome if the crusaders restored his father to the throne. Some crusaders objected, and some left the expedition, but the leaders, including Boniface of Montferrat, accepted. The fleet reached Constantinople in June 1203, and Isaac and Alexios IV were installed. When Alexios IV could not pay the promised sums and was overthrown in a coup in January 1204, the crusaders decided to take the city for themselves. On 12 April 1204, they stormed Constantinople and sacked it over three days, looting churches, palaces, and monasteries, destroying works of art, and killing many inhabitants. They then partitioned the empire, electing Baldwin of Flanders as emperor of a new Latin Empire.

This chapter treats the episode as a problem within crusading history. How did an expedition proclaimed for Egypt end up at Constantinople? Historians stress the role of debt and of the contract with Venice, the weakness of the crusade's leadership and recruitment, the intervention of Byzantine dynastic politics, and the lure of wealth and conquest. They disagree on whether the diversion was planned from the beginning, a view that most scholars now reject, or resulted from a series of improvised responses to financial pressure. Innocent III, who had condemned the attack on Zara and who had not authorized the assault on Constantinople, at first rebuked the crusaders and then, after the fact, accepted the creation of the Latin Empire and the union of the churches, hoping for benefit to the Holy Land.

The sack revealed the contradictions of crusading ideology: a movement intended to defend Christendom turned on a Christian city, and its participants, who had taken vows and received indulgences, plundered the greatest Christian city in the world. For Greek Christians, 1204 became a lasting symbol of Latin hostility and deepened the division between the churches that Chapter 40 described. The fuller consequences for Byzantium, including the Latin Empire, the successor states at Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond, the Byzantine restoration of 1261, and the later transformation of the Balkans, belong to Chapter 41. Here it is enough to say that the Fourth Crusade shows how the practical needs of finance, shipping, and politics could overwhelm the stated aims of an expedition.

Section 47.12

The Thirteenth Century Crusades: Egypt, Diplomacy, and the End of the Mainland States

The strategic emphasis shifted toward Egypt in the thirteenth century, since the Ayyubid rulers of Egypt controlled the resources that supported their power in Syria and Palestine. The Fifth Crusade (1217 to 1221), called by Innocent III's Lateran Council of 1215 and led after his death by his successor Honorius III, captured the Egyptian port of Damietta in 1219 after a long siege. The papal legate Pelagius insisted on advancing toward Cairo and rejected an offer by the Ayyubid sultan al-Kamil to exchange Damietta for Jerusalem and other territory. The army, caught by the flooding of the Nile, was forced to surrender in 1221. The expedition showed the risk of papal direction of campaigns.

Frederick II, who had taken the cross in 1215 and repeatedly delayed his departure, finally sailed in 1228 under the pressure of Pope Gregory IX, who had excommunicated him in 1227 for his failure to go. Chapter 46 described Frederick's imperial politics, and the present chapter is concerned with what the expedition reveals about crusading. Frederick, married to the heiress of Jerusalem and thus claiming its crown, negotiated with al-Kamil, who was preoccupied with disputes among the Ayyubids. In February 1229 they agreed to a treaty granting Frederick Jerusalem, Bethlehem, and a corridor to the coast, while leaving the Muslim holy places on the Temple Mount under Muslim control. Frederick crowned himself in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in March 1229. The outcome was recovery without a battle, but it was denounced by the Latin patriarch and by many in the Latin East, and it demonstrated the tensions among crusading, diplomacy, and papal authority. The papacy, in conflict with the emperor, was unwilling to endorse a negotiated success achieved by an excommunicate.

Jerusalem was lost again in 1244 when Khwarazmian soldiers, displaced by the Mongol advance, seized the city. King Louis IX of France took the cross in 1244 and in 1248 led a large expedition to Egypt, capturing Damietta in 1249. His army was defeated at Mansurah in February 1250, and he was captured, ransomed, and went to the Latin East, where he spent four years strengthening fortifications and acting as an arbiter among the Latin lords. Meanwhile, in Egypt in 1250 the Ayyubid regime was overthrown by its slave soldiers, the Mamluks, who established a new dynasty. Under Sultan Baybars, who came to power in 1260 after the Mamluk victory over the Mongols at Ain Jalut, the Mamluks launched a systematic campaign against the Latin states. They took Caesarea and Arsuf in 1265, Antioch in 1268, and other strongholds.

Louis IX led a second expedition, to Tunis, in 1270, where he died of disease. Latin leaders and the papacy also explored diplomatic ties with the Mongols, who had destroyed Baghdad in 1258 and who hoped for allies against the Mamluks, but the contacts produced no durable alliance. The Second Council of Lyon in 1274 considered plans for a new crusade, which did not materialize. The Latin kingdom, reduced to a coastal strip and divided by rivalry, fell stage by stage. Tripoli was captured in 1289, and Acre, the last great Latin stronghold, fell to the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf Khalil on 18 May 1291 after a siege in which the military orders played a notable part. The remaining coastal strongholds were soon abandoned. The loss of the mainland states did not, however, end the crusading movement, as later sections show.

Section 47.13

Iberia: Christian Expansion and the Problem of the "Reconquista"

Iberia in the eleventh century was divided among several Christian and Muslim polities. The Umayyad caliphate of Córdoba, which had dominated most of the peninsula, collapsed in the early 1030s and broke into numerous small successor kingdoms, the taifas. In the north, the Christian kingdoms of León, Castile, Navarre, Aragon, and the counties of Catalonia competed with one another as much as with their Muslim neighbors. Christian rulers exploited taifa weakness by demanding tribute, called parias, and by conquest. Alfonso VI of León-Castile captured Toledo in 1085, an important Christian gain that prompted the taifa kings to call on the Almoravids, a Berber movement from North Africa, who defeated Alfonso at Sagrajas in 1086 and unified much of al-Andalus under their rule. The Almohads, another Berber movement, replaced them in the twelfth century.

Relations across religious lines were complex. Rulers made alliances and treaties with those of another faith, and warriors served whichever lord paid them. The Castilian noble Rodrigo Díaz, known as El Cid, served the Muslim ruler of Zaragoza before conquering Valencia in 1094 and ruling it until his death. Christians, Muslims, and Jews lived in the same cities under successive regimes, and Christians who lived under Muslim rule, called Mozarabs, and Muslims who lived under Christian rule, later called Mudejars, retained their religion and laws, subject to taxation and restrictions. Historians have debated whether this pattern of coexistence, sometimes called convivencia, was a model of tolerance or a hierarchy that depended on the dominance of one group. Both cautions are warranted: the evidence shows genuine cooperation and cultural exchange, examined further in Chapter 48, as well as inequality and episodes of violence and persecution, including under Almohad rule against Christians and Jews.

Papal involvement grew in the twelfth century. Urban II had encouraged the restoration of Tarragona, and Pope Calixtus II extended crusading privileges to Iberian campaigns in the 1120s. Alfonso I of Aragon took Zaragoza in 1118. The capture of Lisbon in 1147 and the campaigns of the Second Crusade integrated Iberia explicitly into the movement. The military orders of Calatrava, Santiago, and Alcántara in Castile and León, and of Avis in Portugal, were founded in the later twelfth century and took on defense of the frontier, receiving castles and lands. Rulers also used the language of crusade to justify taxes and to attract foreign knights.

The decisive campaign came in 1212, after the Almohad caliph al-Mansur had defeated Alfonso VIII of Castile at Alarcos in 1195 and his son al-Nasir continued to threaten the frontier. Innocent III proclaimed a crusade, and a coalition of the kings of Castile, Aragon, and Navarre, with crusaders from beyond the Pyrenees, defeated the Almohads at Las Navas de Tolosa on 16 July 1212. The victory opened Andalusia to Christian conquest. After the Almohad empire fragmented, Ferdinand III of Castile took Córdoba in 1236 and Seville in 1248, James I of Aragon took Valencia in 1238, and the Portuguese completed the conquest of the Algarve by about 1249. Only the kingdom of Granada, under the Nasrid dynasty, survived as a Muslim state until 1492, usually as a tributary of Castile. The conquered lands were settled by Christian colonists, and Muslim populations, many of whom were expelled or emigrated, either remained as Mudejars or left. The distribution of land and the treatment of conquered populations shaped Iberian society for centuries, and the later history appears in Chapter 68 and Chapter 75.

The term Reconquista, reconquest, deserves caution. Some medieval writers did describe the Christian kingdoms as restoring the lost Visigothic realm, and the idea of recovery was real. But the modern notion of a single, continuous, eight hundred year Christian war of national liberation, culminating inevitably in 1492, is a retrospective construction of later centuries, especially the nineteenth and twentieth, that imposes a national narrative on a fragmented and shifting medieval reality. Christian kingdoms fought each other, made alliances with Muslim rulers, and pursued dynastic and economic interests. The Iberian wars were sometimes crusades and sometimes ordinary politics, and the history of al-Andalus, with its long cultural and intellectual achievements, cannot be reduced to a prelude to Christian conquest.

Section 47.14

Northern and Baltic Crusades

The northern frontier of Latin Christendom in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries included peoples who remained pagan: the Wends and other Slavic groups on the southern Baltic coast, and the Baltic and Finnic peoples to the east, including Prussians, Lithuanians, Livonians, Latvians, and Estonians. As Chapter 35 described, Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, and Scandinavia had already accepted Christianity. The expansion of Latin Christianity into the remaining areas was driven by missionary aims, by the interests of German, Danish, and Swedish rulers, by the ambitions of bishops, and by commerce, including the growing trade of north German towns, which Chapter 54 examines.

The Wendish Crusade of 1147 inaugurated papal authorization of campaigns on this frontier. It achieved little in its immediate aim, but Saxon, Danish, and Polish rulers continued to attack and settle among the Wends, and the territory east of the Elbe became part of the German and Danish spheres in the decades that followed. In Livonia, Bishop Albert of Riga, who founded the city of Riga in 1201, recruited crusaders and, in 1202, established the Order of the Sword Brothers, a military order that conquered much of Livonia. The Danes under Valdemar II conquered northern Estonia in 1219, and German and Danish rivalries shaped the region. After a devastating defeat by Samogitian and Semigallian forces at the Battle of Saule in 1236, the surviving Sword Brothers were incorporated into the Teutonic Order in 1237.

The Teutonic Order, invited around 1226 by Duke Conrad of Masovia to help against the pagan Prussians, received imperial and papal confirmation of its rights and began a prolonged conquest of Prussia, which lasted until the 1280s. Prussian resistance was fierce, and the conquest brought destructive warfare, forced submission, settlement reorganization, and substantial immigration by German and other Christian colonists, along with the founding of towns and the building of castles. Native Prussian communities nevertheless remained an important part of the population under the new regime, although many were gradually assimilated culturally and linguistically over the following centuries. The Order created a territorial state, which in the fourteenth century became a major power in the Baltic. The conversion of the pagan peoples was a stated aim, but it was often achieved through military conquest, and the distinction between missionary activity and coercive expansion is difficult to maintain. Lithuania successfully resisted and became a major power in its own right, examined in Chapter 54. Its rulers remained formally pagan until Jogaila's baptism in 1386; organized Christianization of Lithuania followed in 1387, while Samogitia converted later.

Students should note the different character of the northern crusades. They were not directed against Muslims, and they involved the settlement of colonists and the formation of new states as well as the defeat of enemies. They also interacted with the Orthodox world: conflicts between the Catholic Swedes, Danes, and Germans and the Orthodox Russian principalities, including the clash at Lake Peipus in 1242 when Alexander Nevsky of Novgorod defeated the Teutonic Order's forces, have been heavily mythologized in later national histories and should be approached critically. The sources, mainly chronicles written by clergy of the invading orders and bishoprics, reflect the viewpoint of the conquerors, and the evidence for the pagan societies is limited and largely archaeological.

Section 47.15

Crusading within Latin Christendom

The extension of crusading to heretics and political enemies within Latin Christendom shows how a religious instrument directed outward could be turned inward. The most prominent example is the Albigensian Crusade. In southern France, particularly in the county of Toulouse and its neighbors, a religious movement that opponents called Cathar, and which historians interpret in differing ways, had gained a following, and the Church considered it heretical. After the murder in January 1208 of the papal legate Peter of Castelnau, Innocent III called a crusade against the heretics and their protectors, offering the same indulgences as for the Holy Land. Northern French nobles and others responded. The crusaders captured Béziers in July 1209, where a large part of the population was slaughtered, and Carcassonne, and Simon de Montfort emerged as the leader of the campaign.

The war was complicated by political interests. The count of Toulouse, Raymond VI, and his allies, including the king of Aragon, Peter II, who was killed at Muret in 1213, resisted the northern invaders, and the conflict became partly a struggle over lordship in the south. The French crown, initially hesitant, intervened more directly, and the Treaty of Paris in 1229 ended the main war, with the count's lands passing in time to the French royal family. The crusade thus advanced the extension of Capetian power into the south. It also stimulated new methods of suppressing heresy, notably the development of inquisitorial procedures in the 1230s, which Chapter 49 will discuss in connection with religious life.

Popes also used crusading privileges against political opponents. Innocent III proclaimed a crusade against Markward of Anweiler, an imperial agent in Sicily, in 1199. Later popes called crusades against Frederick II and his heirs in the 1240s and 1250s, and against Manfred in southern Italy, the campaign ultimately carried out by Charles of Anjou, as Chapter 46 described. In 1284 and 1285, Pope Martin IV proclaimed a crusade against the king of Aragon, Peter III, after the Sicilian Vespers. These political crusades were controversial. Contemporary critics, including some churchmen, complained that crusading was being diverted from the Holy Land and used to defend papal territorial and dynastic interests. Defenders argued that the papacy was defending the Church against its enemies, whoever they were.

The development raises a central question for historians: whether it represents a corruption of an originally pure ideal or the natural extension of the logic of crusading from the start. The earliest expedition had already attacked fellow Christians, and the foundation of the movement lay in papal authority to define legitimate war. The use of crusading privileges against heretics and political enemies was consistent with that authority, though it provoked resistance and criticism. It also strengthened the papal monarchy while contributing to the later political and fiscal tensions between the papacy and the rulers of France, England, and the Empire, as later chapters will show.

Regional Variation

Five Crusading Frontiers

The Levant: A region of Latin lordships among Muslim, Eastern Christian, and Jewish populations, defended by castles and military orders and dependent on Mediterranean shipping, with the principal political aim of holding Jerusalem and the coast.
Egypt and North Africa: The target of expeditions in 1218 to 1221, 1249 to 1250, and 1270, because the Ayyubid and Mamluk rulers drew their resources from Egypt, and the region was a focus of Mediterranean commerce.
Iberia: A peninsula of competing Christian and Muslim states in which crusading privileges were added to frontier warfare, settlement, and long term coexistence, resulting in conquest of most of the south by 1250.
The Baltic: A frontier of conversion, settlement, and the creation of new territorial states by military orders and bishops, in which German and Danish interests were prominent.
Southern France and Italy: Regions within Latin Christendom where crusading was used against heretics and the papacy's political enemies, with consequences for local lordship and for royal power.
Section 47.16

Consequences, Limits, Memory, and the Meaning of the Crusading Movement

The fall of Acre in 1291 did not end crusading. Plans and expeditions to recover the Holy Land continued for generations, including proposals by writers and rulers, naval leagues in the eastern Mediterranean, the Hospitallers' conquest of Rhodes in the early fourteenth century, and expeditions against the Ottoman Turks in the late fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, which Chapter 41 examines in connection with Ottoman expansion. The Teutonic Order fought in the Baltic into the fourteenth century. Popes continued to proclaim crusades and to grant indulgences. The movement changed but persisted, and its character shifted toward defense against the expanding Ottoman power in southeastern Europe. Treating 1291 as the end artificially concludes a process that continued.

The consequences for Latin Christendom were broad. Crusading strengthened the papacy as the authority that could proclaim wars, grant indulgences, and levy taxes, and it contributed to the development of papal government, though it also brought criticism and, later, tension with kings. It reinforced an aristocratic religious culture in which knighthood was associated with service to Christ, shaping the ideals and literature of chivalry. It supported the growth of Italian maritime commerce, since Venice, Genoa, and Pisa profited from transporting crusaders and from trading privileges in the Levant. It stimulated experiments in taxation and finance, and it encouraged the development of military orders as permanent religious and financial institutions. It also contributed to a vocabulary of religious hostility that affected Latin attitudes toward Jews, Muslims, heretics, and Greeks.

For other societies the consequences were different. In the Muslim world, the Frankish presence prompted the consolidation of states and the development of jihad ideology, and it left a mixed legacy of memory: in some later periods the crusades were treated as one episode among many, and only in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries did they become central to modern political narratives. For Jewish communities, the crusades brought periodic violence, shaped liturgy and memory, and contributed to a deteriorating legal and social position in Latin Europe. For Eastern Christians, the effects ranged from cooperation to subordination, and the sack of 1204 left a deep mark on Greek memory. For the peoples of the Baltic and Iberia, crusading was entwined with conquest, settlement, and conversion.

The memory of the crusades has been repeatedly reshaped. Early modern writers used them as examples in religious controversy. Romantic and nineteenth century authors, including popular novelists and historians, celebrated them as chivalric adventures or, alternatively, condemned them as fanaticism. European imperial powers invoked crusading imagery, and nationalist movements in many regions claimed medieval episodes as part of their own histories, including the Iberian Reconquista and the Baltic conquests. In recent decades, political speakers and movements of widely differing outlooks have used the word crusade and the image of the Franks as metaphors. Historians distinguish these later uses from the medieval phenomenon, which must be studied through its own evidence. The meaning of the crusading movement, as this chapter has argued, lies in how a penitential, papally authorized practice of war became a durable institution of Latin Christendom that operated across several theaters and affected many societies.

Historical Debate

Why Did People Go on Crusade?

The question: What motivated those who took the cross: religious devotion, the pursuit of land and wealth, social and family pressures, or a combination?

Interpretation A: Material and political motives were primary. Older accounts, especially from the Enlightenment and the nineteenth century, emphasized the hope of gain, arguing that landless younger sons of the nobility and ambitious lords used religion as cover for conquest and plunder. They pointed to the establishment of principalities such as Antioch and Edessa and to the commercial advantages won by Italian cities.
Interpretation B: Religious devotion was central, though not exclusive. Many recent historians, drawing on charters and on the evidence of the enormous cost of the journey, argue that most participants gave up substantial wealth and often did not return, that the language of penance and pilgrimage was sincerely held, and that family and lordship networks encouraged participation. They stress that the idea of religious and material motives as opposed is misleading, since medieval people did not separate them as modern readers might.

Evidence: Both views rely on chronicles, charters, letters, and the outcomes of the expeditions. Interpretation A emphasizes the careers of the beneficiaries. Interpretation B emphasizes the financial burden documented in charters and the high mortality of participants, as well as the religious vocabulary of sources. Because most participants left no records of their own, motives must be inferred from indirect evidence.

Why it matters: How historians weigh motives affects whether crusading is understood as an expression of a particular religious culture, as a form of expansionism, or both, and it influences how it is compared with later colonial enterprises. The scholarship is better described as overlapping than as two opposed camps, since few historians now claim that either piety or ambition alone explains the movement.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence from this chapter that support each interpretation, and explain what further evidence would help to assess the motives of ordinary participants as distinct from leaders.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Crusading Idea from 1095 to the Late Thirteenth Century

What Changed

  • The movement shifted from an ad hoc pilgrimage in arms to an institution with taxation, preaching, legal privileges, and permanent military orders
  • Its geography widened from Jerusalem to Egypt, Iberia, the Baltic, southern France, and Italy
  • Its leadership moved from nobles and papal legates to kings and, eventually, to papal political direction
  • The Latin states in the East were created, expanded, and then lost

What Stayed the Same

  • Papal authorization, the vow, the sign of the cross, and the indulgence remained its core features
  • Participation depended on family, lordship, and finance, and the costs fell heavily on households
  • Violence against civilians, including Jews and other noncombatants, recurred in different forms
  • Maritime transport by Italian cities remained essential to expeditions

Who experienced change most directly: The inhabitants of the Levant, Iberia, and the Baltic experienced the greatest transformation, through conquest, loss of rule, or settlement. Who experienced greater continuity: Most rural populations in Latin Europe, for whom crusading meant taxation and preaching rather than travel.

Why the pattern matters: The combination shows a movement that adapted to circumstances while retaining its defining features, which is why the chapter treats crusading as a long term institution and not a series of separate events.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the crusading movement was a distinctive institution of Latin Christian Europe that developed from the appeal of Pope Urban II in 1095 into a durable religious, political, and military practice applied across several theaters. It defined a crusade as a penitential war proclaimed under papal authority, entered by vow, and rewarded with spiritual and legal privileges, while noting that medieval people did not use a single word for it and that the modern numbered sequence of crusades is a convenience that obscures the movement's continuity and variety.

The movement emerged from a religious culture of pilgrimage and penance, from the Church's efforts to restrain and redirect aristocratic violence, from the reforming papacy's claim to leadership of Latin Christendom, and from a Byzantine appeal for help after Manzikert. These elements converged without converging in expectation: Urban, Alexios I, and the western knights understood the enterprise differently. The mobilization of 1095 and 1096 depended on preaching, kinship and lordship networks, and the willingness of monasteries to supply cash, and it was costly for families who sold or pledged property to take part.

Popular expeditions in 1096 showed how crusading enthusiasm could become destructive. Attacks on Jewish communities at Speyer, Worms, Mainz, and other Rhineland towns, recorded in Latin and Hebrew sources, killed thousands and left a lasting mark on Jewish memory, even as bishops and other Christians tried to protect their neighbors. The main armies, meanwhile, endured the march across Anatolia, the siege of Antioch, and the assault on Jerusalem, succeeding in large part because of Muslim political division and Byzantine assistance. The capture of Jerusalem on 15 July 1099, accompanied by massacre, established the Latin East.

The Latin states of Edessa, Antioch, Jerusalem, and Tripoli governed populations that were mostly Muslim, Eastern Christian, and Jewish, and they developed institutions blending western and local practices. Their survival depended on Italian maritime cities, on pilgrims and crusaders, and on military orders such as the Templars and Hospitallers, which combined religious vows with armed service and became wealthy, independent institutions. Together with preaching, indulgences for financial contributions, taxation such as the Saladin tithe, and legal privileges, these institutions made crusading durable.

Muslim responses were varied and slow to coordinate. Seljuk fragmentation and Fatimid weakness aided the first crusaders. Over the twelfth century Zengi, Nur al-Din, and Saladin consolidated Syria and Egypt and used jihad as a source of legitimacy, culminating in the victory at Hattin and the recovery of Jerusalem in 1187. The Second Crusade failed in the East but, in 1147, extended crusading to Iberia and the Baltic. The Third Crusade, a coalition effort led by Richard I and Philip II, recovered the coast but not Jerusalem and ended in a negotiated settlement.

The Fourth Crusade demonstrated the contradictions of the movement. Debt to Venice, dynastic politics in Constantinople, and the weakness of leadership led to the sack of the Byzantine capital in 1204, which Chapter 41 examines in its fuller consequences. In the thirteenth century, strategy shifted to Egypt, with the Fifth Crusade, the negotiated recovery of Jerusalem by Frederick II in 1229, and the expeditions of Louis IX, while the Mamluks, who took power in Egypt in 1250, systematically conquered the Latin states, ending with the fall of Acre in 1291.

Crusading also operated beyond the Levant. In Iberia, papal privileges and military orders reinforced Christian expansion against the Almoravids, Almohads, and their successors, including the victory at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212, though the term Reconquista should be used with caution. In the Baltic, the Sword Brothers and the Teutonic Order conquered and settled Livonia and Prussia. In Latin Christendom itself, crusades were launched against heretics in southern France and against political enemies of the papacy, including the Hohenstaufen, an extension that provoked criticism and strengthened papal and royal power.

The consequences were wide. Crusading strengthened papal authority, shaped aristocratic religious culture, promoted Italian commerce and new methods of taxation and finance, and contributed to hostility toward Jews, Muslims, heretics, and Greeks. It also continued beyond 1291, shifting toward defense against the Ottomans, and its memory has been repeatedly reshaped by later generations for religious, national, imperial, and political purposes. Historians distinguish these later uses from the medieval movement, which is best understood through its own varied evidence and with attention to all the societies it affected.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the crusading movement was more than a series of wars in the Holy Land. It was a way in which Latin Christendom organized religious devotion, aristocratic violence, finance, and papal authority, and it was applied wherever the Church and its allies identified an enemy. Seen across its full range, from Jerusalem to Lisbon, Riga, and Béziers, the movement reveals both the confidence and the capacity for harm of the High Middle Ages.

The chapter also illuminates larger processes in Europe's history. The growth of papal government, the development of taxation, the expansion of Italian maritime commerce, the creation of religious orders with military functions, and the formation of new states on the Baltic and in Iberia all drew on crusading. At the same time, the movement drew Latin Europe into sustained contact with Byzantium, the Muslim world, and the societies of the Baltic, contact that included war and violence but also trade, diplomacy, and cultural exchange. Chapter 48 will turn to the universities and learning in which some of that exchange left its mark, and Chapter 49 to the medieval society that experienced both the benefits and burdens of these changes.

Within Volume II, crusading adds an essential dimension to the argument that medieval Europe was a world of several overlapping systems. Chapter 46 showed an empire whose emperors contested with the papacy, and the crusading movement shows how that same papacy sought to lead Christendom through a different instrument. The Fourth Crusade connects this chapter to the history of Byzantium and Orthodox Europe in Chapter 41, a reminder that Latin Christendom was not the whole of Christian Europe.

Finally, the chapter invites caution about the use of the past. The crusades have been invoked so often, and with such varied intentions, that it is easy to mistake later meanings for medieval ones. A careful historical approach asks what participants, victims, and observers actually said and did, and it recognizes that the same events appeared very differently from different positions. The questions the movement raises, about the relationship of religion and violence, about coexistence and conflict among faiths, and about how societies remember, remain important, and they are best approached through evidence and not through inherited legend.
