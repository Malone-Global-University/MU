The Formation of England, France, and the German Kingdom

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 899 and 1016, three political worlds of the post-Carolingian era learned how to outlast their kings. None of them became a modern state, and none of them was fated to become one. What they acquired was more modest and more durable: ways of making legitimate rulers, of persuading regional elites and bishops to cooperate with them, and of surviving the deaths of individual kings.

At Pentecost in 973, in the old Roman town of Bath, Edgar, king of the English, was consecrated in a ceremony conducted by the archbishops of Canterbury and York. The rite followed the liturgical models of Frankish and Christian kingship, and later English coronation ceremonies would descend from it. Fourteen years later, in the early summer of 987, a group of West Frankish magnates and bishops, led in the accounts we have by Archbishop Adalbero of Reims, chose Hugh, duke of the Franks, as their king, passing over a surviving Carolingian claimant. In February 962, in Rome, the Saxon king Otto I received the imperial crown from Pope John XII. Three ceremonies, in three places, involved three rulers who did not know one another as rivals or allies in any simple way, yet all three belong to one story: the way in which the fragments left by Carolingian division and Scandinavian, Magyar, and other pressures were reassembled into political communities that could reproduce themselves.

This chapter answers the question that Chapter 32 deliberately left open. That chapter showed how the Carolingian world broke into regional kingdoms and principalities, how Scandinavian raiders became settlers and sometimes rulers, how Magyar horsemen raided across central Europe, and how local powers adapted through fortification, regional leadership, and negotiated authority. It did not explain which of those adaptive structures would prove lasting. Here the question is direct: after the fragmentation and warfare of the ninth and early tenth centuries, why did England, West Francia, and East Francia develop into durable kingdoms, and why did consolidation take a different form in each?

The chapter's argument is comparative and resists a national teleology. It does not describe the sudden appearance of modern England, France, and Germany. The kingdom of the English of the 950s, the West Frankish kingdom of the 990s, and the East Frankish kingdom that historians often call the German kingdom of the Ottonians were not embryonic nation-states. They were monarchies whose rulers depended on aristocratic cooperation, ecclesiastical support, military success, inherited administrative practice, and a good deal of luck in the matter of heirs. Their durability lay not in centralization, which was limited in all three, but in the capacity to reproduce legitimate kingship, to share power with regional elites and churches, to organize defense and justice, and to survive succession crises and, in England's case, foreign conquest.

The three cases differ sharply. In England, a West Saxon dynasty working with Mercian allies conquered Scandinavian-controlled territories and built a kingdom whose shires, assemblies, laws, and coinage gave it unusual administrative depth. In West Francia, kings retained great prestige while the direct royal domain shrank around Paris and Orléans, and power in practice rested with princes in Normandy, Flanders, Aquitaine, Burgundy, Anjou, and elsewhere; yet after 987 the Capetian dynasty achieved a continuity that few contemporaries could have predicted. In East Francia, a Saxon dynasty won kingship by negotiation with dukes, defeated rebels and Magyar armies, and turned its predominance into an imperial coronation in 962 that gave the kingdom a distinctive relationship with Italy and the papacy.

The evidence for these developments is uneven and often produced by the winners. English history draws on charters, law codes, coin hoards, place names, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle in several versions, and the archaeology of fortified towns. West Frankish history relies heavily on the histories of Flodoard of Reims and Richer of Reims, on charters, and on the letters attributed to Gerbert of Aurillac. East Frankish history depends on the Saxon chronicler Widukind of Corvey, on Liudprand of Cremona, on Thietmar of Merseburg, and on royal diplomas. Each of these sources has its own patron, purpose, and blind spots, and much of the chapter concerns how historians reason with them.

This chapter belongs to Part V, which traces the formation of Latin Christendom after Rome, and it stands at the hinge between the fragmentation studied in Chapter 32 and the social structures examined in Chapter 34, which turns to lordship, dependency, castles, and the debate over feudalism. It also anticipates later volumes' treatment of medieval monarchy. Norman England and the growth of stronger monarchies are the subject of Chapter 45, and the Holy Roman Empire as a political system is examined in Chapter 46. The present chapter therefore stops where its own question stops: at the point where these three kingdoms had shown that they could survive.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what historians mean by political consolidation and state formation in the tenth century, and distinguish it from modern centralization.
  2. Trace the expansion of West Saxon and Mercian power from Edward the Elder and Æthelflæd through Æthelstan and Edgar, and assess the roles of fortification, alliance, and conquest.
  3. Analyze the institutions of English royal government, including shires, hundreds, assemblies, law codes, and coinage, and explain why they made the kingdom reproducible.
  4. Assess why the English kingdom survived Cnut's conquest in 1016 rather than being dismantled by it.
  5. Describe the political structure of West Francia after 888, and explain why weak direct royal control did not make kingship irrelevant.
  6. Interpret the election of Hugh Capet in 987 and the early Capetian practice of association and coronation of heirs.
  7. Explain how East Frankish kingship passed from the Carolingians to Conrad I and then to the Saxon dynasty, and why Henry I and Otto I relied on negotiated authority.
  8. Contextualize Otto I's imperial coronation of 962 without treating it as the foundation of the Holy Roman Empire.
  9. Compare the three paths to durability and identify what they shared and where they differed.
  10. Evaluate the evidence, including charters, laws, chronicles, and coinage, on which our understanding of tenth-century kingship rests, and identify its limitations.
  11. Distinguish tenth-century political communities from later national identities and avoid projecting modern nations backward.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What makes a kingdom durable, and how can historians tell durability from mere survival?
  2. Why did some post-Carolingian political arrangements endure while others dissolved?
  3. In what ways was English royal government in the tenth century more integrated than West Frankish or East Frankish government, and what accounts for the difference?
  4. How did kings who directly controlled little territory, as the early Capetians did, nevertheless build lasting dynasties?
  5. What did it mean for a king to be elected in a world where hereditary claims also mattered?
  6. How did churches, bishops, and monasteries contribute to political consolidation, and what did they gain in return?
  7. What are the strengths and limits of the phrases "first king of England," "the founding of France," and "the German kingdom" as descriptions of tenth-century realities?
  8. How did ordinary households, whose daily lives depended on local lords and courts, experience these dynastic changes?

Historical Context

The Carolingian empire, treated in Chapter 31, had governed a huge territory through a system of counts, bishops, assemblies, oaths, and royal itineration that depended heavily on the personal abilities of the ruler and the cooperation of aristocratic families. Its division among Charlemagne's grandsons, including the settlement at Verdun in 843, produced kingdoms that were dynastic divisions, not planned nation-states, as Chapter 32 emphasized. Over the following decades the western and eastern kingdoms passed through partitions, reunifications, and disputed successions. The empire was briefly reunited under Charles the Fat and then broke apart again after his deposition in 887 and death in 888. From that point the western Frankish world and the eastern Frankish world were ruled separately by kings drawn from varying families.

At the same time, external pressure and internal regionalism shaped what followed. Scandinavian fleets and armies raided and then settled in the Frankish and British worlds, creating territories such as the Scandinavian-ruled kingdom of York and the Scandinavian settlements of eastern and northern England, and the region around Rouen that would become Normandy. Magyar horsemen from the Carpathian basin raided East Francia, Italy, and parts of West Francia in the early tenth century. Kings who could not protect their people lost authority to those who could: counts, dukes, bishops, and local lords who built fortifications, raised armies, and negotiated truces. The result was a political landscape in which regional elites were often more immediately powerful than kings, and in which royal authority was maintained through consensus, ritual, gifts, and the manipulation of office and land rather than through bureaucratic command.

Three features of this landscape shaped what came next. First, the ideology of Christian kingship survived and was in some ways strengthened. Rulers were anointed, prayed for, and instructed by bishops who saw kingship as a religious office with duties of defense, justice, and protection of the church. Second, the practical base of power varied. English kings inherited Alfred's system of fortified burhs and a fiscal and military system tied to the hide, an assessed unit of land. West Frankish kings inherited a prestigious title but a fragmented land. East Frankish kings, after 911, faced strong duchies whose leaders had built local followings in the face of Magyar attack. Third, the dynastic question was open. In both Francia and England, the death of a king without an adult heir, or the failure of a dynasty, could produce elections, rival claimants, or foreign intervention.

Historians need care with the language of "kingdoms," "duchies," and "nations." The political communities of the tenth century were defined by kings, dynasties, aristocratic networks, churches, and laws, not by shared national identity. The words Angli or Engle could refer to the peoples of England, and West Saxon and Mercian rulers cultivated an English identity as a political tool, but that identity was constructed, promoted, and contested. West Frankish rulers were reges Francorum, kings of the Franks, a title that referred to the Frankish people of the Carolingian past, not to a modern French nation. East Frankish rulers used similar titles, and the term "German kingdom" (regnum Teutonicorum) appears in sources only later, largely in the eleventh century. The phrase is a modern convenience and this chapter uses it with care.

Evidence deserves early attention because the surviving sources are unevenly distributed and often partial. Royal charters record grants of land and privileges, list witnesses, and show how rulers styled themselves, but they survive mainly in ecclesiastical archives and can be forged or altered. Law codes show what kings wished to regulate but not always what was enforced. Coins, which bear the names of kings and moneyers, allow historians to reconstruct monetary systems, mints, and the reach of royal authority. Narrative sources such as the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Flodoard's Annals, Richer's Histories, and Widukind's Deeds of the Saxons were written with particular audiences and interests, and several were composed decades after the events they describe. Archaeology, including the excavation of fortified towns, cemeteries, and settlements, provides an independent check. Historians therefore reason from a combination of sources and must ask, in every case, who produced the evidence and for whom.

Section 33.1

From Fragmentation to Consolidation, c. 900

Around 900, no observer could have predicted the outcomes this chapter describes. In West Francia, the kingship had passed in 888 to Odo, count of Paris, a son of the Robertian family, who had won reputation by defending Paris against a Scandinavian siege. He was challenged by supporters of the Carolingian Charles the Simple, and the kingdom came to be governed alongside the growing autonomy of dukes and counts. In East Francia, the last Carolingian, Louis the Child, was a minor whose reign was dominated by magnates and bishops and marked by Magyar raiding. In England, the kingdom of Wessex under Alfred and his son Edward stood beside Mercia, under a lord and later a lady who ruled largely in their own right, while large stretches of the east and north lay under Scandinavian leaders, and the kingdom of York had its own rulers.

This chapter uses the phrase "state formation" carefully. It does not mean the creation of a modern centralized state with a bureaucracy, a monopoly on violence, and defined borders. It means the gradual creation of arrangements capable of surviving individual rulers and dynastic accidents: shared expectations about who could be king and how, institutions of justice and taxation, a system for calling assemblies and armies, and a working relationship with bishops and regional aristocrats. Historians disagree about how much these arrangements were planned and how much they arose from improvisation, but they agree that durability was neither automatic nor equal in the three regions.

Three questions organize the chapter. The first concerns dynasty: could a family maintain a claim to kingship across generations, and how did that claim interact with election and designation? The second concerns cooperation: could kings secure the willing participation of aristocrats and churches, since none could rule without them? The third concerns capacity: could kings organize defense, justice, and revenue at a scale and with a continuity that enabled the kingdom to survive shocks? Each of the three kingdoms answered these questions differently. The chapter takes them in turn, beginning with England, then West Francia, and then East Francia, before comparing the three.

A caution about periodization applies at once. The years around 900 do not mark a clean turning point in every region. Consolidation began earlier in England, where Alfred's defensive reforms had already created a base, and came later in West Francia, where Capetian durability was not evident until well into the eleventh century. The ordering of sections is therefore not a chronological march but a comparative arrangement, and the reader should keep in mind that events in the three regions overlapped, occasionally intersected, and sometimes influenced one another. One marriage illustrates the connections: the sister of Æthelstan, Eadgyth (Edith), married Otto, the future king of East Francia, around 929 or 930, linking the Saxon dynasty of the German lands with the West Saxon dynasty of England.

Section 33.2

Edward the Elder, Æthelflæd, and the Expansion of Wessex

Alfred's death in 899 left his son Edward with a defensive inheritance whose central feature was the burh system, examined in Chapter 32: a network of fortified places, garrisoned by local levies, each assessed by the number of hides that supported it. The document known as the Burghal Hidage lists about thirty such places in Wessex and neighboring areas and assigns each a hidage, although it survives in later copies and its date and purpose remain debated. What is clear from written and archaeological evidence is that the system gave the West Saxon crown fortified nodes in the landscape, and that those nodes became centers of markets, mints, and royal administration. Edward, and his sister Æthelflæd in Mercia, extended this system into offensive action.

Their cooperation began against a background of tension. Mercia had suffered heavily from Scandinavian attack and settlement, and its western half was governed in the 890s and 900s by Æthelred, whose authority was formally under the West Saxon king though he acted with considerable independence. Æthelred was married to Æthelflæd, Alfred's daughter. When Æthelred fell ill and eventually died around 911, Æthelflæd took the leadership of Mercia, and is described in the Mercian Register, a section of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, as Lady of the Mercians. Edward at the same moment took direct control of London and Oxford, previously part of the Mercian sphere, an episode that shows both alliance and rivalry within the ruling house.

Æthelflæd's rule deserves independent treatment. She was not simply an agent of her brother. She made decisions about Mercian defense, directed the building of burhs at places such as Bridgnorth, Tamworth, Stafford, Warwick, Chirbury, and Runcorn, and led campaigns in her own name. In 916, she sent an army into Wales and destroyed the crannog of a Welsh king at Llangorse (Brecenanmere), capturing his wife and others. In 917 her forces took Derby from the Scandinavian rulers, at the cost of heavy casualties; in 918 the people of Leicester submitted to her, and the Chronicle reports that the men of York offered pledges of submission shortly before her death on 12 June 918. The sources that record her deeds include not only the Mercian Register but also Welsh and Irish annals, and the range of these records indicates that contemporaries regarded her as a ruler of real consequence. She was buried at Gloucester in a church, St Peter's, that she and her husband had patronized.

Edward's own campaigns proceeded on a parallel track. In 910, at Tettenhall in Staffordshire, a joint West Saxon and Mercian army defeated a force of Northumbrian Scandinavians, a victory the Chronicle presents as decisive. Over the next years Edward built new burhs at places including Hertford, Witham, Maldon, Colchester, Towcester, and Bedford, advancing gradually into territory held by the armies of eastern England. By 917 the Scandinavian armies of East Anglia and the eastern Midlands had submitted to him, according to the Chronicle, and after Æthelflæd's death in 918 Edward secured the submission of the Mercians. Her daughter Ælfwynn was briefly recognized in Mercia but was deprived of authority and taken into Wessex. The Mercian Register places Ælfwynn's removal in its annal for 919, although its chronology has led many historians to date the event to late 918. The episode shows that Mercian identity and leadership were still real and that West Saxon incorporation was the result of deliberate political action.

The consequences deserve careful description. Edward's kingdom was larger and more coherent than Alfred's, but it was not yet a kingdom of all the English. The Chronicle for 920 describes rulers from the north and west choosing Edward as father and lord, though modern historians debate whether this represents a submission by equals or a claim of overlordship made by West Saxon writers. Northumbria, including York, remained outside direct control. The expansion also did not mean the elimination of Scandinavian populations. Many settlers remained on their lands under West Saxon kingship, and place names, law, and personal names in the eastern Midlands and East Anglia show Scandinavian influence that persisted. What Edward and Æthelflæd created was a broader territorial and institutional base, not a finished polity.

Historical Vocabulary
Burh

A burh was an Old English fortified place, ranging from a reused Roman town or Iron Age hillfort to a newly laid out settlement with ramparts, gates, streets, and a garrison drawn from the surrounding countryside. Alfred, Edward, and Æthelflæd built or restored burhs as a system of defense and, increasingly, as instruments of territorial control. Many burhs became towns, with markets and mints; the name survives in many modern English place names ending in "-bury" or "-borough."

The term should not be equated with a medieval castle, which appears in later chapters and is associated with private lordship. A burh was public, under royal authority, and tied to the obligations of a district. It matters to this chapter because the burh system is a key example of how English kings used inherited structures of assessment and obligation to build durability.

Section 33.3

Æthelstan and the Emergence of a Kingdom of the English

Æthelstan, a son of Edward by a mother whose status is unclear in the sources, was raised at least partly in Mercia and had strong ties to the Mercian aristocracy. When Edward died in 924, the succession was contested; Æthelstan seems to have been accepted first in Mercia and was crowned at Kingston upon Thames in September 925. His reign is the moment when the political experiment of Wessex and Mercia becomes a recognizable kingdom of the English, though historians caution against turning it into a birth certificate.

The decisive event was the seizure of York in 927. When Sihtric, the Scandinavian ruler of York, died, his relative Guthfrith attempted to succeed him. Æthelstan took York, drove out Guthfrith, and demolished the fortifications of the city, according to later reports. Shortly afterward, at a meeting at Eamont near Penrith, rulers including Constantine II of the Scots and Hywel Dda of Deheubarth acknowledged Æthelstan's authority. The sources, some of them written later, describe these events as submissions; Scottish and Welsh perspectives are poorly documented, and modern historians debate whether the arrangements were tribute-paying subordination or a temporary recognition of overlordship. What is clear is that Æthelstan's court after 927 attracted rulers from across Britain, and that his charters employed unusually grand titles, styling the king as king of the English and, in some charters, ruler of all Britain.

Government in Æthelstan's reign was active and written. The witan, meeting at royal estates, produced law codes that survive in several versions. These laws address theft, the responsibility of lords for their men, the supervision of moneyers and markets, the payment of tithes, and the duties of officials. They also complain that earlier laws had not been enforced, a remark that reminds historians that laws state ambitions rather than describe practice. In the monetary sphere, Æthelstan's legislation required a single coinage across the kingdom and specified where coins could be struck, placing minting in towns under royal control. Coins of the period bear the king's name and the names of the moneyers and mints, allowing historians to plot the reach of royal authority. The king also patronized churches and monasteries, gathered relics from across the continent, and created a court culture that drew scholars from Francia and elsewhere, contributing to the religious and intellectual revival associated with the later tenth century.

Military success supported this political structure. In 934 Æthelstan led a large force into Scotland; in 937 he and his brother Edmund defeated at Brunanburh a coalition that included Olaf Guthfrithson, the Norse king of Dublin, Constantine of the Scots, and Owain of Strathclyde. The battle is celebrated in a poem preserved in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, but its location is unknown, and contemporary accounts vary in detail. The victory gave Æthelstan enormous prestige and demonstrated the capacity of the English kingdom to raise and sustain a major army.

The limits of this achievement became obvious after his death in October 939. Olaf Guthfrithson took York and parts of the east Midlands soon afterward, and Æthelstan's successor Edmund (r. 939–946) and then Eadred (r. 946–955) had to reconquer them. The last Scandinavian ruler of York, Eric, known in later tradition as Eric Bloodaxe, was expelled in 954, and only then was the northern kingdom fully incorporated. Æthelstan therefore exercised unprecedented rule over the English kingdoms, but political control in the north remained contested for a decade and a half after his death. This is why historians describe his reign as a major stage in the making of the kingdom, not its moment of birth. Sarah Foot's biography, titled Æthelstan: The First King of England, uses the traditional epithet while showing how contingent and incomplete the accomplishment was.

Primary Source Window

Æthelstan's Charters and Law Codes, 920s and 930s CE

Creator: Royal scribes and clerics at the court of Æthelstan, acting with the witan · Date: c. 925–939 · Type: Royal charters (land grants, usually to churches or thegns) and law codes issued after meetings of the king's council

Context: Æthelstan's charters were produced, in the late 920s and early 930s, largely by a single skilled scribe whom scholars call "Æthelstan A." They are distinctive for their elaborate Latin, learned vocabulary, and grand royal titles. The law codes, preserved in later manuscripts, record decisions made at assemblies at places including Grately, Exeter, and Whittlebury.

Paraphrase of representative content The charters style Æthelstan as king of the English, and in some documents as ruler of the whole of Britain, and they list among the witnesses bishops, ealdormen, thegns, and on some occasions Welsh and Scottish rulers designated as subkings. The laws state that the king, with his bishops and counselors, orders that theft be punished, that lords answer for their dependents, that coins be struck only in towns, and that royal officials called reeves and bishops are to ensure that the decrees are enforced. One law code complains that the peace has not been kept as the king wishes. This is a synthesis of the content of several documents and not a translation of a single passage.

Analysis: These sources show a king who claims authority over multiple peoples, who legislates at national assemblies, and who depends on bishops and lay magnates to execute royal decisions. The witness lists reveal who was present at court and thus who cooperated with the king. The grand titles are ideology as much as description: they express what the court wished to claim. The complaint about enforcement in the laws shows the gap between royal intention and local practice. The sources are the products of a learned ecclesiastical milieu, and they say little about Scandinavian-descended landholders, peasants, or women outside the elite. They also cannot reveal whether the northern population regarded themselves as subjects of Æthelstan.

Questions for students:

  1. What claims about kingship and territory do these sources make?
  2. Who created them, and what purposes might grand titles and law codes have served?
  3. What can historians learn from witness lists and legislative complaints about royal government?
  4. What can historians not establish from charters and laws alone about how ordinary people experienced royal authority?
Section 33.4

Edgar, Royal Government, and the Integration of England

The reign of Edgar (r. 959–975) shifts the focus from conquest to durability. Edgar had been made king of the Mercians and Northumbrians in 957 after a dispute with his brother Eadwig, and became king of all England in 959. His reign is traditionally described as peaceful, which may reflect both real stability and the flattering perspective of the churchmen who recorded it. The important question is why a kingdom created by conquest proved administratively reproducible, meaning capable of working and recurring without the constant presence of a warrior king.

Several features of English government explain this. The first is the shire, a territorial unit with its own court that met periodically. Ealdormen exercised regional authority, often over several shires, while royal reeves or shire-reeves increasingly handled local fiscal, judicial, and administrative business. The shires of Wessex were older; the Mercian shires, of which many took their names from burhs like Bedford, Derby, and Warwick, were organized in the tenth century, probably in connection with the West Saxon incorporation of Mercia. The second is the hundred, a subdivision of the shire that first appears in written legislation during the reign of Edgar's father Edmund and is elaborated in later tenth-century laws, and whose courts handled disputes and policing. In areas of Scandinavian settlement, the corresponding unit is often called a wapentake, and Edgar's law recognized that regions of Danish settlement could keep their own customs, showing a willingness to incorporate rather than eradicate regional difference.

The third is coinage. Around 973, Edgar's government introduced a new uniform coinage across the kingdom, with standard designs, and subsequent kings recalled and reissued coin at intervals. The scheme required a network of dozens of mints in burhs, controlled by royal supervision, and produced revenue for the crown. Historians debate the exact scale, but the uniformity of the coinage across regions is striking and unmatched elsewhere in western Europe at the time. The fourth is the royal itinerary. Edgar and his successors moved among royal estates, holding assemblies, hearing cases, and consuming food rents, so that the king was seen across the kingdom while the bureaucracy remained small. The fifth is the church. Under Edgar, the reforming bishops Dunstan of Canterbury, Æthelwold of Winchester, and Oswald of Worcester and York promoted a monastic renewal, expressed in the Regularis Concordia, a code for monastic life composed around 970, and the crown provided land and protection in return for prayer and support. The bishops were also administrators, advisors, and writers whose work supported royal legitimacy.

The ceremony at Bath in 973 fits this pattern. The Chronicle emphasizes that Edgar was in his thirtieth year at the 973 ceremony, at which Dunstan and Oswald officiated. Historians have connected this age to clerical consecration and to Christological symbolism, but the reason for the unusually late, and possibly repeated, royal consecration remains debated. The ritual, later reworked, provided a template for English coronations for centuries. Later sources also report that several rulers of neighboring realms met Edgar at Chester and pledged loyalty, though the details are embroidered in later tradition and the historical core remains debated. The link between imperial ritual, ecclesiastical reform, and kingship is characteristic of the age and parallel to Ottonian practice in East Francia.

Students should be careful not to credit Edgar with creating every institution associated with late Anglo-Saxon government. Historians have shown that shires, hundreds, mints, and law-making evolved across several reigns, with contributions from Alfred, Edward, Æthelstan, Edmund, and Edgar, and that many features are attested in surviving sources only after Edgar's death. What can be said is that by the 970s the English kingdom possessed a combination of assemblies, local courts, uniform coin, and ecclesiastical support that gave it an unusual depth of administration and a consistent way of reproducing royal authority. The kingdom's resilience was tested after Edgar's early death in 975, when his sons Edward and Æthelred succeeded in turn, the former murdered in 978 in circumstances that remain obscure.

Historical Debate

When Did England Become One Kingdom?

The question: Historians commonly refer to a "kingdom of England" by the tenth century, but they disagree about when it should be said to exist. Was it Alfred, Edward and Æthelflæd, Æthelstan, Edgar, or a longer process?

Interpretation A: A reign-centered view emphasizing Æthelstan. Some historians, drawing on the events of 927, the grand titles in charters, the legislation, and the unified coinage, argue that Æthelstan's reign was the decisive stage. On this view, he was the first king to exercise authority over all the English kingdoms and to claim that authority in a sustained political and ideological program.
Interpretation B: A longer process of incorporation and administrative integration. Other historians stress that Alfred's defensive reforms and the propaganda of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle created a West Saxon vision of Englishness, that Edward and Æthelflæd carried out much of the territorial expansion, that Æthelstan's gains were partly lost after 939, and that the kingdom of York was not permanently absorbed until 954. Administrative integration through shires, hundreds, and coinage continued under Edgar and later kings, and the kingdom passed a decisive test only when it survived conquest by Cnut. On this view, England was made over several generations.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same sources: the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, charters and law codes, coins, and archaeological evidence for burhs and settlement. They differ in how they weigh royal claims, which are abundant in Æthelstan's reign, against evidence of implementation and durability, which appears more clearly under later kings. Some scholars also note that the Chronicle's West Saxon perspective can exaggerate the unity of English identity.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they narrate the making of England, how much agency they give to Mercians, Scandinavian settlers, and northern elites, and whether they treat the kingdom as a product of one great king or of a collective and contested process.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation. Then explain what additional evidence would help historians decide when the kingdom of England should be said to exist, and why a single date may be less useful than a sequence of stages.

Section 33.5

Renewed Scandinavian Conquest and the Test of English Kingship

Durable political formation is best demonstrated by the ability to survive a shock that could have destroyed it. For England, that shock came after 980, when Scandinavian fleets returned in force. Chapter 32 has already told the earlier story of raiding and settlement, and this section is concerned with what the second wave reveals about the kingdom Edgar's generation had built. Under Æthelred II, who came to the throne in 978 as a boy after the murder of his half-brother Edward, attacks intensified. In 991, an English force under Ealdorman Byrhtnoth was defeated at Maldon in Essex, an event commemorated in a poem that survives in a partial later copy, and Æthelred's advisers agreed to buy off the raiders with silver. Payments continued, and the Chronicle reports rising sums over two decades, into the tens of thousands of pounds of silver; historians treat the figures with caution but accept that the payments were large and were raised through the geld, a land tax assessed on the hide and collected through shires and hundreds.

Æthelred's reign is often remembered as a failure, and the nickname "Unready," from an Old English word meaning ill-advised, dates from later centuries. Recent scholarship stresses that the kingdom's administrative machinery functioned well enough to mobilize and collect immense sums, to maintain a coinage that was regularly renewed, and to issue laws. Its weakness was in political cohesion: king and magnates disagreed about strategy, some ealdormen switched sides, and the killings ordered in 1002, conventionally called the St Brice's Day massacre, became part of an escalating cycle of violence, although their precise scale, targets, and causal relationship to subsequent Scandinavian campaigns remain debated. Recent scholarship has suggested that the victims may have included recently demobilized members of invading armies rather than every Scandinavian resident. The discovery of a mass burial at St John's College, Oxford, of young men who died violently around this time has led some scholars to connect the remains with it. Swein Forkbeard of Denmark launched a full-scale invasion in 1013, and by the end of the year Æthelred had fled to Normandy, where his wife Emma, a sister of the Norman duke, had family.

Swein died in February 1014, and Æthelred returned. After his death in April 1016, his son Edmund Ironside continued the fight against Swein's son Cnut, and the kingdom was effectively divided before a decisive battle at Assandun in October 1016 and Edmund's death in November. Cnut was accepted as king of the whole kingdom. In 1017 he married Æthelred's widow Emma, linking his regime to the previous royal household and strengthening the legitimacy and political alliances of his new kingship.

What is most instructive for this chapter is the way Cnut ruled. He did not abolish the English kingdom, and he did not replace it with a separate Scandinavian system. He governed through the shires, the witan, the mints, and the church, reissued laws in the tradition of earlier kings (with the help of Archbishop Wulfstan of York, who had also drafted Æthelred's laws), and maintained a currency that continued the design and standards of earlier issues. He placed his own followers in high positions, executing or exiling some English magnates and dividing the kingdom for a time into large earldoms, but retained many English officials, and he presented himself as a Christian king, patron of the church, and heir to English legitimacy. In 1027 he wrote to the English people from Rome, a letter that survives in chronicle sources, describing his duties as ruler. After conquering Norway in 1028, he ruled a North Sea empire that included England, Denmark, and Norway, but England remained the wealthiest part and provided much of the revenue.

The fact that a conqueror found the English kingdom useful, and chose to rule through it, shows how deeply it was embedded. A kingdom that could be taken over by a foreign dynasty and remain intact was a kingdom whose institutions were understood to be worth preserving. After Cnut's death in 1035, his sons Harold Harefoot and Harthacnut ruled in turn, and in 1042 the crown passed to Edward, the son of Æthelred and Emma, known as Edward the Confessor, who had spent years in exile in Normandy. This chapter stops here. The crisis of the mid-eleventh century, the Norman Conquest of 1066, and the transformations that followed belong to Chapter 45. The essential point for the present analysis is that the kingdom of the English had passed through dynastic collapse, foreign conquest, and dynastic restoration and had continued.

Section 33.6

West Francia after 888: Kings and Powerful Principalities

West Francia presents a very different pattern. The kingdom, stretching from the Meuse and Scheldt westward to the Atlantic and southward to the Loire and beyond, into Aquitaine, Burgundy, and Toulouse and the fringes of the Spanish March, was large, diverse, and increasingly divided among regional lords. The kings who ruled after 888 came from two competing groups: the Carolingians, descendants of Charlemagne, and the Robertians, a family of great counts and lay abbots in the Loire and Seine regions. Odo (r. 888–898), a Robertian, was succeeded by the Carolingian Charles the Simple (r. 898–922), who was overthrown by aristocratic opposition in 922 and replaced by Robert I, who died in battle in 923. The next king, Rudolf of Burgundy, ruled until 936, while Charles remained a prisoner until his death in 929.

In the meantime the great principalities took shape. In the north, Flanders was consolidated by a line of counts descended from Baldwin Iron-Arm. In the northwest, the Scandinavian leader Rollo was granted lands around Rouen by Charles the Simple, according to a settlement traditionally dated to 911 and associated with Saint-Clair-sur-Epte. No contemporary charter of the arrangement survives, and the later account by Dudo of Saint-Quentin, written around 1000 for the Norman dukes, has to be used with care. What is clear is that Rollo and his successors, William Longsword and Richard I, extended their territory, accepted Christianity, adopted Frankish practices, and by the late tenth century ruled a coherent duchy of Normandy. In the south and west, the dukes of Aquitaine, counts of Poitou, Toulouse, and Anjou, and the dukes of Burgundy consolidated regional bases, and in the middle Loire the counts of Blois and Chartres competed for influence.

Kingship in this environment could look weak, and in terms of directly controlled territory it was. The Carolingian kings of the mid-tenth century, Louis IV (r. 936–954) and Lothair (r. 954–986), held the region around Laon, a fortress and episcopal city, and little else. The Robertian Hugh the Great, duke of the Franks and son of King Robert I, held far more land, and much of Louis IV's reign was spent in conflict or uneasy partnership with him. Flodoard of Reims, who records these years in his Annals, portrays a world of frequent conflict, shifting alliances, and negotiated settlements among kings, dukes, counts, and bishops, and his priorities as a cleric of the church of Reims color his account.

Yet it would be a serious mistake to conclude that kingship was irrelevant. The king remained the person whose consent gave legitimacy to titles, whose court was a stage for political display, whose coronation and anointing made him a sacred figure, and whose support could be decisive in local disputes. Counts and dukes competed for royal favor, sought confirmation of their offices, and attended royal assemblies. Even Hugh the Great, whose power exceeded the king's in many respects, did not take the crown for himself in the 940s or 950s. The apparent paradox, that weak direct royal control coexisted with the continuing prestige of kingship, is the central fact of West Frankish politics. Great princes needed a king to legitimate their own local authority, and that need protected the institution even as the king's power was limited.

The political geography also mattered. The north and west, close to the Channel coast and the Seine, faced Scandinavian pressure, which encouraged militarization and local defense. The south was distant from the king and largely governed regionally. Lotharingia, the region between the Rhine and the Meuse, was contested with East Francia; West Frankish kings periodically claimed it, and Lothair's seizure of Aachen in 978 provoked a counterattack by the East Frankish king Otto II, who reached the outskirts of Paris before a settlement in 980 confirmed East Frankish control. The episode shows that the two kingdoms' interests were entangled and that the border between them was a zone of contest, not a fixed line.

Section 33.7

The End of the West Frankish Carolingians and the Election of Hugh Capet

The end of the West Frankish Carolingian line came suddenly and, to contemporaries, without an obvious plan. Lothair died in 986 and was succeeded by his son Louis V, who died childless in May 987 after a short reign. The Carolingian claimant was Louis's uncle, Charles of Lorraine, a son of Louis IV who held the duchy of Lower Lotharingia as a vassal of the East Frankish king. Against him stood Hugh, duke of the Franks, called Capet in later sources, son of Hugh the Great, grandson of King Robert I, and a powerful figure in the Paris-Orléans region and beyond, supported by important churchmen.

The events of 987 are known chiefly from Richer of Reims, whose Histories were composed in the 990s, and from the letters associated with Gerbert of Aurillac, a scholar who served Archbishop Adalbero of Reims and later became pope as Sylvester II. According to these sources, Adalbero played a leading role in gathering the magnates at an assembly at Senlis and arguing that the kingdom should not pass by inheritance alone but should be given to a man of merit, whom the assembled lords chose. The commonly accepted chronology places Hugh's consecration or coronation at Noyon on 3 July 987, although the precise sequence and dating of the inauguration rites remain disputed. Charles of Lorraine resisted, and in 991 was captured through treachery and imprisoned in Orléans, where he died. Both Richer and Gerbert have their own agendas, and Richer's speeches are literary compositions, not transcripts. Modern historians accept the essentials but read the accounts as arguments for the legitimacy of the new king.

Several elements explain why Hugh succeeded. The Robertians had held royal office twice already, in the persons of Odo and Robert I, and their family possessed extensive lands and counties. Hugh had the support of major bishops, and the church's role in legitimating a king through anointing gave clerical backing great weight. Charles of Lorraine was compromised by his connection with the East Frankish king and by his own lack of a strong regional base in West Francia, and his marriage to a woman of lower rank was criticized by opponents. Some historians have emphasized that Hugh's comparatively limited direct territorial power may have made his kingship acceptable to great princes whose own regional autonomy remained strong. This is an interpretation of the political balance, however, rather than a motive that surviving sources allow historians to assign uniformly to the magnates.

The election does not mean that the West Frankish kingdom became a full elective monarchy, nor that hereditary claims disappeared. In the tenth-century Frankish world, kingship combined descent, designation by predecessors, and acceptance by leading men, and different sources stressed different elements to suit their purposes. Hugh's supporters emphasized election and merit; later Capetian apologists emphasized the anointing and continuity. Students should also avoid the shorthand that France was founded in 987. Hugh became king of the Franks in a West Frankish political world dominated by powerful princes, and the nature of the kingdom as a territorial and political community continued to evolve for centuries.

Historical Vocabulary
Election, Designation, and Acclamation

In the early medieval kingdoms, election did not mean a secret ballot or a formal vote by an electorate. It usually meant that leading men, lay and ecclesiastical, assembled and expressed consent to a candidate whose claim rested on descent, family standing, or the choice of a predecessor. Designation refers to a ruler naming an heir, as Conrad I is reported to have done for Henry of Saxony and as Otto I did for his son. Acclamation was the public approval, often by shouts, that marked recognition in an assembly or church. These practices overlapped, and most successions involved all three.

The modern contrast between hereditary and elective monarchy is therefore misleading for this period. Kings might be both descendants of earlier rulers and chosen by nobles, and different observers emphasized different aspects. The word matters here because Hugh Capet in 987 and Henry I in 919 were both described as chosen by the magnates, yet both also represented dynastic and regional power bases.

Section 33.8

Early Capetian Kingship and the Making of France

The early Capetians present a striking case of dynastic durability preceding territorial dominance. Hugh Capet and his son Robert II (r. 996–1031) directly controlled a comparatively small royal domain, concentrated around Paris, Orléans, and a chain of estates and abbeys along the Seine and Loire. In this region they possessed counties, castles, and rights over churches, but they were far weaker than the dukes of Normandy, Aquitaine, or Burgundy or the counts of Flanders and Anjou in their own territories. Fulk Nerra of Anjou, who ruled from 987 to 1040, built an aggressive regional power, and the dukes of Normandy under Richard I and Richard II became among the wealthiest and best-organized lords in the west.

The Capetians nevertheless achieved something rarer than territorial power: continuity. Hugh had his son Robert crowned and anointed at Orléans in late December 987, with the precise day reported differently in the surviving evidence, associating him with the kingship during Hugh's own lifetime, a practice known as association. This device made the succession much harder to challenge, since the heir already held the royal title and had been recognized by bishops and magnates. When Hugh died in 996, Robert succeeded without opposition. Robert, in turn, had his eldest son Hugh crowned in 1017, and after that son's death, his son Henry, crowned in 1027 and succeeding in 1031. The practice of associating the heir continued for roughly two centuries and gave the dynasty an unbroken line of kings whose title was secure, with the direct male line lasting until 1328, as later chapters will show.

Relations with bishops and great princes were central. The king was a patron of churches in his domain, and bishops and abbots, especially those of Reims, Chartres, Orléans, and Paris, supported him. He also cooperated with the great princes, attending their weddings, seeking their consent to important acts, and receiving their homage in principle if not always in practice. Robert II's reign, sometimes called pious, involved marriage disputes with the church, wars in Burgundy, and a reputation for learning and charity. His court maintained the ritual and diplomatic forms of Carolingian kingship, and charters issued in his name show a court that was small but steady.

The title itself deserves attention. The Capetians called themselves king of the Franks (rex Francorum), the same title used by Carolingian rulers, and the territory over which they claimed authority was called Francia, a term that could refer at various times to the region around Paris, to the western Frankish kingdom as a whole, or to the Frankish people. Only gradually, over the eleventh and twelfth centuries and beyond, did the phrase king of France (rex Franciae) become common and did the political community come to be associated with a defined kingdom and, eventually, a national identity. The Capetian kingdom of 1000 was not modern France, and its people did not think of themselves as French in the nineteenth-century sense.

It would also be wrong to regard the early Capetians as politically insignificant because their domain was limited. Dynastic legitimacy and secure succession were enormous assets in a world where rival lords and rulers could and did collapse when heirs failed. The Capetian court retained the sacral character of kingship, and the Capetians presided over the coronation ritual at Reims, which gradually became the customary place of consecration. Their later expansion, based on this legitimacy and on eventual military and administrative reforms, belongs to the High Middle Ages and is treated in later chapters. Here the crucial observation is that the dynasty's durability came first, and its territorial power followed.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life Under Changing Kings

For many inhabitants, dynastic change was experienced indirectly through local courts, rents, military obligations, lordship, and episodes of warfare rather than through regular contact with the king. In tenth-century England, most people lived in small settlements, often dispersed hamlets or nucleated villages, cultivating grain, keeping livestock, and rendering food rents, labor services, and, increasingly, coin. Free peasants (ceorls) and thegns, a class of local landholders who owed service to the king, met obligations that included repairing bridges and burhs and serving in the fyrd, the local army. The hundred court and shire court were places where disputes over land and theft were settled, and oaths and the testimony of neighbors mattered more than written proof. Enslaved people, present in all three regions and documented in wills, laws, and chronicles, formed a significant but difficult-to-quantify part of the population, and the Viking age created a market for captives who were sold overseas.

In West Francia and East Francia, rural people lived under varied legal and property arrangements. Many owed rents, labor, dues, or judicial obligations to lay or ecclesiastical lords, while free landholding also persisted. The mechanisms of dependency and the growing number of castles will be examined in Chapter 34. In Saxony and elsewhere in East Francia, raiding by Magyar forces led communities to construct refuges and fortifications and to negotiate tribute, and the frequency of violence made local protectors more important. Women appear in the sources principally as elite figures: queens such as Edith (Eadgyth) and Adelheid, abbesses who governed powerful communities, and noblewomen who managed estates and patronized religious houses. Æthelflæd's rule shows that a woman could exercise military and political power in the tenth century, though this was exceptional. The sources rarely record peasant women's experience directly, and historians must infer it from law codes, cemeteries, and estate documents.

Religious life shaped every community. Parish churches were multiplying, monasteries served as centers of prayer, literacy, and estate management, and bishops mediated between ruler and locality. Jewish communities existed in parts of West Francia and the German lands, especially along the Rhine, though documentation for the tenth century is limited, and Muslim raiders and traders touched the southern fringes of the West Frankish world. These minority presences remain part of the picture even when the sources speak mainly of kings and clergy.

Section 33.9

From East Francia to a German Kingdom

East Francia reached the tenth century with a Carolingian king who was a child. Louis the Child, who succeeded in 900 when he was about six, never exercised independent power, and the kingdom was governed by bishops and by the great magnates who led regional defense against Magyar raiders. When Louis died in 911, the East Frankish Carolingian line came to an end. The magnates, meeting at Forchheim, chose Conrad, duke of Franconia, as king. The choice is significant, since the lords did not turn to the West Frankish Carolingian Charles the Simple, who had a hereditary claim, but elected one of their own. East Francia had become a kingdom in which kingship could pass outside the dynasty of Charlemagne.

Conrad's reign (911–918) was difficult. He faced rebellion by the Saxon duke Henry and by other magnates, suffered from Magyar attacks, and lost Lotharingia to Charles the Simple. The great regional leaders, in Saxony, Bavaria, Swabia, Franconia, and Lotharingia, had built bases of power through military leadership and the control of church lands. Modern historians often call these regions "stem duchies," from the German Stammesherzogtümer, a term that reflects nineteenth-century assumptions that they were based on ancient peoples or tribes. Current scholarship stresses that these duchies were political constructs of the late ninth and early tenth centuries, created by ambitious families, and that ethnic identities were fluid and constructed. Chapter 32 already warned against reading ethnic coherence backward, and the warning applies here. Bavaria, Swabia, and Saxony had distinctive laws and customs, but their dukes were powerful because they had built military and territorial networks, not because they were expressions of ancient nations.

Conrad died in December 918, reportedly recommending Henry of Saxony, his most powerful opponent, as his successor. The account comes from later sources, and some historians doubt its details, but the outcome is clear: in 919, at Fritzlar, the Franks and Saxons acclaimed Henry as king. The later Saxon chronicler Widukind of Corvey reports that Henry declined anointing by the archbishop, perhaps because he wished to avoid obligations to the church. The story is a literary account written decades afterward and should be treated with caution; what is certain is that Henry's kingship was built on agreement with other dukes and not on a dramatic assertion of supremacy. The Bavarian duke Arnulf, for example, did not at first accept Henry and was reconciled through a settlement that left him substantial authority, including over the Bavarian church.

The importance of negotiated kingship cannot be overstated. Henry did not abolish the power of the dukes. He treated them as partners, made formal agreements of friendship (amicitia) with them, and left them extensive autonomy. In return they recognized him as king, attended his assemblies, and contributed to his military enterprises. This arrangement, resting on the personal bonds of loyalty and friendship that historians such as Gerd Althoff have emphasized, provided stability by reducing the incentive for magnates to see the king as an enemy. It also means that "the German kingdom" under Henry and Otto was not a centralized state but a coalition of regional powers under a royal dynasty.

Historical Vocabulary
Stem Duchy

A stem duchy (from German Stamm, "stem" or "tribe") is the conventional modern label for the great regional duchies of East Francia: Saxony, Bavaria, Swabia, Franconia, and, in a more contested way, Lotharingia. The label suggests that each was the political home of an ancient people with a fixed identity. Historians now emphasize that duchies were created in the ninth and tenth centuries by families who built regional power, that ethnic labels were applied and reshaped by rulers and writers, and that the duchies changed in size, leadership, and independence across the century.

The term remains useful as shorthand but should not be read as evidence of timeless German tribes. It matters here because Henry I and Otto I governed by negotiating with dukes, and because later German regionalism has sometimes been traced back to these duchies as though they were primordial.

Section 33.10

Henry I: Consolidation, Frontiers, and Royal Authority

Henry I's reign (919–936) shows how consolidation could proceed through coalition-building and careful management of regional authority. His dealings with the duchies illustrate the method. Burchard of Swabia submitted to him early, and after Burchard's death in 926, Henry installed a Franconian, Hermann, as duke of Swabia, an example of a king placing his own allies in regional offices. In Bavaria, the arrangement with Arnulf left the duke in control of much of Bavarian church patronage. In Franconia, the duke Eberhard, brother of the late king Conrad, accepted Henry's rule. Lotharingia was the most difficult region: it had drifted to West Francia under Charles the Simple, but after Charles was overthrown in 922 and with the support of local leaders including Duke Giselbert, Henry took control in 925, and Lotharingia remained part of the eastern kingdom thereafter. The settlement was reinforced by Giselbert's marriage to Henry's daughter Gerberga.

Henry's relations with West Francia are reflected in a pact of friendship concluded in 921 at Bonn, in which the West Frankish king and Henry treated each other as equals, an arrangement that shows how far the notion of one Carolingian realm had receded. The two kingdoms were now distinct political communities, even if their rulers might still act as relatives and rivals.

The Magyar threat and the Slavic frontier shaped the military side of consolidation. According to Widukind of Corvey, Henry obtained a nine-year truce in 926 after a Magyar leader was captured and tribute was agreed; because Widukind wrote decades afterward, the episode's details require caution. Henry is said to have used the respite to reorganize defense, and Widukind reports that he fortified places (Burgen) and trained cavalry, and that a portion of the population was obliged to live in or supply these strongholds. Historians debate how systematic this was and how far Widukind's account reflects later conceptions of Henry as a great builder, but archaeological and documentary evidence supports the general picture of increased fortification in Saxony and adjacent regions. In 933, Henry's forces defeated a Magyar army at a battle usually located at Riade, on the Unstrut, a victory that enhanced his prestige. To the east, in 928 and 929 Henry campaigned against the Slavic peoples of the Elbe and Havel region, taking the fortified site of Brennabor (later Brandenburg), and in 929 Duke Wenceslas of Bohemia accepted his overlordship. In 934 he turned against the Danes at the frontier. These frontier campaigns combined defense with expansion, and they fit into the broader pattern of tribute, raiding, and negotiated submission that characterized relations between Saxons, Slavs, Magyars, and Scandinavians.

Henry's authority rested on cooperation, not on direct administration. He moved among royal estates and ecclesiastical centers, relied on members of his own family, and used assemblies to reach agreement. He designated his son Otto as heir, securing the assent of the magnates, and thereby established the principle that the crown would pass within the Saxon family. Henry died at Memleben in July 936. The passing of the crown to Otto without serious dispute showed that the new dynasty had established itself, not through centralization but through partnership with those who held regional power.

Section 33.11

Otto I, the German Kingdom, and the Renewal of Empire

Otto I, crowned at Aachen in 936, in the church associated with Charlemagne, came to the throne with a more assertive view of royal authority than his father. Early in his reign, he tried to place relatives and supporters in key positions and to limit the autonomy of dukes, and the result was a series of rebellions. His half-brother Thankmar, the duke Eberhard of Franconia, Otto's own brother Henry, and Duke Giselbert of Lotharingia all rebelled in the late 930s and early 940s, often with allies in West Francia. Otto survived, partly through good fortune, since Eberhard and Giselbert died in 939, and partly through political skill, reconciling with his brother Henry, who became duke of Bavaria, and placing family members in the duchies. In 953 and 954, his own son Liudolf, duke of Swabia, and his son-in-law Conrad the Red, duke of Lotharingia, rebelled, and the Magyars took advantage of the turmoil. The rebellion lost momentum as Otto reconciled some opponents, other magnates remained or returned to his side, and changing military circumstances, including renewed Magyar pressure, altered the balance of alliances.

The decisive victory over the Magyars came in August 955, on the Lechfeld near Augsburg, where Otto led a royal army that included contingents from several duchies, while Bishop Ulrich had played a leading role in organizing and sustaining Augsburg's defense. Otto's army defeated a large Magyar force. Conrad the Red, who had by then reconciled with Otto, died in the battle. The victory brought Otto enormous prestige: his army had turned back the most feared enemy of the age, and contemporaries celebrated him as a defender of Christendom. Modern historians emphasize that the battle did not end Magyar presence or raids in a single stroke, but it marked a turning point. After the 950s western raiding declined sharply while Magyar political society underwent further consolidation and changing diplomatic and religious relationships, developments that eventually produced the Christian kingdom of Hungary around 1000, examined in Chapter 35. In the same year, Otto also defeated Slavic forces at the Recknitz.

A central feature of Otto's governance was the use of the church. Bishops and abbots were recipients of royal land, immunities, and rights, including jurisdiction and market and toll rights, and were often chosen from the royal chapel, the body of clerics who served the king. Episcopal office was not transmitted through legitimate hereditary succession, which could make bishoprics useful points of royal influence, but cathedral chapters, aristocratic families, established church rights, and local political interests constrained royal freedom. Historians often call the arrangement the Ottonian Reichskirche, and they debate how far Ottonian reliance on bishops constituted a deliberate "system" rather than an intensified use of practices common to medieval kingship. Otto's brother Bruno, archbishop of Cologne and for a time duke of Lotharingia, exemplified the merging of ecclesiastical and political roles. Whatever its origins, reliance on bishops supplied the crown with administrators, military contingents, hospitality for the itinerant court, and intellectual resources.

Otto's intervention in Italy grew out of these foundations. In 951, he entered northern Italy, married Adelheid, the widow of King Lothar of Italy, and took the title king of the Lombards. A decade later, responding to an appeal from Pope John XII, he returned and on 2 February 962 was crowned emperor in Rome. The imperial title had been held by Carolingian rulers and Italian rulers until 924 and had lapsed, and its revival had long-standing resonance. Otto and the pope concluded an agreement, known as the Ottonianum, that confirmed papal possessions and established terms for imperial involvement in the papal election and consecration process, though the relationship quickly became fraught, and in 963 Otto presided over the deposition of John XII. In 968, Otto established an archbishopric at Magdeburg, a base for mission to the Slavs. Liudprand of Cremona's hostile account of his 968 embassy records sharp disagreement at Constantinople over Otto's imperial status and titulature. The precise extent and timing of Byzantine recognition remain debated; the marriage of Otto II and the Byzantine princess Theophanu in 972 nevertheless marked a major diplomatic rapprochement.

A major caution is required. Otto did not found the Holy Roman Empire in 962. The phrase "Holy Roman Empire" did not appear in the tenth century, and the institutional and ideological structure that later bore that name developed over centuries, in the politics of emperors, princes, Italy, and the church examined in Chapter 46. The imperial coronation matters here because it demonstrates what the East Frankish kingdom, consolidated under the Saxon dynasty, could support: a ruler with enough prestige, military success, and ecclesiastical backing to revive a western imperial title and to link his kingdom to Italy and Rome. It also shows the durable character of the kingdom: Otto II (r. 973–983), Otto III (r. 983–1002), and Henry II (r. 1002–1024) succeeded in turn, and when the Saxon line ended in 1024, the magnates elected Conrad II, a Salian, without the kingdom collapsing. Nor did the period create a German nation-state. The kingdom was a coalition of regional powers under a royal dynasty, and what its people thought of as a shared identity was still forming.

Living Under the New Kingdoms

Æthelflæd governing Mercia, 911 to 918: Her position combined inherited Mercian authority, kinship with the West Saxon king, and her own military leadership. She built burhs and campaigned, but her brother held London and Oxford and, after her death, removed her daughter. Her authority was real and independent but constrained by West Saxon power.

The record, largely English and ecclesiastical, says little about how such people regarded their new rulers.
A West Frankish prince negotiating with the Capetian king: A duke or count of Normandy, Anjou, or Aquitaine could regard the king as a figure of legitimacy and sacred office whose confirmation was useful, yet as a neighbor whose power was smaller than his own. Such a prince might attend the king's court, seek recognition, and act largely on his own in his territory.
A bishop supporting royal succession: Adalbero of Reims in 987, or Dunstan and Oswald in England, acted as counselors, consecrators, and legitimators of kings, and benefited from royal patronage, protection of church lands, and influence over policy. Their writings justified kingship in religious terms and preserved the record.
A Saxon or Bavarian duke under Otto: Dukes held extensive authority, often hereditary or quasi-hereditary, and accepted the king as a partner. Under Otto, some lost independence or rebelled, while others, particularly kinsmen, were integrated into the royal house. Their relationship with the king was personal and renegotiated repeatedly.
A peasant household: Whether in Mercia, Anjou, or Saxony, the immediate authorities were the local lord, the reeve or steward, the parish priest, and the local court. Changes of dynasty mattered mainly when they brought new taxes, military demands, or violence. The sources rarely record peasant opinions, and historians infer them from laws, estate records, and archaeology.
Section 33.12

Three Kingdoms, Three Paths to Durability

A comparison clarifies what the three cases shared and how they differed. Each had a dynasty capable of reproducing itself: the West Saxon house in England, the Capetians in West Francia, and the Saxon Ottonians in East Francia. Each benefited from the church's support for kingship through anointing, prayer, and counsel. Each relied on assemblies, at which decisions were made in the presence of leading men. None of the three kings could rule without the cooperation of regional elites. And each succeeded in doing something that the later Carolingians had often failed to do: pass the crown on without the kingdom breaking apart.

The differences are equally instructive. England's kingdom was the most administratively integrated, having inherited a system of fortified towns, assessed land, local courts, and mints, and having subsumed the regions of Scandinavian settlement without disbanding their customs. Its durability was the ability to be reproduced even through conquest. In West Francia, royal power was the most restricted, and the territorial principalities held the real levers of regional control; but the king's prestige, the anointed and sacral character of the title, and the secure succession after 987 gave the Capetian dynasty an unusual resilience. In East Francia, the crown's power was built on coalition, military success, ecclesiastical resources, and family, and the revived imperial title linked it with Italy and Rome, which would shape its later history, including the politics examined in Chapter 46.

The common denominator is not centralization. None of these kingdoms had a centralized bureaucracy or a modern state apparatus, and historians who look for the origins of modern states in this period risk reading back from later outcomes. The common denominator is the ability to reproduce legitimate kingship, to cooperate with regional elites and churches, to organize defense and justice, and to survive succession. It was also contingent. A different sequence of births and deaths, a different outcome at Assandun or the Lechfeld, or a different choice in 987 might have produced other results.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Carolingian Kingship to Tenth-Century Kingdoms

What Changed

  • Authority was organized in smaller kingdoms rather than in a single vast empire or its large successor realms.
  • Dynasties changed: Robertians and then Capetians in the west, Saxons in the east, and the West Saxon house in England.
  • Regional dukes, counts, and ealdormen gained weight as partners of the crown.
  • English kingship developed uniform coinage, shires, and hundreds that had no direct Carolingian parallel.

What Stayed the Same

  • Anointing and Christian ideology of kingship continued and, if anything, deepened.
  • Assemblies remained the venue for consultation and consent.
  • Bishops and abbots remained central as counselors, administrators, and legitimators.
  • Aristocratic cooperation remained essential, and rulers who ignored it faced rebellion.

Who experienced change most directly: regional aristocrats and bishops, whose positions depended on royal favor and competition, and families in regions of Scandinavian settlement, who found themselves under new rulers. Who experienced greater continuity: most peasant households, whose obligations continued to be defined by local lords and courts. Why the pattern matters: it shows that political consolidation reshaped elites and institutions more than everyday life, and that kings survived by adapting Carolingian models, not by abandoning them.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Some Post-Carolingian Arrangements Become Durable?

Long-term causes

Inherited administrative units such as hides, burhs, counties, and bishoprics; Carolingian ideas of Christian kingship; the prestige of anointing; and regional aristocratic networks that could be drawn into royal service.

Short-term causes

Scandinavian and Magyar pressure that rewarded leaders who could defend; dynastic failures that opened the way for new families; military victories such as Tettenhall, Brunanburh, and the Lechfeld; and astute alliances between kings and churchmen.

Immediate effects

Larger English and East Frankish kingdoms with recognized dynasties; in West Francia, a new dynasty with limited territory but strong legitimacy; and a pattern of kings sharing power with dukes, counts, and bishops.

Long-term consequences

Kingdoms that continued across dynastic change and conquest; a foundation for the later growth of medieval monarchies examined in Chapter 45 and for the imperial politics of Chapter 46; and a European political map organized around kingdoms and principalities.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains durability. Comparable structures failed elsewhere: Lotharingia did not become a lasting kingdom, and the kingdom of York was absorbed. Geography, accident, military talent, and the choices of churchmen and magnates all mattered, and later states that claimed these kingdoms as their ancestors have often exaggerated their continuity.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how three political worlds of the post-Carolingian era, England, West Francia, and East Francia, developed durable forms of kingship between roughly 899 and 1016. Its central argument is that durability did not mean centralization and was not the birth of modern nations. It meant the capacity to reproduce legitimate rulers, to cooperate with regional elites and churches, to organize defense and justice, and to survive dynastic crises. The chapter began from the fragmentation described in Chapter 32 and showed that around 900 no eventual outcome was certain.

In England, the West Saxon house and its Mercian allies transformed Alfred's defensive system into an offensive one. Edward the Elder built burhs and absorbed territory in the eastern Midlands and East Anglia, while Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians, ruled in her own right, fortified Mercia, campaigned against Scandinavian and Welsh opponents, and received submissions before her death in 918. Her daughter's removal, dated in the Mercian Register to 919 but probably occurring in late 918, shows that West Saxon incorporation of Mercia was a deliberate political act. The expansion did not eliminate Scandinavian populations, who continued under English kingship with their own customs.

Æthelstan's reign marked a major stage in the creation of a kingdom of the English. He took York in 927, received the acknowledgment of rulers across Britain, legislated for the kingdom, regulated coinage, patronized the church, and won at Brunanburh in 937. Yet northern control remained contested after 939 and was secured only in 954. The Historical Debate showed why scholars reject a simple birth date and prefer to describe a sequence of stages from Alfred to Edgar, with the kingdom's resilience tested in later decades.

Under Edgar, the kingdom's durability rested on shires, hundreds, assemblies, uniform coinage, monastic reform, and a ritual of kingship at Bath in 973. The renewed Scandinavian attacks of Æthelred's reign and Cnut's conquest in 1016 tested this structure. A conqueror who chose to rule through existing English institutions, laws, and coinage demonstrated how deeply the kingdom was embedded. The restoration of the West Saxon line in 1042 forms the bridge to Chapter 45, which examines the Norman Conquest.

In West Francia, kingship survived within a landscape of powerful principalities. Normandy, Flanders, Aquitaine, Burgundy, Anjou, Blois, and others were governed by dukes and counts who exercised regional authority, while the kings retained sacral prestige and the power to legitimize titles. The apparent paradox of weak direct royal control and durable kingship was resolved by the dependence of princes on the institution. The Robertians and Carolingians alternated on the throne until the Carolingian line failed in 987.

Hugh Capet's election in 987, through an assembly of magnates and with the support of Archbishop Adalbero and other bishops, did not found France. It began a dynasty whose durability preceded its territorial power. By associating his heir in his lifetime, a practice that Robert II and his successors continued, the Capetians secured succession for generations. They ruled from a small domain around Paris and Orléans and called themselves kings of the Franks, and only gradually did their kingdom come to be identified as France.

In East Francia, the extinction of the Carolingian line in 911 led to the election of Conrad I, then to Henry I in 919, who governed by negotiated friendship with the dukes. Henry's fortifications, truce with the Magyars, victory at Riade, and frontier campaigns consolidated the kingdom. Otto I faced rebellions by relatives and dukes but defeated the Magyars at the Lechfeld in 955, relied on bishops and abbots as agents of government, and was crowned emperor in 962. The imperial coronation showed what the consolidated kingdom could support, but it did not found the Holy Roman Empire, whose institutional history is the subject of Chapter 46.

The comparison of the three kingdoms shows that each combined dynasty, elite cooperation, church support, military capacity, and inherited structures in different proportions. England had the deepest administrative integration, West Francia the strongest regional principalities alongside resilient kingship, and East Francia the most elaborate coalition of dukes and bishops under a dynasty that looked toward Italy. Shared features included anointing, assemblies, and reliance on regional cooperation; none had a centralized bureaucracy.

Finally, the chapter emphasized historiographical caution and historical contingency. The phrases "first king of England," "founding of France," "the German kingdom," and "stem duchy" all carry risks of national teleology. Sources such as the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Flodoard, Richer, Widukind, charters, laws, and coins were produced by particular people for particular purposes, and they shape what we know. The consolidated kingdoms were the product of accident and choice as much as of structure. Chapter 34 now turns from kings to the social and economic structures of lordship, dependency, castles, and warriors beneath medieval political power.

Comparing the Sources for Tenth-Century Kingship

This project asks you to compare how different kinds of evidence, produced by different people, shape our understanding of one of the three kingdoms and the extent to which the same developments look different from other perspectives.

  1. Choose a case. Select one episode: Æthelflæd's rule in Mercia, Æthelstan's reign, Hugh Capet's election, Henry I's rule, or Otto's coronation.
  2. Locate the sources. Using library resources, identify at least three types of evidence, such as a chronicle, charters or diplomas, law codes, coins, archaeological reports, or letters, and identify who produced each.
  3. Analyze perspectives. Explain what each source reveals and omits, and whose viewpoints are missing, including lay, female, Scandinavian, Slavic, and peasant perspectives.
  4. Compare interpretations. Read two modern historians on your episode and summarize where they agree and disagree.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4 to 6 page essay or an equivalent presentation, present your conclusions with careful language such as "the evidence suggests" and "historians debate," avoiding overstated certainty.

Library resources to use: historical atlases, published editions and translations of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Flodoard, Richer, and Widukind, charter collections, numismatic catalogues, museum collections of tenth-century coins and manuscripts, and scholarly surveys of early medieval Britain, France, and Germany.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how the fragments left by Carolingian division and outside pressure were reassembled into political communities that could outlast their kings. It does so without telling a story of inevitable nations. The kingdoms studied here were dynastic, aristocratic, and ecclesiastical constructions, the result of negotiation and contingency as well as capacity. Seeing them in this way helps students avoid reading later national histories into the tenth century.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that recurs throughout medieval European history: rulers who lacked overwhelming force survived by sharing power, cultivating legitimacy, and embedding themselves in the religious and legal life of their communities. That pattern links Æthelstan's courts, Hugh Capet's association of his heir, and Otto I's partnership with bishops. It shaped the later growth of medieval monarchies, treated in Chapter 45, and the imperial politics of central Europe, treated in Chapter 46.

Regional connections run through the chapter as well. Scandinavian settlers and raiders shaped the English and Norman worlds, Magyar pressure shaped East Frankish consolidation, the church linked kingdoms through shared ritual and learning, and marriages connected dynasties from Wessex to Saxony to Byzantium. Europe in this period was not a set of sealed national histories but a network of overlapping political communities.

Within the argument of Volume II, this chapter shows the political result of the post-Roman and post-Carolingian transformations. Chapter 34 will turn to the social foundations of power in lordship and dependency, and Chapter 35 will follow Latin Christendom outward into Scandinavia, Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary. The kingdoms that emerged here did not end the diversity of medieval Europe. They gave it durable political forms within which that diversity would develop.
