Industrialization Spreads across Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Industrialization did not leave Britain as a finished package to be unpacked in Liège, Lyon, Chemnitz, or Brno. It crossed frontiers as machines, skilled workers, capital, and ideas, and everywhere it arrived it was changed by coal seams and rivers, by banks and tariffs, by landlords and peasants, and by the political boundaries that cut across the continent.

In 1817 an English-born entrepreneur named John Cockerill purchased a former residence of the prince bishops of Liège at Seraing, on the river Meuse, and began to convert it into a manufacturing establishment. The site was already part of a regional economy of coal pits, iron forges, and metalworking. Cockerill's father, William, had settled on the continent in the 1790s as a builder of textile machinery, and the family's workshops in the Verviers district had already helped to introduce mechanized wool processing. At Seraing the son assembled something larger: steam engines, machine shops, coke fired blast furnaces, and, increasingly, coal mines of the company's own. By the mid 1830s the works built locomotives for the new Belgian railway system. Yet the firm was also fragile. It depended on the credit of banks and on the favor of governments, it fell into serious financial difficulty in the late 1830s, and John Cockerill died in 1840 while the enterprise was still in transition. The history of Seraing therefore contains, in miniature, many of the themes of this chapter: the movement of British expertise, the importance of mineral resources, the reliance on finance and state support, and the risks that accompanied industrial investment.

Seraing was not typical of Europe. It stood in one of the most concentrated zones of early continental industry, within a few days' travel of regions in which most families still lived by farming, local crafts, and seasonal labor, and in which the characteristic industrial technologies of Britain were scarcely known. A traveler moving east from the Meuse valley across the German lands, south into the Habsburg territories, or north into the Baltic and Scandinavian world would have crossed a patchwork of very different economic systems. Some were dense with textile workshops but nearly without steam power. Others possessed iron ore and forests but little coal. Others exported grain, timber, or raw materials and imported the machinery they lacked. This chapter studies that patchwork. It asks how industrial methods spread across Europe between roughly 1800 and 1880, why they spread so unevenly, and what the resulting economic geography meant for states, regions, and societies.

Chapter 93 examined the British breakthrough: the combination of coal, steam, textiles, iron, capital, and transport that made Britain the first industrial economy. The present chapter does not repeat that story country by country. Its central argument is that European societies did not follow one standardized British model. Belgium developed an early concentration of mining and heavy industry. France pursued a more gradual and regionally varied transformation in which workshops and rural production remained important alongside mechanized factories. The German lands combined the political fragmentation of the German Confederation with an expanding customs union and, in time, rapidly growing industrial districts. In central, northern, southern, and eastern Europe, resource endowments, institutions, political conditions, and commercial relationships produced further variations. The outcome was a continent increasingly connected by industrial production, railways, and finance, and yet increasingly unequal in the economic experiences of its regions.

The chapter is organized into twenty sections arranged in five divisions. The first examines the conditions of diffusion: the post 1815 political setting, the geography of resources, and the circulation of technology and skills. The second compares the three principal early continental cases of Belgium, France, and the German lands. The third explains the infrastructural and institutional systems of railways, banks, state policy, and technical education that made expansion possible. The fourth turns to the Habsburg monarchy, Switzerland, the Netherlands, Scandinavia, Italy, and the Iberian Peninsula. The fifth treats eastern and southeastern Europe, offers a systematic quantitative comparison, and synthesizes the causes and consequences of uneven development.

Historians reconstruct this subject from a wide range of evidence. Production statistics for coal, iron, and textiles survive in government and company records, though they vary greatly in reliability. Railway concession documents, company reports, and parliamentary or ministerial papers record investment and construction. Patent registers, engineering journals, exhibition catalogs, and the reports of official travelers to Britain document the circulation of technical knowledge. Censuses and tax records offer evidence about occupations, though they often undercount seasonal and household work, especially that of women and children. Memoirs, travel accounts, and the writings of economists such as Friedrich List show how contemporaries interpreted what they saw. Modern economic historians have also built long run estimates of output and income, and these estimates, while valuable, involve assumptions that this chapter will identify where they matter.

The chapter belongs to Part XV, which explains the nineteenth century transformation of economic life, social classes, political ideology, and national states. It builds on the British case in Chapter 93 and prepares the ground for Chapter 95, which examines the factory towns, working conditions, and labor organization that accompanied industrial growth, and for Chapters 96 to 98, which examine the ideologies and revolutions of the same decades. Within Volume IV, which moves from Enlightenment and revolution through industry to empire, Chapter 94 shows that the economic transformation of the nineteenth century was not a single event that began in one place and radiated outward at an even pace. It was a set of regionally specific processes, and understanding their differences is essential for understanding the Europe of 1880, with its great industrial powers, its agrarian frontiers, and the pressures that would shape the imperial and international rivalries of the following decades.

Historical Context

By about 1815 Britain possessed an economy unlike any other in Europe. As Chapter 93 explained, a combination of abundant and accessible coal, a commercial and financial system tied to overseas empire, agricultural change, a large and mobile labor force, and a sequence of technical innovations in textiles, steam power, and iron had produced mechanized factory production on a scale not yet seen elsewhere. The opening of the Liverpool and Manchester Railway in 1830 then demonstrated that steam traction could carry freight and passengers commercially. Britain's lead mattered to continental Europeans for two reasons. It created a body of technology that could be copied or adapted, and it created competition, since cheap British cotton cloth, iron, and later machinery threatened continental producers in open markets.

Continental Europe in these decades was nevertheless not an unindustrial void waiting for British influence. Historians since the 1970s have used the term protoindustrialization for the widespread rural manufacture of textiles, metalwares, and other goods by households working for merchants, a system whose origins were examined in Chapter 85. Regions such as Flanders, the Rhineland, Saxony, Bohemia, Lombardy, the Lyon district, and the Swiss cantons already contained dense networks of skilled spinners, weavers, and metalworkers, along with merchant capital and commercial habits. Some of these regions became early adopters of machinery precisely because they possessed such a base. Others saw their traditional crafts undermined. The relationship between older rural industry and later factory industry was therefore complex and varied from district to district.

The wars of 1792 to 1815 had disrupted this landscape. The Continental System, the territorial reorganizations of the Napoleonic era, and the legal reforms examined in Chapters 90 and 91 changed property relations and removed many old barriers in the territories annexed or influenced by France, while war also destroyed capital, interrupted trade, and cut continental producers off from British machinery and markets. After 1815, the settlement discussed in Chapter 92 restored many dynasties but did not restore the pre revolutionary economic order. Governments in Prussia, the Netherlands, Austria, France, and elsewhere faced the same question: how to respond to British industrial power while managing revenue, social stability, and the interests of landowners and guilds. Their answers differed, and those differences are a major subject of this chapter.

Two further points of context are needed. First, Europe in this period remained overwhelmingly agricultural. Even in the most industrialized continental regions, a majority of the population in 1850 lived in rural settings, and across eastern and southern Europe the proportion was far higher. Industrial development took the form of concentrated districts surrounded by large agrarian regions, not a uniform national transformation. Second, the chapter's chronological frame, approximately 1800 to 1880, stops short of the mature steel, chemical, and electrical industries of the later nineteenth century, which belong to Chapter 101, and it treats German and Italian political unification, discussed in Chapter 100, only where it bears directly on economic integration.

Evidence for the chapter requires methodological care. Nineteenth century statistics were collected by governments, chambers of commerce, and companies for administrative, fiscal, or promotional purposes, and definitions varied from state to state. Output series for coal and iron are generally better than series for textiles or for household production. Modern estimates of national income and industrial output, such as those by Paul Bairoch and by scholars working from compilations like those of B. R. Mitchell, rest on reconstructions that involve judgment, and the figures quoted in this chapter are therefore rounded and described as approximate. Contemporary accounts, including the reports of officials sent to study British factories, are valuable but were written by observers with specific purposes, such as persuading a ministry to fund a technical school or a railway.

Section 94.1

Industrialization beyond Britain: Diffusion, Adaptation, and Uneven Development

Historians use the term industrial diffusion for the movement of machinery, production methods, investment, and technical knowledge from one region to another. The word can mislead if it suggests a smooth ripple spreading outward from a British center. What moved was rarely a complete system. A spinning mule might cross the Channel, but the coal to power it, the foremen who could maintain it, the merchants who could sell its yarn, and the banks that could finance its purchase had to be assembled locally. Where those elements existed, diffusion could be rapid. Where they did not, imported machines might stand idle or be used in ways quite different from British practice. Industrialization beyond Britain was therefore a process of adaptation as much as imitation.

The speed of adoption varied widely. A broad contrast can be drawn between regions that began mechanized production in the first decades after 1815, including Belgium, parts of northern France, Alsace, the Rhineland, Saxony, Bohemia, and Switzerland, and regions in which comparable change began only after the middle of the century, such as much of Scandinavia, Italy, Spain, and the Russian Empire, or hardly at all before 1880, as in much of the Balkans. Even these groupings conceal sharp internal differences. Within Prussia, the Rhineland and Silesia industrialized much faster than East Elbian agrarian provinces. Within Italy, Lombardy and Piedmont diverged from the south. Within France, the northeast and Alsace diverged from the Massif Central and the southwest. The relevant unit of analysis was often the district, not the state.

Historians measure industrial development using several kinds of indicator, each with limits. Output of coal and pig iron is relatively well documented and reflects the energy and material base of heavy industry, but says little about textiles, engineering, or luxury crafts. Railway mileage measures infrastructure but not the productivity of the lines. Employment figures show the proportion of the labor force in manufacturing, but definitions of "manufacturing" often included handicraft workers in tiny workshops. Estimates of national income, per capita output, and the share of agriculture in total employment can be assembled for some countries but depend on reconstruction. Section 94.19 returns to these methods in detail. The key point here is that no single indicator captures industrialization, and that the British case, with its early dominance of coal, cotton, and iron, was an unusual benchmark.

Describing Britain as an important reference point but not a universal template has practical consequences for how this chapter proceeds. Continental governments and entrepreneurs certainly looked at Britain, sent observers, and imported machines. But continental conditions differed in ways that mattered. Labor was often cheaper relative to capital, and land and capital markets were organized differently. Many regions possessed charcoal iron industries and abundant waterpower, which made certain British technologies less immediately attractive than they appeared on paper. Political fragmentation, guild regulation, and internal tolls inhibited markets. In many societies, peasants held land under legal and customary arrangements that kept labor on the land. The question for historians is therefore less "why were these places late?" than "what combination of conditions made particular routes to industry feasible in each?"

Historical Vocabulary
Industrial Diffusion

Industrial diffusion is the process by which industrial technologies, organizational methods, capital, and skills spread beyond their place of origin. In nineteenth century Europe, diffusion occurred through the movement of British machinery and engineers, the imitation of factory methods by continental entrepreneurs, state sponsored study missions, technical publications, and cross border investment. The term is sometimes mistakenly equated with simple copying. Historians emphasize that diffusion involved selection and modification, because imported techniques had to fit local coal and ore supplies, wage levels, market sizes, and institutions. The concept matters in this chapter because it explains why industrial regions across Europe were linked to one another and to Britain, yet developed distinct economic structures.

Section 94.2

Continental Europe after the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars

The wars of 1792 to 1815 affected continental economies in contradictory ways. In the territories annexed to or reorganized by France, including Belgium, the Rhineland, and parts of Italy, the revolutionary and imperial governments abolished guilds, ended many feudal dues, standardized weights, measures, and law, and opened internal markets. The Rhineland's left bank, for example, was incorporated into France and experienced legal and administrative change that later industrialists would regard as favorable to enterprise. At the same time, war diverted manpower and capital, destroyed property, and disrupted overseas commerce. The Continental System, Napoleon's attempt to exclude British goods from European markets, offered certain continental manufacturers protection and encouraged some import substituting industries, such as cotton spinning in Ghent, the Rhineland, and Saxony, and beet sugar in France. When peace returned in 1815 and cheap British goods flooded in, many of these protected enterprises faced a harsh adjustment.

The postwar settlement, examined in diplomatic terms in Chapter 92, had important economic effects. Prussia received the Rhineland and Westphalia, bringing much of the future Ruhr industrial area and important Saar coal deposits under Prussian administration alongside its eastern agrarian provinces. The Northern and Southern Netherlands were united under William I, producing a kingdom that combined Dutch commerce and shipping with Belgian coal, textiles, and metalworking. The German Confederation of thirty nine states replaced the old Holy Roman Empire, leaving a patchwork of customs zones, currencies, and legal systems. Austria retained a vast and diverse monarchy but one whose territories were divided by internal tariff lines, notably between Hungary and the rest of the empire. France lost the territories it had held abroad but retained a unified legal and administrative system and a protectionist tariff.

Internal barriers were among the most important constraints. Before its tariff reform of 1818, Prussia had inherited differing customs arrangements across its scattered territories. The Prussian tariff law of 1818 abolished internal customs barriers and established a common external tariff, while many neighboring German states continued to impose their own duties. A trader sending goods across several German states might pay tolls and duties repeatedly, with a cumulative effect on costs. Similar problems affected the Italian states, and the Habsburg monarchy's internal customs line increased the cost of moving Hungarian grain and Austrian manufactures. Rivers were subject to tolls at state frontiers, a problem addressed at the Congress of Vienna by principles of free navigation on international rivers such as the Rhine, though in practice enforcement was uneven for decades.

Agrarian structures also shaped the opportunities for industry. In western Prussia, France, and Belgium, peasant landholding and the abolition of seigneurial obligations generally allowed freer movement of rural labor and, in some cases, created demand for manufactured goods. In Prussia, the reforms associated with Stein and Hardenberg between 1807 and 1815 ended hereditary serfdom, but peasants often had to pay compensation to landlords, and the legal changes consolidated large estates in the east. Further east and south, in the Habsburg lands and the Russian Empire, serfdom or related labor obligations persisted for decades, limiting both labor mobility and consumer demand. These differences in rural social structure, more than any single technology, help explain regional differences in industrial timing.

Political instability formed the final element of the setting. The revolutions of 1830 and 1848, examined in Chapter 98, disrupted trade, destroyed capital, and sometimes accelerated reforms. Belgium's independence in 1830 separated an industrial region from the Dutch market but gave it control over its own policy. The upheavals of 1848 hit banks and railway companies across the continent. Governments in these decades were conscious that industrial growth might also create social unrest, a concern that shaped state policy and that Chapter 95 examines in detail. For economic modernization, the era after 1815 offered opportunities, but they were uneven and depended on political choices.

Section 94.3

Geography, Coal, Waterpower, and the Distribution of Industrial Opportunity

Chapter 2 introduced the physical geography of Europe, and nowhere is it more relevant to the economic history of the nineteenth century than in the distribution of fuel and ore. Industrial growth in the first half of the century depended heavily on coal for steam engines and iron smelting. The major continental coalfields lay in a broad arc running from northern France and the Sambre–Meuse basin in Belgium eastward through the Ruhr and across the Saar, to Upper Silesia, the adjoining Dąbrowa basin in Russian Poland, and parts of Bohemia and Moravia. Smaller basins existed in the Saint-Étienne region of France, in Saxony, and in Asturias in northern Spain. Britain's coal was, however, extraordinarily well placed near ports and iron ore, while many continental fields were located inland and distant from the principal markets.

The Sambre–Meuse basin, running through the Hainaut and Liège regions of Belgium, was the earliest great continental centre of heavy industry. Coal seams lay close to the surface in the Borinage near Mons and around Charleroi and Liège, iron ore and zinc deposits were close by, and the Meuse and Sambre rivers provided transport. The Ruhr coalfield, lying between the Ruhr and Lippe rivers on the right bank of the Rhine, was initially mined in small pits along the river valley, and its larger and deeper seams further north were exploited as shafts and steam pumps made deeper mining possible, an expansion that gathered pace from the 1840s. The Saar coalfield, mined under Prussian and Bavarian administration, supplied nearby ironworks and the Rhine region. Upper Silesia combined coal, iron ore, and zinc and was an important mining region from the late eighteenth century. In each case, the date at which a resource became economically important depended on technology and transport, not simply on geology.

Not every industrializing region relied on coal. Waterpower, supplied by fast flowing streams and rivers, remained crucial in the Alps and Jura, in Alsace's Vosges valleys, in Saxony, in Sweden, and in the Pyrenean and Catalan hill districts, and water turbines, improved in France and Switzerland in the 1820s and 1830s, kept it competitive with steam for decades. Forests supplied charcoal for iron smelting in Sweden, the Urals, parts of France, and Styria and Carinthia in the Austrian lands, and this charcoal iron industry could produce very high quality bar iron. Some regions with plentiful coal lacked adequate iron ore, some with ore lacked coal, and some with both lacked the markets or transport to use them. The Ruhr's iron ore, for example, was poor in quality, and ore was increasingly imported from elsewhere.

Waterways and ports shaped industrial opportunity as much as mineral deposits. The Rhine, the Meuse, the Elbe, the Oder, and the Danube provided cheap transport for bulky goods, and canals extended these networks. Coastal and estuarine ports such as Antwerp, Rotterdam, Hamburg, Le Havre, Marseille, and Genoa connected industrial districts to overseas supplies of raw cotton, wool, and ore, and to export markets. The importance of cheap bulk transport helps explain why the Rhine and the lower Meuse became corridors of industrial development, why Swiss firms could import British coal by water and rail at acceptable cost, and why inland regions remote from navigable water, such as much of central Spain, struggled until railways reduced transport costs.

Resource endowment is a necessary but not a sufficient explanation. The Donets basin in the Russian Empire held vast reserves but was developed on any scale only after railways, foreign capital, and state policy arrived in the 1860s and 1870s. Spain had coal in Asturias and iron ore in the Basque Country, but weak internal transport, limited capital, and small markets delayed its integrated use. The Netherlands and Switzerland, with little coal, nevertheless built successful industrial economies through trade, finance, specialization, and imported fuel. Conversely, regions with excellent endowments but constraints in law, market access, or capital stayed undeveloped. Industrial opportunity was thus the product of geology interacting with institutions, technology, and markets, a theme this chapter develops throughout.

Section 94.4

Technology Transfer, Skilled Workers, and the Circulation of Industrial Knowledge

Britain attempted for many decades to preserve its technological lead by restricting the emigration of skilled artisans and the export of machinery. These controls were relaxed in the mid 1820s and largely abandoned by the early 1840s, but they were never fully effective. British mechanics, engineers, and foremen crossed to the continent in considerable numbers, often under contract to individual entrepreneurs or governments. The Cockerills, with their workshops in the Verviers region and later at Seraing, were the most famous example, but they were one of many. British technicians helped establish engineering works, textile mills, ironworks, and mines in France, Belgium, the German states, Switzerland, Russia, and elsewhere. British engineers, among them figures associated with Robert Stephenson's firm, were also employed on early railways.

Technology transfer took several forms. Machinery was purchased directly, smuggled, copied, or built under license. Technical knowledge circulated through patents, scientific and engineering journals, exhibitions, and visits. Governments dispatched observers: Prussian officials traveled to Britain in the 1810s and 1820s to study factories and steam engines, and Alfred Krupp, as a young manufacturer, visited Britain in the late 1830s to learn about production methods. Merchants, bankers, and travelers served as informal channels. Industrial exhibitions, culminating in the Great Exhibition in London in 1851 and followed by similar events in Paris and elsewhere, displayed machines and products to an international audience and encouraged comparison and emulation.

Imported technologies were rarely applied unchanged. In iron production, coke smelting and puddling, the two core British techniques, had to be adapted to local fuels and ores. Many continental ironmasters continued to use charcoal for longer than British counterparts, in part because the quality of the resulting iron was prized and charcoal was available. Textile machinery was modified for local fibers and labor conditions, and in several regions mechanization of spinning proceeded faster than that of weaving, leaving handloom weavers to work alongside mechanized spinning mills for decades. In machine building, continental firms sometimes concentrated on specialized products, such as precision tools, textile machinery, or locomotives, for which their own markets offered demand. The pattern of selective adoption and local modification is central to the argument that Europe did not follow one British model.

Workshops and mechanics formed the human basis of transfer. Skilled workers who could build, repair, and adjust machinery were scarce, and firms that trained them acted as schools for later enterprises. Many of Germany's leading engineering entrepreneurs, for example, began as workers or managers in earlier workshops. Entrepreneurs such as Friedrich Harkort in Westphalia, who established a mechanical workshop at Wetter in 1819 with British technical help, illustrate the role of individuals who combined commercial vision with a willingness to import expertise, though his firm, like Cockerill's, encountered financial difficulty. Cross border commercial relationships mattered too. Firms in Alsace and Switzerland drew on contacts in Britain and in each other's regions, and merchant houses in Hamburg, Antwerp, and Trieste connected industrial districts to overseas markets and suppliers.

The circulation of knowledge was not one directional. Continental inventors and engineers, including those in France, contributed innovations in areas such as the water turbine, the Jacquard loom for patterned silk weaving, chemistry, and railway engineering, and British firms in turn adopted some continental techniques. The eventual development of independent technical capacity on the continent, examined in Section 94.12, meant that by the 1860s and 1870s several continental industries no longer depended on British models and in some fields, such as chemicals and later steel, would challenge British leadership, themes treated in Chapter 101.

Section 94.5

Belgium: The First Major Continental Industrial Breakthrough

Belgium is the clearest early example of continental industrialization, and it is also a reminder that the state of Belgium postdates much of its industrial foundation. Before the revolution of 1830, the territories that became Belgium had been part of the Austrian Netherlands, were annexed to France from 1795 to 1814, and from 1815 formed the southern provinces of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands under William I. They had a long inheritance of manufacturing: linen and woolen textiles in Flanders and the Verviers district, ironworking and coal mining in the Liège and Hainaut regions, glassmaking and nail making around Charleroi, and dense urban markets. French rule opened a large protected market, and the Dutch period under William I brought active government promotion, including support for banking, canals, and enterprises such as Cockerill's.

Coal was the foundation. Output rose from perhaps two to three million tonnes around 1830 to roughly six million by 1850 and about fourteen million by 1870, according to rounded standard estimates. Mining in the Borinage and around Charleroi and Liège supplied steam engines, ironworks, glassworks, and zinc smelters. The Liège basin also developed a major zinc industry, with firms such as the Vieille Montagne company, founded in 1837, exporting widely. Iron production shifted toward coke smelting and puddling in the 1820s and 1830s, with Cockerill's Seraing works and other firms around Liège and Charleroi leading the way. Seraing's combination of mines, furnaces, and machine shops was unusual in its vertical integration, a form that later became more common in continental heavy industry.

Textiles followed a different trajectory. Ghent's cotton mills, which had begun to mechanize in the late 1790s with imported British machinery, grew after 1815 behind favorable conditions in Dutch colonial trade, and Verviers became a leading woolen center. But Flanders' vast rural linen industry, based on household spinning and weaving, was damaged by competition from machine spun yarn, and by the 1840s the region suffered a severe crisis, worsened by the potato blight and harvest failures of 1845 to 1850. Belgium thus combined the continent's most dynamic mining and metal region with a deeply distressed handloom economy, a contrast that illustrates the uneven effects of industrial change within a single small country.

Finance and the state were important. The Société Générale des Pays Bas, established in 1822 with royal backing, lent heavily to mining and industrial firms and became involved in ownership as well as credit. After independence in 1830, the new government pursued active policies, most notably in railways. A law of 1834 committed the state to building a national network, and the Brussels to Mechelen line opened in 1835, making Belgium the first continental country with a planned railway system. State ownership of the trunk lines, shaped partly by strategic considerations and the wish to preserve the position of Antwerp against Dutch competition, was combined with private enterprise in other sectors. The railway network created demand for iron, locomotives, and coal, and helped integrate the Sambre–Meuse industries with the ports.

Historians distinguish carefully between industrial development before and after independence. The base was laid under French and Dutch rule, and the Dutch government's investments and banking support were considerable, even though the Belgian revolution reflected grievances that included perceived Dutch favoritism. After 1830 independence removed Belgian industry from privileged access to Dutch colonial markets but gave domestic policymakers freedom to build railways, seek new markets in Germany and France, and expand heavy industry. Belgium's relatively small size made it unusually dependent on exports, and its economy became tightly connected to the German customs union, to France, and to Britain.

Multiple Perspectives

Industrial Change in the Belgian Provinces, 1820s to 1840s

From the perspective of an industrial investor or banker: Coal, ironworks, and railways offered large returns but also large risks. Capital was committed for long periods, profits depended on the stability of governments and credit, and a single failure, such as the financial crisis that troubled Cockerill's enterprises in the late 1830s, could threaten a lender as well as a manufacturer. From the perspective of an engineer or skilled worker: Mechanics and foremen, many of them British or trained in British workshops, were in demand. They commanded wages above those of ordinary laborers and shaped how machines were built and operated, but they also depended on employers' contracts and were part of a labor market in which skills were scarce and mobility across borders was common.
From the perspective of a Flemish handloom weaver or rural linen household: Mechanized spinning reduced the value of household spinning and eventually pressed weavers' earnings downward. When harvests failed in the 1840s, households already weakened by falling textile income faced hunger and migration. For them, industrialization elsewhere in the country appeared as a loss of livelihood, not as progress.
From the perspective of an agricultural producer in Hainaut or the Ardennes edge: Mining and ironworks created new markets for food, fodder, and timber and new opportunities for seasonal or part time labor, but they also drew labor from farms, altered land values, and sometimes damaged fields and water. Farmers' responses varied between those who profited from nearby industrial demand and those who found their way of life disrupted.

Why these perspectives matter: The groups did not hold equal power or bear equal risk. Investors and managers could shape policy, whereas weavers and miners largely experienced industrial change as something imposed. The social consequences of these divisions are examined more fully in Chapter 95.

Section 94.6

France: Gradual Industrialization and Regional Economic Diversity

French industrialization is often presented as the main contrast to Britain's: slower, less concentrated, and more dependent on small enterprises. There is truth in the description, but it requires qualification. France entered the nineteenth century with a large population, a prosperous agriculture, a long tradition of luxury and artisanal manufacturing, and a strong state. The Revolution abolished guilds and seigneurial dues and created a unified legal and fiscal system. These changes arguably promoted a flexible commercial environment, though they also entrenched peasant landholding and a large rural economy.

French industry developed in distinct regional clusters. In the north, around Lille, Roubaix, and Tourcoing, cotton and woolen textiles mechanized gradually and the coalfields of Nord and Pas de Calais, extended from the Belgian basin, became increasingly important from the 1840s onward. Alsace, with Mulhouse at its center, became a major cotton printing and spinning region, supported by local entrepreneurial families, technical societies such as the Société industrielle de Mulhouse founded in 1826, and the waterpower of the Vosges valleys. Lyon was the center of silk weaving, in which the Jacquard loom, perfected in the first years of the century, allowed complex patterns, and the industry remained largely organized through merchants who put out work to skilled weavers, the canuts, working in workshops and homes. Saint-Étienne combined coal mining, arms manufacture, and ribbon weaving and was the site of the first French railway lines, which opened from the late 1820s to carry coal. The Creusot works in Burgundy, taken over by the Schneider brothers in 1836, became a leading iron and engineering firm. Paris remained a center for luxury goods, printing, fashion, and a wide range of skilled trades, as well as the main financial market.

Statistics bear out both the progress and the limits. Coal output grew from under two million tonnes around 1830 to roughly four to five million around 1850 and about thirteen million around 1870 on rounded estimates, substantial in absolute terms but far below Britain's. France was also a net importer of coal, particularly from Belgium and Britain, because its coalfields were scattered and often far from iron ore and from the major markets. Pig iron output was modest by British standards, and charcoal smelting persisted into the 1840s and beyond in many regions. Railways expanded after the law of 1842 organized construction through a partnership in which the state provided land and infrastructure while private companies built and operated the lines, and by 1870 the network was extensive, centered on Paris.

Workshop and rural production remained very important. Many French firms were small or medium sized and family run, and large sections of manufacturing, such as clothing, furniture, ironmongery, and luxury goods, depended on skilled labor more than on heavy machinery. Many peasant households combined farming with seasonal manufacturing. Mechanization often proceeded alongside, rather than in place of, these traditional forms. The country's population grew more slowly than Britain's or Germany's in the nineteenth century, which affected labor supply and domestic demand, and French agriculture's productivity and peasant proprietorship kept many families on the land.

The state and finance mattered. Governments under the Restoration, the July Monarchy, and the Second Empire built roads, canals, and ports, maintained technical schools, and protected many industries by tariffs, though the Second Empire moved toward freer trade, symbolized by the Cobden–Chevalier treaty of 1860. The Second Empire also saw rapid expansion of joint stock banking, including the Crédit Mobilier (1852), the Crédit Lyonnais (1863), and the Société Générale (1864), which financed railways, mines, and urban development. The Second Empire's urban rebuilding of Paris, directed by Baron Haussmann, became a major stimulus to construction and investment. The question for historians is not whether France industrialized, since it plainly did, but what pattern the process followed and how it should be evaluated.

Historical Debate

Was French Industrialization Slow, or Did It Follow a Different Path?

The question: Did France industrialize more slowly and less successfully than Britain in the nineteenth century, because of specific obstacles, or did it follow an alternative route that produced comparable economic growth by different means?

Interpretation A — France lagged because of identifiable constraints: Older and influential accounts argued that France fell behind Britain because of factors such as limited and poorly located coal, slow population growth, and the persistence of small scale peasant agriculture, which restricted domestic demand and the supply of mobile labor. In this view, the revolutionary and Napoleonic wars had left a legacy of lost markets and capital, and French industry remained too dependent on small workshops and protected markets. Some economists and historians described a pattern of retardation compared with Britain and, later, Germany.
Interpretation B — France followed a different but successful pattern: Revisionist research, associated with scholars such as Patrick O'Brien and Çağlar Keyder, whose 1978 comparison of British and French growth was influential, argued that French output per head grew at rates broadly comparable to Britain's over the long nineteenth century, but through different means. Higher agricultural productivity, steadier population, skilled artisanal production, and a more gradual reallocation of labor from farm to industry produced growth without the sharp concentration of factory districts. On this view, French "slowness" was an artifact of using the British pattern of coal, cotton, and rapid urban growth as the standard.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same national statistics, but interpret them differently. Coal and iron output data, railway mileage, and patterns of urbanization support the claim that France's heavy industry was less concentrated than Britain's. Estimates of per capita income, productivity growth, and the persistence of high value artisanal exports support the revisionist case. Because the national income reconstructions for the early nineteenth century involve considerable estimation, scholars continue to debate their precision.

Why it matters: If French industrialization is judged by British standards, it appears deficient. If it is judged by its own context, it appears as one of several viable European paths. The debate therefore bears directly on this chapter's argument that Britain was a reference point and not a universal template. Many historians today combine the positions, accepting that France industrialized more gradually and regionally, while avoiding the older claim that it was economically stagnant.

Section 94.7

The German Lands: Political Fragmentation, Economic Integration, and the Zollverein

After 1815 the German lands were organized in the German Confederation, a loose league of thirty nine sovereign states led by Austria and Prussia, with no common economic policy. The economic map was a mosaic. The Rhineland and Westphalia, with their metalworking and textile traditions and nearby coal, lay in Prussia's western provinces. Saxony was a densely populated manufacturing state. The Hanseatic cities, Hamburg, Bremen, and Lübeck, depended on maritime trade. The southern states of Bavaria, Württemberg, and Baden combined agriculture with crafts and some industry. East of the Elbe lay Prussian and Mecklenburg estates producing grain for export. Each jurisdiction had its own currency, tariffs, and regulations.

Prussia led the first movement toward economic integration. Its reform era between 1807 and 1815 had ended serfdom in principle, introduced freedom of trade in 1810 and 1811, and reorganized the administration. In 1818 the Prussian government replaced the many internal tariffs across its scattered territories with a single customs law that imposed moderate duties on imports at the external frontier and allowed free movement within. This law, designed in part for fiscal reasons, also served commercial ones. Prussian territories then pressed neighboring states into customs agreements, partly by making transit across Prussian lands costly. In 1828 a Bavarian Württemberg customs union appeared in the south, and a Central German commercial union formed in rivalry. In 1834 the German Customs Union, the Zollverein, brought together eighteen states with roughly twenty three million people, a figure widely cited and approximate.

The Zollverein's membership and the arrangement for distributing duties grew over time. Further states joined in the following decades, including Hanover and others in the 1850s, and the union extended across most of the German Confederation excluding Austria and the Hanseatic cities, which joined later. Monetary coordination also developed, with agreements in 1837 and 1838 establishing coinage standards that linked the southern gulden zone and northern thaler zone, an important step though not full currency unity. Common tariffs were modest in the early period and mostly aimed at revenue, but they helped create a larger internal market for goods and encouraged railway planning.

Historians disagree about how much the Zollverein itself caused German industrial growth. It clearly reduced transaction costs, increased trade within its territory, and gave the member states a shared framework. It also strengthened Prussia's economic and political influence, because it excluded Austria. Yet industrial development in the Rhineland, Silesia, and Saxony was also driven by resource endowments, entrepreneurship, railway investment, and demand, and the union's tariffs were not sufficient on their own to build heavy industry. Economic integration and political fragmentation coexisted: Germans lived in many sovereign states but traded within a single customs area. The political and military story of unification belongs to Chapter 100, and this section should not be read as implying that economic union made political union inevitable.

Other conditions matter. Prussia developed technical education, including the Berlin Industrial Institute founded in 1821 under the official Peter Beuth, and promoted infrastructure through state road and later railway policy. Agricultural reform, including the abolition of serfdom and the spread of new crops and methods, increased rural productivity and, eventually, released labor for industry and cities. Population growth in the German lands was strong in this period, providing a growing labor supply and domestic market, though also contributing to rural poverty and migration, matters Chapter 95 examines. Together, these developments established conditions in which, after the 1840s, German industry grew quickly.

Historical Vocabulary
Zollverein

The Zollverein, or German Customs Union, was an agreement among German states, led by Prussia and effective from 1 January 1834, to abolish internal customs duties among members and adopt a common external tariff, with the revenue shared among the participants. It was a customs union and not a political union, and it initially excluded Austria. Modern readers sometimes treat it as a direct precursor of national unification, but historians emphasize that it was a limited economic arrangement whose political consequences were contingent. It matters here because it shows how markets could be integrated across political fragmentation, and because later discussions of European economic integration sometimes invoke it, a comparison that should be made cautiously, since the nineteenth century union had no supranational political institutions of the kind later developed (see Chapter 133).

Section 94.8

The Rhineland, Ruhr, Saxony, and the Rise of German Heavy Industry

From the 1840s, German industry entered a phase of rapid growth centered on coal, iron, machinery, and textiles. The pace was strongly linked to railway construction, which created enormous demand for rails, locomotives, and machinery. Germany's railways grew from a few hundred kilometers in the early 1840s to about six thousand by 1850 and about nineteen thousand by 1870, on rounded estimates, and domestic firms increasingly supplied the needed iron and engines, replacing British imports.

The Ruhr became the emblematic heavy industrial region. Coal had been mined for centuries in small pits near the Ruhr river, but the opening of deeper mines northward, using steam pumping and sinking shafts through overlying marl, transformed output. Rounded estimates suggest Ruhr production of about 1.5 to 2 million tonnes around 1850, around four million around 1860, and more than eleven million by 1870. The discovery that Ruhr coal could be coked effectively, and the finding of iron ores in the region, such as blackband ironstone, supported blast furnace construction, though later growth also relied on imported ores. The Cologne to Minden railway, completed in 1847, linked the Rhine to the Weser and the lines of northern Germany, and railways and canals knit the Ruhr to the Rhine ports. Firms such as the Bochumer Verein, which began casting steel in the 1840s, and later the Phoenix and Hoesch companies, developed in this environment.

The Krupp firm of Essen exemplifies the combination of family enterprise, technical innovation, and market opportunity. Friedrich Krupp founded a cast steel works in 1811, attempting to rival British crucible steel, but the business struggled until his son Alfred took over in 1826. Alfred Krupp slowly built the works into a significant producer of cast steel items, notably in the 1840s and 1850s railway equipment such as axles and seamless wheel tires, then guns, with the firm displaying a huge steel ingot at the Great Exhibition in London in 1851. By the 1860s Krupp employed thousands of workers, though its growth was repeatedly threatened by financial strain and depended on credit and, later, on state orders. Other firms, such as August Borsig's locomotive works in Berlin, founded in 1837, and Hartmann's in Chemnitz, show the growth of machine building outside the Ruhr.

Saxony, by contrast, was an older manufacturing region, with textiles, mining in the Erzgebirge, and a strong tradition of technical and commercial education. Chemnitz became a leading machine building and textile center, while Leipzig was a trading and publishing city and the starting point of one of Germany's first long distance railways, the line to Dresden opened in 1839. The Rhineland developed textile, metal, and chemical industries, and Cologne and Düsseldorf grew into commercial and financial centers. In Silesia, the Prussian state long supported mining and metallurgy, and Upper Silesia developed coal, iron, and zinc industries, often dominated by large landed magnates who invested in mines on their estates, an example of how agrarian elites could take part in industrial development. The Saar, under Prussian and Bavarian administration, supplied coal and iron and became closely linked to the Rhineland and to Lorraine.

These districts differed greatly. The Ruhr's industries were relatively new, capital intensive, and growing in a region of previously small towns and rural settlement, which attracted migrants from other regions. Saxon manufacturing grew from established craft and commercial roots. Silesian heavy industry was linked to aristocratic estates and to eastern markets. The Rhineland benefited from river transport and an old network of cities. By around 1870 the combined output of these regions had placed the German lands, taken together, ahead of France in coal and roughly comparable in pig iron, though still far behind Britain. The mature steel, chemical, and electrical industries that would soon make Germany an industrial leader belong to Chapter 101 and are not narrated here.

Section 94.9

Railways, Canals, and the Integration of Continental Markets

No single innovation shaped the continental economy of the mid nineteenth century more visibly than the railway. Earlier transport depended on roads, rivers, and canals, which were slow, seasonal, and expensive for bulky goods. Canals, such as those built in France, Belgium, and the German states, and improved river navigation remained important throughout the century, but railways reduced transport costs, shortened journey times, and, crucially, could be built where rivers did not run. They connected mines to ironworks, ironworks to manufacturers, farms to cities, and cities to ports.

Continental construction began slowly, then accelerated. Early lines were short and local: the Saint-Étienne lines in France carried coal from the late 1820s, and the Budweis to Linz horse drawn line in the Habsburg lands of 1832 was a precursor. Steam passenger and freight lines followed in the mid 1830s: Brussels to Mechelen in 1835, Nuremberg to Fürth in the same year, the Nordbahn from Vienna in 1837, and Paris to Saint Germain in 1837. The 1840s brought the first great wave of construction, interrupted by the financial and political crises of 1847 and 1848, and the 1850s and 1860s brought another, supported by new investment banks. A harmonized historical infrastructure series reports approximately 5,900 kilometers of railway in the German lands in 1850 and 18,900 in 1870, approximately 2,900 and 15,500 in France, approximately 850 and 2,900 in Belgium, and approximately 1,350 and 6,100 in the Habsburg territories. Historical compilations do not always agree, especially for Habsburg mileage, because coverage and counting conventions differ. These figures follow the same TIE infrastructure series used in Table 94.2, rather than combining incompatible estimates.

Railways were both consequence and stimulus. They depended on existing industrial capacity: iron for rails, locomotives, and bridges, coal for fuel, engineering skills, and capital. Early lines on the continent relied heavily on British rails and locomotives, but domestic suppliers soon expanded, and the demand from railways became a major force in the growth of ironworks and machine shops in Belgium, northern France, and the German lands. Railway construction also employed large numbers of laborers, often migrants, and created a new class of engineers and managers. At the same time, railways altered markets. They made it economical to ship coal and iron further, supported specialization by region, widened the reach of urban markets for agricultural produce, and fostered the growth of cities and ports.

National approaches differed. Belgium committed its government to build and operate the main trunk lines, which it completed in the 1840s, while allowing private companies later to build secondary lines. France, after protracted debate and experimentation, adopted in 1842 a mixed system in which the state acquired land and built infrastructure while private companies laid track and operated the lines under concessions, and a network radiating from Paris developed. The German states followed varied approaches: in Prussia, private companies predominated, with the state later playing a larger part, while Baden, Württemberg, Hanover, and Brunswick built state lines. In the Habsburg monarchy, the state built some lines but later turned to private investors, often assisted by foreign capital. Differences in ownership affected the pattern and pace of construction, tariffs, and the relationship between railway companies and manufacturers.

Railways did not only integrate markets, they also redistributed economic advantage. Regions linked early to major markets and ports enjoyed lower costs and new opportunities, while places bypassed by lines could stagnate. Railways could also expose local producers to competition, as cheap goods from distant factories undercut local workshops. In regions without domestic iron industries, such as Spain or the Danubian lands, railway construction relied on imported materials and foreign capital, creating dependence rather than stimulating domestic industry to the same extent. This chapter therefore treats railways as one of the principal mechanisms of both integration and inequality.

Section 94.10

Banking, Industrial Capital, and the Financing of Economic Expansion

Industrial enterprises needed capital for buildings, machinery, mines, and working stock, and the scale of capital required grew as industry moved from workshops to mines, blast furnaces, and railways. In Britain, as Chapter 93 explained, much early industrial finance came from reinvested profits, partnerships, local banks, and merchant credit, with a sophisticated commercial and financial market in London. Continental economies met similar needs through a range of arrangements that differed by country and period.

In the early decades, family firms and private bankers played the dominant role. Merchant banking houses, such as the Rothschilds in Paris, Vienna, and elsewhere, the Protestant banking families of Paris and Geneva, and the private bankers of Cologne and Frankfurt, provided short and medium term credit and handled large state loans. Many early entrepreneurs, such as the Krupps, relied on family capital, local credit, and relationships with suppliers and customers. Partnerships limited by shares, the French société en commandite, allowed investors to supply capital without managing the firm, and were widely used. General limited liability for joint stock companies was introduced later, in France in 1867 and in the German states in the 1860s and 1870s, and earlier forms of incorporation generally required state authorization.

Banks that lent to industry were especially important in Belgium. The Société Générale, established in 1822, and the Banque de Belgique, founded in 1835, extended credit to mining, metallurgical, and railway enterprises, and took ownership stakes in companies. This mixed banking practice, in which a bank both lent to and held shares in industrial firms, was adopted in various forms elsewhere. In France, the brothers Émile and Isaac Pereire, with the Crédit Mobilier in 1852, created a joint stock bank that raised funds by issuing securities and invested in railways, mines, gas, and other enterprises. Although the Crédit Mobilier failed in the 1860s, it influenced other institutions, including the Crédit Lyonnais and the Société Générale of 1864 in France and several German banks. In the German lands, the Schaaffhausen'scher Bankverein in Cologne, established in the 1840s, the Darmstädter Bank of 1853, and later the Disconto Gesellschaft and Deutsche Bank (1870) became associated with industrial and railway finance.

Economic historians, notably Alexander Gerschenkron in a famous essay published in 1962, argued that the later a country began industrializing, the more it would rely on banks and the state to supply the large amounts of capital that earlier industrializers had accumulated slowly, so that continental investment banks "substituted" for British forms of finance. This thesis, though influential, has been challenged by later research showing that internal profits and family funds remained important in many German and Belgian enterprises, and that the role of banks varied widely. It remains a useful framework for understanding why continental financial systems differed from Britain's, as long as it is not applied mechanically.

Railways imposed particular financial demands. Construction required enormous sums over several years before revenue appeared, and investors in many countries bought shares and bonds on securities exchanges in Paris, Brussels, Berlin, Vienna, and elsewhere. The boom and collapse in railway shares contributed to financial crises, especially in 1847 and 1857, and the investment banks sometimes overextended themselves. Foreign investment also became significant. British, French, and Belgian capital flowed into railways, mines, and manufacturing in Spain, Italy, Russia, the Ottoman Empire, and the Danubian lands, linking financial markets across Europe and creating relationships of dependency in some cases. These capital flows, and the debates they provoked about foreign control, are an important theme in Sections 94.16 to 94.18.

Historical Vocabulary
Industrial Banking and the Joint Stock Company

A joint stock company raises capital by issuing shares to many investors, whose liability may be limited to their investment, and whose ownership can be transferred. An investment bank or mixed bank, as developed in several continental countries, raised funds by deposits and securities and used them to finance or take stakes in industrial and railway companies, as opposed to confining itself to short term commercial credit. These forms mattered because the capital needs of mines, railways, and heavy industry exceeded what most families could supply. Modern readers should remember that the legal conditions varied: in many states, joint stock status required government approval until the 1860s. The terms also have different connotations in different countries and should not be assumed to describe identical institutions.

Section 94.11

Governments, Tariffs, Industrial Policy, and Economic Modernization

European governments influenced industrial development in many ways: through tariffs, public works, subsidies, education, legal reform, and administration. Historians have long debated the relative importance of states and markets, and the evidence suggests that neither unrestricted markets nor government direction produced industrial growth everywhere. Outcomes depended on what governments did, how effective their administrations were, and how policy interacted with resources and private enterprise.

Tariff policy was a central issue. Britain moved toward free trade in the 1840s and 1850s, notably through the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846, and British industrialists argued that open markets would benefit all. Continental manufacturers, facing established British competitors, often sought protection. France retained a high protective tariff until the 1860s, when Napoleon III's government negotiated the Cobden–Chevalier treaty of 1860 and subsequent agreements, which reduced duties and prompted similar treaties across Europe, creating a period of relatively liberal trade in the 1860s and early 1870s. The Zollverein's external tariff was moderate in the early decades, reflecting the interests of its member states, some of which exported grain and favored low duties, while manufacturers in others sought higher ones. Austria and Russia maintained prohibitive or very high tariffs for long periods. Belgium, a small and export dependent economy, generally pursued comparatively liberal commercial policies. The wave of protection that returned in the late 1870s, when the depression that began in 1873 intensified competition, belongs to later chapters.

Friedrich List gave theoretical form to the argument for protection. Born in Reutlingen in 1789, he had been involved in political controversies in Württemberg, spent several years in the United States in the 1820s, and returned to Germany in the 1830s as an advocate of railway construction and of a customs union. His major work, The National System of Political Economy (1841), argued that nations should foster their productive powers, not only maximize present wealth, and that temporary protection for young industries could be justified for countries seeking to catch up with a leader, though he considered free trade appropriate among nations at similar levels of development. His ideas were widely discussed in Germany, and influenced economic debates in other countries. The extent of their influence on actual policy is disputed, because the Zollverein's tariffs were not shaped by List alone, and later governments adopted protection for varied reasons, including revenue and the interests of landowners and industrialists.

States intervened through infrastructure and education, which probably mattered more than tariffs. Governments built roads, canals, ports, and in several cases railways, established technical schools and mining academies, standardized weights and measures, and reformed commercial law. Prussia's bureaucracy, with its mining administration and technical institutions, promoted industrial knowledge. The French state maintained the Corps of Bridges and Roads and mining engineers trained at elite schools. In Russia and the Habsburg lands, the state sponsored enterprises and sometimes subsidized private firms, though results were uneven. Many governments also protected landed interests and guilds, and fiscal priorities such as military spending often constrained investment in development. State intervention could succeed or fail depending on administrative capacity and on the willingness of governments to work with entrepreneurs.

Comparison shows the limits of generalization. Belgium combined an active state in railways with open trade and private banks. France combined protection with state led infrastructure and later liberalized. German states integrated markets without a central government. Britain, the supposed model of laissez faire, had in fact benefited from earlier protection and from a strong fiscal and naval state, a point List and later historians stressed. The comparative lesson is that industrial growth was supported by a range of policies, and that the effectiveness of any particular policy depended on timing, institutions, and local conditions.

He contended that countries seeking to catch up with a leading industrial power might legitimately protect their young industries for a time, while holding that Britain's advocacy of universal free trade reflected the interests of an already dominant manufacturer. This paraphrase condenses arguments made across several parts of the book and is not a verbatim passage.

It does not tell us how much protection the Zollverein actually adopted, how successful tariffs were in nurturing particular industries, or how workers, peasants, or merchants who bore the costs of protection viewed the policy. It also represents an educated, male, German speaking perspective and reflects List's own experiences and assumptions about national development.

Section 94.12

Engineering, Technical Education, and the Development of Continental Industry

Importing machines was one thing, maintaining, improving, and eventually designing them another. Continental societies therefore built institutions for technical education and the circulation of engineering knowledge, and these institutions formed an important part of the capacity to industrialize independently. The French Revolution had left a distinctive legacy. The École polytechnique, founded in 1794, trained scientifically educated engineers for state service and became a model for many later institutions, while the Conservatoire national des arts et métiers, also established in 1794, preserved machines and instruments and offered public instruction. The École centrale des arts et manufactures, founded in 1829 by private initiative, trained engineers for private industry, and mining engineers trained at the École des mines served the state.

In the German lands and the Habsburg monarchy, polytechnic institutes spread rapidly. The Prague Polytechnic dates from 1806 and the Vienna Polytechnic from 1815, the Berlin Industrial Institute from 1821, and polytechnic schools at Karlsruhe (1825), Munich, Dresden, Stuttgart, Hanover, and elsewhere followed in the following decades. Many of these started as trade schools and gradually raised their standards, and by the 1860s several had the status of technical universities in all but name. Mining academies, such as that at Freiberg in Saxony, had long traditions of training officials and engineers. Switzerland created the Federal Polytechnic in Zurich in 1855. In Scandinavia, technical institutes were established in Stockholm, Gothenburg, and Copenhagen in the late 1820s, and the Netherlands developed a polytechnic school at Delft in the 1840s and 1860s. Belgium's mining and engineering schools at Liège and elsewhere supported its industries.

The relationship between universities, engineering schools, and industry differed from country to country. In Britain, engineers were largely trained through apprenticeship and practical experience. In France, the state trained an elite corps of engineers in specialized schools who were then employed in public service or sometimes in private firms. In the German states, a combination of state supported technical schools and the strong tradition of scientific research at universities encouraged close links between science and industry, a connection that would be especially significant in chemistry later in the century. Technical education was not only for elites. Vocational and Sunday schools, apprenticeships, and guild traditions supplied skilled workers, and workers' own mutual associations sometimes arranged instruction.

Scientific societies, journals, industrial exhibitions, and professional associations further spread knowledge. The Society of Industry at Mulhouse, the Gewerbeverein in Prussia, and similar bodies encouraged innovation, awarded prizes, and published reports. National and international exhibitions, beginning with industrial exhibitions in France in the early nineteenth century and culminating in the Great Exhibition of 1851, exposed manufacturers to new techniques and gave states a means to display economic achievement. Patent systems, though weaker and more varied on the continent than in Britain, influenced how inventors protected their work.

By the 1860s and 1870s, the results were visible. Continental engineering firms such as Cockerill in Belgium, Schneider at Le Creusot, Borsig in Berlin, and Escher Wyss in Zurich built locomotives, steam engines, ships, and machinery that competed with British products, and continental engineers contributed to developments in railways, hydraulics, and metallurgy. The capacity to maintain and improve imported technology gave continental economies a degree of independence, though they continued to rely on Britain in various sectors, notably for some machine tools and for capital. The institutional foundations laid by technical schools became even more important in the later industries discussed in Chapter 101.

Section 94.13

The Habsburg Monarchy: Bohemia, Austria, Hungary, and Regional Industrial Inequality

The Habsburg monarchy was one of Europe's largest states, spanning the Austrian lands, the Bohemian crown lands of Bohemia and Moravia, Galicia, Hungary and its associated territories, Transylvania, Croatia, and, until 1859 and 1866, parts of northern Italy. It contained speakers of German, Czech, Hungarian, Polish, Ukrainian, Romanian, Italian, Slovene, Serbo-Croatian, and other languages, and its economic life was correspondingly varied. Because the monarchy was a composite of territories with different laws and institutions, industrial development depended on how these units were connected and governed. After 1867, when the Compromise created the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary, Austria and Hungary conducted separate economic policies within a customs and currency framework renegotiated periodically.

The industrial core lay in the Bohemian lands and Lower Austria. Bohemia had long traditions of textile production, glassmaking, and mining. Cotton spinning and weaving, using imported British machines and later domestic equipment, grew in northern Bohemia around Liberec (Reichenberg), and linen and woolen manufacturing continued. Moravia's capital Brno (Brünn) became a major woolen center, sometimes called the Moravian Manchester, and the Ostrava region in Moravian and Austrian Silesia developed coal mining, with the Vítkovice (Witkowitz) ironworks, founded in 1828 under the patronage of Archbishop Rudolf of Olomouc and later acquired by the Rothschild banking family, becoming a major producer. Lower Austria, with Vienna and its surrounding districts, hosted machine building, textile finishing, and consumer goods manufacture, and Styria and Carinthia remained centers of charcoal based ironmaking with a long history. Later in the century, Plzeň (Pilsen) developed heavy engineering around what became the Škoda works, after Emil Škoda purchased an engineering firm there in 1866.

Hungary and much of the east presented a contrasting picture. The Hungarian economy was based on grain, livestock, wine, and other agricultural produce, grown on large estates and by peasant farmers, with manufacturing concentrated in towns and in a few mining regions. Serfdom was abolished in 1848 during the revolutions, and the postrevolutionary decades of the 1850s and 1860s saw construction of railways, steam mills, and engineering works. Budapest became one of Europe's leading flour milling centers, supplied by wheat from the Great Hungarian Plain, and the Ganz works, founded in 1844 by the Swiss born Ábrahám Ganz, grew into a significant engineering firm. Steam navigation on the Danube, organized from 1829 by the Danube Steam Navigation Company, and railway links to Vienna and Trieste promoted export agriculture. But heavy industry and mechanized textile manufacturing were limited, and a large share of the population remained in agriculture, an imbalance that persisted past 1880.

The monarchy's institutions shaped these contrasts. The internal customs line between Austria and Hungary, a legacy of earlier policy intended partly to protect Austrian manufacturing and Hungarian agriculture, was abolished in 1850, creating a common customs area, though political tensions with Hungary continued. The state promoted railways, mining, and technical education but struggled with chronic fiscal problems and heavy military spending, and the financial crises of 1848 and 1859 constrained investment. The establishment of the Creditanstalt in Vienna in 1855, a joint stock bank modeled partly on the Crédit Mobilier and promoted by the Rothschilds and others, provided a vehicle for industrial and railway finance. After the military defeat of 1866 and the Compromise of 1867, the Hungarian government adopted policies aimed at promoting domestic industry, though the results before 1880 were modest.

Regional inequality within the monarchy overlapped with ethnic and linguistic divisions, and historians caution against assigning economic outcomes to national categories in an anachronistic way. Industrial entrepreneurs in Bohemia included German speaking and Czech speaking families, and urban working populations were often mixed. Meanwhile, rural Slavic, Hungarian, and Romanian speaking peasants, whose circumstances differed by region, supplied much of the agricultural output. Economic differences between Bohemia and Hungary, or between Lower Austria and Galicia, later became entangled with nationalist politics, but this entanglement was a historical development, not a given. The monarchy's industrial regions were connected by imperial markets, railways, and administrative structures, and its agrarian regions provided grain and raw materials, a division of labor that made the whole interdependent while leaving some regions persistently poorer.

Section 94.14

Switzerland and the Netherlands: Industrialization without a Single Heavy Industry Model

Switzerland and the Netherlands, both small and both lacking large domestic coalfields, demonstrate that industrial success did not depend on reproducing the British combination of coal, iron, and large factories. Their experience shows how specialization, trade, finance, and skills created alternative pathways.

Switzerland, a confederation of cantons with considerable local autonomy, had a long history of rural textile production, especially in eastern and northern regions. Cotton spinning mechanized early in the nineteenth century using water power, with mills along rivers in the cantons of Zurich, St. Gallen, and elsewhere. Embroidery and silk weaving became important specialties. Watchmaking, concentrated in Geneva and the Jura, relied on skilled workers and gradually introduced more machinery and division of labor, particularly in the middle decades of the century, and exported widely. Engineering grew from textile machinery workshops: Escher Wyss in Zurich, whose origins date to 1805, and Sulzer in Winterthur, founded in 1834, produced steam engines, turbines, and later locomotives and ships. Swiss firms imported coal and raw materials by river and rail and exported high value finished goods, which offset the transport costs of imported fuel.

Political change supported economic change. The Swiss federal constitution of 1848, following a brief civil war in 1847, created a single customs area by abolishing internal tolls, introduced a common currency, and gave the federal state responsibility for posts and customs. The establishment of the Federal Polytechnic in Zurich in 1855 provided advanced technical training, and the railway network expanded in the 1850s and 1860s, though major projects such as the Gotthard tunnel belong to the 1870s and 1880s. Switzerland also became a significant financial center, with banks in Zurich, Basel, and Geneva, a development that would deepen after 1880. The Swiss pattern, combining waterpower, specialization, skilled labor, export orientation, and technical education, shows that industrialization could succeed in a mountainous, fuel poor environment.

The Netherlands followed a different trajectory. In the seventeenth century the Dutch Republic had been Europe's leading commercial and financial power, as discussed in Chapter 77, and Dutch merchants, shipping, and financial institutions remained important in the nineteenth century. Yet industrialization on the British model was comparatively slow. The loss of the Belgian provinces in 1830 removed a major industrial region from the kingdom, and the northern Netherlands had limited coal, scattered population, and a commercial elite with abundant investment opportunities in trade, shipping, colonial enterprises, and foreign securities. William I tried to stimulate industry and trade, founding the Netherlands Trading Society in 1824 to promote commerce and shipping, and the colonial Cultivation System introduced in Java in 1830 produced revenues for the Dutch state, though its human costs for Javanese cultivators were severe. Chapter 105 treats European empires in Asia more fully.

Dutch manufacturing nevertheless developed. Textile production in the eastern region of Twente, using steam and imported machinery, grew from the middle of the century. Maastricht became a center of ceramics and glass, and engineering firms such as Stork, founded in Hengelo in 1868, emerged. Railways, beginning with the Amsterdam to Haarlem line in 1839, spread more slowly than in Belgium. Rotterdam and Amsterdam developed as ports for the Rhine trade and for overseas commerce, and the Rhine's connection to the Ruhr gave the Dutch ports a growing role in carrying German coal, ore, and goods. Historians have debated whether the Dutch economy was "backward" or simply followed a commercial and agricultural specialization that fit its resources, with many suggesting that the latter is more accurate. By the 1870s Dutch industry was expanding more quickly, and the long term reorientation of the Dutch economy toward its role as a gateway for the Rhine region and as a commercial and financial center was well underway.

Together, the two cases show that economic success could come from commerce, finance, specialized manufacture, and skilled labor. They also illustrate how political institutions mattered: Swiss federal reform created a common market, whereas Dutch policy supported trade and colonial enterprise. Neither can be explained by resources alone.

Section 94.15

Scandinavia and the Nordic Economies: Resources, Reform, and Later Industrial Growth

The Nordic economies lay on the northern edge of the European industrial system and had very different starting points from the western European centers. In the early nineteenth century, Sweden, Denmark, and Norway were predominantly agricultural societies with strong traditions of peasant farming, fishing, shipping, timber, and iron. Denmark and Norway had been united under the Danish crown until 1814, when Norway entered a union with Sweden while retaining its own constitution and institutions. Finland, a Grand Duchy under the Russian emperor after 1809, followed a distinct northern economic trajectory, discussed briefly below rather than subsumed into the history of Russian heavy industry.

Sweden's economy was long based on iron. Swedish bar iron, made with charcoal from local forests and high quality ore, was a leading European export in the eighteenth century, and the industry remained important in the nineteenth century, though British coke based production reduced its relative position. Swedish ironmasters adapted by improving charcoal methods, adopting the Lancashire hearth process, and, from 1858, producing Bessemer steel at Sandviken, where Göran Göransson ran one of the earliest commercially successful Bessemer operations, using high purity Swedish iron. Timber became a major export in the middle decades, particularly after British tariff reductions in the 1850s and the introduction of steam powered sawmills along the northern rivers. Engineering works, such as Motala Verkstad, founded in 1822 in connection with the Göta Canal, produced machinery and later locomotives and ships. State railways began in 1856 under the engineer Nils Ericson, with the government taking responsibility for trunk lines, while private companies built others.

Institutional reform helped. Sweden abolished many guild restrictions in 1846 and gradually liberalized trade in the 1850s and 1860s, and a bank act and expansion of private commercial banks in the 1850s improved credit. Agricultural change, including enclosure and consolidation of landholdings, increased output and, together with population growth, stimulated emigration, particularly to North America, an important outlet from the 1860s. The Swedish state established technical education institutions, including the Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, whose origins lie in the 1820s, and Chalmers in Gothenburg, founded in 1829.

Denmark's economy relied on agriculture, particularly grain exports to Britain and Germany, and on shipping and commerce through Copenhagen. After the abolition of absolutism in 1849 and further liberal reforms, trade restrictions were eased. The engineering firm Burmeister & Wain, which began in Copenhagen in the 1840s and grew into a major shipbuilder and engine producer, illustrates early industrial growth. Danish railway construction began in 1847 and agricultural transformation toward dairying and livestock, later associated with cooperative creameries, would become the foundation of Danish export success in the 1870s and 1880s, though this development belongs mostly to the period after the chapter's main focus. The war with Prussia and Austria in 1864, which cost Denmark Schleswig, Holstein, and Lauenburg, disrupted the economy and shaped political debate about the state's future.

Norway combined timber, fishing, mining, and especially shipping. Norwegian merchant shipping expanded rapidly in the mid nineteenth century, aided by British repeal of the Navigation Acts in 1849, and became one of the largest in Europe, with Norwegian shipowners and seamen carrying cargoes for much of the world. Industrial manufacturing grew more modestly, with workshops such as Akers mekaniske Verksted in Christiania (now Oslo), founded in 1841, and later textile mills and sawmills using waterpower. The first railway, from Christiania to Eidsvoll, opened in 1854 with British engineering support. Norway's industrial development would accelerate in the later nineteenth century with hydroelectric power, a topic beyond this chapter's boundaries.

Finland combined an important forest economy with selective industrial growth while remaining a Grand Duchy within the Russian Empire. Cotton manufacturing expanded at Tampere, timber and sawmilling benefited from both Baltic trade and Russian markets, and the Saimaa Canal opened in 1856. The first railway between Helsinki and Hämeenlinna opened in 1862, with a connection to St. Petersburg completed in 1870. These developments occurred amid continued dependence on farming, forestry, and local waterpower; they should not be confused with the industrial history of Russia as a whole. Compared with western and central Europe, Nordic industrial growth before 1880 was gradual and based on the processing and export of primary resources, supported by institutional reform, improved transport, and growing trade with Britain, Germany, and the Russian Empire. The foundations for later industrial growth in these countries, including a literate and technically educated population, expanding transport networks, and specialized export industries, developed in these decades, but the mature industries of the period after 1880 should not be read back into the earlier period.

Section 94.16

Italy and the Iberian Peninsula: Industrialization amid Regional Divisions

Southern Europe offers some of the sharpest examples of regional contrast within states. The Italian peninsula, divided before 1861 among the Kingdom of Sardinia (Piedmont), the Austrian ruled Lombardy and Venetia, the Papal States, the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, and smaller states, had no single market. Spain and Portugal, though politically unified, were geographically fragmented, with mountain barriers and poor internal transport. Industrial activity concentrated in a few districts, surrounded by agrarian economies that had little connection with them.

In northern Italy, industry grew out of silk, which was central to the Lombard and Piedmontese economies. Peasant households raised silkworms on mulberry leaves, and reeling and throwing of silk thread were carried out in workshops and increasingly in mechanized steam powered establishments, with the silk exported to France, Britain, and Switzerland. Cotton and woolen manufacture developed in Lombardy and Piedmont. Piedmont, under the Kingdom of Sardinia, saw government investment under ministers including Camillo Cavour in railways, transport infrastructure, and the port of Genoa. Cavour also promoted a major irrigation canal, but the canal named for him was constructed in 1863 to 1866, after his death in 1861, under the new Kingdom of Italy. Genoa's Ansaldo works, established in the 1850s, built locomotives and engines. Liguria combined shipbuilding with commerce. Railway construction began with the Naples to Portici line in 1839 and Lombardo-Venetian lines in the 1840s, and Piedmont's network expanded in the 1850s. The south had some early industrial initiatives, such as the Pietrarsa works near Naples where locomotives were made, but these remained isolated, and a large share of southern Italian agriculture was organized on large estates and small peasant plots with limited capital investment. The unification of 1861 created a national market and a large common tariff area, whose effects on regional development are debated, but unification itself belongs to Chapter 100.

In Spain, industrial development concentrated in Catalonia, where textile manufacturing grew from the late eighteenth century. Barcelona and surrounding towns mechanized cotton spinning, and the Bonaplata enterprise, organized in 1831 and reorganized in 1832, brought a steam powered textile factory into operation in 1833; the works were burned by a crowd in 1835 amid urban unrest. Textile firms in Catalonia relied on imported raw cotton and coal, which was expensive, and on protective tariffs, which Catalan manufacturers vigorously defended. In the north, the Basque Country had a long tradition of iron production and shipping, and from the 1860s and 1870s Bilbao's high quality iron ore, particularly well suited to Bessemer steelmaking, was exported in large volumes to Britain, creating wealth for Basque mine owners and shippers and later financing local ironworks. Asturias had coal but limited transport, and the mercury mines of Almadén and the lead and copper mines of the south, including Río Tinto, attracted foreign capital. The disentailment of church and municipal lands in the 1830s and 1850s changed property relations and stimulated railway investment, but often consolidated landholding and did not produce a broad domestic market. Railways, such as the Barcelona to Mataró line of 1848 and the Madrid to Aranjuez line of 1851, spread after the law of 1855, which encouraged foreign investors, particularly French companies. Heavy reliance on imported rails and equipment limited the industrial benefits.

Portugal's industrial development was more limited and concentrated around Lisbon and Porto, where textile, food, and small engineering enterprises developed. The first railway, from Lisbon to Carregado, opened in 1856 during the period of public works and financial reform associated with the minister Fontes Pereira de Melo, and the network grew with British and French capital. Portugal's economy remained largely agrarian, with significant wine exports and links to colonial territories.

Why did industry remain geographically confined? Historians point to several factors: mountainous terrain and poor internal transport, high costs of fuel in regions distant from coal, small and poor domestic markets in agrarian regions, agricultural structures such as latifundia and small peasant holdings with little commercialization, political instability and civil wars in Spain and Portugal, limited domestic capital, and reliance on foreign investment that sometimes extracted profits abroad. Tariffs supported the Catalan textile industry but raised costs for other consumers. The result was a pattern of industrial islands in an agrarian sea, a pattern that would continue for decades.

Everyday Life and Social History

Two Kinds of Household: Rural Production and the Emerging Industrial Community

Note: the households below are analytical composites reflecting patterns documented in regional studies, not accounts of particular named families.

Consider first a peasant household in the Lombard or Piedmontese countryside around the middle of the century. Its income came from several sources: grain and vegetables grown on rented or owned land, livestock, and the raising of silkworms. The silk season, when women and children gathered mulberry leaves, fed the worms, and handled cocoons in the household's own rooms, was a period of intense work that could yield cash essential for rent and taxes. Some women and girls then worked seasonally in silk reeling shops, often living away from home for part of the year. The household's rhythm followed the agricultural calendar, its security depended on weather and the price of cocoons, and its authority structure was shaped by the control that the male head exercised over land and by the claims of landlords or sharecropping contracts. Industrial change was present, but it came in the form of seasonal wage work and market dependence.

Consider next a family arriving in an industrializing district such as the textile towns of Catalonia, the Ruhr, or the Sambre–Meuse region. Work was divided differently: wages in cash, shifts regulated by factory or mine bells, children and women employed in spinning and ancillary tasks, and men in heavy labor or skilled trades. Housing was often crowded and sometimes provided by employers. Without the garden plots and village support networks of rural life, families relied on wage earnings, mutual aid societies, and neighbors, and were exposed to unemployment and market crises. These conditions, along with sanitation, disease, and labor organization, are examined in Chapter 95.

The comparison shows that industrialization changed the organization of time, work, and family income, but also that rural and industrial households were connected. Many industrial workers had rural origins and kept ties to the countryside, and rural families supplied seasonal labor and depended on industrial demand. The evidence for such households comes from household surveys, parish and civil registers, factory records, and reformers' reports, which tend to describe working class life from the viewpoint of outsiders, and for rural women's work especially, official records are often incomplete.

Section 94.17

Poland, the Russian Empire, and the Early Industrialization of Eastern Europe

Eastern Europe was not a region without industry, though its industrial development was geographically concentrated and limited in comparison with the west. The partitions of Poland in the late eighteenth century, discussed in Chapter 79, had divided the former Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth among Prussia, Austria, and Russia. After 1815 the central region became the Kingdom of Poland, with its own administration under the Russian tsar, while Poznania and Silesian lands remained under Prussia and Galicia under Austria. Industrial development in these territories therefore took place within three different imperial economic systems, and not within a Polish national economy.

Łódź offers the best known example of rapid industrial growth. In the 1820s the government of the Kingdom of Poland encouraged the settlement of weavers, many of them from German speaking regions, in what had been a small town, and Łódź grew as a center of woolen and then cotton textile manufacturing. Ludwik Geyer established a steam powered cotton spinning mill in the late 1830s, and industrialists such as Karl Scheibler, who built large mills in the following decades, helped make the city a major textile center. The abolition of the customs border between the Kingdom of Poland and the Russian Empire in 1851 gave its manufacturers access to the vast Russian market, and the city became closely tied to Russian demand. Its population was highly mixed, including Poles, Germans, Jews, and others, and ethnic and religious divisions shaped its social and economic life. The Dąbrowa coal basin and the iron industries in the Kingdom of Poland developed in this period, along with the railway from Warsaw to Vienna, opened in 1848.

Within Prussian Upper Silesia, as noted in Section 94.8, coal, iron, and zinc industries grew in the nineteenth century, often owned by great landowning families and managed by state educated engineers. Galicia in the Austrian monarchy remained poor and agrarian, though it contained oil and salt resources, and the Wieliczka salt mines, near Kraków, had been important for centuries. The Poznań region of Prussia had productive agriculture and grew modestly in processing industries. The political and national movements of Poles in these territories, including the uprisings of 1830 to 1831 and 1863, affected economic life, as the Russian authorities sometimes imposed restrictions and punitive measures.

In the Russian Empire's European provinces, industrialization before 1880 was uneven. Textile manufacturing grew in the Moscow region and in towns north and east, often organized around merchant entrepreneurs, many from Old Believer communities or from former serf families, and employed both free and serf workers. St. Petersburg developed metalworking and machine building. The Ural ironworks, which had been a leading world producer in the eighteenth century, relied on charcoal and on bonded labor, and faced increasing competition and technological stagnation in the nineteenth century. Ukraine's sugar beet industry expanded from the 1830s, and the Donets coal basin began to be developed on a larger scale only in the 1860s and 1870s, when the Welsh engineer John Hughes founded ironworks at what became Yuzovka. Railways began with the short line to Tsarskoye Selo in 1837, followed by the St. Petersburg to Moscow railway, completed in 1851, and the construction boom of the 1860s and 1870s, much of it financed with foreign capital and supported by state guarantees.

Serfdom was central to the economic debate. Before its abolition, landlords controlled the labor of millions of peasants, which limited mobility, restricted the growth of a free labor market, and discouraged investment in agricultural productivity, though some serf owners also established manufacturing enterprises on their estates. The emancipation manifesto of 19 February 1861 (Old Style, equivalent to 3 March 1861 in the Gregorian calendar), part of the Great Reforms of Alexander II, freed the serfs but required peasants to pay redemption payments for land and kept many within village communes. The consequences were mixed: it created legal conditions for a more mobile labor force and a more commercial agriculture, but peasants often remained burdened by debt and limited land. Historians disagree about how fundamentally emancipation changed industrial development in the following decades.

Between about 1860 and 1880, the Russian state promoted railways, relaxed some restrictions, and tolerated foreign investment, but industrial output per head remained low in comparison with western Europe. The rapid acceleration of Russian industrialization associated with the 1890s and the policies of Sergei Witte lies outside this chapter and should be understood only in brief perspective here, as a later phase that built on earlier developments. The relationship between Russian history and the broader European system is treated in this textbook where they intersect, and a separate Russian history textbook covers the subject more fully.

Section 94.18

Southeastern Europe and the Limits of Industrial Development

Southeastern Europe in the nineteenth century included the European provinces of the Ottoman Empire, the principalities of Wallachia and Moldavia, which united to form the basis of Romania in 1859 and 1862, Serbia, which gained autonomy within the Ottoman Empire after the revolts of the early century, Greece, an independent kingdom from 1832, Bulgarian lands under Ottoman rule until 1878, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Habsburg territories such as Croatia and Dalmatia. The region was diverse in religion, language, landholding, and political status, and its experience of the nineteenth century economy cannot be summarized in a single formula. It is also important to avoid depicting the region as economically inactive.

Agriculture and trade dominated. Wallachia and Moldavia became major grain exporters after the Treaty of Adrianople of 1829 removed Ottoman restrictions on their trade, and Danube ports such as Brăila and Galați handled growing shipments of wheat and maize to Britain and western Europe. The European Commission of the Danube, created after the Crimean War in 1856, improved navigation at the river's mouth. Serbia exported livestock, particularly pigs, and plums. Bulgarian lands supplied cloth, wool, tobacco, and other goods, and a rising commercial class of merchants and artisans emerged. The Ottoman government signed commercial treaties with Britain (1838) and other powers that lowered tariffs and opened markets to European imports, which brought cheap manufactured goods and damaged some local crafts while supporting commodity exports. The Ottoman economy was thus increasingly integrated into European commercial and financial systems, as Chapter 79 and later chapters discuss, but on terms that tended to favor raw material exports and manufactured imports.

Some manufacturing developed. In the Bulgarian lands, a woolen cloth factory established at Sliven in 1834 is often cited as an early example of mechanized production, and the town of Gabrovo became known for textiles and crafts. Greece developed shipping and a small number of workshops and mills, with the island port of Syros (Ermoupoli) becoming a commercial and manufacturing center. Bosnia, under Ottoman rule until 1878, had mining and handicraft production. In general, however, mechanized factory industry remained limited, and most manufacturing was done by artisans and households. The reasons included small and poor domestic markets, limited internal transport, scarce capital, competition from imported goods, political instability, and, in the Ottoman territories, financial pressure from state debts.

Transport projects were often built with foreign capital and engineers and aimed at moving commodities. A railway linking the Danube at Cernavodă with the Black Sea port of Constanța opened in 1860, and a line from Rusçuk (Ruse) to Varna was completed in 1866, both built by British interests. Concessions for railways linking Constantinople to central Europe, associated with the financier Baron Maurice de Hirsch from 1869, would extend the Oriental Railways network, though many of these lines were completed after 1870. The Ottoman state borrowed heavily from European lenders, and the financial crisis of 1875 led to default and, in 1881, the establishment of the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, giving European creditors control over certain revenues. These arrangements were part of a broader pattern in which European financial and commercial power shaped the economies of the periphery, a term that should here be used descriptively and not as a civilizational ranking.

Political independence did not automatically produce industrial transformation. Serbia, Greece, and Romania, as new or autonomous states, faced the costs of building administrations and armies, depended on agricultural exports, and had weak domestic capital. Land reform and peasant landholding patterns, which varied, affected rural society: in Serbia, small peasant property predominated, while in Romania, large estates and a large peasantry bound by obligations shaped the agrarian regime until reforms in 1864. State policies of protection and industrial promotion appeared later in the century. The experience of southeastern Europe therefore shows the limits of industrial diffusion, but also its variety: these societies were not static, and their commercial, agricultural, and urban development was considerable, even though mechanized heavy industry remained marginal before 1880.

Several patterns emerge. In absolute terms, Britain remained far ahead in coal and iron production in 1870, even though continental output had risen substantially. Belgium's per capita output of coal and iron was exceptionally high relative to its size. The German lands overtook France in coal during the 1850s and 1860s, largely because of the rapid growth of the Ruhr and Silesia. Railway length shows a different pattern: France and Germany both built extensive networks by 1870, while Spain and Italy built many kilometers later and Russia's network grew rapidly in the 1860s. Railways, as Section 94.9 noted, could be built with imported capital and materials and so do not map perfectly onto domestic industrial capacity.

Per capita measures of industrial development complicate the picture. Paul Bairoch's widely cited estimates of per capita industrialization, which index industrial output per head with Britain in 1900 equal to 100, placed Britain well ahead around 1860, with Belgium and Switzerland next, France and Germany at substantially lower levels, and Austria-Hungary, Italy, Spain, Sweden, and Russia lower still. Such estimates are reconstructions from limited data, and other economic historians have proposed different figures, especially for the early nineteenth century, so they are best used to show relative magnitudes and not exact values. Employment evidence points the same way: by around 1870, the share of the labor force working in agriculture probably still approached or exceeded half in France and Germany, remained higher in Italy and Austria-Hungary, and exceeded two thirds or more in Russia, the Balkans, and much of eastern Europe, according to rounded modern estimates, while Britain and Belgium were much more industrialized in occupational structure.

The statistics also conceal important realities. National averages hide sharp regional contrasts: Silesia versus East Prussia, Lombardy versus Calabria, Bohemia versus Galicia. Household and workshop production, much of it by women and children and often seasonal, is poorly captured by censuses and trade statistics. Productivity, the output per worker or per unit of capital, is harder to estimate than output, and growth in total output can come from adding workers as well as from technical improvement. Price changes, differing definitions of industry, and boundary changes all affect comparisons. Historians therefore combine statistics with qualitative evidence from company records, travel accounts, and local studies. The comparison above is a means of showing relative scale and not of ranking societies by merit.

Rapid industrial transformation in some districts coexisted with the persistence of agrarian life elsewhere. Even in the leading regions, much of the population worked in agriculture or traditional crafts. In the Ruhr or the Sambre–Meuse valley, mining and metallurgy altered the landscape and social life within a few decades, but a few hours' journey away peasants might still farm with methods little changed. This coexistence is itself one of the main characteristics of nineteenth century European industrialization, not a failure of it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Mechanized and Nonmechanized Production, c. 1815 to c. 1880

What Changed

  • Steam power, coke smelting, and machine building became established in Belgium, northern France, the Rhineland and Ruhr, Saxony, Silesia, and parts of central Europe
  • Railways created national and transnational transport networks and large demand for iron, coal, and engineering
  • Banks, joint stock companies, and technical schools developed to support large scale enterprise
  • Mechanized spinning and, later, weaving displaced much household textile production in affected regions

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture remained the largest source of employment in nearly every European country
  • Artisanal workshops, putting out systems, and household production continued in textiles, clothing, metalwares, and luxury goods, particularly in France, Switzerland, and the Habsburg lands
  • Charcoal iron, waterpower, and horse drawn transport persisted alongside coke, steam, and rail in many regions
  • Regional differences in wealth and development were not eliminated and often widened

Who experienced change most directly: Miners, ironworkers, factory operatives, railway builders, and migrants to industrial districts experienced the most direct transformation, as did handloom weavers and other artisans whose trades were displaced. Who experienced greater continuity: Most peasants, small farmers, and artisans in rural and southern and eastern regions saw gradual change in methods and markets, though many were affected indirectly by railways, prices, and taxation.

Why the pattern matters: It cautions against imagining a clean break between a traditional and an industrial Europe. For decades the two coexisted, and the persistence of older forms was part of how industrial economies functioned, supplying labor, materials, and markets.

Section 94.20

The Uneven Industrialization of Europe: Comparative Outcomes and Historical Consequences

The preceding sections allow a comparative synthesis. Europe's industrial trajectories differed because of the interaction of several factors rather than because of any single cause. Resource endowments mattered: coal and iron near transport shaped the Sambre–Meuse basin, the Ruhr, and Silesia, while fuel poor regions such as Switzerland and Catalonia relied on waterpower, imports, and specialization. Financial systems mattered: Belgium's mixed banks, France's investment banks, German joint stock banks, and the role of foreign capital in Spain, Italy, Russia, and the Ottoman territories shaped who could mobilize funds for large projects. Institutions mattered: the legal reforms of the revolutionary era, the abolition of serfdom, guild regulation, customs unions, and the Swiss constitution of 1848 shaped markets and labor. Government policy mattered, but its effects depended on administrative capacity and context. Agricultural structures mattered, since peasant landholding, large estates, and serfdom conditioned labor supply and consumer demand. Technology transfer mattered, as did access to markets and ports. Finally, global relationships mattered: Britain's trade, empire, and capital exports, as well as the imperial and commercial connections of the Dutch, French, and later others, shaped the conditions in which continental industries operated, as Chapter 73 and later chapters discuss.

Industrialization strengthened some regions and states more than others. Belgium's prosperity, the rise of the German lands as an industrial power, and the growth of industrial districts in France, Bohemia, Saxony, and elsewhere altered the economic weights of European states. The growing coal and iron output of the Ruhr and Silesia would later underpin German military and economic power, though no such outcome was determined in 1870. Industrial growth also intensified competition. Producers in different countries competed in each other's markets, and governments reconsidered tariffs and trade policy, as the changes of the 1870s would show. Railways and steamships knit regions together, lowered transport costs, and brought agricultural produce from the east and overseas to western markets, a development with consequences for landowners and farmers across Europe that later chapters trace.

The relationship between industrial and agrarian societies changed. Industrial districts needed food, raw materials, and labor from the countryside and sometimes from distant regions, and rural areas increasingly sold products to urban markets. Some agrarian regions, such as Hungary, Romania, and Ukraine, specialized in grain exports, tying their economies to western demand and price fluctuations. Others sent migrants to industrial towns or overseas. Within industrial states, tensions grew between landowners and manufacturers over tariffs and policy. Between states, the differences in economic development reinforced inequalities in power and resources, shaping the diplomatic and military balance examined in Chapters 106 and 107.

The organization of work changed too. Factories, mines, and railway construction gathered workers in large numbers under supervision, introducing disciplined schedules, wage labor, and, in many places, the employment of women and children. New industrial towns grew rapidly in the Ruhr, the Sambre–Meuse basin, Łódź, northern France, Saxony, and elsewhere, drawing migrants from the countryside. Alongside these, artisans and household workers continued in many trades. The social consequences, including housing, health, working conditions, and the beginnings of labor organization, are the subject of Chapter 95, and the political and ideological responses are examined in Chapters 96 to 98.

Cause and Effect

Why Industrialization Was Uneven across Europe

Long-term causes

Differences in coal, ore, and water resources; older traditions of rural manufacturing and commerce; agrarian structures such as serfdom or peasant proprietorship; the density of towns and transport networks; and the institutional legacies of states, guilds, and legal systems.

Short-term causes

The disruptions and reforms of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic era; the opening of British technology and capital after 1815; the timing of railways, customs unions, and banking institutions; and decisions of governments and entrepreneurs in specific decades.

Event or transformation

A pattern of industrial districts, ports, and rail corridors emerged across Europe, surrounded by large agrarian regions, with Belgium, northern France, the Rhineland, Saxony, Bohemia, and parts of Switzerland at the forefront.

Immediate effects

Rapid growth of mining, metallurgy, and engineering in some districts; expansion of railways and banks; displacement of some traditional crafts; growth of industrial towns; and growing trade among regions.

Long-term consequences

Persistent regional inequality; changes in the balance of economic and military power among states; intensified economic competition; and social and political questions about labor, welfare, and class, addressed in Chapters 95 to 98.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the outcome. Resource-rich regions failed to develop without capital or transport, resource-poor regions succeeded through trade and skill, and policy sometimes helped and sometimes hindered. The causes interacted differently in each place.

Historical complexity: Because the same factor, such as state intervention or foreign capital, could produce quite different results in different circumstances, historians generally explain European industrialization through combinations of conditions and not through a universal formula.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that industrialization spread across nineteenth century Europe through the transfer and adaptation of technologies, the expansion of transport networks, the mobilization of capital, institutional reform, state policy, and changing commercial relationships, but that it did not follow one universal pattern. Britain's breakthrough, examined in Chapter 93, provided machines, methods, and competitive pressure, yet continental societies encountered those models under very different conditions and developed their own industrial systems.

The setting after 1815 shaped the opportunities. The Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars had disrupted trade and destroyed capital but had also reformed laws, abolished many guilds and feudal obligations in annexed territories, and reorganized state boundaries. The Vienna settlement gave Prussia the Rhineland and Westphalia, united the Netherlands, and left the German lands and Italy politically divided, with internal customs barriers that raised the costs of trade. Agrarian structures differed sharply, from peasant proprietors in France and Belgium to persisting serfdom in the Habsburg and Russian lands.

Geography influenced where industrial districts arose. Coalfields in the Sambre–Meuse basin, the Ruhr, the Saar, Upper Silesia, and northern France, together with iron ore, rivers, canals, and ports, defined the principal heavy industrial zones. Waterpower, charcoal, and imported fuel supported industry elsewhere, as in Switzerland, Sweden, Alsace, and Catalonia. Resources were necessary in many cases but never sufficient: capital, transport, markets, institutions, and skills determined whether they were exploited.

Technology moved through machinery, patents, publications, official study missions, and, above all, people. British mechanics and entrepreneurs such as the Cockerill family, continental observers, and local workshops transmitted techniques, which were modified for local fuels, labor, and markets. By the 1860s continental engineering schools, exhibitions, and firms had created the capacity to maintain, improve, and sometimes lead technological development.

Belgium combined an inherited tradition of manufacturing, rich coal and iron resources, supportive banks, and an active state to become the first major continental industrial economy, though its experience included the distress of Flemish linen households. France followed a more gradual, regionally varied path in which workshops, rural production, and artisanal luxury goods persisted beside mechanized districts in the north, Alsace, Lyon, and Saint-Étienne. The Historical Debate on this subject showed why many historians now avoid describing France as simply slow. The German lands integrated markets through the Zollverein without political union, and rapid growth in the Ruhr, Saxony, Silesia, and the Rhineland, driven partly by railway demand, raised German coal and iron output above that of France by around 1870.

Railways, banks, state policies, and technical education were the systems that made expansion possible. Railways linked mines, factories, farms, and ports and stimulated demand for iron and engineering, while differences in ownership and finance reflected national institutions. Banks and joint stock companies mobilized capital beyond the resources of families, with Belgium's Société Générale, the Crédit Mobilier, and German joint stock banks as prominent examples. Governments shaped industry through tariffs, infrastructure, education, and law, and Friedrich List's arguments exemplified the contemporary debate about protection and catching up.

Other regions followed distinct pathways. The Habsburg monarchy contained industrial Bohemia, Moravia, and Lower Austria alongside the largely agrarian economies of Hungary and Galicia. Switzerland and the Netherlands succeeded through specialization, commerce, and skill without large domestic coal. The Nordic economies built on iron, timber, shipping, and agricultural export, with reforms and railways preparing later growth. Northern Italy, Catalonia, and the Basque Country became industrial islands within agrarian southern Europe, constrained by transport, markets, and capital.

In eastern and southeastern Europe, genuine industrial developments, notably at Łódź, in Upper Silesia, in Moscow and St. Petersburg, and in the beginnings of the Donets basin, coexisted with serfdom and its aftermath, limited markets, and heavy reliance on foreign capital. In the Balkans and Danubian lands, grain exports, commercial treaties, and foreign financed railways integrated the region into European trade, but mechanized manufacturing remained marginal before 1880, and political independence alone did not bring industrial transformation.

Quantitative evidence on coal, iron, railways, and employment confirms the unevenness while illustrating the limits of nineteenth century statistics. Britain retained a large lead around 1870, Belgium was highly industrialized for its size, Germany had overtaken France in coal, and most of Europe still employed a large share of its labor force in agriculture. Mechanized and traditional production coexisted for decades, and the persistence of older forms was part of how industrial economies functioned.

The consequences extended beyond production. Industrialization altered the economic weight of states and regions, intensified competition, expanded networks of transport and finance, and changed the relationship between agrarian and industrial societies. It created industrial towns and new forms of work that raised questions of housing, health, and labor, which Chapter 95 examines, and political and ideological questions that Chapters 96 to 98 take up. By about 1880, Europe was a continent increasingly connected by industrial production and yet deeply unequal in its economic development, a condition that would shape its politics, its empires, and its rivalries in the decades that followed.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Industrial diffusion
The spread of industrial technologies, methods, capital, and skills from one region to another, involving adaptation to local conditions and not simple copying.
Protoindustrialization
The widespread rural manufacture of goods, especially textiles, by households working for merchants, which preceded and in some regions contributed to factory industry.
Technology transfer
The movement of machines, techniques, and expertise across regions or borders, through trade, migration of skilled workers, publications, and official study missions.
Coke smelting
Iron smelting that uses coke, a fuel made from coal, in place of charcoal, allowing larger scale production where suitable coal was available.
Puddling
A process for converting pig iron into wrought iron by stirring it in a reverberatory furnace, widely adopted in the early nineteenth century.
Charcoal iron
Iron smelted with charcoal, which persisted in Sweden, the Urals, Styria, and parts of France because of abundant forests and the quality of the metal.
Joint stock company
A business financed by the sale of transferable shares to many investors, often with limited liability, which in many states required government authorization until the 1860s.
Investment bank (mixed bank)
A bank that raised funds through deposits and securities and used them to finance or hold shares in industrial and railway firms, as with the Crédit Mobilier and several German banks.
Société Générale des Pays Bas
A bank founded at Brussels in 1822 that lent heavily to mining and metallurgical enterprises in the territories that became Belgium.
Customs union
An agreement among states to abolish customs duties among themselves and adopt a common tariff toward outsiders.
Zollverein
The German customs union, effective from 1 January 1834 and led by Prussia, which integrated markets among many German states without political union.
Protectionism
The use of tariffs and other measures to shield domestic producers from foreign competition.
Infant industry argument
The claim, associated with Friedrich List, that new industries in a late developing country may deserve temporary protection until they can compete.
Free trade
A policy of minimal tariffs and restrictions on trade, advanced in Britain in the 1840s and widely adopted in a series of European commercial treaties in the 1860s.
Concession
A grant by a state allowing a private company to build or operate a railway or other enterprise on specified terms, as in French railway policy after 1842.
Polytechnic
An institution for advanced technical and scientific education, such as those at Prague, Vienna, Karlsruhe, and Zurich, that trained engineers and managers.
Factory system
A mode of production gathering workers and machines under one roof with supervised, regulated work, which spread unevenly across Europe.
Putting out system
An arrangement in which merchants supplied raw materials to households or small workshops and collected finished goods, common in textile regions such as Lyon and Flanders.
Vertical integration
The combination within one enterprise of successive stages of production, such as mines, furnaces, and machine shops at Seraing.
Economic backwardness
A concept associated with Alexander Gerschenkron, describing the relative position of later industrializing countries; its use is debated because it can imply a single path of development.
Serfdom
A system of legally bound agricultural labor and obligations to landlords, abolished at different times: in Prussia from 1807, in Habsburg Hungary in 1848, and in Russia in 1861.
Redemption payments
Payments owed by Russian peasants to the state, which compensated landlords, in return for land received at emancipation in 1861.
Cultivation System
A Dutch colonial arrangement in Java from 1830 requiring cultivators to grow export crops, providing revenue to the Dutch state.
Productivity
Output per worker or per unit of input, a measure harder to reconstruct historically than total output.
Regional inequality
Differences in income, industry, and infrastructure among areas of a single state or between states, a defining feature of nineteenth century industrialization.
Heavy industry
Industry based on large scale processing of materials, such as mining, iron and steel production, and heavy engineering.
Sambre–Meuse basin
The Belgian industrial corridor of coal, iron, glass, and zinc through Charleroi and Liège, the earliest great continental heavy industrial region.
Ruhr
The coal and iron region on the right bank of the lower Rhine whose output grew rapidly from the 1840s, becoming the core of German heavy industry.
Old Style and New Style
The Julian calendar used in Russia, twelve days behind the Gregorian calendar in the nineteenth century, so dates such as 19 February 1861 correspond to 3 March 1861.
Latifundia
Large landed estates, characteristic of parts of southern Italy and Iberia, often worked by tenants or laborers with limited capital investment.
Crédit Mobilier
A French investment bank founded in 1852 by the Pereire brothers that financed railways and industry and served as a model for similar institutions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it replaces a simple picture of European industrialization, in which one British model radiated outward, with a more accurate picture of interacting regional pathways. Seen across the continent, industrialization was a set of processes shaped by coalfields and rivers, by banks and railway companies, by customs unions and serfdom, and by the choices of governments, entrepreneurs, and workers. Its history cannot be told from the viewpoint of one country without distortion.

The chapter also shows how economic change connected regions that remained politically divided. The Zollverein linked German markets without political union, railways tied Belgian mines to Rhenish markets and Hungarian wheat to Viennese mills, and capital moved from Paris, Brussels, and London to Spanish railways and Danubian ports. These connections created interdependence, but they also created hierarchy, since some regions exported manufactures and capital while others exported grain and raw materials. The terms center and periphery are useful only as descriptions of these specific relationships, not as permanent rankings.

Later developments built on these foundations without being predetermined by them. The social consequences of industrial growth, including crowded towns, factory labor, and new forms of organization, are the subject of Chapter 95, and the ideological responses of liberalism, socialism, and nationalism fill the following chapters. The economic weight of the Ruhr, Silesia, and Bohemia would matter in the diplomacy and wars of the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries, as Chapters 100 and 101 and the volumes that follow explain, but no later outcome was inevitable in 1870.

For Volume IV, the chapter contributes a central argument: that the transformations of the age of Enlightenment, revolution, industry, and empire were uneven, contingent, and entangled. Industrial Europe in 1880 was a mosaic of mines, factories, workshops, and farms, connected by rail and credit and divided by wealth. Understanding that mosaic is essential for understanding the Europe that moved toward the imperial rivalries and mass politics of the decades before 1914.
