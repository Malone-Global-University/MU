Poland, the Fall of France, and the Battle of Britain

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between September 1939 and December 1940 the military map of Europe was redrawn faster than at any time since the Napoleonic era. Poland was defeated and partitioned, Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg and France were overrun, and Britain, alone among the major western belligerents, kept its armed forces in the field and its air defenses intact.

Shortly before dawn on 1 September 1939, the old German battleship Schleswig-Holstein, which had arrived at Danzig on a courtesy visit, opened fire on the Polish military transit depot at Westerplatte, a sandy peninsula at the entrance to the harbor. Within hours German ground forces crossed the Polish frontier along a line of more than a thousand miles, and German aircraft were over towns and railway junctions from Silesia to the Baltic. Chapter 116 explained why the diplomacy of the 1930s ended in this way. This chapter begins where that account stops, with soldiers, aircraft, ships and civilians, and asks a different kind of question: how was the war actually fought in its opening phase, and why did the outcome differ so sharply from one theater to the next?

The answers matter because the first fifteen months of World War II shaped everything that followed. Poland, a state of roughly thirty-five million people, was overrun in about five weeks by two invading powers, Germany from the west and north and the Soviet Union from the east, acting within the framework of the German-Soviet agreements of August 1939. In the spring of 1940 the German army defeated the combined forces of France, Britain, Belgium and the Netherlands in a campaign of roughly six weeks, a result that almost no contemporary observer in London, Paris or Berlin had confidently predicted. By midsummer the great continental power that had been regarded in 1914 as the strongest army in Europe had signed an armistice, a collaborationist regime was installed in the spa town of Vichy, and a little-known brigadier general was broadcasting from London in the name of a different France.

Yet the same year also produced a very different outcome. The German air force, which had contributed so much to the victories in Poland and France, failed over the English Channel and southern England to destroy the Royal Air Force or to establish the conditions required for an invasion. The explanation for that difference, as this chapter argues, cannot be found in the courage or character of the peoples involved. Polish soldiers fought hard against heavy odds, French soldiers fought hard in many sectors, and British survival rested not on a unique national spirit but on identifiable conditions: a sea barrier, a navy, an integrated air-defense system, a productive industrial base and access to the resources of an empire and, increasingly, of neutral and allied states.

The chapter therefore follows a governing argument. German success in 1939 and 1940 resulted from the interaction of careful operational planning, the concentration of mobile forces at a decisive point, effective communications, decentralized tactical initiative, close air and ground cooperation, and serious strategic errors on the Allied side. It did not demonstrate a universal German military superiority, and it did not reflect an unwillingness to fight on the part of the defeated. Equally, the failure of the Luftwaffe over Britain did not mean the defeat of Nazi Germany, which in the winter of 1940 dominated most of western and central Europe. The war had entered a new and unresolved phase.

Historians reconstruct these campaigns from an unusually rich body of evidence, but the evidence has distinctive limits. Operational orders, war diaries and staff studies from the German, French, British and Polish armies survive in large quantities, though many Polish records were destroyed or scattered, and some French records were lost in the collapse. Naval reports, such as those prepared for the evacuation from Dunkirk, and the records of RAF Fighter Command allow detailed reconstruction of the air and sea campaigns. At the same time, contemporary newspapers, official communiqués, parliamentary debates, memoirs and diaries often reveal how events were understood at the time, and they also show how quickly the memory of these campaigns hardened into national legend. The Dunkirk evacuation, the Battle of Britain and the fall of France were all remembered, in Britain and in France, through narratives that served postwar political purposes.

Civilian experience is as central to this chapter as military operations. The invasion of Poland brought, from its first days, the bombing of towns and the shooting of civilians. The campaign in the west set millions of people on the roads of Belgium and France in one of the largest civilian displacements in modern European history. The air war over Britain exposed civilian populations to bombardment for months. These experiences connect the military narrative to the social and political history of occupation, collaboration, resistance and genocide that follows in Chapters 120 and 121, and this chapter introduces the origins of that history without reproducing it.

Chapter 117 opens Part XVIII and Volume V's treatment of the Second World War in Europe. It is organized in five divisions and twenty numbered sections: the invasion and partition of Poland; the Phoney War and the campaigns in northern Europe; the western offensive and the evacuation from Dunkirk; the fall of France and the political transformation of western Europe; and the Battle of Britain and the Blitz. Chapter 118 will turn to the Mediterranean and Balkan wars, and Chapter 119 to the German invasion of the Soviet Union, the campaign that would ultimately determine the course of the war in Europe.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how German combined-arms operations, Polish strategic circumstances and the Soviet invasion of 17 September 1939 produced the defeat and partition of Poland.
  2. Distinguish the German and Soviet invasions of Poland, their operational objectives, their occupation zones and the methods of violence each employed against Polish civilians.
  3. Analyze why Britain and France did not launch a decisive offensive in autumn 1939, and assess the limits of the label Phoney War.
  4. Interpret the strategic importance of Norway and the consequences of the Norwegian campaign for Allied command and British political leadership.
  5. Trace the German offensive of 10 May 1940 from the Low Countries through the Ardennes and Sedan to the Channel coast, identifying how an operational penetration became a strategic breakthrough.
  6. Evaluate Operation Dynamo as both an operational success and a consequence of Allied defeat, including the contributions of French forces and civilian craft.
  7. Describe the exode of 1940 as a social history of military defeat, and explain how civilian displacement interacted with military operations.
  8. Compare the roles of the Franco-German armistice, Vichy France and Free France, distinguishing legal arrangements from political claims to legitimacy.
  9. Assess the interacting causes of the rapid French defeat while rejecting explanations based on national character, cowardice or a single decisive weapon.
  10. Explain how the Dowding System, German strategic choices, geography and production contributed to the survival of British resistance, and distinguish the Battle of Britain from the Blitz.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the German army defeat Poland and France so quickly, and how far does the term Blitzkrieg help or hinder historians who try to explain those victories?
  2. How did Polish strategic geography, alliance expectations and the Soviet invasion shape the options available to Polish commanders and civilians in September 1939?
  3. Why did the Allies fail to turn the autumn of 1939 and the winter of 1940 into an opportunity, and what did their planning assume about the next war?
  4. In what sense was the decisive German thrust of May 1940 a gamble, and which Allied decisions made that gamble succeed?
  5. How should historians weigh the evidence for the German halt order of 24 May 1940, given that the order is documented but its motives are disputed?
  6. What did military defeat mean differently for soldiers, refugees, governments, colonial administrators and exiles in France in June 1940?
  7. Why did the Luftwaffe fail to achieve air superiority over southern England, and why was that failure not equivalent to a German defeat in the war?
  8. How do the national legends of Dunkirk, the fall of France and the Battle of Britain differ from the evidence that historians can reconstruct from contemporary sources?

Historical Context

The war that began on 1 September 1939 arrived after two decades in which European governments had tried, with varying conviction, to prevent a repetition of 1914 to 1918. Chapter 113 examined the Versailles settlement and the new states of central and eastern Europe, including a restored Poland whose borders contained substantial German, Ukrainian, Belarusian and Jewish minorities, and whose access to the Baltic ran through a corridor that separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany. Chapter 116 traced the German annexations of Austria and the Czech lands, the failure of appeasement, the German-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact of 23 August 1939 with its secret protocol dividing eastern Europe into spheres of interest, and the British and French guarantees to Poland. Those events are taken as established here.

The military memory of the First World War shaped expectations in every capital. The Western Front of 1914 to 1918, discussed in Chapter 109, had been dominated by trenches, artillery and enormous casualties, and French planners in particular concluded that defensive firepower and fortification offered the best protection of a nation with a smaller population than Germany's. Britain's first priority was naval and imperial security, and its army remained small until conscription was introduced in April and May 1939. Germany, by contrast, had spent the 1930s rebuilding its armed forces under conditions of rapid expansion, and its officer corps had studied how to restore mobility to warfare by combining tanks, motorized infantry, artillery, engineers and aircraft under a doctrine that stressed speed and local initiative.

The balance of forces in 1939 and 1940 was more complicated than later memory suggests. Germany had the advantage in organization, in the quality of its radios and its air-ground liaison, and in the willingness of its commanders to concentrate armor at a single point. The French army, however, possessed numerous tanks, some of which were individually superior to German types in armor and gunpower, a large and modern artillery arm, and one of the largest armies in Europe. Poland had a large army, but its resources were limited by a weaker industrial base. Britain had the largest navy in Europe, a growing fighter force and a newly developed radar network. None of these facts guaranteed any particular outcome, and this chapter treats the campaigns as contingent events in which planning, command decisions, geography and chance all played parts.

The geography examined in Chapter 2 also shaped the campaigns. Poland's long frontiers, flat terrain and limited mountain barriers favored the attacker. The Ardennes, a wooded and hilly region of Luxembourg, Belgium and northern France crossed by few good roads, was widely regarded as a difficult obstacle for armored forces, a judgment that made it an attractive point of attack once German planners decided to exploit that assumption. The English Channel, only about twenty miles wide at the Strait of Dover, offered Britain a protection that Poland and France did not possess. That distinction is fundamental to the comparison this chapter makes between rapid defeat on the continent and successful defense across the sea.

Finally, the chapter relies on evidence that varies widely in kind and reliability. Official communiqués, parliamentary speeches, memoirs written after the war and the unit histories assembled by military institutions were all produced with audiences in mind, and some reflect the political needs of the later French and British states. The most valuable sources for the operational story are contemporary orders, war diaries, intelligence summaries, naval and RAF records and the testimony of participants recorded soon after events. For civilians, evidence comes from letters, diaries, press reports, relief organization papers, photographs, newsreels and the surveys of public opinion carried out in Britain by Mass-Observation and the Ministry of Information. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes contemporary evidence from later tradition, and documented facts from disputed interpretations.

The Invasion and Defeat of Poland

September to October 1939
Section 117.1

Europe at War: Military Preparations, Strategy, and the Polish Defense

By 1 September 1939 the diplomatic crisis described in Chapter 116 had left Poland with a difficult strategic position. Its western frontier with Germany, including the border with East Prussia and, after March 1939, the border with German-occupied Czech lands and the German client state of Slovakia, was exposed on three sides. The Polish Corridor, which gave Poland access to the Baltic coast and the new port of Gdynia, was a narrow salient surrounded by German territory on both flanks. The free city of Danzig, under League of Nations protection but dominated by a Nazi administration, lay at its mouth. From a military standpoint the Polish heartland, with its capital Warsaw only about a hundred and fifty miles from the German frontier, was open terrain with few natural barriers.

Polish planning rested on three assumptions. The first was that Britain and France, bound by guarantees of March and August 1939, would open a western front within a matter of weeks. The Franco-Polish military convention of May 1939 had promised French offensive action by about the fifteenth day of general mobilization, and Polish planners expected that German forces would be pulled westward in consequence. The second was that the Polish army would have to defend forward, close to the frontier, to protect the industrial and population centers of the western provinces. The third was that mobilization would have time to complete. Here events defeated the plan. A general mobilization ordered on 29 August was postponed under pressure from Britain and France, who feared that it would provoke Germany, and was reissued on 30 August. When the invasion came, many reserve units had not reached their assigned positions, and the railway system, essential to the movement of troops, was soon disrupted by air attack.

The balance of forces strongly favored Germany, though not so completely as popular memory sometimes suggests. Germany committed approximately 1.5 million soldiers to the campaign, including some fifty-odd divisions, six of them armored and several others motorized or light divisions, supported by more than a thousand combat aircraft of the Luftwaffe. Poland mobilized roughly a million men organized into some thirty infantry divisions and a number of cavalry brigades, with hundreds of tanks and tankettes and several hundred first-line aircraft. Many of the Polish armored vehicles were light types, and the Polish air force, whose fighters such as the P.11 had been outclassed by modern German aircraft, was heavily outnumbered. The Polish army possessed good artillery and anti-tank rifles, but fewer anti-tank guns than the planners wished, little in the way of mobile reserves and a command-and-communications network that depended heavily on fixed telephone lines.

The German plan, known as Case White (Fall Weiss), envisioned a double envelopment. Army Group North under General Fedor von Bock would attack from Pomerania and East Prussia, link up across the Corridor and drive toward Warsaw from the north. Army Group South under General Gerd von Rundstedt would attack from Silesia and Slovakia, with its main thrust of the Tenth Army directed toward Warsaw and the Vistula. The aim was to destroy the main Polish forces west of the Vistula before they could withdraw or reorganize. German units were organized for speed. Armored divisions, combining tanks, motorized infantry, artillery, engineers and reconnaissance, were designed to penetrate and exploit, while the much larger mass of the German army, which marched on foot and relied on horse-drawn transport, followed to consolidate and clear the ground. The Luftwaffe worked with both army groups, striking airfields, railway lines, concentration areas and the roads on which Polish reserves moved.

Historical Vocabulary
Blitzkrieg, Combined Arms, Mobilization, and Operational Tempo

Blitzkrieg (German, "lightning war") is a term widely used for the German campaigns of 1939 to 1941, in which armored and motorized formations supported by aircraft achieved rapid victories. It was used in the German and foreign press at the time, but it was not an official German doctrine, and the German army did not describe its own operational methods in this way in its regulations. Many historians therefore treat it as a useful descriptive label rather than a complete explanation, and some, emphasizing that the German army remained largely foot-borne and horse-drawn, consider it misleading if it suggests an army that was uniformly mechanized or that planned economic and strategic warfare around short wars.

Combined arms refers to the coordinated use of different types of military units, such as tanks, infantry, artillery, engineers and aircraft, so that the strengths of each compensate for the weaknesses of others. German success depended less on any single weapon than on the habit of integrating them.

Mobilization is the process of bringing an army from peacetime strength to wartime readiness by calling up reservists, assembling units, distributing equipment and moving forces to assigned positions. It required railways, schedules and time, and a state's decision to begin or delay it was itself a diplomatic act.

Operational tempo is the speed at which a military force makes decisions and carries out movements relative to its opponent. An army that acts faster than the enemy can respond may disrupt the enemy's command even if its numbers are not superior.

Why it matters: These terms provide the vocabulary for explaining German victories in Poland and France without resorting to myth, and the modern equivalents can mislead if applied without attention to the specific conditions of 1939 and 1940.

Section 117.2

Germany's Invasion: Combined Arms, Air Warfare, and Rapid Advance

The fighting began almost simultaneously at several points. At Westerplatte, after the bombardment from the Schleswig-Holstein, about two hundred Polish soldiers under Major Henryk Sucharski resisted German naval infantry and army assault forces for a week, holding out until 7 September and becoming a lasting symbol of Polish resistance. In Danzig itself, the Polish post office was defended by its employees for about fourteen hours before it fell. At dawn, German air units attacked airfields and railway lines across the country, and German ground forces advanced along the entire frontier. In Upper Silesia, the Polish defenders were quickly outflanked by the Fourteenth Army moving through Slovakia and the passes of the Carpathians. In the Corridor, the German Fourth Army from Pomerania and the Third Army from East Prussia pressed toward each other, and within days Polish forces in the Corridor were cut off.

The Luftwaffe's operations are among the most discussed features of the campaign. Its first mission targeted Polish airfields, but aircraft dispersed beforehand to auxiliary strips survived and continued fighting. Its second mission was to interdict Polish movement. Bombers and dive bombers attacked railway junctions, bridges, and the roads on which reserves moved, which contributed substantially to the failure of Polish mobilization. A third category of attack involved towns and civilians. On the morning of 1 September, German bombers attacked the town of Wieluń in central Poland, which had no significant military garrison, and the raid destroyed much of the town. Casualty estimates are disputed, though figures in the region of a thousand or more dead are frequently cited, and the bombing of Wieluń, which occurred before the attack on Warsaw, has been the subject of extensive Polish and German scholarship about whether it was a deliberate attack on a civilian target or a military operation directed at nominal military targets. Historians agree that large numbers of civilians were killed, and that German aircraft also strafed refugee columns on the roads.

On the ground the German army achieved rapid breakthroughs. The armored divisions of the Tenth Army, led by General Walter von Reichenau, advanced about fifty miles in two days, passing between Polish infantry divisions and reaching the Warta River within days. Polish forces held on in some sectors, such as at Mokra on 1 September, where the Wołyńska Cavalry Brigade, fighting dismounted with artillery and anti-tank support, inflicted heavy losses on the German 4th Panzer Division. At Krojanty on the same day, a squadron of the 18th Pomeranian Uhlans charged German infantry in an open field and was then attacked by German armored cars. This incident, distorted by German propaganda and later by retelling, gave rise to a lasting myth that Polish cavalry charged German tanks. The Polish cavalry brigades were in fact mobile infantry and mounted reconnaissance units, equipped with anti-tank rifles, light artillery and a few armored vehicles, who used horses for movement and generally dismounted to fight, and they did not charge tanks in any systematic way.

The Polish defense was weakened less by any shortage of courage than by the structure of its deployments and the speed of the advance. The Polish commander in chief, Marshal Edward Rydz-Śmigły, had few mobile reserves, and the telephone and radio networks on which he depended were disrupted by air attack. Within five days, on about 5 and 6 September, it became clear that the Polish plan of forward defense had failed, and the Polish high command ordered a general withdrawal toward the Vistula and the San, a movement made difficult by the fact that German armored formations were already moving between and behind the retreating columns. The Polish government left Warsaw on 5 September, and the high command left on 7 September, leaving a headquarters organization that became disconnected from many of its subordinate armies.

Section 117.3

Polish Resistance, the Soviet Invasion, and the September Campaign

The most important Polish counteroffensive began on 9 September on the Bzura River, west of Warsaw. The Poznań and Pomorze Armies, which had been left in the northwest by the German advance, were commanded by General Tadeusz Kutrzeba, who recognized that the German Eighth Army, advancing along the northern flank of the Tenth Army, was vulnerable to attack from the side. The Polish attack caught the German 30th Infantry Division and elements of the 24th by surprise, and within two days Polish troops had advanced some distance, taken thousands of prisoners and created a real threat to the German flank. The battle developed into the largest of the campaign, involving perhaps a quarter of a million Polish soldiers and a comparable number of Germans, and it forced the German high command to redirect forces, including armored divisions, toward the Bzura and to concentrate Luftwaffe attention on the Polish positions.

The German response was effective. By 12 September the Germans had brought up reinforcements and reorganized, and German aircraft attacked the Polish positions on the river and the Kampinos Forest to which the army was retreating. By 17 or 18 September the Polish forces were surrounded and under heavy air and artillery attack, and though some units broke through to Warsaw and the fortress of Modlin, the bulk of the two armies was destroyed or captured by 19 September.

Elsewhere, defenders held out in fortified places. Warsaw was bombarded from the air and by artillery, with German air attacks on 25 September involving hundreds of aircraft and incendiary bombs. The garrison, under General Juliusz Rómmel, and the civilian population, mobilized by the mayor Stefan Starzyński, defended the city for about three weeks, and substantial parts of the city were damaged or destroyed. The fortress of Modlin north of Warsaw held until 29 September. On the Hel Peninsula, a narrow strip of land extending into the Baltic, the naval garrison resisted until 2 October. The group of Polish forces known as the Independent Operational Group Polesie, commanded by General Franciszek Kleeberg, fought a series of engagements against German and Soviet forces and its last major battle, at Kock, was fought between 2 and 5 October, ending with Kleeberg's surrender on 6 October.

On 17 September 1939 the Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland. Troops of the Red Army's Belorussian and Ukrainian Fronts, perhaps 400,000 to more than 600,000 men in the first days, crossed the frontier along its entire length. The Soviet government justified the action by claiming that the Polish state had ceased to exist, that its government had abandoned the country, and that the Red Army was entering to protect the Ukrainian and Belarusian populations of eastern Poland. The justification was a political claim, not an accurate description of the situation, since the Polish government still existed and was in Polish territory until the night of 17 September, when it crossed into Romania. The invasion was the implementation of the secret protocol of the German-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact of 23 August 1939, which had provided for a division of spheres of interest in Poland and eastern Europe, and German and Soviet officials had been discussing the timing of the Soviet entry for some days.

The Soviet invasion had decisive consequences for the Polish command. The Polish plan, in the last resort, had envisaged a withdrawal to the southeast of the country, the so-called Romanian bridgehead, where forces might hold out until help came from the west. The Soviet invasion made that impossible. The order was interpreted and obeyed unevenly, and some units fought the Soviet forces, notably at Szack and Grodno, and in the defense of Lwów (Lviv), which surrendered to the Red Army on 22 September. Many Polish soldiers who surrendered to the Red Army were taken prisoner, and a number of them, as discussed in section 117.4, were later murdered.

The costs of the campaign were large. Polish military deaths are commonly estimated at roughly sixty to seventy thousand; wounded and prisoner totals differ by source and category. German deaths are generally estimated at roughly fourteen thousand, and Soviet military deaths at a much smaller number, as the Red Army met limited organized resistance. Civilian deaths during the campaign were very large, with estimates of the tens of thousands. The figures should be understood as scholarly estimates rather than documented counts, and the margins of uncertainty are considerable.

Section 117.4

Poland Partitioned: Occupation, Civilian Terror, and the Continuation of Resistance

On 28 September 1939, the German and Soviet governments signed in Moscow the Boundary and Friendship Treaty, which revised the line agreed in August. Germany relinquished its claim to Lithuania, which was assigned to the Soviet sphere, in exchange for a larger share of central Poland, including the province of Lublin. The new line ran approximately along the Narew, Bug and San rivers. The two powers had acted in a coordinated way, but they were distinct states with different ideologies, institutions and aims, and the occupation regimes they established require separate analysis.

Germany incorporated into the Reich the western provinces, in decrees of 8 and 12 October 1939, forming the Reichsgau Wartheland, a province of Danzig-West Prussia, and enlarging East Prussia and Silesia. German officials began expelling Polish and Jewish residents and settling ethnic Germans in annexed districts. The remaining German-occupied territory, including Warsaw, Kraków and Lublin, became a separate unit known as the General Government, under Hans Frank, formally established on 26 October. German authorities closed Polish schools above the elementary level, universities and cultural institutions, and the Polish language was suppressed in public life in the incorporated territories.

The violence in the first months was immense. During the invasion itself, German units, police and Einsatzgruppen murdered civilians and members of the Polish elite. In the autumn and winter of 1939 and 1940, German police and SS units carried out operations, sometimes under the names Tannenberg and later the AB-Aktion, directed against the Polish intelligentsia, clergy, officials, teachers, landowners and political leaders, with the aim of eliminating those who might lead resistance. Tens of thousands of Poles were killed in these actions, including at Piaśnica in Pomerania and at Palmiry near Warsaw. Polish Jews were targeted from the outset, with shootings, burning of synagogues, forced labor and humiliating public abuse. In autumn 1939 the Germans began to herd Jewish populations into restricted areas, an early step on the path toward the ghettos (the first major one, in Łódź, was sealed in the spring of 1940, and the Warsaw ghetto in the autumn). The USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia documents the escalation of this violence in the first weeks. These developments are introduced here to establish the origins of the occupation regime, and they are treated systematically in Chapters 120 and 121.

Soviet rule in the eastern territories proceeded by different methods. The NKVD arrested officials, officers, landowners, clergy and political opponents while Soviet authorities nationalized property and dismantled institutions. After the invasion, Soviet authorities organized elections to People's Assemblies on 22 October 1939, under conditions of coercion and with a single list of candidates, and these assemblies petitioned for incorporation into the Ukrainian and Belorussian Soviet republics, which the Supreme Soviet approved in early November. In four large waves of deportation, beginning in February 1940, hundreds of thousands of people, including Poles, Ukrainians, Jews and Belarusians, were deported to Siberia, Kazakhstan and the Soviet north, in conditions of severe hardship and with high mortality. Estimates of the total number of deportees vary widely, and the numbers have been revised as Soviet archives became available. Some local Ukrainian, Belarusian and Jewish communities welcomed the arrival of Soviet troops, for reasons of hostility to the Polish state or fear of the Germans, and many later found that Soviet rule brought its own repression.

Among the Soviet actions, the Katyn massacres are the best known. On 5 March 1940, the Soviet Politburo approved a proposal of the NKVD chief, Lavrentii Beria, to execute Polish prisoners held in camps and prisons, including army and police officers and members of the civil service, and in April and May about twenty-two thousand people were shot, at Katyn forest near Smolensk, at Kalinin (Tver) and at Kharkiv, and buried in mass graves. Soviet authorities denied responsibility for decades and attributed the killings to Germany, which discovered the graves at Katyn in 1943. The Soviet government acknowledged responsibility in 1990, and documents released in 1992 confirmed the order. The precise number of victims is usually given as about 21,800 to 22,000, a figure drawn from the documents, but counts of individual sites vary slightly.

Polish political authority continued abroad. The recognized government-in-exile under Władysław Raczkiewicz and Władysław Sikorski organized armed forces in France and, later, Britain. In Poland itself an underground began to form almost at once. The Service for Poland's Victory (Służba Zwycięstwu Polski) was established in late September 1939 in besieged Warsaw, and in the following months it was succeeded by the Union of Armed Struggle and later the Home Army, which is discussed in Chapter 120. The Polish state, therefore, was defeated in the field in 1939, partitioned, and subjected to terror, but it did not surrender, and its continuation in exile and underground was a political fact of the war.

The Phoney War and the Northern European Campaigns

September 1939 to May 1940
Section 117.5

Britain and France Enter the War: The Phoney War

On 3 September 1939 the British government, whose ultimatum to Germany had expired at eleven in the morning, declared war, and France followed that afternoon. The declarations were serious acts, and both governments mobilized, but they did not produce immediate military assistance to Poland. The British Expeditionary Force began to cross to France almost at once, and by the end of October about 150,000 British soldiers had been landed. The Dominions followed Britain at different speeds, as section 117.17 explains, and Britain and France thus began the war as the leaders of a coalition whose military strength lay largely in the future.

The only substantial Allied land operation in the west in 1939 was the Saar offensive. Beginning on 7 September, French forces advanced a few miles into the Saar region against light opposition, occupied a number of abandoned villages and then halted. By about 12 September the offensive had stopped, and in October the French forces withdrew to their starting positions. The French commander in chief, General Maurice Gamelin, had promised the Poles an offensive in about fifteen days. The Saar operation was far smaller than that, and it was not intended to engage the main German defenses. The Germans in the west, with only about thirty-odd divisions, mostly of lower quality, were thinly stretched, and some German generals later argued that a determined French attack in September might have had serious consequences.

The expression Phoney War, or in French drôle de guerre and in German Sitzkrieg, describes the months of limited land fighting that followed, from September 1939 to May 1940. It was coined by contemporaries, and it captures a real sense that the war had not yet begun in earnest in the west, with soldiers digging in, civilians who had been evacuated drifting back to the cities, and newspapers reporting little. But the label has serious limits. At sea the war was immediate. The sinking of the aircraft carrier Courageous on 17 September, the sinking of the battleship Royal Oak at Scapa Flow on 14 October by a German submarine, and the Battle of the River Plate in December, which ended with the scuttling of the German pocket battleship Admiral Graf Spee off Montevideo on 17 December, were serious engagements. Merchant ships were sunk in large numbers by submarines and by mines, and the British blockade began to affect German supplies. Mobilization of industry and manpower also continued on all sides, and for Poland, Czechoslovakia, and the Jewish communities under German rule, the war was already one of occupation and persecution.

Why did Britain and France not attack in force in the autumn of 1939? The question has several parts. The first concerns doctrine. French military thought, shaped by the memory of 1914 to 1918, held that an attack against prepared positions without overwhelming artillery and a large reserve of munitions would lead to heavy casualties. The French army was organized for a methodical, centrally controlled battle, in which infantry advanced under the cover of artillery fire planned in advance. The second concerns mobilization and readiness. France mobilized about five million men, which removed skilled workers from industry and slowed production, and the British army was only beginning to build its large forces. The third concerns strategic assessment. Allied leaders expected a long war that would be won by blockade, the build-up of production and the use of Allied economic strength, and they believed that time was on their side. They did not foresee that Germany, which also needed time, might use it to launch a decisive offensive. The Allied governments also did not wish to bomb German cities, in part from fear of retaliation against their own, and in part because they did not want to alienate neutral opinion, particularly in the United States.

Section 117.6

Defensive Strategy, the Maginot Line, and Allied Military Planning

French strategy in 1939 rested on a clear logic. The French population, of about forty million, was smaller than that of Germany, which, after the annexation of Austria and the Sudeten territories, had nearly eighty million. French casualties in the First World War, of about 1.4 million dead, had left a heavy demographic burden, and French leaders were determined to avoid a repetition of the slaughter of 1914 to 1918. French military doctrine, known as the "methodical battle," emphasized firepower, centralized control and the careful preparation of each operation.

The Maginot Line, named after the minister of war André Maginot, was constructed between about 1929 and 1938, along the frontier with Germany and, in a lighter form, with Italy. Its most elaborate sections, composed of large underground fortresses (ouvrages) with artillery, barracks, railways and ventilation systems, protected the industrial regions of Alsace and Lorraine. The Line's purpose was not to provide an impenetrable wall. It was intended to allow the French army to economize forces on part of the frontier, to protect Alsace and Lorraine from a surprise attack, to give time for mobilization, and to channel any German attack toward Belgium, where the French and British armies would meet it. It performed its functions, and the Germans never seriously attempted to storm it in 1940.

Allied command structure was complicated. Gamelin, the commander in chief of French land forces and nominally of Allied forces in France, directed the war from a headquarters at Vincennes, outside Paris, at a deliberate distance from the field. Below him, General Alphonse Georges commanded the northeastern front, and General Gaston Billotte coordinated the army group that would advance into Belgium. The British Expeditionary Force under General Lord Gort, which numbered ten divisions by May 1940, operated under Gamelin's strategic direction but reported to the British government. Gort had a right of appeal to London on matters affecting the safety of his army. The Belgian army, which had withdrawn into neutrality in 1936 and refused staff talks with the French and British, was not integrated into the Allied plan, and the Dutch army was neutral.

The Allied operational plan, completed in the autumn of 1939 and known as the Dyle Plan, envisaged that if Germany attacked through Belgium, the best French and British units, including the French First Army, the BEF and the French Seventh Army, would advance into Belgium and take positions along the Dyle River, east of Brussels, to meet the German advance, shortening the front and keeping the fighting away from French industrial regions. The Seventh Army under General Henri Giraud, on the left flank, was in March extended to move to Breda in the Netherlands to link with the Dutch, a variant that drew the best French mobile reserves farther north and left the center, at the hinge of the Meuse, in the hands of weaker formations. This choice reflected a reasonable political and strategic calculation, namely to keep Belgium and the Netherlands in the fight and to defend a shorter line. It also meant committing Allied forces northward to a position in which they would be exposed if the center was broken.

The strengths and weaknesses of the French army deserve careful assessment. In numbers and in the quality of some equipment, France was not weak. It had about 3,000 tanks of the types in service in 1940, among them the heavily armored Char B1 bis and the fast and well-armed Somua S35, which were individually superior to most German tanks in armor and in the power of their guns. It had large artillery, good anti-tank guns, and a respectable air force of which the modern fighter types were arriving in increasing numbers. But the French armor was mostly dispersed in infantry-support units and in light mechanized divisions, with only a few armored divisions that had been formed late. Many French tanks lacked radios, so that tank commanders could not receive orders quickly in a fluid battle. French communications relied on the civilian telephone network and on written messages carried by couriers, a system that functioned in a slow-moving battle and failed in a rapid one. Allied air forces were smaller than the Luftwaffe and too thinly spread.

Section 117.7

Denmark and Norway: The Northern War, April to June 1940

Scandinavia mattered to both sides for economic and naval reasons. Swedish iron ore traveled through the Norwegian port of Narvik when Baltic winter ice restricted shipping, and Germany depended on this maritime route. For the Allies, interrupting this traffic was an attractive possibility. For the German navy, Norway offered ports and airfields that would free it from the confinement of the North Sea and give access to the Atlantic, and the German naval commander, Admiral Erich Raeder, urged Hitler in the winter of 1939 to 1940 to consider occupying Norwegian bases before the British did.

The Soviet-Finnish Winter War provided context. The Soviet Union attacked Finland on 30 November 1939, after Finland rejected Soviet demands for territorial adjustments around Leningrad, and the Finnish army, much smaller than the Red Army, resisted with unexpected effectiveness through the winter, inflicting heavy losses before the Soviet forces reorganized and broke through the Mannerheim Line in February and March 1940. In the Moscow Peace Treaty of 12 March 1940, which took effect on 13 March, Finland ceded the Karelian Isthmus, the city of Viipuri (Vyborg) and other territory but kept its independence. In Britain and France the war aroused sympathy for Finland, and the Allies prepared an expeditionary force, partly intended to help Finland and partly to occupy the Norwegian and Swedish ore fields on the way. The episode is relevant here because it shows how Allied strategy in the north was driven by economic and political calculations and was prepared with limited attention to the military difficulties.

In early April, British ships began laying mines in Norwegian waters, and on 9 April German forces, which had been put to sea some days before, landed at points along the entire Norwegian coast, from Narvik in the north to Oslo, Kristiansand and Bergen in the south, as well as in Denmark. Operation Weserübung was a bold combined operation, involving the navy, the army, paratroops and the Luftwaffe. Norway fought. In Oslofjord, the old fortress of Oscarsborg sank the heavy cruiser Blücher, delaying the German landing at Oslo long enough for the Norwegian royal family, the government and the gold reserves to escape north. The Norwegian government, led by King Haakon VII and refusing the German demand for capitulation and the establishment of a puppet government under Vidkun Quisling, ordered resistance.

The campaign unfolded over the following two months. British and French expeditions landed at Namsos and Åndalsnes, but lacked adequate air cover and withdrew from central Norway. German forces took the main ports and airfields in the first days, with the aid of the Luftwaffe, which dominated the air. In the north, at Narvik, the battle took a different course. British destroyers, in two actions on 10 and 13 April, destroyed the German destroyers that had carried the landing force, which left the German troops, under General Eduard Dietl, isolated. An Allied force of British, French, Polish and Norwegian troops, under the command of General Pierse Mackesy and later General Claude Auchinleck and Lord Cork, besieged Narvik and recaptured the town on 28 May. But by then the German offensive in the west had begun, and the Allies decided to evacuate the whole of northern Norway. The last Allied troops left between 4 and 8 June, King Haakon and the government sailed to Britain on 7 June, and the Norwegian armed forces on the mainland ceased fighting on 10 June.

The campaign had large consequences. The failed expedition contributed to the Commons debate on May 7–8, undermining Chamberlain and precipitating Churchill's coalition government. The Kriegsmarine suffered heavy losses, including the Blücher, the light cruisers Königsberg and Karlsruhe, and ten destroyers at Narvik, which left it unable to support an invasion of Britain in the summer. Germany gained airfields, naval bases and security for its ore supply, and it incurred the occupation of a country that was never reconciled. In Britain, the failure of the Norwegian expedition produced a political crisis. Chamberlain resigned on 10 May, the day that Germany attacked in the west, and Winston Churchill, who had been First Lord of the Admiralty and had been closely associated with the Norwegian operation, replaced him. Historians note the irony, but also emphasize that Churchill's rise rested on a broader political shift. The RAF's official campaign histories provide useful evidence of the air dimension of the campaign, in which naval aviation and the lack of airfields played a central role.

Section 117.8

Germany and the Western Allies on the Eve of the May Offensive

The German plan for the western offensive was not originally the one that was executed. Case Yellow (Fall Gelb), as issued in the autumn of 1939 by the Army High Command under General Walther von Brauchitsch and his chief of staff General Franz Halder, called for a main attack by Army Group B through Belgium and the Netherlands, in a version of the earlier Schlieffen Plan, with a supporting role for Army Group A to the south. This plan promised a hard fight in country that the Allies were prepared to defend, and several senior German officers doubted that it could produce a decisive victory. It would, they feared, push the Allies back without destroying them.

A different conception grew in the winter in the headquarters of Army Group A, whose chief of staff was General Erich von Manstein. Manstein proposed shifting the main weight of the attack to Army Group A, which would advance through the Ardennes and across the Meuse and then turn northwest toward the Channel, cutting off the Allied armies that had advanced into Belgium. The proposal was developed in several memoranda, and it was resisted by the Army High Command, which transferred Manstein to command an infantry corps in January 1940. It gained ground for other reasons. On 10 January, a German aircraft carrying an officer with parts of the original plan made a forced landing in Belgium, the Mechelen incident, and it became clear that the plan had been compromised. Hitler, who had been thinking along similar lines, and who was attracted by the audacity of the idea, became its principal supporter, and after meeting Manstein on 17 February he ordered that the plan be revised. The revised plan, completed in March, is often called the Sichelschnitt, or sickle cut, though the name is usually applied retrospectively.

The revised plan was a gamble. Army Group A, under Rundstedt, would contain seven of the ten German armored divisions, and these would be concentrated under a single armored command, Panzer Group Kleist, which was to move through the Ardennes in four or five days, cross the Meuse and move to the sea. The German force would have its flanks exposed during the advance to the Channel. And Army Group A would be moving into a region where French forces, though of lower quality, were not absent. Some German generals, including Rundstedt and Halder, remained uneasy. The plan therefore depended on speed, on the assumption that the French would respond slowly and on the successful crossing of a major river.

The Allied intelligence picture was incomplete and the Allied command was divided in its estimate of the German intentions. French intelligence had indications of German activity in the Ardennes, and some officers expressed concern, but French doctrine held that the region was unsuitable for a large armored attack, since any advance through it would be slow, and that the French would have time to bring up reserves to meet a German crossing of the Meuse, which, according to French planning, would need several days of preparation for an attack with massed artillery. This assumption proved to be critical. The Allied defense of the Ardennes was thin relative to the threat, but it was not nonexistent. French cavalry and Belgian Chasseurs Ardennais fought delaying actions in the forest, and the defending divisions on the Meuse were in position, though they were inferior in training, in equipment and in numbers of anti-tank guns and anti-aircraft guns to the forces that would hit them.

Historical Vocabulary
Maginot Line, Dyle Plan, Strategic Reserve, and Operational Breakthrough

The Maginot Line was the system of fortifications built by France along its border with Germany in the 1930s. It is often misrepresented as a continuous wall in which French commanders placed total trust. In fact it was a sophisticated but incomplete system of fortresses meant to economize forces, protect Alsace and Lorraine and direct a German attack to the north. Its failure to prevent the German victory in 1940 reflects the choice made by the Germans to bypass it, not a failure of the fortifications themselves.

The Dyle Plan was the Allied scheme to advance into Belgium to the Dyle River line, east of Brussels, if Germany attacked through the Low Countries. It was meant to meet the Germans far forward and to bring Belgium into the Allied defense, but it committed Allied mobile forces to the north.

A strategic reserve is a force held back from the front to respond to emergencies, counterattack or exploit success. In May 1940 the French had committed most of their mobile reserves to the Dyle and Breda moves, which left little available to meet a breakthrough in the center.

An operational breakthrough is a penetration of the enemy front that disrupts the defender's ability to maintain a coherent line, usually followed by the movement of mobile forces into the rear. Distinguished from a tactical gain, it becomes strategic when it allows the attacker to achieve the aims of the campaign, as at Sedan.

Why it matters: These four terms capture the interaction of Allied plan and German gamble, and each is easily misunderstood in popular memory.

The German Western Offensive and Dunkirk

10 May to 4 June 1940
Section 117.9

The Invasions of the Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg

At about 5:30 on the morning of 10 May 1940, German aircraft struck airfields in the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg and France, and German ground forces crossed the frontiers. Luxembourg, which had no army to speak of, was overrun within hours, and its grand ducal family and government left for exile. In the Netherlands, Army Group B under General Fedor von Bock attacked with the Eighteenth Army, supported by airborne troops. Paratroops and air-landing infantry were dropped near The Hague, Rotterdam and the bridges at Moerdijk and Dordrecht, with the aim of seizing the main crossings and the government. But the bridges were seized and held, and Dutch defenses were fatally disrupted.

In Belgium, the attack opened with an operation that demonstrated German tactical innovation. German glider troops landed on Fort Eben-Emael and disabled its artillery with hollow-charge explosives, enabling the seizure of key canal crossings. The fortress of Eben-Emael, near the Albert Canal, was considered one of the strongest in Europe and commanded the crossings of the canal near Maastricht. The bridges across the canal were taken, in several cases intact, and the Belgian line was turned. The success of the operation relied on surprise, on training and on a new weapon, and not on overwhelming force. It had an effect on morale out of proportion to its military significance, but it also compromised the Belgian defense in the north.

The Allied response was the immediate activation of the Dyle Plan. Beginning on the morning of 10 May, French and British forces moved into Belgium, and by 12 to 14 May they had taken up positions along the Dyle. The French Seventh Army, with some of the best French mobile formations, moved to the north, to Breda, and was caught up in the fighting in the Netherlands. On 13 May it had to withdraw, having failed to link with the Dutch. The attack in the north was a feint in the strategic sense, intended to draw Allied forces northward and to keep their attention fixed there, while the decisive blow was prepared in the Ardennes, though the northern attacks were genuine operations, and the Germans did not regard them as secondary in the sense that their losses did not matter.

The Dutch campaign ended abruptly. Queen Wilhelmina and the government left for London on 13 May, and the Dutch army, though it fought well in places, was short of aircraft, armor and heavy weapons and had no strategic depth. On 14 May the German command, to break the resistance in Rotterdam and to link its airborne troops with the Panzer division approaching from the south, demanded the city's surrender and sent bombers while negotiations were under way. The bombing began in the afternoon, and the center of Rotterdam was devastated by the raid and by the fires that followed. Casualty estimates were early exaggerated, at tens of thousands, and later work places the number of civilian deaths at about 800 to 900, with tens of thousands left homeless, though the figure is still discussed. The Dutch commander in chief, General Henri Winkelman, ordered the capitulation of the main Dutch forces on 15 May, partly because a similar attack on Utrecht had been threatened. Fighting continued in the province of Zeeland, where French and Dutch forces held out for several more days, and the Dutch colonial empire in the East Indies, Suriname and the Caribbean remained under the control of the government-in-exile.

Belgian forces, about twenty-two divisions, resisted and made a fighting withdrawal toward the Scheldt and the Lys. The relationship between the Belgian king and his government would remain a source of acute political division in postwar Belgium, a topic that belongs more to later chapters. Multinational command made coordination difficult in the north. Gort took orders from Gamelin through the army group commander Billotte, and Leopold took his own decisions, with limited exchange of information with the Allies. These difficulties did not cause the defeat, but they magnified it, and they would reach a critical point in the last week of May.

Section 117.10

The Ardennes Breakthrough and the Battle of Sedan

While the Allied armies were moving north into Belgium, the German Army Group A was moving through the Ardennes. The German columns crossed Luxembourg and southern Belgium on 10 and 11 May, passed the Belgian frontier defenses and French cavalry screens, and reached the Meuse on 12 May, faster than the French had predicted, and more rapidly than a number of German commanders had thought safe. The columns were attacked by Allied aircraft on several occasions, but the air attacks were limited in scale, and German fighters and anti-aircraft guns gave the columns reasonable protection. The traffic jams that feared planners had predicted did occur, but the timetable held.

The Meuse was the crucial obstacle. A broad river in a steep, wooded valley, it was held by the French Ninth Army, under General André Corap, north of Sedan, and the Second Army, under General Charles Huntziger, around Sedan. These armies had been given a defensive role and the divisions in them were mostly of the less well trained and equipped reserve class. Their positions, constructed over the winter, were incomplete, and the river line was covered by a limited number of bunkers and field works. The French command believed that the Germans would need several days to bring up artillery and prepare a crossing, which would allow time to bring up reserves.

At Sedan on 13 May, Guderian demonstrated that this assumption was mistaken. Rather than wait for heavy artillery, he coordinated a massed Luftwaffe bombardment to disrupt the French defenses. The effects of the bombardment were more psychological than physical, since the number of French casualties was modest, but the sirens of the Stukas, the continuous attack and the absence of French aircraft or anti-aircraft fire disrupted the French artillery, which did not fire effectively, and shook the troops in the forward positions, many of them reservists. German infantry crossed in rubber boats in the late afternoon, supported by direct fire from tanks and assault engineers, and by nightfall they held a bridgehead on the left bank.

The crossing was risky, and in the next days the outcome was in the balance. The bridgehead was narrow, the bridges took time to build, and French forces had the chance to counterattack. French counterattacks were delayed and uncoordinated, permitting German forces to consolidate. The French had a reserve armored division and several infantry divisions nearby, and the 3rd Armored Division and the 3rd Motorized Division, and the 10th Infantry Division, were ordered to counterattack on 14 May. At Stonne, a village on a ridge near Sedan, the French and Germans fought a series of fierce actions for several days, and the village changed hands repeatedly. But the main axis of the German advance had already moved on. By 14 May, German engineers had completed a pontoon bridge, and tanks of Guderian's corps were crossing to the west bank. The French had the opportunity to bomb the bridges, and British and French aircraft made attempts on 14 May that cost heavy losses, particularly in the British Battle and Blenheim squadrons, but the bridges survived.

On 15 May, French Prime Minister Paul Reynaud telephoned Churchill, according to Churchill's later account, to say that France had been defeated. The Ninth Army had been broken up. Guderian, who had been told to halt on the bridgehead and who persuaded his superiors to allow a continued advance as a reconnaissance in force, chose to press on. The panzer divisions moved more than fifty miles on 16 May, and on 20 May the leading elements of the 2nd Panzer Division reached Abbeville, on the Somme near the Channel, and Noyelles at the estuary. By the evening of 20 May the Allied armies in Belgium and northern France had been cut off from the main French forces to the south.

The German success at Sedan was an operational penetration that became a strategic breakthrough because several conditions held together. The Germans concentrated armor and air power at a single point, they relied on the initiative of subordinate commanders to push forward without waiting for orders from above, they used radio communication to coordinate movement and they maintained a rapid tempo that outpaced the French ability to respond. The French command was slow, was out of contact with the front, and the commander in chief, Gamelin, had no strategic reserve to commit. The opportunity for the Allies was available, but they did not seize it.

Distinguish clearly between frontiers, which in 1940 were those of the established states, and the fluid military lines of the campaign, which moved daily and were known only approximately even to the commanders at the time.

Section 117.11

The Allied Encirclement: Northern France, Belgium, and the Channel Coast

After the breakthrough, the Allies tried to restore the front, and the effort was not hopeless in concept. On 17 May, Colonel Charles de Gaulle, commanding the newly formed 4th Armored Division, attacked northward from the area of Laon toward Montcornet, striking the German flank and causing a brief disturbance, though his force was too small to stop the advance. On 19 May, Gamelin was dismissed, and General Maxime Weygand, who was seventy-three, was recalled from Syria to take his place. He assumed command on 20 May. He proposed a counteroffensive, in which the Allied forces in the north would attack southward toward the Somme while French forces attacked northward from the south, to cut off the German armor. The plan was discussed on 21 and 22 May, but Weygand's own information was poor, communication between the headquarters was slow and the Allied armies in the north were being pressed hard. The plan was not carried out as designed.

The one significant Allied counterattack was at Arras on 21 May. A mixed British force, Frankforce, including two battalions of the Royal Tank Regiment with Matilda infantry tanks, with a French light mechanized division, struck the flank of Rommel's 7th Panzer Division and the SS Totenkopf Division south of Arras, with limited infantry support. The attack was repulsed by the evening, but it contributed to the nervousness of the German command about the vulnerability of the flank, and it may have contributed to the decision to halt on 24 May. Its effect should not be exaggerated, since the force involved was small.

In the three days after 20 May the German armored forces turned northward along the coast. They isolated and invested Boulogne, which fell on 25 May, and Calais, where a garrison of British and French troops, including the Rifle Brigade and the 60th Rifles, held out until 26 May, and in doing so delayed the 10th Panzer Division. On 24 May, however, the German advance on Dunkirk was halted. The orders, issued from Hitler's headquarters after discussion with Rundstedt, who commanded Army Group A, stopped the armored divisions on the line of the Aa Canal, about ten miles from Dunkirk, for roughly two days. The halt order of 24 May is documented, and its existence is not disputed. Its motives, however, are. Several explanations have been given. Rundstedt and others were concerned about the exhaustion of the tank units and the losses they had suffered, and wished to keep armored strength for the second phase of the campaign against the French armies in the south, and the Flanders terrain, with canals and marshes, was thought unsuitable for tanks. Göring promised that the Luftwaffe could finish the job from the air. And Hitler, according to some historians, was cautious about the British and hoped to reach an understanding. That diplomatic explanation remains weakly supported; military caution and conservation of armored forces are better evidenced. The halt order was lifted on 26 May.

The days between 24 and 26 May were critical for the Allies. On 25 May, Gort, who had been told by Weygand to attack south and who knew that the Belgian army was weakening, decided that the only prospect of saving the BEF was to withdraw to the coast, and he cancelled the planned counterattack and sent two divisions, the 5th and 50th, to hold the line of the Belgian front on the Ypres-Comines Canal. On 26 May, the British government authorized the evacuation, and the planning of Operation Dynamo began. Allied commanders in the north, who had lost direct communication with the main French army, now had a perimeter to defend.

The Belgian army, after fighting on the Lys for a week, was running short of ammunition and was pressed by German attacks and bombing of the cities. King Leopold III decided that further resistance was futile and capitulated on 28 May, unconditionally, without consulting the Allies or his government, who regarded the capitulation as unconstitutional. Belgian soldiers fought to the end, but the surrender opened a gap on the left of the BEF, and British and French forces had to extend and fill it. Around Lille, the French First Army continued fighting and delayed German formations even after the Belgian capitulation. The French contribution to the defense of the evacuation perimeter, which was too often neglected in British retellings, was substantial.

Section 117.12

Operation Dynamo: Dunkirk and the Evacuation of Allied Forces

Operation Dynamo was named after the room at Dover Castle, a former dynamo room in the tunnels beneath the cliffs, where Vice Admiral Bertram Ramsay, the Flag Officer Dover, directed the operation. The damaged harbor forced mass embarkation from the East Mole breakwater, where larger ships could dock, and from beaches served by smaller craft. The Admiralty had begun to assemble ships even before the formal order of 26 May, expecting that perhaps 45,000 men might be saved in two days. Along the beaches east of the town, at Bray-Dunes and La Panne, soldiers waited in long lines, wading into the water to be taken off by small boats which carried them to larger ships offshore.

Ramsay's force was made up of destroyers, minesweepers, passenger ferries, coasters, fishing vessels, lifeboats and a large number of small civilian craft, many of them manned by naval personnel and by their civilian owners. The Admiralty requisitioned many of the small boats, and a number were crewed by volunteers. English Heritage records 933 vessels involved and 236 lost; lower counts reflect differing definitions of participating craft. The Royal Navy's destroyers carried the greatest numbers, and the Mole was the most important embarkation point. The operation was supported by the RAF, which flew many sorties over the beaches from British bases, and in doing so lost a considerable number of aircraft, and soldiers on the beaches often saw no British aircraft when the German bombers were attacking. The RAF's contribution was important but less visible than it could have been.

The evacuation proceeded in stages. On 27 May, about 7,500 men were embarked, and the next day about 17,800. On 29 May the figure rose to about 47,000, and on the 31st, to some 68,000, the peak day. By 4 June a total of 338,226 men had been taken off, according to the figure usually cited, and the figure is drawn from British naval records. The proportion of French soldiers rose in the last days, as the French became the main defenders of the perimeter. Of the 338,226 evacuated, English Heritage records 224,320 British and 113,906 other Allied personnel, predominantly French; other counts may use different scopes.

The rear guard on the perimeter was composed of British and French troops, and from 1 June it consisted principally of French divisions, notably the 12th and 68th Divisions, who held the line while the last evacuation took place. French troops fought on until 4 June, and the tens of thousands of defenders who could not be embarked, estimated at about 35,000, were captured. French vessels, including destroyers and auxiliary ships, also transported evacuees. The Dunkirk operation was therefore a joint Franco-British undertaking, and later narratives that reduce it to an episode of British civil courage, however moving, misrepresent it.

Dunkirk was both an operational success and a consequence of defeat. The rescue of over 300,000 trained soldiers, who would form the core of the British army in the following years, was an achievement of improvisation under pressure, and it preserved the military manpower that Britain would need. But the BEF left behind nearly all its heavy equipment, including about 2,400 guns, some 63,000 vehicles and large quantities of ammunition and stores, and the army was for some months unfit to meet an invasion. The campaign in France had cost the BEF about 68,000 casualties, including killed, wounded and captured. The Allies had lost the battle for France, and Churchill, in his speech of 4 June, warned the House of Commons that wars are not won by evacuations. The evacuation was followed by others, since a substantial number of British and Allied forces remained in France south of the Somme, which is discussed in section 117.13. Operation Cycle, from Le Havre in 10 to 13 June, rescued about eleven thousand people, though the main body of the 51st Highland Division, cut off from the port, was forced to surrender at Saint-Valery-en-Caux on 12 June. Operation Aerial, from 15 to 25 June, from ports in Normandy, Brittany and the Atlantic coast, embarked nearly 190,000 British, Polish, Czechoslovak and French soldiers, airmen and civilians. On 17 June the liner Lancastria, loaded with troops and civilians off Saint-Nazaire, was bombed and sunk with a loss of life that has been estimated at several thousand, and may have been higher, in what was probably the greatest single loss of life in British maritime history. The British government, anxious about morale, suppressed news of it for some time.

Multiple Perspectives

The Evacuation from Dunkirk: Soldiers, Sailors, Civilian Crews, and Evacuees

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical syntheses of patterns documented in contemporary reports and later testimony. They are not quotations from named individuals.

British soldiers on the beaches: Many experienced the evacuation as a long and frustrating wait, under air attack and often without food or shelter, interrupted by the discipline of queueing at the water's edge, and often without knowing the larger picture. Some units retained their order and some did not. Many arrived in England exhausted and angered by what they saw as the failure of leadership, a sentiment that coexisted with relief at survival.
French soldiers and defenders of the perimeter: For the French, Dunkirk was a defeat in which their own army was being destroyed in the north. Those on the perimeter fought to protect the departure of others, often knowing that many of them would be captured, and in the days when French troops were the main force holding the perimeter, some felt that their allies were leaving them behind. Others were evacuated, taken to Britain and returned to France just before the armistice.
Naval crews and civilian boat crews: Royal Navy crews of destroyers worked under repeated air attack for days without rest, and a number of ships were sunk with great loss of life. Civilian volunteers who took small boats across the Channel were often exposed to hazards they had not anticipated, and many of the boats were in fact worked by naval personnel, while some civilian owners piloted their own craft. Their courage was real, though the number and proportion of civilian vessels in the evacuation have often been exaggerated in legend.
Belgian civilians, refugees, and the abandoned: Belgian civilians and refugees, packed into the coastal area, were among those who could not be taken off, and many of the Belgian soldiers who had been fighting alongside the British were left behind after the capitulation. The abandoned and the captured, who numbered in the tens of thousands, spent years as prisoners of war, and they appear little in the dominant narratives of the evacuation.

Why the perspectives matter: The groups did not have equal power or equal freedom to choose. Soldiers on the beaches and refugees could not decide who was evacuated, whereas commanders and the Admiralty made such decisions. The comparison shows that Dunkirk was experienced as rescue, as defeat and as abandonment, and often as all three at once.

The Fall of France and the Transformation of Western Europe

June to July 1940
Section 117.13

Case Red: The German Offensive against the Remaining French Armies

When Dunkirk fell on 4 June, the French army had lost the northern armies, about thirty divisions of its best mobile forces and much of its armor, but it had not ceased to exist. Weygand organized the remaining forces, about sixty-odd divisions, many of them understrength and hastily reconstituted, along a line from the Channel coast at the mouth of the Somme along that river and the Aisne and then to the Maginot Line. The Weygand Line was not a continuous fortification. Weygand, who had understood the nature of the German attack, ordered the creation of hedgehog positions, in which villages and woods were fortified as strongpoints with all-around defense, to be held even when bypassed, so that German armor would be slowed and exposed to counterattack. The order was a sensible adaptation, and in some sectors it worked. Its execution depended on the training and morale of the units in these positions, on the ammunition at hand and on the availability of reserves.

The German offensive, Case Red (Fall Rot), began on 5 June with an attack by Army Group B on the lower Somme and, from 9 June, by Army Group A on the Aisne. German troops also killed captured Black French colonial soldiers, including at Chasselay; these crimes demand attention alongside the fighting. The Germans now had the advantage of experience, and several of the armored divisions had been refitted, though they had suffered losses of about a quarter of their tanks in the first phase and were not at full strength. In the first days the French resisted fiercely in many places. The German infantry, which was less well armored than its tanks, suffered heavy casualties in attacks on the hedgehog positions, in the first two days, and the Germans did not achieve an immediate breakthrough. A leading historian of these events estimates the total of such killings in the thousands.

By 9 and 10 June, the French line was broken in several places. On the Somme, German armor crossed, advanced toward the Seine, and a French army was pushed back. On the Aisne, Guderian's panzer group, now commanding two corps, broke through at Rethel and advanced south toward the Plateau de Langres and the Swiss frontier, which would cut off French forces in the Maginot Line from the rest of France. On 10 June the French government left Paris for Tours, and on 11 June Weygand advised the government that the army could no longer maintain a continuous front. The French army, with reserves committed and little room to maneuver, was now fighting a series of local actions, often without orders from above. On 14 June the German Army Group C attacked the Maginot Line from the front and the rear, though the fortresses that were attacked mostly held, and their garrisons surrendered only after the armistice, on the instructions of the French command.

The record shows that French forces continued to resist after Dunkirk, and that the question of the collapse is not one of unwillingness. French casualties in June 1940 were very high, with thousands of men killed in a few days of heavy fighting, and in the whole campaign between May and June, the French army lost, according to current estimates, between about 60,000 and 90,000 dead, a range that reflects uncertainty in the sources, and the German army lost about 27,000 dead and about 18,000 missing. In several places, such as the defense of Saumur by the cadets of the cavalry school, who held the Loire crossings on 19 to 20 June, small French forces fought with remarkable determination. What failed in June was the coherence of the operational front, the capacity of the high command to coordinate resistance, and the ability of the civil authorities and the military to continue a war in metropolitan France. The distinction between the collapse of an organized front and a lack of determination or armed forces is at the center of the interpretation of the campaign.

Section 117.14

Paris, Refugees, and the Human Experience of Defeat

As the German armies advanced, the French government left Paris on 10 June for Tours, and then Bordeaux, and the military governor of Paris, General Pierre Héring, declared the city an open city to spare it from destruction, with the decision announced on 12 June. German troops entered Paris on the morning of 14 June, and swastika flags were raised over the Eiffel Tower and public buildings. German troops marched down the Champs-Élysées, and the German military authorities began to establish their administration. Many of the Parisians who had remained watched the arrival of the Germans in silence. The occupation of the capital, without fighting, was both a symbolic and a practical blow, because Paris was the center of French administration, finance and communications.

The fall of the capital was preceded by one of the largest civilian displacements in modern European history. The exode, the mass flight of French civilians, began with the German attack in May, when Belgian and Dutch refugees began to arrive in northern France, and grew in June as the German armies advanced. Estimates of the total number of people displaced vary, between about six and ten million, in a French population of about forty million. In the Belgian case, some two million people took to the roads in May. Parisians left in very large numbers, with the population of the city reduced dramatically in a few days, and with similar departures from Lille, Reims, Rouen and the towns of the Somme and Aisne. The flight was a result of fear, spread by rumor, of memories of the German occupation of northern France in 1914 to 1918, and of the sight of the bombing of towns, and it was partly encouraged and partly disrupted by officials who did not have plans for the evacuation of a population on such a scale.

The refugees traveled by train, by car, by bicycle, in horse-drawn carts and on foot, along roads that were soon blocked by columns of vehicles, which also obstructed the movement of the army, and prevented military reinforcements from reaching the front. They were exposed to attack. German aircraft strafed and bombed the columns on a number of occasions. Food and water, fuel and shelter became scarce along the routes, and in the towns that they reached, officials and charitable organizations such as the Red Cross, churches and local committees improvised reception, with varying success. Families were separated, and children were lost, and in many cases it took weeks to find them. The sick and the elderly died on the roads. In many places, farmers and townspeople gave food and shelter, while in others the arrival of refugees caused tension, and some of those who fled encountered hostility. After the armistice, millions returned home, though many found their houses looted or destroyed, and others, from Alsace and Lorraine, from the north and from the Jewish communities, could not return in the same way.

On 10 June, as the French government was leaving Paris, Italy declared war on Britain and France. Mussolini, who had been neutral since September 1939 while declaring Italy a non-belligerent, calculated that the war was nearly over and that Italy must join in order to obtain a share of the spoils. The Italian army attacked in the Alps between 20 and 24 June, with little success against small French forces in strong positions, and the Italian involvement was militarily limited in France. Its significance lay elsewhere, in opening the Mediterranean as a theater of war, which is treated in Chapter 118.

Everyday Life and Social History

French Civilian Refugees during the Exode

The exode must be understood as a social experience before it is understood as a military problem. The people on the roads included families who had decided in a few hours what to take, often a mattress strapped to a car, a few valuables, documents and food. Mothers carried infants, and elderly relatives were placed on carts. Refugees who had come from Belgium and the Netherlands, and some from Poland and from Spain who had been in France since 1939, were already displaced, and many moved a second or third time. The hospitals and asylums of the northern towns had to be evacuated, and the staff and patients joined the flow.

The evidence for these experiences is varied. Contemporary letters and diaries, the records of the departmental prefects and of relief organizations, newsreels and photographs, and the testimony collected afterwards give a detailed picture, though it is shaped by the viewpoint of those who left a record, and by the patriotic and political uses to which the exode was put. Official reports often stressed disorder and panic, while personal accounts also recorded solidarity. The experience varied by class, region and resources, since those with a car and money could go farther and faster than those without, and it varied by exposure to attack, since the unlucky found themselves under air attack in the open.

The exode matters for the history of the fall of France because it was both a consequence and a cause of military defeat. It amplified the sense of catastrophe, and it blocked roads. It also shaped the political atmosphere in which the armistice was accepted, by convincing many French people that the war was lost and that an end to the fighting would at least stop the suffering. For many families, it was a formative event, remembered for generations.

Section 117.15

Armistice, Vichy France, and the Emergence of Free France

On the evening of 16 June 1940, after the cabinet had rejected the proposed union with Britain and Reynaud's colleagues had voted for an armistice inquiry, Reynaud resigned. President Albert Lebrun asked Pétain, who was eighty-four, to form a government, and he did so that night. On 17 June, Pétain broadcast to the French people that he was asking the enemy to end the fighting, a message that many listeners understood as an order to cease fire, which caused further disarray in the army. The Franco-German armistice was signed on 22 June; a separate Franco-Italian armistice followed on 24 June, and both took effect on 25 June. The distinction between signing and effect is important, since fighting continued until the second date.

The armistice divided France. The north and the entire Atlantic coast, about three fifths of the country, including Paris, became an occupied zone, and the south, the so-called free zone, was left under French administration. Alsace and Lorraine were, in practice, annexed by Germany, though the armistice did not say so, and the northern departments of Nord and Pas-de-Calais were attached to the German military administration in Brussels. France was to pay the costs of the occupation, fixed at a very high daily sum, and about one and a half million French prisoners of war would remain in German captivity until the peace. The army was limited to 100,000 men, the fleet would be demobilized and disarmed under German and Italian control, and France was required to hand over German and Austrian refugees on its territory on German demand, a provision that had grave consequences. The French government retained nominal sovereignty over the whole country, together with the empire, and kept its fleet, and, as the Germans calculated, this gave Pétain an incentive to keep the empire out of the British camp.

The armistice was bitterly resisted by some. In London, Reynaud's former junior minister, Brigadier General Charles de Gaulle, had flown to Britain on 17 June, and on 18 June he spoke on the BBC, in a short broadcast that few heard live and of which no recording survives, urging French soldiers and engineers to join him and declaring that France had lost a battle but not the war. A second appeal, on 22 June, of which a recording exists, is the source of the best known words associated with him. In August Félix Éboué's administration in Chad and authorities in several equatorial African colonies rallied to Free France, while much of France's empire initially remained aligned with Vichy. The British government recognized him as leader of the Free French on 28 June, and he began to recruit volunteers and rally territories of the empire. A French court sentenced de Gaulle to death in absentia in August. His legitimacy was disputed by many French officers and officials, who regarded Pétain's government as the lawful authority and de Gaulle as a rebel. Initially he commanded a small force, and the number of French who rallied to him in 1940 was only a few thousand.

The tension between British and French ambitions took violent form on 3 July, when a British naval force, in Operation Catapult, attacked the French fleet at Mers-el-Kébir in Algeria after its commander refused to join the British or to disarm. The attack killed about 1,300 French sailors and heightened French resentment. It showed British determination to prevent French ships from falling into German hands, and it also showed the limits of British ability to win French support.

On 1 July the French government moved to Vichy, a spa town in the center of the country with plenty of hotels, and on 10 July the National Assembly, composed of the Chamber of Deputies and Senate, voted by 569 to 80, with 20 abstentions, to give Pétain full powers to draft a new constitution. The next day he issued constitutional acts that made him Head of the French State and suspended the Third Republic's institutions. The legality of the vote has been disputed. Its supporters stressed that it followed the constitutional procedure, and it was approved by a large majority of the parliamentarians present. Its critics noted that it was held under conditions of military defeat, occupation and pressure, that many deputies who might have opposed it were absent, including a group who had sailed to North Africa on the ship Massilia, and that the vote gave powers that the constitutional laws of 1875 did not envisage. The collaboration, resistance and repression that followed belong to Chapter 120.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The French Government, Sovereignty, and State Institutions before and after June 1940

What Changed

  • The parliamentary republic of the Third Republic, established in 1870, was replaced by an authoritarian regime under Pétain, with the title Head of the French State
  • France was divided into an occupied zone, a nominally sovereign unoccupied zone, annexed or attached territories and an empire whose loyalties were contested
  • The army and fleet were reduced and restricted, and over a million French soldiers became prisoners of war
  • A rival claim to French legitimacy, Free France, emerged outside French territory

What Stayed the Same

  • The civil service, the prefects, the police, the courts and most local administration continued to operate, often under the same personnel
  • The French empire, apart from the territories that rallied to de Gaulle, remained under Vichy administration
  • The legal continuity of the state was claimed by Vichy and by Free France alike, each from a different position
  • The ordinary French population continued to work, to farm and to seek food and shelter under altered conditions

Who experienced change most directly? The residents of the annexed regions, the prisoners of war and their families, the Jews and foreign refugees affected by new legislation, and those who held offices in the republican system saw the sharpest changes. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural families in the south and officials of the administration often found that the routines of life continued, at least for a time.

Why the pattern matters: The mixture of rupture and continuity helps explain why many French people could accept the new regime initially, and why historians reject both the idea that the Vichy regime was a mere imposition and the idea that it was a continuation of republican France.

Section 117.16

Why France Fell: Military Operations, Strategic Decisions, and Historical Interpretation

The speed of the French defeat shocked contemporaries, and the question of its causes began to be debated almost at once. Within France, the Vichy regime blamed the republic, the Popular Front, the decadence of French society and the supposed effects of the shorter working week, and the opponents of Vichy blamed the generals and the elite who had led the country to defeat. The historian Marc Bloch, an officer in the campaign who wrote a short book in 1940, titled Strange Defeat, blamed the failures of the high command, which he thought had been intellectually unprepared for a war of movement. Later historians, working with the archives, have produced a more detailed and more balanced account.

The most basic fact is that the Allies were not significantly inferior to Germany in numbers or in material. The Allied armies had about as many divisions as the Germans, more artillery and more tanks, and the French tanks were in many cases technically better than the German. The Germans had an advantage in the air, with more aircraft in operation, though not overwhelmingly so in total numbers. The explanation for the defeat is therefore not mainly one of material. The Germans had chosen the point of attack, concentrated their armor there, in seven of ten Panzer divisions under one command, and used air power to support it. French and British armor was dispersed, used to support infantry or in small formations, and much of it was not at the decisive point.

A second set of factors concerns tempo and command. German formations were trained to make decisions quickly at the lower levels, within an understanding of the commander's intent, and used radio to coordinate. French command was centralized, relying on orders issued from the top and passed down through an elaborate chain, and at the speed of the German advance the French chain of command was out of date by the time orders arrived. Gamelin's headquarters at Vincennes had no radio link to the front, and he was reliant on telephone and couriers. The movement of reserves was slow, and the units that did reach the battlefield often arrived piecemeal. Air-ground coordination favored the Germans, whose Stukas and other aircraft were used to strike at the points where the army wanted them, within a system of liaison officers and radio, while the Allied air forces were less well coordinated with the army.

A third set concerns strategic choices. The decision to commit the best Allied forces to the north, in the Dyle and Breda moves, left the center weak, and was a major cause of the defeat. The failure to anticipate the Ardennes thrust was a failure of imagination partly shaped by doctrine. And the slow response of the French command to the breakthrough, in the vital days from 13 to 20 May, allowed the Germans to establish themselves behind the Allied armies.

At the same time, German success was not a certainty, and it involved considerable risk and luck. The German commanders took gambles, and had the French response been faster, as the case at Stonne and the counterattacks of de Gaulle suggested it might be, the outcome could have been different. The Germans were also fortunate in the way the crossing at Sedan went. Their logistic support was strained, and their infantry divisions were slow to follow the armor. The campaign was won in six weeks, but it might have gone otherwise.

Cause and Effect

Interacting Causes of the Rapid French Military Defeat

Long-term causes

A strategic culture shaped by the losses of 1914 to 1918, a doctrine of the methodical battle, a smaller population than Germany's, interwar political divisions that complicated rearmament and a command structure designed for a slower war.

Short-term causes

The Allied decision to advance into Belgium, the weakness of the central reserves, the German concentration of armor and air power at Sedan, and slow French decision making in the days from 13 to 20 May.

The event

A German operational penetration at the Meuse that became a strategic breakthrough, followed by the encirclement of the northern Allied armies and the collapse of the southern front in June.

Immediate effects

Dunkirk and the loss of the best French mobile forces, the exode, the flight of the government, the armistice, and the division of France into occupied and unoccupied zones.

Long-term consequences

German control of western Europe, the emergence of Vichy and Free France as rival authorities, Britain's isolation among the major western belligerents and the opening of the Mediterranean to war, with effects extending to the occupation, collaboration and resistance of later years.

Historical complexity: The French defeat cannot be reduced to national character, cowardice, simple numerical inferiority or one decisive weapon. Military collapse and political collapse also need to be distinguished, since the army's operational failure came first and the decision to seek an armistice, taken by a government divided on the question, followed. The outcome was not guaranteed before May 1940.

The Battle of Britain and the Survival of British Resistance

May to December 1940, with the Blitz continuing into May 1941
Section 117.17

Britain after France: Churchill, Imperial Resources, and the Threat of Invasion

Winston Churchill became prime minister on the evening of 10 May 1940, after Chamberlain's resignation, and formed a coalition that included the Labour leaders Clement Attlee and Arthur Greenwood, as well as Conservatives and Liberals, with Lord Halifax remaining as foreign secretary. Churchill was not trusted by all Conservatives, many of whom had doubts about his judgment, and the new government was formed in the middle of the greatest military crisis in modern British history. In late May, as the military situation in France deteriorated, the War Cabinet discussed whether Britain should explore the possibility of a negotiated settlement through Mussolini's mediation. Halifax favored sounding out the Italians, and Churchill, supported by Chamberlain, who remained in the cabinet, and by the Labour ministers, resisted. What is clear is that by 28 May the cabinet had decided to continue the war, and that the fall of France did not alter that decision.

The military position of Britain after June 1940 rested on several conditions. The first was geography. The English Channel, which at its narrowest is about twenty miles wide, was an obstacle that no European army had crossed in force since 1066, and any invasion would depend on crossing it by sea. The second was the Royal Navy, which in 1940 was the largest in Europe, with many more cruisers and destroyers than the German navy, whose surface fleet had suffered severe losses in the Norwegian campaign. A German invasion would need control of the Channel against the Royal Navy, and that would require control of the air. The third was industrial capacity. British production of aircraft, particularly of fighters, expanded rapidly under the new Ministry of Aircraft Production, headed from May by Lord Beaverbrook, and by the summer of 1940 British factories were producing more fighters each month than German factories. The fourth was access to resources overseas, through the sea lanes and the empire.

The German invasion plan, Operation Sea Lion (Seelöwe), emerged slowly. Hitler had assumed that Britain would make peace after the defeat of France, and the German armed forces had not prepared for an invasion. On 16 July Hitler issued Directive 16, ordering preparations for a landing, on the condition that air superiority was achieved. The army wanted a wide landing front, and the navy a narrow one. The Luftwaffe's task was to destroy the RAF and to keep the Royal Navy away. German naval officers doubted they could protect slow invasion barges against the much stronger Royal Navy without control of the air.

The phrase that Britain stood alone in 1940 needs qualification. It is true that Britain was isolated among the principal western European belligerents. But Britain did not fight alone in a literal sense. The Dominions and India gave it military and material support, though under different constitutional circumstances. Australia and New Zealand declared war in September 1939 with Britain. Canada declared war separately, on 10 September, after a parliamentary vote, and sent its first division to Britain in December 1939, and Canadian forces would help to defend the island in 1940. South Africa, after a division in its cabinet, voted to enter the war on 6 September under Jan Smuts. India was declared at war by the British viceroy without consulting Indian political leaders, which led to the resignation of the Congress provincial governments in the winter of 1939, and it contributed a very large volunteer army. Ireland remained neutral. The colonies provided troops, labor and raw materials, and the empire's trade and finance underpinned the war effort.

In addition, Britain became the base for the governments and forces of occupied Europe. The Polish, Norwegian, Dutch and Belgian governments established themselves in London, along with the Czechoslovak National Committee and, from June, de Gaulle's Free French. Their soldiers, sailors and airmen formed units that fought alongside the British, and, in the case of Polish and Czechoslovak airmen, contributed substantially to the air defense in the summer. The United States, though neutral, moved toward assistance. The Cash and Carry arrangement of November 1939 had allowed belligerents to buy arms for cash, and in September 1940 the United States agreed to transfer fifty old destroyers to Britain in return for leases on bases in British territories. The Lend-Lease Act of March 1941 would go much further, though it belongs to later chapters.

Winston Churchill, Speech to the House of Commons, 18 June 1940

Creator: Winston Churchill, British prime minister · Date: 18 June 1940 · Place: House of Commons, Westminster · Type: A parliamentary speech recorded in the official parliamentary report, Hansard, which for this debate is found in volume 362, columns 51 to 61

Context: Churchill delivered the speech in the House of Commons on 18 June 1940. The chamber was not wired for live broadcast; he personally repeated the speech for BBC radio that evening, and a contemporaneous recording survives. By contrast, his 4 June Commons speech was not rebroadcast by him that day, and its familiar recordings were made later. Students should distinguish Hansard, the June 18 BBC broadcast and later excerpts or recordings.

Paraphrase and brief quotation Churchill told the Commons that the military events in France had been a colossal disaster, and he reviewed the evacuation from Dunkirk and the position of the British army and air force. He argued that the Channel, the navy and the air defense gave Britain a strong position, and he stressed that the British Empire and the United States could provide the resources needed to continue. He concluded that "the Battle of France is over" and that he expected the Battle of Britain to begin. He asked the House to "brace ourselves to our duties," and predicted that if the British Empire lasted a thousand years, men would say that this was their finest hour. (Quotations are brief extracts, and students should consult Hansard for the full text.)

Analysis: The speech is valuable as evidence of how the British government, in the days of the French collapse, wished to present its military position and its determination to the House and the world. It shows Churchill's assessment of the strategic position, in which he stressed the navy, the air defense and imperial resources, and his use of historical rhetoric, which linked the crisis to the long history of the Empire and of European civilization. It is important to note what it does not show. It does not show how the public reacted, which is better known from the surveys of Mass-Observation and the Ministry of Information's Home Intelligence reports, and from letters and diaries. It was a political statement by a leader with an interest in sustaining morale and supporting the case for continued war, and it may have been more optimistic than the information available in the cabinet warranted. It also omits the perspectives of those who had doubts about the war, of colonial subjects who had not been consulted, and of the governments in exile who were not in the room.

Section 117.18

Fighter Command, the Luftwaffe, and Britain's Integrated Air Defense

The RAF's Fighter Command had been created in 1936 under Air Chief Marshal Hugh Dowding, who led it until November 1940. Dowding, a cautious and exacting officer, had advocated since the mid-1930s the combination of modern monoplane fighters, radar and a centralized system of control. Fighter Command was divided into groups, each responsible for a geographic area. The most heavily involved was No. 11 Group, under Air Vice-Marshal Keith Park, covering London and southeastern England, which bore the brunt of the German attack. No. 12 Group, under Trafford Leigh-Mallory, covered the Midlands. Park favored prompt interception by small formations; Leigh-Mallory advocated larger formations, an argument later called the Big Wing controversy.

The fighters themselves were the Hawker Hurricane and the Supermarine Spitfire. The Hurricane, which was easier to build and to repair, made up the majority of Fighter Command's strength and shot down more German aircraft in the battle than the Spitfire, which was faster and more maneuverable and was used mainly to deal with German fighters. The German Bf 109E was an effective escort fighter but its limited fuel endurance restricted combat time over southern England. The twin-engine Messerschmitt Bf 110, intended as a long-range escort, proved vulnerable to the single-engine fighters. The German bomber force consisted of the Heinkel He 111, the Dornier Do 17 and the Junkers Ju 88, medium bombers carrying modest bomb loads, with the Junkers Ju 87 Stuka dive bomber, which had been effective in Poland and France, but which suffered heavy losses over Britain and was largely withdrawn from the battle.

The Luftwaffe was organized in air fleets, or Luftflotten. Luftflotte 2 under Albert Kesselring, based in the Low Countries and northeastern France, and Luftflotte 3 under Hugo Sperrle, in Normandy and Brittany, carried the main attack, and Luftflotte 5 under Hans-Jürgen Stumpff, in Norway and Denmark, attacked from the north. In early August the Luftwaffe had about 2,500 aircraft of the main types available for operations, against Fighter Command's fewer than 800 serviceable fighters, though such figures vary by source and by the date and the kind of aircraft counted. Its leaders had come from the successes in Poland and France, and Göring, in particular, was overconfident. The Luftwaffe had been designed chiefly for continental campaigns and lacked a large long-range bomber force. The Luftwaffe's intelligence of the RAF was poor. The Germans consistently underestimated British fighter strength and production, and misunderstood the function of the radar stations and the control system.

The core of the British defense was the system of command and control that came to be associated with Dowding. The Chain Home radar stations, a line of tall transmitter and receiver towers along the south and east coasts, could detect formations of aircraft at ranges of about a hundred miles at high altitude, though they were less effective against low-flying aircraft, which were covered by a supplementary system of lower-power stations. Their data was combined with reports from the Observer Corps, some thirty thousand volunteers in posts and plotting centers who tracked aircraft over land by sight and sound. Both were relayed by telephone to the Fighter Command headquarters at Bentley Priory, where the filter room reconciled the reports and passed them to the group and sector operations rooms. The sector stations, such as Biggin Hill, Kenley, North Weald, Hornchurch, Tangmere and Northolt, directed squadrons by radio telephone toward the incoming formations. The Dowding System also depended on ground crews, who refueled and rearmed the aircraft in minutes, on the staff of the plotting rooms, many of them members of the Women's Auxiliary Air Force, on the signals operators, on the repair units and on the production and supply networks.

The aircrew of Fighter Command during the battle were drawn from many countries. About 2,900 aircrew flew in the battle, and roughly one in five came from outside Britain, including pilots from Poland, Czechoslovakia, Canada, New Zealand, Australia, South Africa, Belgium, France, Ireland and the United States. Polish pilots, who numbered about 145, had flown in the September campaign or in France, and the Polish No. 303 Squadron became the highest-scoring squadron in the battle. About 90 Czechoslovak airmen also served. The multinational character of the force is a reminder that the defense of Britain in 1940 was an Allied effort, and that the airmen of occupied Europe, whose own countries had been defeated, were a significant part of it.

Historical Vocabulary
Fighter Command, Chain Home, the Dowding System, and Air Superiority

Fighter Command was the RAF command responsible for the air defense of the United Kingdom, organized in groups and sectors under Hugh Dowding. It was a command and an organization, not merely a collection of aircraft.

Chain Home was the British network of coastal radar stations, which detected approaching aircraft at long range. Its data were useful only when connected to the rest of the defense system.

The Dowding System is the term often used for the integrated arrangement of radar, Observer Corps, filter room, group and sector control, radio telephone communication, fighter squadrons and ground support. Radar was a vital component of it, but not an independent technological solution, and it would have been of little use without the human and organizational network.

Air superiority is a condition in which one side can operate in an area of air without prohibitive interference from the other. It does not mean the complete destruction of the enemy air force. For a German invasion of Britain, air superiority over the Channel and the landing areas was a necessary condition, and the Luftwaffe failed to achieve it.

Why it matters: The terms help students avoid the idea that radar or fighter pilots alone won the battle, and they emphasize the organizational basis of the British defense.

Section 117.19

The Battle of Britain: July to October 1940

The official British dates of the Battle of Britain, fixed after the war, are 10 July to 31 October 1940, and they are used here as a convention. Historians have noted that the chronology is a matter of interpretation. German air attacks on shipping and ports began in June, and the Blitz on cities continued after October, and the phases within the battle were not clearly separated by the participants. The RAF Museum's chronology of the campaign distinguishes several phases, and this account follows a similar scheme.

From 10 July to the middle of August, the Luftwaffe attacked convoys in the Channel and coastal targets, in what the Germans called the Kanalkampf. The aim was partly to close the Channel to British shipping and partly to draw up RAF fighters and wear them down. On 12 August German aircraft attacked several radar stations and airfields on the south coast. The radar stations were damaged but were quickly restored, and the Germans, who did not appreciate the importance of the stations, did not persist in the attacks.

On 13 August, called Adlertag, or Eagle Day, the Luftwaffe began the main offensive, which was intended to destroy Fighter Command in a few days. A poor coordination of the opening raids, owing to bad weather and failures of communication, reduced its effect. The heaviest days of fighting followed, on 15 August, when the Luftwaffe attacked in great strength from Norway as well as from France, and on 18 August, often called the Hardest Day. The raids that month were directed at airfields, sector stations and aircraft factories. Between 24 August and 6 September, the Germans concentrated on the airfields and sector stations of 11 Group, such as Biggin Hill, Kenley, Hornchurch and North Weald. These were the most dangerous days for the RAF. Damaged sector stations and airfields strained repair crews and exhausted fighter pilots. In this period, the strain on the RAF was severe, and some historians have regarded it as the moment when the battle could have been lost.

On the night of 24 and 25 August, German bombers that lost their way bombed central London, probably by accident. Churchill ordered a retaliatory raid on Berlin, and the RAF carried out a series of small raids on the German capital in the following days. Hitler, who had forbidden attacks on London, made a speech on 4 September in which he promised to destroy British cities in return. On 7 September, the Luftwaffe launched a large daylight attack on London, with several hundred bombers escorted by hundreds of fighters, striking the docks and the East End, and a number of fires burned for days. The move toward London diverted sustained pressure from RAF airfields. The shift gave Fighter Command a respite. Fewer airfields were attacked, and the RAF had time to repair and replenish.

The climactic day of the battle, which later became commemorated as Battle of Britain Day, was 15 September, when the Luftwaffe sent large formations against London in two waves, and Fighter Command met them with nearly all the squadrons of 11 Group and those of 12 Group. The British claimed the destruction of 185 German aircraft, a figure that was announced at the time, but postwar research by comparison with German records suggests that the German losses were about sixty. The British losses were about thirty. The overclaiming was common on both sides and arose from the confusion of aerial combat. Two days later, on 17 September, Hitler postponed Sea Lion indefinitely. The operation was never formally cancelled in 1940. In October it was postponed to the spring of 1941, and the preparations were gradually dismantled as German attention turned eastward.

How is the outcome to be explained? Historians stress several factors, and the relative weight of each is debated. Aircraft production was higher than the Germans realized, and British factories replaced losses, while the number of trained pilots, a more serious constraint, was kept up by the transfer of pilots from other commands and the arrival of foreign airmen. Geography mattered. The Bf 109's limited range made escort difficult, and the Bf 110 was too vulnerable. The Luftwaffe's intelligence was poor, and its leaders changed targets repeatedly, from shipping to radar, then airfields, and then London, without sustaining a campaign long enough to break the RAF. And Britain had the benefit of the Channel, which meant that without air superiority no invasion was feasible. Approximate figures commonly cited are that Fighter Command lost about 1,000 aircraft and over 500 aircrew killed during the official period, while the Luftwaffe lost between 1,700 and 1,900 aircraft, though the totals vary by definition and source.

Section 117.20

The Blitz, Civilian Endurance, and the Strategic Outcome of 1940

The Battle of Britain and the Blitz are related but different. The Blitz was a campaign of bombardment of British cities, mostly by night after October 1940, intended to damage industry and to undermine morale. After 7 September, German aircraft bombed London for fifty-seven consecutive nights, until early November, and then attacked other cities. Attacks continued, with varying intensity, until about 10 and 11 May 1941, when the Luftwaffe turned its attention eastward. Coventry, an important center of aircraft and engineering production, was bombed on the night of 14 to 15 November 1940 by about 500 aircraft, in an operation that the Germans called Moonlight Sonata, and the raid destroyed much of the medieval city center, including the cathedral, and killed about 550 to 570 people. Other cities, including Birmingham, Liverpool, Manchester, Bristol, Southampton, Plymouth, Glasgow, Belfast and Hull, were also attacked. By the end of May 1941 the raids had killed in the order of 40,000 to 43,000 British civilians, about half of them in London, and had injured many more and destroyed or damaged more than a million houses in London alone.

Civilians had to adapt. Anderson shelters, small corrugated steel structures that could be installed in gardens, had been distributed to millions of households. In densely populated districts such as London's East End, private garden shelters were often unavailable and public facilities overcrowded. Many people took to the London Underground stations, initially against official policy, and the government eventually accepted this and improved the facilities. The Morrison shelter, an indoor steel table-like structure, was introduced in 1941. Evacuation, which had been carried out on a large scale in September 1939, when about 1.5 million people, mostly children and mothers, were moved from cities to the countryside, was repeated in smaller form in the summer and autumn of 1940, and children were also sent abroad privately to Canada and elsewhere. Many of the evacuees in 1939 had returned during the Phoney War.

The ability to take shelter and to recover varied. Those with money could leave the cities or take rooms in the country, while the poor stayed in vulnerable housing and relied on public provision. Rescue work, firefighting and medical care were performed by volunteers and professionals, among them many women, and by those in the Home Guard, formed in May 1940 as the Local Defence Volunteers. Dock and railway workers repaired lines. Contemporary reports by Mass-Observation, the Ministry of Information's Home Intelligence and local officials describe both determination and fear, and they record complaints about inadequate shelters, delays in rehousing, looting by a minority, and anger at perceived inequality.

The popular memory of the Blitz as a time of national unity and cheerful endurance, often summarized in the phrase Blitz spirit, contains truth, but it hides complexity. Historians such as Angus Calder, in his study of the myth of the Blitz, have shown that morale was not uniformly high, that class resentments were visible, particularly after the bombing of the East End in September and the visit of the royal family to damaged areas, that the poorest areas often suffered most and received least, and that there was fear, exhaustion and a measure of dissent, including criticism of the government and the rise of strikes and political agitation in some sectors. Experiences also varied by region. London, Coventry, Liverpool and Belfast had distinct experiences, and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland were affected differently, as were the many refugees and minority communities, including Jewish refugees from Europe, who had their own concerns.

By the end of 1940, the outcome of the opening phase of the European war could be summarized by comparison. Poland and France had suffered military defeat, in each case in a campaign of a few weeks, and each was occupied. Poland, however, continued the struggle in exile and underground, and the French armed forces, in one form, continued under de Gaulle. At the same time, Germany dominated much of western and central Europe. It controlled Poland, Denmark, Norway, the Low Countries and northern France, was allied with Italy and was bound by an agreement with the Soviet Union. The British position was defensive, and Britain lacked the means to dislodge Germany from the continent. World War II was far from over. In 1941 it would expand to the Mediterranean and the Balkans, treated in Chapter 118, and to the Soviet Union, treated in Chapter 119, and the German occupation regimes would develop into the systems examined in Chapters 120 and 121.

Historical Debate

Why Did Britain Avoid Defeat after France's Collapse?

The question: After the fall of France, Britain remained in the war and was not invaded. Historians disagree about how to weigh the factors that explain this outcome, including the British air defense, the Royal Navy, German planning, and political decisions in London.

Interpretation A: The air defense and British resolve were decisive. Some historians, drawing on the official histories and on the narrative of the pilots and commanders, stress that Fighter Command, with its integrated system of control, defeated the Luftwaffe in the summer of 1940, and that the political decision to continue the war, taken in the cabinet in late May, was essential to the outcome. In this view, the Battle of Britain was a genuine turning point, which prevented a German invasion and kept Britain in the war.
Interpretation B: German weakness and structural conditions made invasion unlikely. Other historians, among them Richard Overy in his study of the myth and reality of the battle, argue that Sea Lion was never a realistic option, given the weakness of the German navy, the unsuitability of the landing craft, the strength of the Royal Navy and the logistical problems of an amphibious operation across the Channel. In this view, the defeat of the Luftwaffe was less decisive than the underlying strategic circumstances, and British survival owed much to geography, sea power and industrial and imperial strength. The significance of the battle lies, in part, in its effect on morale at home and abroad.

Evidence: Interpretation A draws on Fighter Command records, the operational records of the groups and the testimony of participants. Interpretation B draws on German planning documents, naval staff studies and the records of the preparations, as well as on comparative production figures. The two interpretations overlap more than the framing suggests, since most historians accept that several factors worked together, and the disagreement concerns weighting.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question affects how they understand the relationship between military performance and structural conditions, how they assess the long-term effects of the battle, which included the survival of Britain as a base for later operations, and how they treat the national legend that has grown around the Battle of Britain.

Student task: Identify at least one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence, such as German invasion planning documents or British production records, would help to determine the relative importance of the factors. Then write a short assessment of which combination of explanations you find most persuasive.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the opening campaigns of World War II in Europe, from the German and Soviet invasions of Poland in September 1939 to the Battle of Britain and the Blitz of 1940 and 1941. Its central argument is that the extraordinary speed of German victories in Poland and France resulted from a combination of operational planning, the concentration of mobile forces at a decisive point, effective communication, decentralized initiative and air-ground cooperation, together with serious Allied errors. Those victories did not demonstrate a universal German military superiority, nor did they reflect any unwillingness to fight on the part of the defeated peoples.

In Poland, German forces attacked on 1 September 1939 with superior numbers, armor and air power, against a Polish army whose forward deployment, incomplete mobilization and limited mobile reserves left it exposed. Polish forces fought hard, at Westerplatte, Mokra, the Bzura and Warsaw, and inflicted real losses, but the campaign was decided within about two weeks. The Soviet invasion of 17 September, carried out under the secret protocol of the German-Soviet pact, destroyed any prospect of a last stand in the east. Warsaw capitulated on 28 September, and the last major regular forces ceased fighting on 6 October. The Polish state, however, never capitulated. Its government, army and air force continued the war from exile, and an underground state began to form.

The partition of Poland by Germany and the Soviet Union established two distinct occupation regimes. German authorities incorporated western territories, created the General Government and began a campaign of terror against the Polish elite, clergy and Jews, while the Soviet authorities annexed the eastern provinces, nationalized property, arrested and deported hundreds of thousands, and carried out the Katyn massacres in the spring of 1940. These developments are introduced here to establish the origins of the occupation, and their full treatment belongs to Chapters 120 and 121.

The Phoney War, as the months from September 1939 to May 1940 are called, was not a period without war, since naval battles, submarine attacks and blockade continued, but Britain and France did not launch a major land offensive. French strategy emphasized the methodical battle, defensive fortification and the long mobilization of resources, and the Maginot Line was designed to economize forces and to channel a German attack toward Belgium. The Allied Dyle Plan committed the best mobile forces northward, leaving the center weak. The Norwegian campaign of April to June 1940 extended the war to Scandinavia, brought heavy German naval losses and an Allied evacuation, and contributed to the fall of Chamberlain and the rise of Churchill.

The German offensive of 10 May 1940 combined attacks in the Low Countries with the decisive thrust through the Ardennes. The crossing of the Meuse at Sedan on 13 May, supported by massed air attack and carried out with exceptional speed, produced an operational penetration that became a strategic breakthrough when German armor reached the Channel on 20 May. The Allied armies in the north were encircled. A German halt order of 24 May, whose motives remain disputed, together with Gort's withdrawal, the defense of Calais and Lille and the improvisation of Operation Dynamo, allowed the evacuation of about 338,000 Allied personnel by 4 June, including many French soldiers, though the heavy equipment was lost and the evacuation was a consequence of defeat.

The fall of France was both a military and a political collapse, and the two must be distinguished. French forces fought on after Dunkirk, along the Somme and Aisne, and suffered heavy casualties, but the operational front disintegrated in June. Paris was occupied on 14 June, millions of civilians fled in the exode, Italy entered the war, and the government, divided and under pressure, turned from Reynaud to Pétain. The armistice, signed on 22 June and effective on 25 June, divided the country, and in July the National Assembly voted full powers to Pétain, creating the regime at Vichy, while de Gaulle's appeal of 18 June began the Free French movement. The causes of the defeat were interacting: doctrine, command structure, the commitment of forces to the north, the German concentration of armor and air power, and the speed of German decision making.

Britain's survival depended on different conditions. Churchill's government decided to continue the war after the fall of France, and the Channel, the Royal Navy, industrial production and the resources of the empire and of exiled Allied forces, as well as the growing support of the United States, gave Britain the means to do so. The integrated Dowding System, which combined radar, observers, command and control and the work of thousands of people on the ground, allowed Fighter Command to defend southern England against the Luftwaffe from July to October 1940, and the Luftwaffe's failure to achieve air superiority led Hitler to postpone Operation Sea Lion. The Blitz continued the air war against British cities until May 1941 and tested civilian endurance, with complexities of class, region and experience that popular memory conceals.

By the end of 1940, Germany dominated much of western and central Europe, but it had failed to eliminate Britain's capacity to continue the war, and Poland and exiled governments continued the struggle. The war had entered a new and unresolved phase. The Mediterranean and Balkan campaigns of Chapter 118 and the invasion of the Soviet Union in Chapter 119 would transform it, and the occupation of Europe would develop into the system of exploitation, collaboration, resistance and genocide examined in Chapters 120 and 121.

Key Terms

Mobilization
The process of bringing armed forces to wartime strength by calling up reservists and moving units to assigned positions; Poland's incomplete mobilization in August 1939 weakened its defense.
Combined arms
The coordinated use of tanks, infantry, artillery, engineers and aircraft so that each arm compensates for the weaknesses of the others.
Operational tempo
The speed at which a force decides and acts relative to its opponent, which in 1940 allowed German forces to move faster than French command could respond.
Encirclement
The surrounding of an enemy force so that it is cut off from supply and reinforcement, as happened to the Allied armies in Belgium and northern France in May 1940.
Blitzkrieg
A popular label for German rapid campaigns of 1939 to 1941; it was not an official doctrine and is regarded by many historians as a descriptive term rather than a full explanation.
Luftwaffe
The German air force, a tactical force designed to support the army, which played a major role in Poland and France and failed to achieve air superiority over Britain.
Wehrmacht
The unified German armed forces of the Nazi period, comprising the army, navy and air force.
Soviet invasion of Poland
The entry of the Red Army into eastern Poland on 17 September 1939 under the secret protocol of the German-Soviet pact, justified by the false claim that the Polish state no longer existed.
Government-in-exile
A government operating outside its national territory, as the Polish, Norwegian, Dutch and Belgian governments did from 1939 and 1940, claiming to represent a state that remained legally in existence.
Phoney War
The period from September 1939 to May 1940 of limited land fighting in the west, a label that obscures continuing naval warfare and preparation.
Maginot Line
The system of French fortifications along the German border, designed to economize forces and channel attack, but not a continuous wall and not extended in full strength along the Belgian frontier.
Dyle Plan
The Allied plan to advance into Belgium to the Dyle River if Germany attacked, which committed the best Allied mobile forces to the north.
Operation Weserübung
The German combined operation of 9 April 1940 that invaded Denmark and Norway.
Strategic reserve
A force held back from the front to meet breakthroughs or exploit opportunities; the French lacked an adequate one in the center in May 1940.
Case Yellow (Fall Gelb)
The German plan for the western offensive of 1940, revised in early 1940 to make the Ardennes thrust the main effort.
Ardennes
A wooded and hilly region of Luxembourg, Belgium and northern France, regarded by the Allies as difficult for armor and traversed by German panzer forces in May 1940.
Meuse crossing
The German crossings of the Meuse River at Dinant, Monthermé and Sedan on 13 May 1940, which opened the breakthrough.
Operational breakthrough
A penetration of the enemy front that prevents a coherent defensive line; it becomes strategic when it achieves campaign aims.
Operation Dynamo
The evacuation of Allied forces from Dunkirk and nearby beaches between 26 May and 4 June 1940, which rescued 338,226 personnel.
British Expeditionary Force
The British army sent to France in 1939 under Lord Gort, which fought in Belgium and France and was evacuated in 1940.
Case Red (Fall Rot)
The German offensive against the remaining French armies, beginning on 5 June 1940 along the Somme and Aisne.
Armistice
An agreement to cease fighting, distinct from a peace treaty; the Franco-German armistice was signed on 22 June 1940 and took effect on 25 June.
Vichy France
The French regime under Pétain established in July 1940, which governed the unoccupied zone and the empire from Vichy and claimed continuity with the French state.
Free France
The movement led by de Gaulle from London, which claimed to continue France's war and rallied parts of the French empire.
Operation Sea Lion
The German plan to invade Britain in 1940, dependent on air superiority and never carried out; postponed in September and October 1940.
Fighter Command
The RAF command responsible for the air defense of the United Kingdom, led by Hugh Dowding.
Chain Home
The British chain of coastal radar stations that detected incoming aircraft at long range and fed data into Fighter Command's control system.
Dowding System
The integrated system of radar, Observer Corps, filter room, group and sector control, radio communication and ground support that directed fighters in 1940.
Air superiority
A condition in which one side can operate in an air space without prohibitive interference; the Luftwaffe failed to gain it over southern England.
The Blitz
The German bombing campaign against British cities from September 1940 to May 1941, distinct from, though overlapping with, the Battle of Britain.

Edward Rydz-Śmigły
Polish commander in chief in 1939, whose plan of forward defense and decision to withdraw to the southeast were overtaken by German speed and the Soviet invasion.
Władysław Sikorski and Władysław Raczkiewicz
Prime minister and commander in chief, and president, of the Polish government-in-exile formed in autumn 1939.
Tadeusz Kutrzeba
Polish general who led the Bzura counteroffensive in September 1939.
Hans Frank
Nazi official appointed head of the German-occupied General Government in Poland in October 1939.
Fedor von Bock and Gerd von Rundstedt
German army group commanders in Poland and in the west, the latter commanding Army Group A in the decisive 1940 thrust.
Heinz Guderian and Erwin Rommel
German armored commanders at Sedan and Dinant whose forward leadership illustrated decentralized initiative.
Erich von Manstein
Staff officer associated with the revised plan that shifted the main German effort to the Ardennes.
Maurice Gamelin and Maxime Weygand
French commanders in chief before and after 19 May 1940.
Lord Gort
Commander of the British Expeditionary Force, whose decision of 25 May to withdraw to the coast preserved it.
Bertram Ramsay
Flag Officer Dover, who directed Operation Dynamo.
Neville Chamberlain and Winston Churchill
British prime ministers before and after 10 May 1940.
Philippe Pétain
French marshal who headed the government from 16 June 1940 and the Vichy regime from July.
Charles de Gaulle
French officer who broadcast the appeal of 18 June 1940 and led Free France.
Hugh Dowding and Keith Park
The chief of Fighter Command and the commander of 11 Group in the Battle of Britain.
Hermann Göring
Luftwaffe commander whose confidence and decisions shaped the air campaigns.
Dunkirk (Dunkerque)
French port from which Allied forces were evacuated between 26 May and 4 June 1940.
Sedan and the Meuse
The crossing point and river where the German breakthrough of 13 May 1940 occurred.
Narvik and Norway
Northern theater and iron-ore port contested in April to June 1940.
Vichy
Spa town that gave its name to the French regime established in July 1940.
Coventry and London
British cities bombed in the autumn of 1940, central to the history of the Blitz.

Chapter Reflection

The campaigns of 1939 and 1940 matter because they destroyed the assumptions on which interwar European security had rested. The speed with which Poland and France were defeated, and the shock to those who had expected a repetition of 1914 to 1918, ended the belief that the next war would be a slow contest of fortification and attrition, and it showed how decisively organization, doctrine and command could shape outcomes. At the same time, the failure of the same methods against Britain showed that operational brilliance was conditioned by geography, sea power and production.

The chapter also reveals how military events and civilian lives were intertwined. The bombing of Wieluń and Rotterdam, the terror in occupied Poland, the exode in France and the Blitz in Britain were not side effects of battlefield operations but central to the experience of the war and to its political consequences. They shaped the legitimacy of governments, the memory of defeat and the willingness of populations to accept or resist what followed.

These campaigns shaped later developments in several ways. The Polish and French governments-in-exile, and the Free French movement, established the principle that defeat in the field did not end a state's claim to continue, and that principle influenced the postwar settlement. The collapse of France brought Vichy into existence and set the terms of collaboration and resistance in Chapter 120. The occupation of Poland created the conditions in which the genocide of Chapter 121 would take shape. The survival of Britain preserved a base from which the Allied return of Chapter 122 would eventually be launched.

In the larger argument of Volume V, the chapter shows the transition from the diplomatic crisis of the 1930s to a continental war whose course was undetermined. The year 1940 ended with Germany dominant in much of Europe but unable to end the war, and with the decisive campaigns of the Mediterranean, the Balkans and the Soviet Union still to come. Chapter 118 turns to the first two of these.
