War, States, Law, and Sovereignty

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Europeans have rarely agreed on who ought to rule, but across more than two thousand years they have argued continuously about the harder questions beneath that one: who may demand money and service, under what law, within which boundaries, and with whose consent.

Consider four documents separated by centuries. A damaged papyrus now in the collection at Giessen preserves part of an edict issued by the Roman emperor Caracalla in 212 CE, extending Roman citizenship to most free inhabitants of the empire. A set of royal charters sealed in 1215 and reissued repeatedly afterward, now known collectively as Magna Carta, recorded a bargain between an English king and rebellious barons over taxation, justice, and the limits of royal discretion. A pair of treaties concluded at Osnabrück and Münster in October 1648 reorganized the relations among the rulers of the Holy Roman Empire and its neighbors after thirty years of devastating war. A treaty signed at Lisbon in 2007 and in force from 1 December 2009 redistributed responsibilities among the governments of twenty-seven states and the institutions they had jointly created. Each document answers a version of the same question, and none of them settled it permanently.

This chapter is the second of five concluding comparative chapters in Part XXI. It does not retell European political history in sequence, a task already accomplished across the preceding one hundred and forty chapters. Instead it asks how warfare, taxation, administration, law, borders, diplomacy, citizenship, and changing conceptions of sovereignty interacted to produce Europe's remarkably diverse forms of government, and why those forms developed differently across regions and periods. Chapter 140 examined the changing relationship between Christianity, religious pluralism, and secularization, and necessarily touched the authority of churches and states. Chapter 141 broadens that inquiry from religious and political authority to the organization of governmental power as a whole.

A familiar narrative holds that European political history moved from weak, fragmented medieval kingdoms toward powerful centralized states, then toward nation-states, and finally, in the late twentieth century, toward a novel supranational union. This chapter argues that such a narrative is badly incomplete. City-states, republics, empires, kingdoms, composite monarchies, confederations, federations, and international organizations were not stages on a single road but different solutions to recurring problems of governance. Some, such as the Republic of Venice, endured for roughly a thousand years. Others, such as the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, were destroyed by neighbors whose own states had developed along different lines. Understanding why requires attention to institutions, resources, and bargaining as well as armies and ideas.

The relationship between war and political organization operated in both directions. Rulers created administrative and fiscal institutions to wage war, but the institutions already available determined how wars could be financed, fought, resisted, and ended. A monarch who could not persuade assemblies to grant taxes, or creditors to lend money, could not field armies regardless of the theoretical extent of his authority. Conversely, a government able to borrow cheaply and collect revenue reliably could convert modest resources into formidable power. The chapter therefore returns repeatedly to a distinction that structures its whole argument: the power a government claims, the resources it can mobilize, the law that authorizes or limits it, and the territory and population it actually governs are not necessarily identical.

The chapter's evidence is correspondingly varied. Historians reconstruct state formation from legal codes and treaties, but also from tax rolls, exchequer accounts, muster lists, court records, diplomatic correspondence, constitutions, maps, census returns, and the judgments of courts. Each type of source reveals what governments attempted and recorded, which is not always what they achieved. A royal edict demonstrates a claim of authority, and a tax register demonstrates an effort at collection, but only evidence of compliance, evasion, litigation, or revolt shows how those claims were received by the people they affected. Throughout the chapter students are asked to ask who produced a document, for what audience, and what evidence of ordinary experience it leaves out.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the foundations of political authority in the Greek, Roman, and late antique worlds. Division II turns to medieval jurisdictions, in which authority was shared among kings, lords, churches, towns, and communities. Division III follows the military, fiscal, and administrative changes that transformed the capacity of governments between the later Middle Ages and the eighteenth century. Division IV traces sovereignty, diplomacy, constitutionalism, nationalism, and citizenship through the age of revolution and national unification. Division V examines the twentieth-century transformation of state power through total war, the construction of a new international legal order, European integration, and the continuing contest over sovereignty after the Cold War.

The chapter has a further purpose within Volume VI and the textbook as a whole. In the early twenty-first century many Europeans lived under overlapping layers of national constitutional law, European Union law, and the human-rights jurisprudence of the European Court of Human Rights, while a major war on the continent was reminding governments of the continuing importance of territory and force. This is a historically contingent political order, not the predetermined culmination of European history, and the chapter's long comparative perspective is meant to show why. Chapter 142 will extend the argument by examining empire, colonialism, migration, and diaspora, subjects that complicate any account of sovereignty confined to the European continent.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among political authority, legitimacy, jurisdiction, sovereignty, and citizenship, and explain why modern definitions of the state cannot be imposed automatically on ancient and medieval societies.
  2. Compare the civic institutions of Greek city-states and the Roman Republic with the administrative and legal systems of the Roman and Eastern Roman empires.
  3. Explain how medieval lordship, customary law, royal justice, canon law, and representative assemblies distributed authority among rulers, elites, churches, towns, and communities.
  4. Evaluate why historians have debated the concept of feudalism and why medieval authority should not be described as simply weak or lawless.
  5. Analyze how military change, taxation, public credit, and administration shaped the capacity of early modern governments, using comparisons among several states.
  6. Assess competing historical interpretations of the relationship between warfare and state formation without assuming that war uniformly strengthened states.
  7. Interpret the Peace of Westphalia and the later development of territorial sovereignty, and explain why the claim that 1648 created the modern sovereign state is an oversimplification.
  8. Trace the development of diplomacy, international law, and the balance of power from the Renaissance to the Congress of Vienna and identify the limits of each.
  9. Differentiate constitutional government, representative government, popular sovereignty, and democracy, using documents from different periods as evidence.
  10. Explain how nationalism, conscription, public education, and nationality law changed citizenship and why national and state boundaries often failed to coincide.
  11. Connect total war, international legal institutions, and European integration to changing relationships among national governments, supranational authority, and individual rights.
  12. Evaluate whether European political development is better understood as increasing centralization or as recurring negotiation among overlapping forms of power, law, and membership.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is the distinction between the power a government claims and the power it can actually exercise so important for understanding European political history?
  2. In what ways did the need to wage war encourage rulers to build administrative and fiscal institutions, and in what circumstances did war instead destroy or weaken them?
  3. Why did some European regimes secure the consent of taxpayers and creditors more successfully than others, and what difference did that make?
  4. How did legal systems, including customary law, Roman law, canon law, and royal justice, both support and restrain political authority?
  5. Why should historians treat city-states, confederations, composite monarchies, and empires as durable political forms rather than as failed attempts to become centralized states?
  6. How did conceptions of sovereignty change between the sixteenth and twentieth centuries, and which older forms of authority persisted alongside them?
  7. What evidence allows historians to distinguish between formal legal claims, administrative practice, and the experience of ordinary subjects or citizens?
  8. How have the meanings of citizenship and political membership expanded and contracted, and who has been included or excluded at different times?

Historical Context

Because Chapter 141 is a comparative synthesis, its historical context consists of the principal findings of the preceding volumes rather than a fresh introduction. Volume I showed that the Greek poleis and the Roman Republic created civic institutions in which citizens shared political and military obligations (Chapters 8 to 10 and 15), and that Roman imperial rule, from Augustus onward, governed an immense territory through law, a professional army, urban elites, and taxation (Chapters 21 and 22). The transformations of the third to fifth centuries, examined in Chapters 24 to 28, reorganized that system rather than simply abolishing it.

Volume II examined the post-Roman kingdoms, the Carolingian experiment, and the political fragmentation that followed (Chapters 29 to 33), together with the development of lordship (Chapter 34), the Investiture Conflict and the growth of medieval monarchy (Chapters 44 and 45), and the decentralized structure of the Holy Roman Empire (Chapter 46). It also followed Byzantium through the creation of an enduring legal and fiscal tradition (Chapters 36 to 41) and the growth of Poland-Lithuania and other powers beyond the western kingdoms (Chapter 54). Late medieval crises, including the Hundred Years' War (Chapter 51), pushed rulers to demand more from their subjects and prompted the military innovations summarized in Chapter 55.

Volume III supplied the essential comparative material for the early modern state. Chapters 74 to 80 examined taxation, armies, bureaucracy, dynastic empire, and the rival models of France, the Habsburg lands, the Dutch Republic, Britain, Brandenburg-Prussia, and the states of northern and eastern Europe. Chapter 67 examined the Thirty Years' War and the Peace of Westphalia. Volume IV followed revolutionary and Napoleonic transformations of citizenship and law (Chapters 87 to 92), nineteenth-century constitutionalism and nationalism (Chapters 97 to 100), and the late nineteenth-century growth of mass politics and imperial rivalry (Chapters 102, 106, and 107). Volumes V and VI added the world wars, authoritarian regimes, the Cold War, European integration, and the post-Cold War security order.

The evidence on which these chapters drew falls into several categories that students should recognize. Legislative and constitutional texts, such as law codes, charters, constitutions, and treaties, state what rulers and assemblies declared. Fiscal records, such as the English pipe rolls (surviving from 1130), Venetian and Dutch accounts, French tax registers, and Prussian and Habsburg budgets, show what governments tried to collect and spend. Court records, petitions, and chronicles show how disputes were resolved and how subjects reacted. Diplomatic correspondence and treaties show how rulers regarded one another. Statistical sources, including censuses, military returns, and population estimates, become more reliable in the modern period, although their categories always reflect the priorities of the governments that produced them. For many early periods, only elite and institutional records survive, and the voices of taxpayers, conscripts, and subordinate communities must be reconstructed indirectly.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, the word "state" and its modern associations should not be projected backward uncritically. Ancient and medieval societies exercised public authority through institutions very different from a modern government, and describing them as weak states or incomplete nation-states obscures how they actually operated. Second, the later success of particular forms of government should not be mistaken for inevitability. The territorial monarchy, the nation-state, and the European Union each emerged from specific circumstances, and each competed with alternatives that contemporaries regarded as entirely viable.

Division I: The Foundations of Political Authority and State Formation · Section 141.1

What Is a State? Authority, Legitimacy, and Sovereignty

Any comparison of European governments across two and a half thousand years requires a vocabulary that is precise but not anachronistic. Political authority is the recognized right to make decisions that others are expected to obey. Government refers to the persons and offices exercising that authority at a given time, while the state is the more durable institutional order, including offices, rules, records, revenue, and coercive capacity, that outlasts any individual ruler. Legitimacy is the belief, among those who matter politically, that a particular claim to command is rightful. Jurisdiction is the authority to decide disputes and apply law within a defined sphere, and sovereignty, in its fully developed modern sense, is the claim to supreme and independent authority within a territory. Citizenship is the legal and political membership that gives a person recognized rights and duties in relation to a political community.

A frequently cited definition, formulated by the German sociologist Max Weber in a lecture of 1919, describes the state as the institution that successfully claims a monopoly of legitimate physical force within a territory. The definition is useful for modern governments but dangerously misleading for much of European history. Medieval lords, bishops, towns, guilds, and kin groups all exercised recognized coercive powers, and rulers often had to ask their subjects to supply the soldiers they needed. Rulers who attempted to monopolize force, as some early modern monarchs did, were engaged in a long and contested project and not in the application of a timeless definition. The word "state" itself, in its modern political sense, spread in European languages only in the later Middle Ages and Renaissance, partly through the Italian stato (see Chapter 60).

The central analytical distinction of this chapter follows from this. A government may claim authority that it cannot enforce, may control resources that its law does not recognize, may be bound by law it cannot ignore, and may govern territory and populations that do not match the boundaries of its claims. Four things should therefore be kept apart: the power claimed, the resources mobilized, the law that authorizes or limits the government, and the territory and population actually governed. Much European political history consists of efforts to align these four, and of the conflicts that arose when rulers, subjects, rival authorities, or neighboring states disagreed about the alignment.

Authority itself could rest on different foundations. In some societies it was personal, resting on loyalty to a chieftain or lord. In others it was dynastic, passing by inheritance within a ruling family, or religious, resting on anointing, divine sanction, or the leadership of a church. It could be corporate, exercised by councils, guilds, cities, or estates, and territorial, attached to a defined region and its inhabitants. In the era of revolutions it could be claimed to be popular, derived from the people or nation. These foundations were rarely exclusive. A medieval king might simultaneously claim dynastic descent, ecclesiastical anointing, a feudal lord's rights, and the consent of a representative assembly, and different audiences might accept different parts of that claim.

A final difficulty concerns the evidence. Charters, tax rolls, law codes, and treaties survive because governments produced and preserved them, and they necessarily emphasize the state's own account of itself. A law code reveals that a ruler regarded a matter as appropriate to regulate, but the existence of the code does not prove that it was applied. Historians therefore read legal and fiscal records alongside petitions, court cases, chronicles, and evidence of resistance to judge how far claims became practice. This chapter treats centralized and distributed forms of authority as two ends of a spectrum on which most European polities occupied changing and often contradictory positions.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty, Legitimacy, and Jurisdiction

Sovereignty is a claim to supreme authority that is not legally subordinate to any other earthly power within a territory. Medieval writers used related expressions, such as the claim that a king was "emperor in his own kingdom," but systematic theories of sovereignty emerged mainly in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Legitimacy concerns the rightfulness of a claim to rule, and rests on tradition, law, religion, consent, or success in providing order. Jurisdiction concerns the authority to judge and to apply law over particular persons, places, or subject matters.

These three ideas are related but not identical. A ruler could be legitimate without being sovereign (a vassal king, for example), or hold extensive jurisdiction without claiming sovereignty (a bishop with a court). Early modern and modern political thought sought to concentrate all three in a single authority, but European history includes many systems in which they were deliberately divided. A modern reader who equates sovereignty with state power as such will misunderstand most of what follows.

Section 141.2

Greek City-States, Republics, and the Organization of Civic Power

The Greek polis (plural poleis) was a political community of citizens organized around an urban center and its surrounding territory, usually small by later standards. Most poleis restricted citizenship to adult males of citizen descent, excluded women from formal political rights, and held a large unfree or partly free population, including enslaved people and, in many cities, resident foreigners. Citizenship carried duties as well as privileges, above all military service, and many poleis tied political participation to the capacity to equip oneself for the citizen army. The precise relationship between landholding, military service, and rights varied from city to city and remains debated. The evidence consists of inscribed decrees, speeches, histories, philosophical works, and, for Athens, a treatise on its constitution attributed to the school of Aristotle that survived on papyrus and was recovered only in 1890.

Athens, examined in Chapters 10 and 12, developed the most extensive system of direct citizen participation. After the reforms associated with Cleisthenes (about 508/507 BCE), citizens were enrolled in local units called demes and grouped into ten tribes, which selected by lot the fifty members each of a council of five hundred. An assembly open to adult male citizens made major decisions, large juries chosen by lot decided cases, and many offices were filled by lot rather than election. A law of about 451/450 BCE, associated with Pericles, restricted citizenship to those whose parents were both Athenian, illustrating that democratic participation within the citizen body coexisted with tightening exclusion from it. Athens also financed its public life without a regular tax on citizens' property. Wealthy citizens were expected to fund warships, festivals, and choruses as public services, and tribute from the allies of the Delian League, along with revenue from silver mines, paid for much of the fleet. A democracy of citizens thus rested, in part, on an empire over other communities.

Sparta offered a contrasting arrangement. A small body of full citizens, the Spartiates, lived communally and trained for war on the labor of the helots, a conquered and subordinate population, while neighboring free communities, the perioikoi, supplied soldiers and crafts. Authority was shared among two kings, a council of elders, annually chosen officials called ephors, and an assembly. Greek writers, many of them admirers or critics writing from outside, described Sparta as a model of stability, which means that modern reconstruction must treat their accounts cautiously. Beyond individual cities, Greek communities organized federal leagues, among them the Boeotian, Achaean, and Aetolian leagues. The Achaean League of the third and second centuries BCE had federal assemblies, magistrates, and shared institutions while its member cities retained local government, and eighteenth-century constitutional writers later studied such leagues, a reminder that Greek federalism has a longer influence than the single example of Athenian democracy.

The Roman Republic, whose institutions are examined in Chapter 15, represented a different organization of civic power. Annually elected magistrates held imperium, the authority to command, but shared office with colleagues and were subject to review. The Senate, composed mainly of former magistrates, guided policy, finance, and foreign affairs, while citizen assemblies elected officials and passed laws, and tribunes of the plebs could protect citizens against magistrates. Voting in the principal assemblies was organized by groups weighted by property and, in some assemblies, by locality, so that wealth and status shaped political influence. The army was recruited principally from property-owning citizens, and allied Italian communities supplied additional contingents under treaty. The Greek historian Polybius, writing in the second century BCE, analyzed the Roman constitution as a balance of monarchical, aristocratic, and popular elements, an interpretation that later European political theorists would repeatedly use.

Students should resist treating either Athens or Rome as the direct ancestor of modern democracy. Athenian participation was direct rather than representative, depended on slavery and excluded women, and was conducted in an intimate civic setting. Roman citizenship, by contrast, could be held by people living far from the city and could be graded, as when allied Italians were fighting in Rome's armies yet excluded from its political rights. That grievance contributed to the Social War of 91 to 87 BCE, after which citizenship was extended across Italy. Both systems reveal an enduring theme: the link between military obligation and claims to political membership, which would reappear in the age of conscription and mass citizenship (see Section 141.16).

Section 141.3

Rome: Conquest, Imperial Administration, Law, and Citizenship

Roman expansion, traced in Chapters 16 and 17, created administrative demands that republican institutions had not been designed to meet. Governing provinces required officials, tax collection, courts, roads, and garrisons, yet the Republic possessed no permanent bureaucracy. Magistrates with imperium governed provinces for limited terms, and private contractors called publicani bid for the right to collect taxes and supply armies. The system worked well enough to be profitable, and indeed the revenue and plunder from conquest allowed Rome to suspend the direct tax on its own citizens in 167 BCE. It also produced abuses by governors and tax collectors, and in 149 BCE Rome established a standing court to hear provincial complaints of extortion. Conquest therefore generated both fiscal capacity and demands for legal accountability, a pairing that recurs in later European history.

The crisis of the Republic (Chapters 19 and 20) was partly an institutional crisis of how to control armies commanded by ambitious generals, recruited increasingly from poor citizens whose prospects depended on their commanders. Augustus, whose settlement is examined in Chapter 21, preserved republican forms while concentrating military and financial power in the emperor. He created a standing professional army, established a treasury for soldiers' pensions funded by new taxes on inheritances and sales, conducted censuses of provincial populations, and developed a corps of procurators and equestrian officials alongside the senatorial governors. Even so, the empire was governed with remarkably few imperial officials relative to its population, which scholars estimate in the tens of millions. Day-to-day government in the provinces rested on the cities, whose local elites collected taxes, maintained order, administered justice in minor matters, and funded public works in return for status and privileges.

Roman law, examined in connection with institutions in Chapter 22, was both a tool of governance and a body of learned reasoning. The Twelve Tables, traditionally dated to about 450 BCE, recorded early rules. Praetors' edicts adapted procedure, and jurists, whose opinions gained authority under the emperors, developed doctrines of property, contract, inheritance, and status. A distinction between the civil law of Roman citizens and the broader law common to all peoples allowed Roman officials to adjudicate disputes involving non-citizens. Provincial communities continued to use local custom, and papyri from Egypt show people litigating, petitioning officials, and invoking Roman rules strategically. Later emperors commissioned compilations: the Theodosian Code of 438 CE in the West and East, and Justinian's Corpus Iuris Civilis (529 to 534) in Constantinople. The latter, rediscovered and studied in medieval Italy, became the foundation of the continental European legal tradition, as discussed in Section 141.6.

Citizenship spread through colonies, grants to individuals and communities, and service in the auxiliary forces, whose discharged soldiers received citizenship recorded on bronze diplomas that survive as evidence. A famous inscription from Lyon preserves a speech of the emperor Claudius (about 48 CE) arguing for the admission of Gallic notables to the Senate, which shows both the arguments for inclusion and the resistance to it. The Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 CE, a decree of Caracalla partly preserved in the Giessen papyrus, extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire, with exceptions whose exact meaning is debated. The motive is also disputed. The contemporary historian Cassius Dio claimed that the emperor wished to increase tax revenue, since citizens paid taxes on inheritance and manumission, but other scholars emphasize ideological or administrative purposes. What historians can say with confidence is that the edict ended the legal division between citizens and most subjects.

The extension of citizenship did not create equality. By the second and third centuries, legal treatment, especially in criminal punishment, increasingly depended on whether a person belonged to the privileged honestiores (senators, equestrians, soldiers, and municipal elites) or the humbler humiliores. Slaves, who could be freed but remained legally subordinate to their former owners in important respects, were governed by a body of law that treated them both as persons and as property. Women could hold property and in practice often managed it, but were excluded from magistracies and public voting. Citizenship in the Roman empire was thus primarily a legal status connected to property, family, and law, as distinct from the political participation associated with the Republican assemblies, which had largely disappeared. Distinguishing formal citizenship from political equality is essential for every later period in this chapter.

Section 141.4

Late Antiquity and the Transformation of Roman Political Authority

The third-century crisis (Chapter 24) and the reforms of Diocletian and Constantine (Chapter 25) produced a larger and more differentiated imperial government. Provincial units were multiplied and grouped into larger regions, civil and military command were increasingly separated, and a bureaucracy of salaried officials expanded. Taxation was reorganized around assessments of land and labor, backed by registers and, increasingly, by payments in kind to supply the army, and Constantine's gold coin, the solidus, provided a stable currency for state payments. The empire of the fourth century was in some respects more thoroughly administered than that of Augustus, although it also bore heavier fiscal burdens, which fell most severely on tenant farmers and small landowners whose obligations were enforced by officials and by landlords.

In the West, the processes examined in Chapters 27 and 28 produced successor kingdoms, including the Ostrogoths in Italy, the Visigoths in Gaul and later Iberia, the Burgundians, the Vandals in North Africa, the Franks in Gaul, and the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms in Britain. Their rulers faced the problem of governing populations of Roman descent while supporting warrior followings. Many relied on Roman administrators and legal practices. The Ostrogothic king Theodoric (died 526) governed Italy through Roman officials and the Senate, and the letters compiled by his minister Cassiodorus preserve the language of Roman administration. The Visigothic kings issued a code for their Roman subjects, the Breviary of Alaric (506), drawing on Roman law, and the Franks recorded their own customary law in Latin in the Lex Salica. The earliest English law, issued by King Æthelberht of Kent around 600, was written in Old English, signaling both the writing of royal law and a weaker continuity with Roman institutions in Britain.

How these kingdoms supported armies is an interpretive question treated further in Chapter 29. Some scholars, notably Walter Goffart, have argued that settlement involved shares of Roman tax revenue assigned to soldiers rather than the seizure of land. Others, including Chris Wickham, emphasize that the old tax system decayed in much of the West and that kings and aristocrats came to depend increasingly on rents from land and on personal followings, which changed the kind of state that could exist. These positions differ about mechanisms, evidence, and the speed and regional pattern of change, and neither suggests that Roman institutions vanished overnight. The most useful conclusion for the comparison pursued here is that fiscal resources, military organization, and the relations of kings with local elites and bishops determined what each kingdom could accomplish, more than a simple distinction between the survival and collapse of Rome.

The Eastern Roman Empire, examined in Chapters 36 and 37, preserved a tax-collecting state and a Greek-speaking bureaucracy even after the loss of provinces in the seventh century to Persian and then Arab conquests. Contraction forced reorganization. Large provincial armies were settled in military districts, later called themes, and emperors depended on a smaller fiscal base concentrated in Anatolia and the Balkans. Law continued to develop in Greek, through the Ecloga of the eighth century and the Basilika, a large Greek restatement of Justinianic law completed in the late ninth or early tenth century. Eastern Roman governance rested on a vocabulary of continuity, since its rulers regarded their empire as Roman, and modern scholars use the term "Byzantine" for convenience. This continuity helps explain why the empire survived for another eight centuries in changing forms, until the Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453 (Chapter 41).

Across the former empire, the Christian church became an institution of governance. Bishops mediated between communities and rulers, arbitrated disputes, administered charity, and in some cities took on tasks that municipal councils had once performed. Church courts, councils, and a developing body of canon law (Chapter 26) created jurisdictions parallel to royal and imperial ones. Late antique legal practice also recognized that different peoples might be judged by their own laws, a principle sometimes called the personality of law, which survived into the early Middle Ages. The key transition of the period was therefore from a single, bureaucratically administered empire toward a plurality of political authorities, secular and ecclesiastical, whose relationships had to be negotiated. Division II examines the results.

Division II: Medieval Jurisdictions and Competing Political Orders · Section 141.5

Lordship, Landholding, and Fragmented Political Authority

The Carolingian rulers examined in Chapter 31 governed through counts, bishops, royal envoys called missi dominici, written capitularies, and assemblies of the aristocracy. After the division of the empire and the pressures of Viking, Magyar, and Muslim raiding examined in Chapter 32, effective authority in many regions of western Europe passed to local lords who built fortifications, judged disputes, and demanded service and payments from the people around them. Historians such as Georges Duby described a transformation around the year 1000 in which public justice was replaced by private lordship. Other scholars, among them Dominique Barthélemy and Timothy Reuter, argued that the change was more gradual and that older public institutions survived in many places. The debate illustrates a recurring problem: the surviving sources, mostly produced by monastic and ecclesiastical writers, may exaggerate violence and rupture in some regions while recording continuity elsewhere.

Lordship, as described in Chapter 34, connected landholding to jurisdiction. A lord held land and often judicial and financial rights over the dependents who farmed it, who owed rents, labor services, and dues. Vassalage was a distinct personal relationship in which a warrior swore homage and fealty to a lord in exchange for protection and usually a grant, called a fief, of land or revenue. These relationships varied greatly across regions and centuries, and the neat pyramid of kings, lords, and vassals found in older textbooks represents a later simplification. In an influential study published in 1994, Susan Reynolds argued that the modern concept of a coherent feudal system rests on legal categories developed by scholars from the twelfth century onward and projected backward onto earlier practice. Elizabeth Brown had made a similar argument two decades earlier, describing the label as a construct that obscures more than it explains.

Students should therefore avoid describing medieval political authority as either weak or lawless. Public institutions persisted and, in some places, were remarkably effective. Anglo-Saxon England was divided into shires and hundreds, each with courts and officials, and levied a national tax, the geld, before the Norman Conquest of 1066, as noted in Chapter 45. The Domesday survey of 1086 required royal commissioners to record landholding and resources across most of England, and the pipe rolls, the annual accounts of the royal exchequer, survive from 1130. In the Ottonian kingdom, discussed in Chapter 46, kings relied on bishops and abbots who held substantial lands and acted as agents of royal government, a practice which later became one source of the Investiture Conflict examined in Chapter 44. Authority was often local, personal, and negotiated, but it was not absent.

Regional variation was extensive. Along the frontiers of Iberia, Christian rulers granted privileges called fueros to settlers and town militias in exchange for military service. In northern Italy, urban communes organized their own governments, courts, and armies and defied imperial claims. In Scandinavia, assemblies of free landowners, called things, made and proclaimed law, and Iceland established a general assembly, the Althing, in about 930 and maintained a commonwealth without a king until the thirteenth century. In Rus lands, princes and their retinues shared power with city assemblies and local elites (Chapter 39), while in Byzantium emperors continued to claim authority over a hierarchical bureaucracy and a landed aristocracy whose power grew over time. These examples do not fit a single trajectory from weakness to strength.

Private violence was also regulated rather than absent. Customary rules recognized feud, a form of retaliation to settle injuries, but churches promoted the Peace of God and the Truce of God, which sought to protect clergy, peasants, and certain days from warfare, and kings and princes later issued general peace ordinances. In the German lands lawful feuding persisted until the imperial Perpetual Public Peace of 1495, a reminder, to which Section 141.8 returns, that the monopolization of force by public authority was a gradual achievement. The lord's court, the royal court, and the church court coexisted, and which one a particular person could use depended on status, location, and the matter in dispute, as the next section explains. Dependents, including serfs, usually had restricted access to justice against their own lords, and the extent of unfreedom varied widely, as Chapter 143 will examine more fully.

Section 141.6

Medieval Law: Custom, Royal Justice, and Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction

Medieval Europeans lived under several overlapping layers of law. Custom, the remembered practice of a locality or social group, regulated land tenure, inheritance, and local disputes and was often unwritten. Royal law developed through charters, ordinances, and judgments. Roman law, revived as a learned subject, supplied concepts and procedures. Canon law, the law of the church, governed clergy and many matters affecting laypeople. Cities issued their own statutes, and merchants developed practices, sometimes called the law merchant, for commercial disputes. Religious minorities also operated within legal frameworks. Jewish communities in many realms maintained their own courts for internal disputes under privileges granted by rulers, and in parts of Iberia Muslim communities under Christian rule, called mudéjars, retained religious judges for matters of family and community law for varying periods. Law was thus a system of plural jurisdictions rather than a uniform code.

The twelfth century saw a major intellectual and institutional shift. Teachers at Bologna, beginning with Irnerius around 1100, studied Justinian's compilation systematically, producing glosses and commentaries that culminated in the large standard gloss compiled by Accursius in the early thirteenth century. The monk Gratian compiled the Decretum, a systematic collection of church law, around 1140, and Pope Gregory IX issued an authoritative collection of papal decrees in 1234. Together, Roman and canon law formed what scholars call the ius commune, a shared learned tradition taught in the universities discussed in Chapter 48. University-trained jurists entered royal, papal, and municipal service, and their methods influenced legislation and procedure. The Constitutions of Melfi, issued in 1231 for the Kingdom of Sicily by Frederick II, show how a ruler could combine Roman concepts with administrative control, and in Castile the Siete Partidas, compiled under Alfonso X in the thirteenth century, drew heavily on Roman and canon law, although its practical authority developed unevenly.

England developed a distinctive system of royal justice, examined in Chapter 45. Under Henry II (reigned 1154 to 1189) the crown expanded the use of standard writs, itinerant royal judges, and inquiries by groups of local people sworn to report accurately, as in the Assize of Clarendon of 1166. Royal courts offered procedures that many litigants preferred to local or baronial courts, and they generated fees and fines that benefited the crown. Over time, the decisions of these courts created a body of law common across the kingdom, and treatises such as the one attributed to Glanvill (about 1188) and the later work associated with Bracton described it. A decision of the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215, which prohibited clergy from participating in trial by ordeal, contributed to the shift toward trial by jury in England. The system extended the power of the crown, but it also created a body of rules and professional lawyers who would later use the law against royal claims.

The continental pattern differed. In France, the royal court, which by the thirteenth century included a specialized judicial body that became the Parlement of Paris, heard appeals from lords' courts, and Louis IX (reigned 1226 to 1270) restricted judicial combat in the royal domain. Northern France remained a region of customary law, which jurists such as Philippe de Beaumanoir recorded in the late thirteenth century, while the south was more influenced by written Roman law. In the German lands, legal compilations such as the Sachsenspiegel (about 1220 to 1235) recorded custom in the vernacular, while territorial princes and imperial institutions worked to preserve peace. Different regions therefore accommodated the revived learned law in different degrees, and by the sixteenth century continental Europe was divided between jurisdictions strongly shaped by Roman law and England, whose lawyers prided themselves on a separate tradition.

Ecclesiastical courts competed and cooperated with secular ones. They claimed jurisdiction over clergy, marriage, wills, tithes, and offenses such as heresy and blasphemy, and their procedures included written pleadings and, in inquisitorial cases, interrogation. A bull of Pope Innocent IV in 1252 authorized the use of torture in inquisitions against heresy, indicating how the revival of learned procedure could also enlarge coercion. Conflicts over the boundaries of church and royal jurisdiction, such as the dispute between Henry II and Thomas Becket over the Constitutions of Clarendon (1164), remained persistent. Competing jurisdictions could constrain rulers, because litigants could appeal to alternatives, but they could also multiply conflicts. Students should distinguish the existence of law from equality before it. Free and unfree persons, men and women, Christians and Jews, nobles and commoners, and clergy and laypeople encountered different rules and different chances of obtaining justice.

Historical Vocabulary
Feudalism and Legal Pluralism

Feudalism is a term coined by later writers, not used by medieval people, to describe a system of political and social relations organized around lordship, vassalage, and the fief. Historians disagree about whether it names a coherent medieval system, a fragmented set of regional practices, or a retrospective legal and ideological construction. The related adjective feudal is also used by some Marxist historians to describe an economic and social system based on the extraction of surplus from dependent peasants, a broader usage that should be distinguished from the narrower legal meaning.

Legal pluralism describes a situation in which several legal systems operate within the same territory, each applying to particular persons, places, or matters. Medieval Europe was characterized by legal pluralism, with royal, seigneurial, ecclesiastical, municipal, mercantile, and community law coexisting. The concept helps students avoid the assumption that a single law should be expected to apply to everyone, an expectation that arose mainly with the modern territorial state, and it reminds them that overlapping jurisdictions gave some people more choices and others fewer protections.

Section 141.7

Kings, Councils, Estates, and the Negotiation of Power

Medieval rulers regularly needed resources that they could not command by routine right. Wars, ransoms, marriages, and crusades required money and service, while the ruler's own revenues from estates, justice, and customary dues were often insufficient. Kings therefore consulted their great men, and from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries they increasingly summoned representatives of towns and lesser landowners as well. Lawyers and churchmen adapted a principle from Roman and canon law, that what touches all should be approved by all, to justify seeking consent for exceptional demands. Representation in this period meant that a delegate with full authority spoke for a community or corporate body and could bind it, as distinct from modern representation of individual voters.

The English example is the best documented. In 1215, after the loss of Normandy and years of heavy financial demands, King John faced a rebellion by barons and agreed to a charter at Runnymede on 15 June. It addressed feudal payments such as the sum owed on inheriting an estate, the rights of widows, access to royal justice, the conduct of officials, and the principle that certain taxes should not be levied without the common counsel of the kingdom. Pope Innocent III annulled the charter within weeks, and civil war followed. Under John's son Henry III the charter was reissued in 1216, 1217, and, in its definitive form, 1225, in exchange for a tax grant, and the clause on consent to taxation was dropped. Magna Carta was then neither a democratic constitution nor a statement of universal rights. It was a negotiated settlement among elites that nonetheless articulated the idea that the king was bound by law, which later generations, particularly in the seventeenth century, would reinterpret.

The institution of Parliament developed through further crises. The Provisions of Oxford (1258) sought to impose baronial supervision on Henry III, and Simon de Montfort summoned an assembly in 1265 that included knights of the shires and burgesses from towns. Under Edward I, assemblies that included representatives of shires and towns met regularly, and the summons of 1295, often called the Model Parliament, explicitly invoked the principle that what touches all must be approved by all. The confirmation of the charters in 1297 recognized that extraordinary taxation required consent. By the fourteenth century the Commons were established as a distinct body whose agreement was needed for taxation and whose petitions shaped legislation. In France, the first general assembly of the three estates (clergy, nobility, and towns) was convened by Philip IV in 1302 amid his conflict with Pope Boniface VIII. The Estates-General met intermittently, in crisis during the 1350s under Étienne Marcel when the king was a prisoner of the English, and the last meeting before the Revolution took place in 1614. Provincial estates in places such as Languedoc, Brittany, and Burgundy continued to negotiate taxes with the crown.

In Iberia, assemblies called Cortes included representatives of towns from the late twelfth century; those of León in 1188 are frequently cited as an early example. The kingdoms of the Crown of Aragon developed powerful assemblies and legal institutions, such as the Justicia of Aragon, the privileges granted in 1283 and 1287, and Catalonia's later standing committee, the Generalitat, which collected and supervised taxes. In Hungary, the Golden Bull of 1222 granted privileges to nobles, including a clause on resistance to royal violations. Sweden's Riksdag came to include peasants as an estate, as did assemblies in Tyrol and Frisia, while in Poland and Lithuania local noble assemblies and the national Sejm gradually acquired authority over taxation and legislation, as discussed in Chapter 54. The Reichstag of the Holy Roman Empire, organized in separate colleges of electors, princes, and cities, will be examined in Section 141.8.

Students should be cautious in interpreting these assemblies. They represented privileged groups and corporate bodies, not individuals, and their members were selected by methods ranging from election to royal nomination. Their strength depended heavily on circumstances. In England, rulers who needed money repeatedly had to return to Parliament. In France, the crown's ability to levy a standing tax on its subjects, developed after the 1430s, allowed kings to avoid summoning the Estates-General, while in Castile revenue sources such as a sales tax reduced royal dependence on the Cortes. The historian Thomas Ertman has argued that the character of local government and the timing of military pressure shaped whether European states developed representative institutions, and his analysis shows why later constitutional outcomes cannot be read back from medieval assemblies. Representation was a means of negotiating privilege and obligation, and its later association with democracy was neither inevitable nor continuous.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxes, Courts, and the Medieval Household

For most medieval households, political authority was encountered through payments and obligations rather than constitutional debate. A peasant family might owe rent, labor on the lord's land, a portion of the harvest to the church as tithe, fees for using the lord's mill or oven, and, in an emergency, a royal tax. When England introduced poll taxes in the late fourteenth century, the resentment they created contributed to the rebellion of 1381, examined in Chapter 52. Manorial court records, which survive in large numbers in England, show villagers settling disputes over boundaries and debts, regulating the use of common fields, and punishing minor offenses, and they reveal that villagers often shaped local law themselves.

Participation in justice was often a burden as well as a privilege. Men were required to serve as jurors, answer summonses, raise the hue and cry when a crime occurred, and, in England, participate in groups that guaranteed one another's good conduct. Travel to a distant court was costly, and the language of the proceedings might be Latin, French, or a vernacular unfamiliar to the parties. Women's legal position depended on status and marital condition. A married woman's property was typically under her husband's control, while a widow might hold a portion of the estate for life and in some places conducted business in her own name. Jews were often subject to special royal taxation and protections, as in England until the expulsion of 1290, and in the empire they were regarded as servants of the royal chamber, a status that gave the ruler a financial interest in them and offered only precarious security. Taxes, courts, and household strategies thus connected ordinary people to the political structures described in this chapter.

Section 141.8

Empires, Republics, Confederations, and Europe's Alternative Political Systems

Europe contained many durable political systems that did not resemble a centralized territorial monarchy. The Holy Roman Empire, examined in Chapter 46, survived from the coronation of Otto I in 962 until its dissolution in 1806, although its structure changed dramatically. The emperor was elected by a group of princes, formally fixed as seven electors in the Golden Bull of 1356, while territorial princes, prince-bishops, imperial cities, and lesser nobles held substantial rights of local rule. Late fifteenth-century reforms created institutions to manage this complexity, including the Perpetual Public Peace of 1495, the Imperial Chamber Court (Reichskammergericht), and the grouping of territories in imperial circles. The imperial courts handled many cases, including appeals by subjects against their territorial rulers, which reveals that the empire functioned as a legal and constitutional framework even when the emperor had limited coercive power.

Older interpretations, partly shaped by the nineteenth-century desire for a unified Germany, treated the empire as a failure. The jurist Samuel Pufendorf, writing in 1667, had already described it as an irregular body resembling a monster. More recent scholars, among them Peter Wilson, argue that the empire provided a durable framework of law, arbitration, and protection for small states, and that judging it by the standard of a modern state misses its function. It was not an incomplete Germany, as the refinements governing this textbook caution, and its territory included Italian, Bohemian, and Burgundian lands as well as German-speaking ones.

Italian republics presented another model. Venice's Great Council was closed to new families in 1297, effectively creating a hereditary patrician governing class, while an elected doge, councils, and later a Council of Ten balanced individual power. The republic financed its maritime empire in part through a funded public debt, and Genoa and Florence also developed forms of public credit (see Chapter 56). Italian cities experienced factional conflict and often turned to rule by single families or lords, but Venice endured until Napoleon's forces ended the republic in 1797. The Swiss Confederation developed from alliances among Alpine communities, traditionally dated from 1291, and expanded in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries into a league of cantons with differing constitutions, some governed by communal assemblies. Swiss soldiers became renowned across Europe, while the Confederation's separation from imperial jurisdiction received recognition in 1648. It became a federal state only after a brief civil war and a new constitution in 1848. The Dutch Republic, whose revolt against Habsburg Spain is discussed in Chapters 66 and 77, was likewise a confederation of provinces in which the States General, provincial estates, city governments, and the office of stadtholder (a provincial governor) shared power.

Poland and Lithuania entered a dynastic union following the agreement of Krewo in 1385, but remained distinct political communities. The Union of Lublin in 1569 established the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, an elective monarchy in which a numerous nobility, perhaps eight to ten percent of the population by common estimates, held extensive political rights, deliberated in local and national assemblies, and defended what it called its liberties. The Warsaw Confederation of 1573 guaranteed religious toleration among nobles of different confessions. The system limited royal power, and in the seventeenth century the practice emerged that a single deputy could dissolve a Sejm session by objecting, known as the liberum veto, which hindered action. The Commonwealth collected relatively low revenue and maintained small standing forces, and it was partitioned by Russia, Prussia, and Austria in 1772, 1793, and 1795. Historians now emphasize that internal paralysis was only one factor, and that great-power rivalry and the military pressure of neighbors with more effective fiscal systems were decisive. The Constitution of 3 May 1791 represented a serious reform effort that ended with the state's destruction (Chapter 79).

The Ottoman Empire governed large parts of southeastern and east-central Europe from the fourteenth to the early twentieth century (see Chapters 41 and 79). Its government combined sultanic authority, a body of sultanic regulations called kanun, and Islamic law applied by judges. Revenues from agricultural land were assigned to cavalrymen under the timar system, and from the sixteenth century cash taxation and tax farming grew, with life-term tax contracts introduced in 1695. Non-Muslim communities paid special taxes and managed religious and communal matters through their own institutions, including Orthodox, Armenian, and Jewish authorities, although the term millet for this arrangement was systematized later than often assumed. Wallachia, Moldavia, and Transylvania were tributary principalities with their own rulers or assemblies, and the maritime republic of Ragusa (Dubrovnik) paid tribute and maintained wide autonomy. Treating the Ottoman Empire as an external occupier of Europe would obscure these arrangements, which governed millions of Europeans and interacted continuously with Habsburg and Venetian diplomacy.

The political scientist Hendrik Spruyt has argued that city-states, urban leagues, and territorial monarchies competed in later medieval and early modern Europe, and that the territorial state did not prevail because of any inherent superiority but because particular conditions favored its fiscal and diplomatic advantages. The point is crucial for the comparison undertaken here. Layered jurisdiction, negotiated authority, and confederal organization were not primitive stages that preceded the modern state, and they must be assessed by what they accomplished, including security, justice, commerce, and religious accommodation, in their own contexts. Division III turns to the forces that changed the balance between these alternatives.

Division III: Warfare, Fiscal Power, and the Early Modern State · Section 141.9

The Changing Military Foundations of European Political Power

Medieval armies were assembled in several ways. Kings and lords relied on household retinues, on tenants who owed military service for limited periods, and on the towns' militias. As campaigns lengthened, rulers increasingly paid soldiers through contracts, as with the indentures through which English kings hired captains in the fourteenth century, and commuted service into money, as with the English scutage. Urban militias could defeat armored cavalry, as the Flemish townsmen demonstrated at Courtrai in 1302, and the infantry of the Swiss cantons became a model for later armies. During pauses in the Hundred Years' War (Chapter 51), unemployed soldiers formed free companies that pillaged the countryside, and in Italy captains called condottieri served cities and princes under written contracts, called condotte. Military power therefore depended on the capacity to pay and to control those who were paid.

Gunpowder artillery, discussed in Chapter 55, changed siegecraft in the fifteenth century. The French royal artillery contributed to the rapid reduction of English-held fortresses in Normandy and Gascony in 1449 to 1453, and the Ottoman guns used against Constantinople in 1453 helped end the Byzantine Empire. Defenders responded with low, thick earthen bastions, an Italian design called the trace italienne, which became widespread after the French invasion of Italy in 1494. Such fortifications were expensive, protracted sieges, and required larger garrisons and besieging armies. Armies also integrated pikemen and musketeers, and commanders such as Maurice of Nassau in the Dutch Republic drilled infantry intensively in the 1590s, while the Spanish tercios and later Swedish formations under Gustavus Adolphus combined firepower and shock.

Historians have long debated whether these developments constituted a "Military Revolution." The historian Michael Roberts proposed the term in 1955 for tactical changes between about 1560 and 1660. Geoffrey Parker later broadened the argument, stressing fortification and the growth of army size from about 1500 to 1800. Critics have questioned the chronology and causation. Some, such as Clifford Rogers, have found important changes in the late medieval period, and others, including David Parrott, have emphasized that private contractors and entrepreneurs, not state bureaucracies, organized much seventeenth-century warfare. Jeremy Black questioned the term itself and argued that change was more gradual and varied. The most defensible conclusion is that military technology and organization changed significantly, but that the consequences depended on the political and fiscal conditions of each state, and that Ottoman, Russian, and western European armies adopted similar techniques at different speeds.

Naval power added further demands. The Venetian Arsenal produced galleys on an assembly-line basis, while Atlantic powers invested in sailing warships and supported commerce raiders. The failure of Spain's Armada in 1588, the Anglo-Dutch wars, and the naval buildup of France under Louis XIV revealed that sea power required shipyards, timber, officers, and credit. Chapter 71 and Chapter 72 showed that the same commercial companies and navies that sustained overseas empire also supplied revenue and strategic strength to European states.

Military organization also determined how rulers related to their subjects. Rulers who depended on contractors, such as Albrecht von Wallenstein, who raised large armies for the emperor during the Thirty Years' War, risked losing control over their forces. The mutiny of unpaid Spanish troops in the Netherlands and the sack of Antwerp in November 1576, known as the Spanish Fury, showed how financial failure turned an army into a threat to the ruler's own subjects and policies. Over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, rulers sought to bring recruitment, pay, and discipline under direct state control, though officers' commissions were often purchased and aristocratic officer corps retained substantial independence. In the Habsburg lands, a Military Frontier organized in the sixteenth century settled soldier-farmers, many of them Orthodox Serbs and Catholic Croats, along the border with the Ottoman Empire, linking military obligation to land and special legal status. The costs and consequences of these changes led directly to the problem of finance examined next.

Section 141.10

Taxation, Public Credit, and the Fiscal-Military State

Early modern governments raised revenue from many sources: income from royal or princely estates, customs and tolls, direct taxes on land or households, excise taxes on consumption, monopolies such as the French salt tax, the sale of offices and privileges, and loans. War expenditure repeatedly outran ordinary revenue, and the capacity to supplement it determined which governments could sustain long conflicts. The financial history of Habsburg Spain illustrates the dangers. Silver from the Americas (see Chapters 69 and 75) supplemented Castilian taxes, but the crown also borrowed heavily from bankers, and it suspended payments to creditors repeatedly, in 1557, 1575, 1596, and on later occasions, damaging confidence and forcing renegotiation. The wealth of an empire did not prevent insolvency when expenditures were larger still.

The Dutch Republic, with far fewer natural resources, developed unusually effective finance. The provinces and cities, particularly Holland and Amsterdam, funded the long war with Spain through excise taxes on consumption and loans in the form of bonds that were backed by specific revenues. The regents who governed the cities were often themselves creditors and taxpayers, which gave investors confidence that the debt would be honored, and interest rates were low for the period. Amsterdam's commercial institutions, including its exchange bank founded in 1609, supported a deep capital market. The Dutch case shows that a decentralized, confederal system could be fiscally powerful, because credit depended on institutional trust and not only on a central coercive apparatus.

England's fiscal transformation followed the political settlement after 1688. The civil wars and the Interregnum had introduced new excise taxes, and after the Glorious Revolution the principle that Parliament controlled taxation was secure. The Bank of England, established in 1694, organized loans to the government, and the national debt expanded through the wars against Louis XIV and in the eighteenth century. John Brewer, in The Sinews of Power (1989), described Britain as a "fiscal-military state" with high taxes, an efficient revenue administration, especially the Excise, and public credit that enabled it to sustain a large navy and army. Some economists and historians, including Douglass North and Barry Weingast, argued that creditors trusted a government answerable to Parliament, whereas others have questioned how far institutions alone explain the result, pointing to commercial wealth, naval strength, and the political coalition behind the war effort.

France, with a larger population and economy, had a less efficient fiscal system. Taxes fell unevenly. The nobility and clergy enjoyed extensive exemptions, regions such as Brittany and Languedoc retained their own estates and bargained over contributions, and private financiers collected many taxes through tax farming, while the crown also sold offices, whose holders gained status and an income. This system enabled the monarchy to borrow against future revenue and to sustain Louis XIV's wars, but it made reform politically difficult. By the 1780s the interest payments on debt consumed a very large share of royal revenue, commonly estimated at nearly half, and the failure of ministers such as Calonne and Necker to secure agreement on new taxes led to the summoning of the Estates-General in 1789 (Chapter 87). The crisis resulted from the interaction of war debt, privilege, and the monarchy's reliance on institutions that it could not reform without negotiation.

Other states followed distinctive paths. In the Habsburg monarchy, the reforms after 1748 associated with Friedrich Wilhelm von Haugwitz centralized tax collection in the Austrian and Bohemian lands, while the Hungarian nobility preserved its exemption from taxation and its diet. Court financiers, among them Jewish bankers such as Samuel Oppenheimer, who supplied Leopold I's armies, and later Dutch lenders, supported Habsburg wars. Prussia devoted a majority of its modest revenues to its army, relying on strict administration, and Russia introduced a per-capita tax in the early eighteenth century to support its army. The Ottoman Empire expanded tax farming and in 1695 created life-term contracts, which strengthened local notables and altered the balance between center and provinces. Fiscal success depended on credit as much as revenue, and credit depended on trust. Coercion could still provoke resistance, as tax revolts in France (the Croquants, the Bonnets Rouges of 1675), Naples in 1647, and Catalonia in 1640 demonstrated, and the Fronde of 1648 to 1653 combined fiscal grievances with elite opposition to royal government.

Historical Vocabulary
The Fiscal-Military State

The fiscal-military state is a term used by historians, notably John Brewer, to describe governments whose organization was shaped by the need to finance and sustain large armed forces, through taxation, administrative machinery, and public borrowing. It is a descriptive label for a capacity, not a single constitutional type. Britain, the Dutch Republic, France, Austria, Prussia, and others all developed fiscal-military capabilities, but through very different political bargains, and their outcomes differed sharply.

The term should not be taken to imply that a state devoted to war was necessarily efficient, centralized, or modern. It also reminds students that creditors, tax collectors, contractors, and representative assemblies were as important as generals, and that a state's ability to borrow depended on whether lenders believed that debts would be repaid.

Historical Debate

Did Warfare Create Stronger European States?

The question: Many historians argue that the pressures of war, particularly between the later Middle Ages and the eighteenth century, forced European rulers to build more effective systems of taxation, administration, and credit. Others argue that this account is too simple. To what extent, and in what circumstances, did war strengthen states?

Interpretation A: War as a driver of state formation. The sociologist and historian Charles Tilly, in Coercion, Capital, and European States (1990), argued that European states took shape through preparation for and conduct of war, summarized in his remark that war made the state and the state made war. In this view, rulers extracted resources from their populations, built administrations, and bargained with capital-holders, and the states that proved most successful at combining coercion and capital prevailed. Brewer's work on Britain and studies of Prussia and the Dutch Republic support the argument that war expenditure expanded administrative and fiscal institutions.
Interpretation B: War as a conditional and sometimes destructive force. Other historians emphasize that war did not strengthen all states. Spain suffered repeated bankruptcies, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth was partitioned, and the German lands were devastated in the Thirty Years' War. Thomas Ertman argued that the form of local government and the presence of representative institutions before intense military competition shaped outcomes, while Hendrik Spruyt emphasized that the territorial state's advantages depended on how it handled competing forms of organization. Philip Hoffman has stressed that competition with high rewards for military success fostered innovation but also made war frequent. Critics add that factors such as religion, law, legal learning, and commercial growth also shaped state capacity.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on fiscal data (revenues, expenditures, and debt), army sizes, accounts of administrative growth, case studies of rebellion and bankruptcy, and comparisons among states. Disputes arise partly because estimates of revenue and army size vary and partly because the cases selected for comparison affect the conclusions.

Why it matters: If war alone explains state-building, then the rise of centralized territorial states appears nearly inevitable. If it does not, then the particular institutions, bargains, and resources of each polity become central, and confederations, republics, and composite monarchies appear as real alternatives, not failures.

Section 141.11

Standing Armies, Mobilization, Logistics, and Civilian Society

Armies grew substantially between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, though exact figures are uncertain and historians rely on muster rolls, paymaster accounts, and contemporary estimates that often differ. French armies under Louis XIV reached several hundred thousand men at their peak, according to scholarly estimates, and the forces of the great powers in the Seven Years' War were larger than those of any earlier European conflict. Recruitment methods varied. Rulers paid enlistment bounties to volunteers, impressed men into service, hired entire units from other rulers, and compelled peasants. In Brandenburg-Prussia, a cantonal system from 1733 assigned regiments to districts from which they drew recruits, while Russia imposed a recruit levy on communities from 1705. Britain relied heavily on volunteers, the press gang for the navy, and subsidized foreign troops.

Supplying armies was a continual problem. Troops required food, fodder for horses, weapons, clothing, and shelter, and could not simply live off territory without devastating it. Commanders established magazines (storage depots), used rivers and roads, and imposed regular "contributions" on occupied territory, a method associated with Wallenstein's armies in the Thirty Years' War. Seasonal campaigning was constrained by the availability of pasture and the condition of roads. Disease killed far more soldiers than combat, and rulers established veterans' hospitals, such as the Hôtel des Invalides in Paris (begun in 1670) and the Royal Hospital at Chelsea in England, as part of an emerging administrative responsibility for soldiers' welfare. Military administration grew into one of the largest components of government, and officials who managed supply, payment, and recruitment formed an expanding corps of civil servants.

Civilians bore the burdens of war directly. Soldiers were quartered in private homes or towns, local authorities were required to supply food and transport, and armies demanded payments from regions they occupied. The destruction of Magdeburg in 1631 became a symbol of the Thirty Years' War, and across large parts of the German lands the combination of fighting, plague, and food shortage caused serious population loss. Overall losses are often estimated at around a fifth or more of the population, with much higher figures in the worst-affected districts, though historians continue to revise regional estimates. Quartered soldiers could also be used as an instrument of policy, as when Louis XIV's dragonnades in the 1680s used billeted troops to pressure Protestant families to convert (Chapter 76). Soldiers' wives and children often followed armies or depended on remittances, and wounded or elderly veterans could find themselves dependent on charity.

The transformation of mobilization during the French Revolution altered these relationships. The levée en masse decreed in August 1793 proclaimed that the whole population was obliged to serve the war effort, and the Jourdan law of 1798 institutionalized conscription, creating large citizen armies whose size and ideological commitment surprised older monarchies (Chapters 89 and 90). Prussia, after defeat by Napoleon, introduced a broader system of service in 1814 (see Chapter 91). Britain did not introduce general conscription until 1916. Conscription redefined military service as an obligation of citizenship, and it linked the state to individual families on an unprecedented scale. The consequences for taxation, citizenship, and national identity are explored in Section 141.16. The central lesson of this section is that military power rested on economic and social resources, including the labor, food, transport, and tolerance of civilian populations, and on administrative capacity to organize them, more than on tactical innovation alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Living with Military Mobilization in Seventeenth-Century Europe

From the perspective of a ruler's minister: Armies were essential to security and prestige, yet each campaign required negotiating with estates, bankers, and local officials. The minister's task was to convert uncertain revenue into reliable pay and supply, knowing that failure could bring mutiny, bankruptcy, or defeat.
From the perspective of a peasant household in a war zone: Taxes, quartering, requisitions, and the risk of plunder arrived together, often from several competing armies. Councils negotiated contributions to protect their citizens and commerce from worse exactions.
From the perspective of a religious minority in a garrisoned region: Soldiers could be both protectors and instruments of coercion. Communities such as Protestants in France or Jews in imperial cities might be protected by a ruler's interest or exposed to billeting, fines, and forced conversion, depending on policy and local circumstances.
Section 141.12

Bureaucracy, Royal Authority, and the Limits of Centralization

The growth of war finance was accompanied by the growth of administration. Rulers expanded councils, appointed secretaries and commissioners, created courts and revenue offices, and increasingly relied on written records. Habsburg Spain governed its far-flung territories through a system of councils, including those of Castile, Aragon, Italy, and the Indies, and Philip II acquired a reputation for supervising paperwork. Such governance operated within a composite monarchy, a term historians use for rulers holding several territories, each with its own laws and institutions. When the Count-Duke of Olivares attempted in the 1620s to require greater contributions from all the crown's kingdoms through the Union of Arms, the result was resistance, and revolts in Catalonia and Portugal in 1640 followed, after which Portugal regained its independence (see Chapter 75).

France under Henry IV, Richelieu, and Louis XIV expanded royal administration, discussed in Chapter 76. Intendants, officials commissioned by the crown to supervise justice, police, and finance in the provinces, increased in importance from the 1630s, while the sale of offices, regularized by an annual fee after 1604, created a body of officials with property rights in their positions. Louis XIV built the palace of Versailles, in part to involve the high nobility in court life, and his ministers, especially Colbert, promoted commercial and fiscal reforms. The king's rule has often been described as absolutism, but historians now emphasize the extent to which it depended on cooperation with local elites, parlements, and provincial estates. The phrase attributed to Louis, "I am the state," is probably apocryphal, and no reliable contemporary evidence confirms it.

Brandenburg-Prussia, whose rise is examined in Chapter 78, offers a different pattern. Its territories were scattered, its resources limited, and its rulers faced powerful estates. Elector Frederick William, the Great Elector (ruled 1640 to 1688), secured tax grants for a standing army in negotiations with the provincial estates, including a settlement with the Brandenburg nobility in 1653 that confirmed noble privileges, notably authority over their peasants. A military commissariat, which gradually took over tax collection, became a nucleus of central administration, and later rulers expanded it. The nobility, the Junkers, supplied officers, which tied their interests to the army. The Habsburg monarchy governed Austria, Bohemia, Hungary, and other lands, with distinct diets and laws, and the Pragmatic Sanction of 1713 sought to secure the succession. Maria Theresa and Joseph II strengthened central offices, but Joseph's attempts to impose uniform administration, including a language decree in 1784, provoked resistance, especially in Hungary, and most of his measures were revoked at his death in 1790. In Russia, Peter the Great created a Senate, administrative colleges, and a Table of Ranks (1722) to organize service, though local administration remained thin (Chapter 79).

Britain and the Dutch Republic demonstrated constitutional alternatives. Britain had a relatively small central bureaucracy for most purposes but a highly effective revenue administration, and relied on local gentry serving as justices of the peace for much routine governance. The Dutch Republic had weak central institutions and strong provincial and municipal governments, yet it raised substantial funds, as noted above. Both cases contradict the assumption that effective governance required the concentration of power in a single royal administration.

The concept of absolutism has therefore been questioned. The older view treated it as a stage in which monarchs overcame feudal resistance. Revisionist historians such as Nicholas Henshall and William Beik argue that absolutism was a claim and an ideology more than a description of practice, and that rulers governed through negotiation with privileged groups, tolerating local autonomy in exchange for cooperation. Denmark-Norway offers a rare case of formal absolutism established by law in 1660 and 1665, and yet even there practical government depended on cooperation with the nobility and the bureaucracy. Students should distinguish formal royal claims from effective administrative control. Resistance, including the Fronde, parlementary opposition, and provincial revolts, shows that the limits on centralization were real, and the persistence of local jurisdictions meant that central reform often required political bargains that rulers could not always achieve.

Territorial Sovereignty, Borders, and the Westphalian Settlement

Claims to independent authority existed long before the seventeenth century. Medieval jurists argued that a king was emperor in his own kingdom, rejecting imperial overlordship, and Philip IV of France resisted papal claims to supremacy in the dispute with Boniface VIII that produced the bull Unam Sanctam in 1302. Systematic theories of sovereignty developed amid the religious wars discussed in Chapter 66. Jean Bodin, in his Six Books of the Commonwealth (1576), defined sovereignty as the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth and argued that it was indivisible, a response to the disorder of the French civil wars. Thomas Hobbes, writing his Leviathan (1651) in the aftermath of the English civil wars, argued that a single sovereign authority was necessary to prevent chaos. Not every thinker agreed. Johannes Althusius defended a federal conception in which authority was built up from communities, and the Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius, in On the Law of War and Peace (1625), sought to define legal limits on war and the relations among states (Chapter 83 discusses later political thought).

The Peace of Westphalia, consisting of treaties signed at Osnabrück and Münster on 24 October 1648, ended the Thirty Years' War in the empire (Chapter 67). Spain had already recognized Dutch independence in the separate treaty of Münster signed in January 1648, though war between France and Spain continued until 1659. The settlement confirmed the religious provisions of the Peace of Augsburg of 1555 and extended them to Calvinists, fixed 1624 as the reference year for the ownership of church property, restored many territories, and recognized the right of imperial princes to make alliances so long as these were not directed against the emperor or the empire. Sweden gained territories in northern Germany and a seat in the imperial diet, France received rights in Alsace whose precise legal character remained contested, Brandenburg gained additional territory, and the Swiss Confederation was exempted from imperial jurisdiction. Sweden and France became guarantors of the settlement, which meant that foreign powers had a legal basis to intervene in imperial affairs.

The popular claim that Westphalia created the modern sovereign state system has been strongly criticized. The international relations scholar Leo Gross popularized the idea in 1948, and it entered textbooks widely. Later scholars, notably Andreas Osiander, argued that the treaties did not mention sovereignty in the modern sense, were chiefly concerned with the constitutional and religious order of the empire, and became a founding myth largely in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The empire continued to exist for another century and a half, with its courts, diet, and layered jurisdictions, and princes remained members of the empire as well as rulers of their territories. Dynastic claims, composite monarchies, and unequal relationships among states persisted. Westphalia was significant as a major settlement that stabilized the confessional order of the empire, but it should not be treated as an origin point for sovereignty.

Borders also developed gradually. Medieval and early modern frontiers were often zones of overlapping jurisdictions, enclaves, and customary rights, and rulers' claims might rest on dynastic inheritance more than defined lines. The Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659 ceded Roussillon and part of Cerdanya to France and required later commissioners to decide which Cerdanya villages were included, and the resulting dispute left the town of Llívia as a Spanish enclave inside France, as it remains. The Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) between the Habsburgs, Poland, Venice, and the Ottoman Empire led to a commission that surveyed and marked boundaries, an early example of systematic demarcation. Cartographic surveys, such as the topographic mapping of France in the eighteenth century, helped states describe their territory. The Ottoman Empire was formally admitted to the public law and concert of Europe only by the Treaty of Paris of 1856, which shows that recognition by other states, as well as control, defined membership in the system.

Section 141.14

Diplomacy, International Law, and the European Balance of Power

Diplomacy long preceded the modern state system. Medieval rulers sent envoys for specific missions, and the papacy dispatched legates. Resident embassies, permanent missions in foreign courts, developed in fifteenth-century Italy, where states such as Milan under Francesco Sforza maintained ambassadors in rival capitals, and they were shaped by the Peace of Lodi of 1454 and the alliance it produced. Venetian ambassadors' detailed reports, which survive in large numbers, are a major source for the period. In the sixteenth century resident embassies spread across Europe, including to the Ottoman court, where Venice maintained a representative, and France and the Ottoman sultan developed an association traditionally associated with a 1536 agreement whose precise terms and date are debated.

Rules and practices gradually developed. Ambassadors were granted immunity, disputes about precedence were frequent, and the French language replaced Latin as the usual language of diplomacy in the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Multilateral peace conferences, beginning with Westphalia and including Nijmegen (1678), Ryswick (1697), Utrecht (1713), and Rastatt and Baden (1714), brought many powers together. Legal writers, among them Alberico Gentili, Grotius, Samuel Pufendorf, and later Emer de Vattel, whose Law of Nations appeared in 1758, argued that sovereign states were bound by legal norms. Such thought gave rulers a vocabulary of equality and obligation, yet the law lacked reliable enforcement, and powerful states often interpreted it in their own interest.

The idea of a balance of power, in which no single state should be allowed to dominate, appeared in Italian political thought and became central to European diplomacy in the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The War of the Spanish Succession (1701 to 1714), triggered by the death of the childless Charles II of Spain in 1700, was fought to prevent the union of the French and Spanish crowns, and the treaties of Utrecht explicitly invoked the maintenance of a balance. The shifting alliances of the eighteenth century, including the reversal of 1756 that allied Austria with France before the Seven Years' War (Chapter 80), demonstrated that diplomatic alignment depended on interest and calculation. The balance also operated at the expense of weaker parties. The partitions of Poland in 1772, 1793, and 1795 divided a state among three powers in part to avoid war among them, which showed that the preservation of equilibrium among the great powers could coexist with the destruction of a smaller one.

The Congress of Vienna, held from September 1814 to June 1815 and examined in Chapter 92, was the most ambitious diplomatic effort of the era. Statesmen including Castlereagh, Metternich, Talleyrand, Alexander I, and Hardenberg redrew territorial arrangements after twenty-five years of war, invoking principles of legitimacy and compensation. The settlement created a German Confederation, enlarged Prussia and the Netherlands, restored many dynasties, and guaranteed Swiss neutrality. The great powers agreed to consult periodically in what became known as the Concert of Europe, which helped manage crises for decades. It also suppressed liberal and national movements in some cases and could not prevent later wars. Further efforts at institutionalization, including the Hague Conferences of 1899 and 1907, produced conventions on arbitration and the conduct of war, but not a means of preventing the conflict of 1914 (Chapter 107). Diplomatic institutions managed relationships and contained some conflicts, but they repeatedly failed to prevent major wars, because they depended on the willingness of states to restrain themselves.

Section 141.15

Revolution, Constitutionalism, and Popular Sovereignty

The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries introduced new claims about the source of political authority. In England the crisis of 1688 to 1689 ended with Parliament offering the crown to William III and Mary II and issuing a declaration of rights, enacted as the Bill of Rights, which condemned royal suspension of laws and taxation without parliamentary consent and called for regular parliaments and free elections. The settlement established that sovereignty lay in the crown in Parliament, a constitutional monarchy rather than popular government, and it excluded Catholics from the throne. John Locke's Two Treatises of Government, published in 1689, argued that legitimate government rested on consent and could be resisted when it violated natural rights, though his ideas were interpreted differently by later readers (Chapter 83).

The revolution in France that began in 1789 (Chapters 88 and 89) presented a more radical claim. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, adopted on 26 August 1789, asserted that sovereignty originated in the nation, that men were born free and equal in rights, and that law was an expression of the general will. In the same period the assembly abolished feudal privileges, replaced provinces with uniform departments, and secularized church property. The practical meaning of citizenship, however, was contested. The 1791 constitution distinguished active citizens, who paid a minimum tax and could vote, from passive ones. Olympe de Gouges published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman and Citizen in 1791, but women were excluded from political rights. Jews were granted citizenship in 1790 and 1791, and in 1794 the Convention abolished slavery in the colonies, a decision reversed by Napoleon in 1802. The revolution thus produced principles that were inclusive in theory and selective in application.

Napoleon, examined in Chapter 90, consolidated some revolutionary changes while restoring authority. The prefects introduced in 1800 supervised departments, the Concordat of 1801 regulated relations with the papacy, and the Civil Code of 1804 established uniform civil law based on equality of male citizens before the law, protection of property, and secular marriage, while giving husbands extensive authority over wives. French armies exported the codes and administrative models to conquered territories. In 1806, after the establishment of the Confederation of the Rhine under French protection, Emperor Francis II renounced the imperial title, ending the Holy Roman Empire. Napoleon's regime used plebiscites to claim popular approval while limiting political freedom, an early illustration that popular sovereignty and liberty are not the same thing.

Constitutional movements spread widely. The Spanish Cortes of Cádiz promulgated a liberal constitution in 1812, Norway adopted a constitution in 1814, and Belgium in 1831 created a constitutional monarchy after its separation from the Netherlands. The restored French monarchy issued a Charter in 1814 as a grant from the king, while several German states adopted constitutions in the following years. The revolutions of 1848 (Chapter 98) produced a republic in France based on universal male suffrage, a national assembly at Frankfurt, and uprisings in the Habsburg lands, many of which were suppressed. Even so, some outcomes endured, including the abolition of serfdom in the Habsburg lands and the Piedmontese constitution of 1848, which later became the basis of the Italian state. Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte, elected president in 1848 by broad male suffrage, became emperor in 1852 and governed through plebiscitary authority.

These developments require students to distinguish four ideas that are often confused. Constitutional government means that authority is limited by law. Representative government means that elected or chosen assemblies share in governing. Popular sovereignty locates the ultimate source of authority in the people or nation. Democracy implies broad and equal political participation. Britain after 1689 had constitutional and representative government with a very restricted electorate, Napoleonic France invoked popular sovereignty without political liberty, and many nineteenth-century liberal regimes limited the vote by property or education. These categories did not develop together automatically, and the following source comparison allows students to examine how different documents defined legal authority.

None of them alone shows how rights were actually experienced by ordinary people, and later reinterpretation often exaggerated their original scope, as with Magna Carta, whose reputation in the seventeenth century far exceeded its thirteenth-century purpose.

Section 141.16

National Unification, Citizenship, and the Territorial State

Nineteenth-century nationalism, discussed in Chapters 97 and 100, asserted that political units should correspond to nations defined by language, culture, or history. In practice nation-building and state-building proceeded together. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in March 1861 under the House of Savoy after the military and diplomatic campaigns of 1859 to 1860, and it acquired Venetia in 1866 and Rome in 1870. Its government extended Piedmontese laws and a prefectural system modeled on France across a peninsula of diverse traditions, and southern resistance, including a prolonged conflict labeled brigandage by the government, revealed the difficulty of integration. A famous remark attributed to Massimo d'Azeglio that Italy had been made and Italians must now be made is of doubtful origin, but it captures a widespread concern. The German Empire proclaimed in January 1871 under Prussian leadership was a federation of monarchies and free cities in which Prussia dominated, with a federal council representing state governments and a Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage. Legal unification followed, culminating in the Civil Code of 1900.

Citizenship changed with these transformations. Rights of political participation expanded unevenly. British reform acts of 1832, 1867, and 1884 widened the male electorate, France introduced universal male suffrage in 1848, the German Reichstag was elected on that basis, and Italy approached it by 1912. Belgium's system of plural voting persisted until 1919. Women gained the vote at different times, first in Finland in 1906, then in Norway, Denmark, Russia, Germany, and Britain (for women over thirty in 1918), and much later in France (1944), Italy (1945), and Switzerland (federal level, 1971). Nationality law defined who belonged. France's law of 1889 combined descent and birth on French soil, whereas Germany's law of 1913 emphasized descent. The emancipation of Jews proceeded unevenly, achieved in Austria-Hungary in 1867 and the German Empire by 1871, but delayed in Russia and in Romania, where equal citizenship was resisted despite the Congress of Berlin of 1878 and granted generally only after the First World War.

State institutions expanded to shape national membership. France's Ferry laws of 1881 and 1882 established free, compulsory, secular primary education, and historians such as Eugen Weber argued that schools, roads, railways, and military service turned peasants into Frenchmen, a thesis that later scholars have refined by showing that local identities persisted. States adopted universal conscription, expanded civil services, conducted censuses, and created welfare programs. Germany introduced health, accident, and old-age insurance in the 1880s. Census categories, such as the language questions in the Austrian census, influenced identity formation as well as recording it. Theorists including Ernest Gellner, Benedict Anderson, and Eric Hobsbawm emphasized that national identity was constructed through education, print, and political organization, a view that helps students avoid assuming that nations are timeless.

In central and eastern Europe, the multinational empires persisted. The Habsburg Compromise of 1867 created the Dual Monarchy of Austria and Hungary, in which Hungary had its own parliament and government while foreign affairs, defense, and related finance were common, and Croatia negotiated a separate arrangement within Hungary in 1868. Language disputes in Bohemia and elsewhere complicated governance. The Ottoman Empire attempted administrative reform in the Tanzimat era from 1839 and adopted a constitution in 1876, which was soon suspended, while the Russian Empire pursued policies of Russification alongside reform after 1905. The Balkan states achieved autonomy or independence through the century, with Greece's independence recognized in 1830 and the independence of Serbia, Montenegro, and Romania confirmed at the Congress of Berlin in 1878. Each claimed to represent a nation while containing minorities, and populations in places such as Macedonia, Galicia, Alsace-Lorraine, and Schleswig did not correspond neatly to state boundaries. National and state boundaries therefore frequently failed to coincide, a problem that would shape the settlements discussed in Section 141.17.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Citizenship and Political Membership from Antiquity to Modern Europe

What Changed?

  • Membership expanded from small groups of male citizens in poleis to nearly all free inhabitants of the Roman Empire in 212 CE, then to national citizenship defined by legal codes, schooling, and military service
  • Political participation moved from direct assemblies, to corporate estates, to elected representatives, and, in the twentieth century, to near-universal adult suffrage
  • Rights came to be protected by national constitutions and, after 1950, by international courts and European Union law, which individuals could invoke against their own governments

What Remained Continuous?

  • The link between military obligation and claims to membership, from the hoplite and legionary to the conscript of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries
  • The tendency of inclusion to be accompanied by new forms of exclusion, based on gender, property, religion, ancestry, status, or nationality
  • The importance of legal status, including slavery, serfdom, subjecthood, and statelessness, in determining who could claim protection

Who experienced change most directly? Men who gained the vote through successive reforms, Jews who were emancipated, and later women who were enfranchised experienced the most visible gains, while those subject to conscription experienced new obligations. Who experienced greater continuity? The poor, migrants, enslaved and colonized peoples, and people in minority communities often found that formal rights expanded more slowly than the law implied. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that citizenship has never been a single linear progress. Expansion, restriction, and reversal occurred together, and the most severe reversal came in the twentieth century, when states stripped citizenship from entire groups, as Section 141.17 explains.

Division V: Total War, International Institutions, and Contemporary Sovereignty · Section 141.17

Total War and the Expansion of State Power in the Twentieth Century

The First World War (Chapters 108 to 112) required governments to mobilize entire economies and societies. Britain enacted the Defence of the Realm Act in August 1914, created a Ministry of Munitions in 1915, introduced conscription in 1916, and raised the income tax and the national debt to unprecedented levels. In Germany, the War Raw Materials Department organized supplies from 1914, the Hindenburg Programme of 1916 sought to expand armaments, an Auxiliary Service Law in December 1916 imposed labor obligations on men, and the Allied blockade contributed to severe shortages, notably in the winter of 1916 to 1917. France organized armaments production under Albert Thomas and, in 1917, entrusted leadership to Georges Clemenceau. In Austria-Hungary, parliament was suspended from March 1914 until 1917 and military authorities exercised wide control, while in Russia voluntary organizations and industrial committees supplemented a strained administration (Chapter 110). Governments introduced censorship, rationing, propaganda, internment of enemy aliens, and surveillance, and in the Ottoman Empire the government's deportation and massacre of Armenians in 1915 and 1916, which most scholars of genocide characterize as genocide, demonstrated how wartime emergency could be turned against a civilian population.

The war destroyed four imperial orders, as Chapter 112 explained, and the settlements of 1919 and 1920 created or enlarged states, including Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia, and drew borders that left substantial minorities in most of them (Chapter 113). Minority treaties and the League of Nations, founded in 1920, attempted to supervise protection, and a Permanent Court of International Justice began work in 1922. Population exchanges, notably between Greece and Turkey under the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, treated populations as subjects of state policy. Stateless refugees, including those who fled the Russian revolution, were issued identity documents under the League's auspices, the so-called Nansen passport. Passports, visas, and immigration quotas became standard instruments of state control. Democratic regimes were fragile, as Chapter 114 showed, and emergency provisions, such as Article 48 of the Weimar constitution, became tools of rule by decree.

The interwar regimes examined in Chapter 115 organized state power in different ways. In Italy, Mussolini's Fascist movement took office in 1922, established a single-party regime within a surviving monarchy, and eliminated independent political organization while preserving some existing state institutions. In Germany, the Enabling Act of March 1933 allowed Hitler's government to legislate without parliament, the process of coordination subordinated institutions to the party, and the police and security apparatus operated outside normal legal constraints. The Nuremberg Laws of September 1935 deprived Jews of citizenship in the Reich and prohibited marriage and sexual relations between Jews and non-Jews, showing how nationality law could be used to exclude. Soviet communism built a party-state with a planned economy, mass collectivization of agriculture, a forced-labor camp system, and the Great Terror of 1937 to 1938. Other authoritarian regimes, including Piłsudski's Poland after 1926, Salazar's Portugal, and Franco's Spain after 1939, drew on conservative, military, or clerical foundations. These systems should not be conflated, since their ideologies, institutions, and forms of violence differed fundamentally.

The Second World War extended mobilization still further (Chapters 117 to 123). Britain adopted emergency powers in 1939 and formed a coalition government, and in the Soviet Union and Germany, labor, food, and industry were directed to war. German-occupied Europe was governed through varied arrangements, including annexation, protectorates, military administration, the General Government in Poland, and collaborationist regimes such as Vichy France (Chapter 120). Forced labor drew millions of workers from occupied territories into the German economy, with some estimates exceeding ten million. The Holocaust (Chapter 121) was carried out by a state apparatus that used law, administration, police, railways, and local collaboration to register, dispossess, deport, and murder Jews and other groups, and also targeted Roma and others. Such events demonstrated that bureaucratic capacity, when joined to racial ideology and freed from legal restraint, could produce catastrophic violence.

Total war thus intensified state capacity, destabilized empires, redrew borders, and exposed the consequences of authority without effective legal limits. It also changed expectations about what democratic governments owed their citizens, since the shared sacrifice of mobilization fed demands for wider suffrage and for social provision, as with the extension of the franchise to women after 1918 and the welfare reforms discussed in Chapter 126. The next section examines how Europeans tried to reconstruct constitutional and international order after 1945.

Cause and Effect

Total Warfare and the Expansion of State Authority

Long-term causes

Industrial economies, mass armies, railways, and bureaucracies developed during the nineteenth century gave states the means to mobilize on a large scale, while conscription and mass schooling had already connected governments to individual citizens.

Short-term causes

The unexpected length of the First World War, the stalemate on the western front, the blockade, and the demand for munitions forced governments to assume control of production, labor, and food, while similar pressures reappeared in 1939.

Event or transformation

Governments in the combatant states regulated economies, taxed incomes, conscripted labor, controlled information, and used emergency powers. Some regimes institutionalized these controls permanently.

Immediate effects

Empires collapsed, new states were created, minorities were displaced, and large numbers of people were killed or made stateless. Democratic governments expanded welfare and the franchise, while authoritarian regimes seized wide powers.

Long-term consequences

The wars produced new constitutions, international criminal law, human-rights treaties, and security alliances after 1945, as well as permanently larger public budgets and administrative responsibilities.

Historical complexity

Mobilization did not produce a single outcome. Democracies, fascist and Nazi regimes, communist systems, and conservative dictatorships organized wartime power differently, and some societies resisted or reversed emergency measures once war ended.

Why the development cannot be reduced to a single cause: Total war expanded state power only where political, economic, and ideological conditions allowed. The same pressures that strengthened some governments destroyed others, and the legal restraints that existed in democratic systems were absent from regimes that used mobilization to justify mass violence.

Section 141.18

Reconstruction, the Cold War, and the New International Legal Order

After 1945, western European states rebuilt constitutional systems with explicit attention to preventing a recurrence of dictatorship. Italy's constitution entered into force on 1 January 1948 and created a constitutional court, which began work in 1956. West Germany's Basic Law, adopted on 23 May 1949, began with the protection of human dignity, provided that its fundamental principles could not be amended, and was supported by a Federal Constitutional Court established in 1951 (Chapter 127). France adopted a Fourth Republic constitution in 1946 and a Fifth Republic constitution in 1958. Authoritarian regimes survived in Spain and Portugal until the 1970s, as discussed in Chapter 131.

The United Nations Charter, signed in June 1945 and in force from October, prohibited the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of states and established a Security Council in which the permanent members held vetoes, while also preserving domestic jurisdiction. The Nuremberg trial of major German leaders before an International Military Tribunal (1945 to 1946) prosecuted crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity and established that individuals could be held accountable under international law (Chapter 123). Critics then and since have debated whether the proceedings applied law retroactively and whether they amounted to victors' justice, while defenders emphasized the gravity of the crimes and the fairness of the procedure. In December 1948 the General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and the Genocide Convention was approved, followed by the Geneva Conventions of 1949.

Europe nevertheless divided into blocs (Chapters 124 and 125). NATO, created by the North Atlantic Treaty of April 1949, committed members to treat an armed attack on one as an attack on all. The Warsaw Pact, formed in May 1955, institutionalized Soviet military leadership in eastern Europe. The states of eastern Europe were formally sovereign but ruled by communist parties whose policies were constrained by Soviet power, and Soviet intervention in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 (Chapter 129) demonstrated the limits, justified in the latter case by what Western observers called the Brezhnev Doctrine of limited sovereignty. Yugoslavia's break with Moscow in 1948 and Romania's later independent foreign policy show that the bloc was not uniform. A group of neutral or non-aligned states, including Switzerland, Sweden, Ireland, Finland under a 1948 treaty with the Soviet Union, and Austria after the State Treaty of May 1955, adopted differing arrangements.

Germany's position illustrates how sovereignty could be conditional. Occupied by the four Allied powers in 1945, it was divided into two states in 1949. The Federal Republic regained most sovereign powers in 1955 and joined NATO, and it also became a founding member of the European integration project examined in Section 141.19, while the Allies retained certain rights concerning Berlin and Germany as a whole until the Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany (the Two-plus-Four Treaty) of September 1990 and German unification on 3 October 1990. The Helsinki Final Act of 1975 recognized the inviolability of European frontiers and asserted respect for human rights. It was a political commitment, not a treaty, and yet dissident groups in eastern Europe invoked its human-rights provisions to challenge their governments (Chapter 131).

Students should therefore distinguish several layers of authority: national constitutional authority, obligations to alliances, international treaties, and the limited enforcement mechanisms of international law. The United Nations could not prevent interventions by great powers within their own spheres, NATO and the Warsaw Pact bound states to military commitments, and the human-rights instruments that emerged depended on states' consent and compliance. The Council of Europe, created in 1949, and the European Convention on Human Rights, signed in 1950 and in force from 1953, established a distinctive regional framework for the external review of state conduct. The European Court of Human Rights began operating in 1959; its institutional arrangements were subsequently reformed, most notably when Protocol No. 11 established a permanent court and direct individual application in 1998, as discussed further below.

Section 141.19

European Integration and the Sharing of Sovereign Authority

European integration, traced in Chapters 133 and 134, began with a specific proposal. Robert Schuman's declaration of 9 May 1950 proposed placing French and German coal and steel production under a common authority, and the Treaty of Paris of 1951 created the European Coal and Steel Community among six states, with a High Authority empowered to make binding decisions. A proposed European Defence Community failed in 1954 when the French National Assembly refused to ratify it. The Treaties of Rome of 1957 established the European Economic Community and Euratom, which began operating in 1958. Integration was neither inevitable nor linear, as the "empty chair" crisis of 1965 to 1966 showed, when France boycotted Community institutions and the resulting Luxembourg Compromise of January 1966 permitted states to insist on unanimity on matters of vital national interest. The economic historian Alan Milward argued in 1992 that integration helped rescue the European nation-state by enabling governments to deliver growth and welfare, while other scholars, drawing on neofunctionalist theory, emphasized processes by which cooperation in one field led to cooperation in others, and Andrew Moravcsik stressed bargaining among governments. These interpretations differ on whether integration primarily served or transformed national governments.

Law became one of the most distinctive aspects. The Court of Justice of the European Communities, now the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), held in Van Gend en Loos (1963) that Community law could create rights that individuals could invoke in national courts, and in Costa v ENEL (1964) that Community law took precedence over conflicting national law. National courts accepted these doctrines with qualifications. The German Federal Constitutional Court, in decisions beginning with the so-called Solange cases of 1974 and 1986 and in its judgments on the Maastricht Treaty (1993) and the Lisbon Treaty (2009), asserted that it retained authority to review whether Union acts remained within the powers conferred and respected constitutional identity. In October 2021, the Polish Constitutional Tribunal ruled that certain provisions of the EU treaties were incompatible with the Polish constitution, a ruling the European Commission and CJEU rejected as contrary to the primacy of EU law. The relationship between national constitutional courts and the CJEU remains contested.

The institutions expanded over time. The European Parliament, whose members have been directly elected since 1979, gained legislative powers, and under the Lisbon Treaty the ordinary legislative procedure gave it a role equal to that of the Council in most areas. The European Commission proposes legislation and supervises implementation, the Council represents member state governments, the European Council brings together heads of state or government, and the CJEU interprets Union law. The Maastricht Treaty, signed in February 1992 and in force from November 1993, created the European Union, introduced citizenship of the Union, and set the path to economic and monetary union, leading to the euro, launched in 1999 as an accounting currency and in circulation from 2002. The Schengen arrangements, begun in 1985 and implemented from 1995, removed many internal border controls among participating states, which do not coincide exactly with EU membership. The Union operates on the principle of conferral, meaning that it acts only within powers granted by the treaties, and on subsidiarity, which holds that action should be taken at the Union level only where objectives cannot be adequately achieved by member states. The treaties also include a procedure, in Article 50 of the Treaty on European Union, for a member state to withdraw, which the United Kingdom invoked after its 2016 referendum and completed on 31 January 2020 (Chapter 138).

Debate over democratic legitimacy has accompanied integration. Critics speak of a democratic deficit, arguing that decisions are made far from voters and that the Commission and Court are insufficiently accountable. Supporters respond that the Union's legitimacy derives from the democratic governments that conclude treaties and sit in the Council, from the directly elected Parliament, and from the protection of rights. Referendums revealed public hesitation. Danish voters rejected the Maastricht Treaty in June 1992 before accepting it with opt-outs, French voters approved it narrowly in September 1992, Irish voters rejected the Nice and Lisbon treaties before approving revised arrangements, and French and Dutch voters rejected a proposed constitutional treaty in 2005. Integration thus created shared and layered authority without abolishing national sovereignty. Member states remain decisive in amending the founding treaties and retain many core functions, but implementation and enforcement of Union law are also exercised through national courts and administrations, the European Commission, and the Court of Justice within the powers conferred by those treaties. The relationship is therefore one of legally structured shared authority, not exclusive national control over enforcement.

Section 141.20

Sovereignty in Contemporary Europe: Continuity, Conflict, and Political Legitimacy

Contemporary historical cutoff: 19 November 2026. Ongoing legal and security developments are described only to the extent documented for this edition; no prediction about future outcomes is implied.

The end of the Cold War (Chapter 132) transformed the map of Europe through both negotiation and violence. German unification in 1990 was carried out under the Two-plus-Four Treaty, and a separate treaty of November 1990 confirmed the Polish-German border. The Soviet Union was dissolved in December 1991, following agreements reached at Belovezha on 8 December and the Alma-Ata declaration of 21 December, and its successor states largely inherited the borders of former Soviet republics. Czechoslovakia divided peacefully on 1 January 1993. The Baltic states restored their independence, and the Charter of Paris of 1990, adopted by the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe (CSCE), and the subsequent work of that body emphasized the inviolability of frontiers and minority protections. The CSCE was renamed the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) effective 1 January 1995. In practice, the dominant approach of the early 1990s was to treat existing administrative boundaries as state borders, a principle also applied to Yugoslavia, but unresolved questions about minorities and self-determination persisted.

The breakup of Yugoslavia, narrated in Chapter 135, showed how contested sovereignty could produce war. Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in June 1991, and an arbitration commission established by the European Community, the Badinter Commission, advised on recognition. The war in Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992 to 1995) involved ethnic cleansing, sieges, and the killing of more than eight thousand men and boys at Srebrenica in July 1995, which the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia and the International Court of Justice (in a judgment of 2007) found to constitute genocide. The Dayton Peace Agreement, initialed in November and signed in December 1995, ended the war and confirmed the continued international legal existence of Bosnia and Herzegovina within its recognized borders, while establishing a constitutional arrangement comprising two entities, the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and Republika Srpska. NATO's air campaign over Kosovo in 1999 was conducted without explicit Security Council authorization, and Kosovo's declaration of independence in February 2008 received recognition from many states but not from others, including some members of the European Union, while the International Court of Justice in 2010 advised that the declaration did not violate general international law. These episodes demonstrate that borders, minority protections, and recognition remained politically disputed.

Russia's attack on Ukraine provides the most severe recent test and is examined in Chapter 139. Russia annexed Crimea in March 2014, an act that most governments and the United Nations General Assembly refused to recognize, and launched a full-scale invasion on 24 February 2022. Ukraine had been recognized within its existing borders by Russia in bilateral agreements of the 1990s, and in 1994 had joined a memorandum under which it gave up nuclear weapons stationed on its territory in exchange for security assurances. The General Assembly deplored the invasion on 2 March 2022 and, in October 2022, declared the annexation of four further Ukrainian regions invalid. Russian officials have presented historical, security, and identity claims to justify their actions, while Ukraine, the EU, NATO members, and most governments describe the war as aggression against Ukrainian sovereignty. Europe's institutions responded with sanctions, military and financial assistance, and a decision in June 2022 to grant Ukraine and Moldova candidate status for EU membership. Finland joined NATO on 4 April 2023 and Sweden on 7 March 2024. Because the war has continued, and because the sources are journalistic, governmental, and legal rather than the product of mature historical scholarship, this chapter makes no prediction about its outcome. Students should consult current, carefully sourced reporting and distinguish documented events from political claims.

Sovereignty has also been contested within states. Scotland and Wales received devolved institutions in 1999, and Scotland held an independence referendum in September 2014 that was rejected. Spain's constitution of 1978 created autonomous communities, and an independence referendum in Catalonia on 1 October 2017 was declared unconstitutional by the Spanish Constitutional Court. Belgium became a federal state in 1993, and the Good Friday Agreement of April 1998 established power-sharing arrangements in Northern Ireland. In the European Union, the European Commission initiated an Article 7(1) procedure concerning Poland in December 2017, but withdrew its proposal in May 2024 after assessing subsequent reforms and commitments, bringing that procedure to an end. The European Parliament, rather than the Commission, initiated the Article 7(1) procedure concerning Hungary in September 2018; it remained open at this edition's historical cutoff. Separately, the European Union adopted a rule-of-law budget conditionality regulation in 2020, distinct from Article 7. These developments illustrate disputes over judicial independence, constitutional identity, and the powers of Union institutions without implying that the two Article 7 procedures had the same origin or status. Constitutional rights, regional autonomy, supranational law, and national sovereignty thus continue to interact.

The chapter returns, finally, to its central question. Should European political development be understood primarily as increasing state centralization, or as recurring negotiation among overlapping forms of power, law, and political membership? The evidence surveyed here favors the second view, while acknowledging that some European states did centralize authority substantially. Greek poleis and Roman magistrates, medieval lords and assemblies, imperial and confederal systems, fiscal-military monarchies, revolutionary republics, total-war states, and supranational institutions each attempted to reconcile coercive power, public finance, legal authority, territorial jurisdiction, political legitimacy, and the claims of governed populations. None of the outcomes was predetermined, and the contemporary European order, with its overlapping national, European, and international layers, is a historically contingent settlement, not an end point. Chapter 142 turns to empire, colonialism, migration, and diaspora, subjects that show how these questions extended far beyond the European continent and how they returned to reshape Europe itself.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the long relationship among warfare, taxation, administration, law, borders, diplomacy, citizenship, and state formation across European history. Its central argument is that European political development was not a straightforward progression from weak medieval kingdoms to powerful nation-states, and then to supranational integration. It consisted instead of repeated attempts to reconcile coercive power, public finance, legal authority, territorial jurisdiction, political legitimacy, and the claims of governed populations. A recurring analytical distinction structured the account: the power a government claims, the resources it can mobilize, the law that authorizes or limits it, and the territory and population it actually governs are not necessarily identical.

Division I showed that ancient and late antique societies organized authority in very different ways. Greek poleis tied citizenship to civic participation and military obligation, with Athens developing direct participation, Sparta a militarized citizen elite, and federal leagues a distinct alternative. The Roman Republic combined magistracies, a senate, and assemblies, and its conquests created provincial government, taxation, and a developing body of law. The empire governed through a small imperial staff, army, and urban elites, and extended citizenship in 212 CE without producing equality. In late antiquity, fiscal resources, armies, and relations with local elites determined the capacity of successor kingdoms and the Eastern Roman Empire, and institutional continuity mattered more than a simple narrative of collapse.

Division II explained that medieval Europe distributed authority among kings, lords, bishops, towns, and local communities. Lordship connected landholding to jurisdiction, but public institutions persisted, and historians continue to debate the usefulness of the term feudalism. Customary, royal, Roman, canon, municipal, and community law coexisted, and the revival of learned law and the growth of royal justice created both stronger courts and legal restraints on rulers. Assemblies of estates negotiated taxation and privilege, though they represented corporate groups and not individual citizens, and their later influence varied with each ruler's alternatives. The Holy Roman Empire, the Italian republics, the Swiss and Dutch confederations, Poland-Lithuania, and the Ottoman system in southeastern Europe demonstrated durable alternatives to centralized monarchy.

Division III followed the military and fiscal changes that expanded the capacity of governments between the later Middle Ages and the eighteenth century. Gunpowder weapons, new fortifications, larger armies, and navies raised the costs of war. States responded with taxation, public credit, administrative expansion, and bargains with elites, but with sharply different results. The Dutch Republic and Britain built effective fiscal systems on representative institutions and creditor confidence, France was constrained by privilege and venal office, Spain suffered repeated bankruptcy, and Prussia combined a standing army with noble service. Historians debate whether war made states, and the evidence suggests that war strengthened some states, destroyed or weakened others, and depended on prior institutions. Absolutism proved to be a claim more than a practice, and civilians, soldiers, taxpayers, and creditors experienced mobilization very differently.

Division IV traced the changing concepts of sovereignty and membership. Bodin, Hobbes, and Grotius shaped ideas of sovereignty and the law among states, while the Peace of Westphalia is better understood as a constitutional and religious settlement of the empire than as the invention of the sovereign state. Borders became more precisely defined only gradually, and the Ottoman Empire entered the formal European concert in 1856. Diplomacy, resident embassies, and the balance of power helped manage conflict, but the partitions of Poland and the failure of 1914 showed their limits. The English settlement of 1688 to 1689, the French Revolution, and the Napoleonic codes introduced new claims of consent, popular sovereignty, and legal equality, which must be distinguished from constitutional government, representation, and democracy.

Nineteenth-century nationalism and unification created new territorial states in Italy and Germany and encouraged the use of schooling, conscription, nationality law, and administrative standardization to define membership. Citizenship expanded through suffrage reform and the emancipation of religious minorities, yet it often excluded women, the poor, and others, and in central and eastern Europe multinational empires persisted while national and state boundaries seldom coincided. The Primary Source Window compared documents from 1215, 1689, 1789, and 1950 and showed that each used law to restrain arbitrary authority while differing in who created authority, who was protected, and how rights were enforced.

Division V examined the twentieth-century transformation of state power. The world wars required mobilization of economies and populations, expanded taxation and administration, destroyed empires, and redrew borders. The interwar and wartime regimes, including democratic, fascist, Nazi, communist, and conservative authoritarian systems, organized power in different ways, and the Nazi regime's use of law, administration, and racial ideology demonstrated the catastrophic consequences of state authority without legal restraint. After 1945 Europeans rebuilt constitutions, created the United Nations and international criminal law, adopted human-rights commitments, and divided into military and political blocs whose members' sovereignty was conditional in different ways.

European integration then created a distinctive system of shared and layered authority. Beginning with the Coal and Steel Community, member states conferred powers on common institutions, developed a body of Union law applied by national courts and interpreted by the Court of Justice, and created citizenship of the Union and a common currency for some members. This system did not abolish national sovereignty, since states retained control over treaties, withdrawal, and many core functions, and it generated disputes over democratic legitimacy, constitutional courts, and the rule of law. Students must also distinguish the European Union from the Council of Europe, the European Court of Human Rights, and NATO.

The post-Cold War period demonstrated both the persistence and the contestation of territorial sovereignty. The breakup of Yugoslavia and the Soviet Union, the Kosovo dispute, regional autonomy movements, and Russia's attacks on Ukraine in 2014 and 2022 showed that borders, recognition, military force, and the legitimacy of rulers remained central problems. Developments after the preparation of this chapter lie beyond established historical judgment, and the chapter offers no predictions. The overall conclusion is that Europe's political order has been characterized by recurring negotiation among overlapping forms of power, law, and membership, and the modern territorial nation-state was one important outcome among several, not an inevitable culmination.

Sovereignty
The claim to supreme and independent authority within a territory, developed in early modern thought and never fully realized in practice, since overlapping jurisdictions and external obligations persisted.
Legitimacy
The belief among politically significant groups that a ruler or institution has a rightful claim to obedience, grounded in tradition, law, religion, consent, or effectiveness.
Jurisdiction
The authority to hear disputes and apply law over particular persons, places, or subjects, which could be held by kings, lords, churches, towns, or communities.
Polis
The Greek city-state, a political community of citizens organized around an urban center and its territory.
Imperium
In Rome, the authority of magistrates and later emperors to command armies and enforce law.

The edict of 212 CE extending Roman citizenship to most free inhabitants of the empire, partly preserved on a papyrus at Giessen.

The early medieval practice of judging persons by the law of their own people, such as Roman or Gothic law, instead of by a single territorial law.

Authority over land and the people who worked it, often including rights of justice and demands for rents and services.

A modern label for relations of lordship, vassalage, and fiefs, whose coherence as a medieval system is disputed.

The coexistence of several legal systems within one territory, each applying to particular persons or matters.

The shared learned law of medieval and early modern Europe, combining Roman and canon law.

The law of the church, governing clergy and matters such as marriage and wills, and applied in ecclesiastical courts.

The body of law developed through the decisions of royal courts in England, as distinct from continental legal traditions based on Roman law.

Corporate groups, such as clergy, nobility, and towns, whose representatives negotiated taxation and privilege with rulers.

The charter of 1215, reissued in modified forms, in which an English king acknowledged limits on certain royal powers.

A state in which one ruler held several territories with separate laws and institutions, as in Habsburg Spain and the Austrian monarchy.

A contested historical concept describing changes in tactics, fortification, and army size in early modern Europe.

A government organized to raise revenue, credit, and administration for sustained warfare.

A system in which private contractors purchased the right to collect taxes and kept part of the proceeds.

A claim to unrestricted royal authority, which historians now regard as an ideology and aspiration limited in practice by negotiation with elites.

The treaties of 1648 that ended the Thirty Years' War, which established a religious and constitutional order in the empire, not the modern sovereign state system.

The principle and practice of preventing any one state from dominating the others, invoked in the Utrecht settlement of 1713.

The claim that political authority originates in the people or nation, advanced in revolutionary declarations from 1789.

The principle that governmental power is limited by fundamental law, whether or not the government is democratic.

The French decree of 1793 mobilizing the entire population for war, a step toward modern conscription.

The practice, after 1815, of consultation among the great powers to manage European crises.

Authority exercised by institutions above individual states, as the European Union does in fields conferred by treaty.

The European Union principle that action should be taken at the Union level only when objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by member states.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it identifies a pattern that earlier chapters, organized chronologically and regionally, could display only in fragments. Across the Greek, Roman, medieval, early modern, revolutionary, imperial, and contemporary periods, European governments confronted similar problems of raising resources, enforcing decisions, securing consent, defining membership, and managing relations with neighbors. They solved them in different ways, and the differences, not the similarities, explain much of Europe's political diversity. The chapter therefore contributes to the argument of Part XXI that Europe's long-term patterns should be understood comparatively, and not as the history of a single dominant model.

The chapter also complicates the argument that European history was driven by the rise of the sovereign nation-state. Territorial states did become powerful, and the nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought mass citizenship, national legal systems, and total mobilization. But confederations, empires, composite monarchies, and layered jurisdictions endured for centuries, and the late twentieth century produced new forms of shared authority. The history of European integration is therefore best read as one chapter in a longer history of negotiated and overlapping authority, not as a departure from an otherwise unified tradition of state sovereignty or as an inevitable culmination of European history.

The relationship between war and law deserves final emphasis. Warfare expanded administrations, tax systems, and armies, yet legal institutions, representative bodies, courts, and creditors also shaped how much power rulers could use. The twentieth century demonstrated both the scale of state capacity and the horror of its abuse, and the legal and institutional responses after 1945 reflected a conviction that authority required restraint. The renewed importance of territorial sovereignty and military force after 2014 and 2022 shows that these questions remain unsettled, and this chapter has deliberately avoided predicting how they will be resolved.

For Volume VI, the chapter provides a long perspective on the postwar and post-communist transformations of Europe, showing why shared sovereignty, human-rights jurisdiction, and contested borders have deep historical antecedents without being predetermined by them. For the remaining chapters of Part XXI it opens the next question. Chapter 142 will examine how empire, colonialism, migration, and diaspora complicate the history of European states, since those states governed and were reshaped by populations far beyond the continent, and Chapters 143 and 144 will complete the comparative synthesis by examining social change and Europe's place in the wider world.
