Chapter Opening

In the space of eleven years, between the strikes of August 1980 and the evening of December 25, 1991, the political order that had divided Europe since 1945 lost its governments, its alliance system, its largest state, and much of its confidence in its own permanence. No single event, leader, or country caused that result, and none of the participants in 1985 could have predicted its sequence.

On the evening of December 25, 1991, the red flag of the Soviet Union was lowered over the Kremlin in Moscow and the white, blue, and red flag of the Russian Federation was raised in its place. Earlier that day Mikhail Gorbachev, the last president of the Soviet Union, had appeared on television to announce his resignation. A state that had existed since 1922, that had defeated Nazi Germany at immense human cost, that had stationed armies in the center of Europe for more than four decades, and that had possessed one of the two largest nuclear arsenals in the world, no longer had a head of state. The ceremony was brief and, by the standards of earlier revolutions, remarkably quiet. Its quietness conceals how many separate histories had to converge to produce it.

Those histories began in different places. One began in the shipyards of Gdańsk in August 1980, where Polish workers, after years of preparation by dissident intellectuals, church leaders, and underground organizers, forced a communist government to recognize an independent trade union. One began in Moscow in March 1985, when a younger general secretary of the Communist Party came to power convinced that the Soviet economy and political system required fundamental change, though not abolition. One began on the streets of Leipzig, Prague, Budapest, and Timișoara in 1989, where populations that had been taught for decades that opposition was futile discovered that governments could no longer rely on Soviet tanks. Others began in Vilnius, Riga, Tallinn, Kyiv, and Tbilisi, where national movements within the Soviet Union itself turned the reforms of the center into demands for sovereignty. And one began in Bonn and East Berlin, where a division that had once seemed the permanent core of the Cold War was dismantled through elections, treaties, and diplomacy during 1990.

This chapter treats these developments as connected but distinct. The collapse of communist governments across eastern Europe in 1989, the reunification of Germany on October 3, 1990, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991 each had its own chronology, its own actors, and its own causes. Treating them as a single event, as the phrase "the fall of communism" sometimes invites, obscures why they happened when and how they did. The Berlin Wall opened on November 9, 1989, but Germany was not reunified for almost eleven months. Communist parties lost their monopoly in Poland and Czechoslovakia while Soviet institutions in Moscow continued to function for two more years. The central historical question of the chapter is why a Soviet-dominated European order that had survived repeated challenges, in East Germany in 1953, Hungary in 1956, Czechoslovakia in 1968, and Poland in 1980 and 1981, unraveled between 1989 and 1991, and how domestic resistance, Soviet reform, international diplomacy, and national independence movements interacted to bring that about.

The chapter's argument is that the end of the European Cold War resulted from the interaction of several processes rather than from any one of them. Organized opposition in Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia demonstrated that communist governments had lost the ability to command consent. Economic stagnation and institutional rigidity limited the options available to Soviet and eastern European leaders. Gorbachev's reforms weakened the instruments of coercion and censorship that had held the system together, and his foreign policy withdrew the expectation that Soviet military force would reverse political change in allied states. Mass mobilization, often nonviolent, exploited those openings. Negotiation among governments, particularly over Germany, translated popular pressure into new international arrangements. None of these factors alone is sufficient, and the outcome in each country depended on local circumstances that the chapter examines separately.

Historians reconstruct these events from an unusually varied body of evidence. Party and state archives, opened unevenly after 1991, now allow scholars to read Politburo minutes, intelligence reports, and diplomatic cables from the Soviet side. Western and eastern European governments have released records of negotiations, including those surrounding German reunification. Underground publications, strike committee documents, and the records of round tables preserve the voices of opposition movements. Stasi files, opened to citizens and researchers after 1990, document the machinery of surveillance in East Germany. Photographs, film, and television broadcasts record the crowds of 1989, while memoirs, interviews, and oral histories reveal how participants understood events they could not yet see as history. Each category has limits. Memoirs are shaped by later reputations, official records are shaped by official purposes, and the images that became symbols, especially of the Berlin Wall, can overshadow slower and less photogenic processes such as constitutional negotiation and economic restructuring.

Chapter 132 concludes Part XIX, Cold War Europe, and therefore completes the story that began with the division of Germany in Chapter 124 and the formation of rival alliances in Chapter 125. It builds directly on the preceding chapters: the Soviet interventions of 1956 and 1968 examined in Chapter 129, the social movements of 1968 treated in Chapter 130, and the détente, economic shocks, and renewed confrontation of the early 1980s examined in Chapter 131. It also marks a boundary within Volume VI. Chapter 133 returns to the origins of European integration in the 1950s, because Part XX changes the analytical focus: it asks how an integrating Western Europe and a reconfigured Eastern Europe were joined after the Cold War, and the transformation of eastern Europe after 1989 is examined in detail in Chapter 136. This chapter ends with the dissolution of the old order, not with the construction of the new one, and it refuses to treat what followed as foreordained.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the development of Solidarity from the workers' protests of 1976 through the Gdańsk Agreement, martial law, underground survival, and the Round Table of 1989.
  2. Explain the causes and aims of Gorbachev's perestroika and glasnost and distinguish their intended purposes from their unintended consequences.
  3. Analyze how changes in Soviet foreign policy, including arms control and the withdrawal of the expectation of intervention, altered the strategic environment of eastern Europe.
  4. Compare the revolutions of 1989 in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, and Romania, identifying their different mechanisms and outcomes.
  5. Reconstruct the sequence of events that led to the opening of the Berlin Wall on November 9, 1989, and distinguish that opening from German reunification.
  6. Explain German reunification as a democratic, legal, economic, and diplomatic process, including the March 1990 election, monetary union, the Unification Treaty, and the Two Plus Four negotiations.
  7. Distinguish the dissolution of Soviet dominance in eastern Europe from the separate process by which the Soviet Union itself ceased to exist.
  8. Assess the roles of national movements, republican leaders, and the August 1991 coup attempt in the collapse of central Soviet authority.
  9. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the end of the Cold War and explain why no single cause is adequate.
  10. Interpret primary sources from the period, including trade union demands, speeches, treaties, press conferences, and agreements, with attention to provenance and limitations.
  11. Contextualize the end of the Cold War within the larger history of Europe and explain why the outcomes of 1989 to 1991 were contingent rather than inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Polish crises of 1980 and 1981 end in repression, while the negotiations of 1989 produced a political settlement? What had changed in the interval?
  2. How did Gorbachev's attempt to reform the Soviet system weaken the foundations on which that system rested, and was this outcome foreseeable?
  3. Why did Soviet leaders decline to use military force to preserve allied communist governments in 1989, when they and their predecessors had done so in 1953, 1956, and 1968?
  4. In what ways did the revolutions of 1989 differ from one another, and what do those differences reveal about the structure of each communist regime?
  5. What is the relationship between the fall of the Berlin Wall and the reunification of Germany, and why did reunification require international as well as German decisions?
  6. How did national movements within the Soviet Union differ from the opposition movements of eastern Europe, and why did the Soviet Union dissolve when it did?
  7. What kinds of evidence allow historians to weigh the importance of economic weakness, Western policy, Gorbachev's decisions, and popular mobilization?
  8. Why should historians resist treating the outcomes of 1989 to 1991, including democratic institutions, market economies, and European integration, as the inevitable consequence of the Cold War's end?

Historical Context

The European order that ended between 1989 and 1991 had been built in the years after the Second World War, as Chapters 124 and 125 explained. Germany was divided into the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic, Berlin was divided within the Soviet zone, and eastern and central Europe came under communist governments backed by the presence and the potential intervention of Soviet forces. The Warsaw Pact, established in 1955, and the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, known as Comecon, tied the economies and militaries of the Soviet Union's allies to Moscow, while NATO and the institutions of Western integration bound the other half of Europe to the United States and to one another. This was an order of division, but it was also an order of stability, and by the 1970s many Europeans in both halves regarded it as a permanent feature of the political landscape.

That order had been challenged before, and the challenges had been defeated. Chapter 129 examined the East German workers' uprising of 1953, the Hungarian Revolution of 1956, the construction of the Berlin Wall in 1961, and the Prague Spring of 1968, which ended with the invasion of Czechoslovakia by Warsaw Pact forces in August 1968. The justification offered afterward, commonly called the Brezhnev Doctrine, held that the socialist community had a collective right and duty to defend socialism in any member state where it was threatened. Whatever its legal status, the doctrine described a practical expectation shared by communist leaders and by their populations: that Moscow would not tolerate the loss of a communist government in the Warsaw Pact area. The expectation shaped the behavior of governments and oppositions for two decades.

Chapter 131 showed how that expectation coexisted with détente. The Helsinki Final Act of 1975 legitimized existing borders in Europe while also committing signatory states to respect human rights, and that language gave dissidents in the east a vocabulary and an international audience. Economic shocks of the 1970s exposed the weaknesses of centrally planned economies, which had borrowed heavily from Western banks and had difficulty meeting rising consumer expectations. In the early 1980s, the deployment of new intermediate-range nuclear missiles, the Soviet war in Afghanistan, and a harsher American rhetoric renewed tension between the blocs. Eastern Europe in 1980 therefore combined apparent stability with accumulating strain: governments dependent on external support, populations aware of the contrast with Western living standards, and an economic model that was losing the capacity to deliver the improvements on which its legitimacy increasingly rested.

The communist states were not identical, and this chapter depends on recognizing their differences. Poland had a strong Catholic Church and a largely private peasant agriculture, features rare in the bloc. Hungary under János Kádár had developed a limited market-oriented reform and a bargain, sometimes summarized as exchanging political quiescence for relative consumer comfort. East Germany had the most developed economy in the bloc, the largest Soviet garrison, and a uniquely intimate relationship with the Federal Republic, whose existence undermined the claim that the GDR was a separate nation. Czechoslovakia had experienced the repressive "normalization" that followed 1968. Bulgaria was among the most loyal Soviet allies, while Romania pursued an independent foreign policy abroad and a severely repressive regime at home. Yugoslavia and Albania require brief mention because they stood outside the Soviet bloc: Yugoslavia, a communist federation that had broken with Stalin in 1948, was not a member of the Warsaw Pact, and Albania had broken with Moscow in the early 1960s. Their histories were different from those of the Soviet-aligned states and are treated mainly in later chapters, especially Chapter 135 for Yugoslavia.

Finally, the Soviet Union was not the same thing as the Eastern Bloc. It was a multinational federation of fifteen union republics, of which the Russian republic was the largest, held together by the Communist Party, the security services, the military, and a centralized economy. The three Baltic republics, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, were occupied and annexed in 1940 after the secret German–Soviet protocols of 1939 had assigned them to Moscow’s sphere of influence and many of their citizens never accepted the legitimacy of that incorporation. The end of communist rule in Warsaw or Prague did not automatically end the Soviet Union, and the process by which the union itself dissolved involved questions of republican sovereignty, national identity, and institutional design that differed from the issues facing eastern European oppositions. Keeping these two arenas distinct, while showing how they affected one another, is among the central tasks of this chapter.

Section 132.1

Economic Crisis, Workers' Opposition, and the Origins of Solidarity

Poland in the late 1970s was a state whose government had staked its legitimacy on rising living standards. After the violent suppression of strikes on the Baltic coast in December 1970, which brought down Władysław Gomułka, his successor Edward Gierek, first secretary of the Polish United Workers' Party (PZPR), pursued a strategy of growth financed by Western credit. Loans were used to import technology and consumer goods in the expectation that exports would repay them. The strategy worked for a few years and then failed. The oil price shocks and recession in Western markets reduced demand for Polish exports, many imported plants proved inefficient, and the foreign debt, which exceeded twenty billion dollars by 1980 according to common estimates, required an increasing share of export earnings merely to service. Shortages of meat and other basics, queues, informal markets, and wage grievances became part of daily life, and the gap between official claims and lived experience eroded the party's authority.

The government's response to economic strain was to raise prices, and that response repeatedly provoked protest. When the government announced sharp food price increases on June 24, 1976, workers struck at several enterprises, and in Radom and at the Ursus tractor factory near Warsaw the protests were met with police violence. The government withdrew the price increases almost at once, but it also arrested and dismissed participants and prosecuted many of them. The repression produced a response that proved historically important. In September 1976 a group of intellectuals, including Jacek Kuroń, Jan Józef Lipski, and Antoni Macierewicz, established the Workers' Defence Committee (Komitet Obrony Robotników, KOR) to provide legal, financial, and medical assistance to persecuted workers and their families. Its significance lay less in its size than in its principle. Intellectuals who had been critical of the regime in the 1960s, and who had often seen workers as either passive or co-opted, now acted in direct support of workers, and workers increasingly accepted that cooperation. The communist authorities, by contrast, had long relied on keeping these groups apart.

KOR was one part of a wider structure of independent activity. Underground and semi-public publishing, known as samizdat or the "second circulation," produced newsletters, journals, and books outside state censorship. Among them was the workers' paper Robotnik. A network of informal lectures, nicknamed the Flying University, offered courses on history and social science that the official system suppressed. In 1978, activists on the Baltic coast, among them Andrzej Gwiazda, Anna Walentynowicz, and Lech Wałęsa, formed Free Trade Unions of the Coast, which distributed leaflets and documented workplace grievances. None of these groups was large, and all were harassed. Together they created the habits, contacts, and skills of an opposition able to act outside state-controlled institutions, an accomplishment that mattered enormously when a mass movement suddenly emerged in 1980.

The Catholic Church provided another and different institutional base. Poland, unlike most communist states, had a Church that retained large public attendance, extensive parish networks, and relative organizational independence. Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński, Primate of Poland since 1948, had survived imprisonment in the 1950s and had developed a cautious strategy that defended the Church's institutional autonomy while avoiding direct political confrontation. The election of Karol Wojtyła, Archbishop of Kraków, as Pope John Paul II on October 16, 1978, therefore had immediate national significance. His first pilgrimage to Poland, from June 2 to 10, 1979, drew very large crowds, and the public gatherings, with their religious language and orderly self-discipline, demonstrated to Poles that society could organize itself on a mass scale without the party. Historians generally treat the visit as an important catalyst of confidence and national solidarity. They also caution against treating religion as the sole cause of what followed: Solidarity's immediate origins lay in economic grievance and workplace organization, and its members and supporters included believers, nonbelievers, and people of many views about the Church itself.

By 1980 the government faced a crisis it could neither fund nor persuade its way out of. Another round of meat price increases on July 1, 1980, introduced by shifting much meat into higher-priced commercial sales, set off strikes in factories across the country. Local managers, anxious to restore production, often conceded wage increases, which showed workers that strikes could succeed and demonstrated that the central authorities lacked a coherent response. Gierek's government was short of money, short of credibility, and unwilling to risk a repetition of the bloody confrontation of 1970. This was the background to the shipyard strike that began in Gdańsk in the middle of August.

Section 132.2

The Gdańsk Strikes of 1980 and the Birth of Solidarity

The strike at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk began on August 14, 1980. Its immediate cause was the dismissal on August 7 of Anna Walentynowicz, a veteran crane operator and activist in the Free Trade Unions, a few months before her retirement. Workers demanded her reinstatement, along with the reinstatement of Wałęsa, who had been dismissed in 1976, and a wage increase. Wałęsa, who had worked at the shipyard and had been involved in the 1970 protests, arrived at the strike and rapidly became its leading figure. The shipyard management agreed to key demands on August 16, but by then workers from other enterprises had been asking the strikers not to abandon them. The strike committee therefore chose to continue in solidarity with other workplaces, and delegates from many factories formed the Inter-Enterprise Strike Committee (Międzyzakładowy Komitet Strajkowy, MKS). This decision, taken at a moment when the shipyard's own demands had been largely met, converted a local labor dispute into a regional and then national movement. The name Solidarity would soon express it.

The MKS drew up a list of twenty-one demands. Several concerned working conditions and economic welfare, including wage increases linked to inflation, improved supplies of food, better provision for health care and family allowances, and earlier retirement. Others were explicitly political. They called for free trade unions independent of the party and of employers, in line with international labor conventions that Poland had ratified, for recognition of the right to strike and the protection of strikers, for respect for freedom of expression, press, and publication, and for the release of political prisoners. They also called for access to the media for the Church and for public discussion of the economic situation. Strike organizers had to balance two aims. They wanted demands that would secure real change, and they also needed a document that the government could conceivably accept without abandoning the political system. The demand for independent unions was the center of the negotiation, and the Twenty-One Demands, preserved in the UNESCO Memory of the World Register, are among the most important documents of the period.

Historical Vocabulary
Solidarity (Solidarność)

Solidarity was the name adopted for the Independent Self-Governing Trade Union that emerged from the Gdańsk strikes of August 1980. Formally it was a trade union, legally registered in November 1980 after a dispute over how its statutes would refer to the leading role of the Communist Party. In practice it became a national social movement. At its height in 1981 it claimed roughly nine to ten million members, in a country of about 36 million, and it included industrial workers, farmers in the parallel Rural Solidarity, students, teachers, professionals, and intellectual advisers. The English word does not fully capture the moral meaning of the Polish original, which implied mutual obligation among people who had previously been separated by workplace, region, and social position.

The term matters for this chapter because it explains why the Polish crisis differed from earlier challenges to communist power. Earlier movements had arisen in a single city or among a single social group. Solidarity united urban and rural, working and intellectual constituencies around demands that were both economic and civic. Its membership figures are movement and later estimates, not audited counts, and they should be treated as approximate.

The government's negotiators, led by Deputy Prime Minister Mieczysław Jagielski, resisted. The authorities initially tried to confine talks to the Baltic coast and cut off telephone links to the region, but the strike spread, and a parallel strike committee in Szczecin and strikes in the mining districts of Silesia increased the pressure. Intellectual advisers, among them Tadeusz Mazowiecki and Bronisław Geremek, who came to Gdańsk to assist the strikers, helped the committee frame its position, and the Church's public statements encouraged restraint on both sides. The agreement signed on August 31, 1980, at the shipyard, accepted the creation of independent self-governing trade unions and a right to strike, and promised greater access to the media, the release of some political prisoners, and economic improvements. In exchange, the strikers accepted the leading role of the PZPR in the state, the principle of Poland's alliances, and the commitment that the new unions would not claim to be a political party. These concessions were not cosmetic, and they would become the center of later dispute over whether the union could remain within those bounds.

The Gdańsk Agreement must be understood on two levels. Legally, it created a trade union with defined rights and obligations within a one-party state. Historically, it created something broader, an organized public that was neither state nor party. Within weeks, workplaces across Poland were forming local union branches. The new union's national structure was formalized at its first national commission, with Wałęsa as chairman. The registration of the union in November 1980 was delayed by a dispute in which a Warsaw court inserted a statement about the party's leading role into the union's statutes. Solidarity accepted the principle in an appended statement after threatening a general strike, and the Supreme Court confirmed registration on November 10. Meanwhile, Gierek was removed as party leader in September 1980 and replaced by Stanisław Kania, and the party, whose own membership included many who sympathized with the union, found itself uncertain how to respond. The government had accepted a union it could neither absorb nor control.

Section 132.3

Martial Law, Repression, and the Survival of Opposition, 1981 to 1988

During 1981, relations between Solidarity and the government deteriorated through a combination of economic crisis, organizational conflict, and external pressure. The economy continued to decline, with shortages, rationing of basic goods, and falling output. Solidarity itself was divided between those who wanted to consolidate a union within existing constraints and those who sought deeper change in how enterprises and the state were governed. Its first national congress, held in two parts in September and October 1981, adopted a program calling for self-managed enterprises and a more accountable political system, and it addressed a message of support to workers in other Soviet bloc countries, a gesture that alarmed Moscow. Within the party, Wojciech Jaruzelski, who had been defense minister since 1968, became prime minister in February 1981 and succeeded Kania as first secretary in October 1981.

Soviet leaders watched with growing alarm. The Politburo in Moscow debated how to respond, pressed the Polish leadership to act decisively, and organized large Warsaw Pact military exercises on Poland's borders. Documents later opened in Russian and Polish archives show that Soviet leaders were reluctant to commit their own troops to an invasion of Poland while their forces were engaged in Afghanistan, and that they preferred the Polish authorities to resolve the crisis themselves. Jaruzelski and his supporters later argued that martial law was a lesser evil that forestalled a Soviet intervention, and that claim remains a subject of historical debate. Critics respond that the available evidence does not establish that an invasion was imminent, that the Polish leadership at times encouraged Soviet pressure for its own purposes, and that the decision for martial law reflected domestic aims, including preserving the party's monopoly. The question matters for assessing responsibility, and scholars continue to weigh the evidence of Soviet intentions against that of Polish decision-making.

On the night of December 12 to 13, 1981, the authorities imposed martial law. A Military Council of National Salvation led by Jaruzelski assumed control. Telephone lines were cut, travel restricted, public gatherings forbidden, and a curfew imposed. Several thousand Solidarity leaders and activists, including Wałęsa, were interned, and in the following months about ten thousand people were held in internment camps according to commonly cited figures. Strikes at factories and mines were broken by troops and riot police. At the Wujek coal mine in Katowice on December 16, security forces fired on striking miners and nine of them were killed, a massacre that remained a symbol of the repression. The legal framework of independent union activity was dismantled, and Solidarity was banned by law in October 1982. The state also expanded military control over enterprises and strengthened censorship. Martial law was suspended at the end of 1982 and formally lifted in July 1983.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life under Martial Law

For most Poles martial law was experienced less as a political drama than as a sudden change in the conditions of daily life. A curfew limited movement at night, telephone calls were monitored or unavailable, and travel between towns required permission. Soldiers and police checked papers at roadblocks and railway stations. Radio and television were run by uniformed officers, and the announcer in uniform became one of the period's most recognizable images. Workers in major industries were militarized, which meant that absence or protest could be treated as a military offense. Shortages persisted, with ration cards for meat, sugar, and other goods, and family networks, church food distribution, and informal exchange were essential to getting by.

Opposition also became part of everyday life. Families supported interned relatives, and the Church provided shelter, legal aid, and meeting places, including parish halls where banned art exhibitions, theater, and discussion groups took place. Clandestine printing used primitive equipment, and activists distributed newsletters through informal networks of friends and workplaces. Wearing a small badge or symbol could signal allegiance and invite harassment. The cumulative effect was a society that was disciplined but not reconciled, one in which large numbers of people held views they no longer expected the state to change. Sources for this experience include underground publications, church records, security police files, which record the regime's perceptions rather than the whole truth, and oral histories gathered later.

Martial law did not destroy Solidarity as a social force. It removed the movement's legal and public structure, which made large-scale open organization impossible, and it imprisoned or exiled many leaders. But the movement's underground networks survived. A Temporary Coordinating Commission, led at first by Zbigniew Bujak, who had avoided arrest, coordinated clandestine activity. Underground presses produced hundreds of titles and periodicals, and the Catholic Church, supported by Pope John Paul II, who visited Poland again in 1983 and 1987, offered a space for assembly. International pressure included Western economic sanctions, which hurt an already strained economy, and material aid to the opposition from trade unions and church organizations abroad. Wałęsa's award of the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983, accepted in his absence by his wife Danuta, signaled that the movement retained international recognition. The regime's own methods turned lethal in October 1984, when security police officers abducted and killed Father Jerzy Popiełuszko, a priest associated with Solidarity whose sermons had drawn large crowds. The murder, which the authorities were eventually forced to prosecute, provoked outrage and enormous funeral attendance. It showed that coercion could damage the regime's standing as much as the opposition's.

By the mid-1980s the Polish government had stabilized public order but not the economy or its own legitimacy. Jaruzelski tried a managed liberalization that included partial amnesties in 1984 and 1986 and consultative bodies intended to provide a controlled outlet for opinion, but the regime found no reliable partners who would work within it. A new price increase in early 1988 and a wave of strikes in April and May 1988, followed by larger strikes in August at the Gdańsk shipyard, the Silesian mines, and other workplaces, demonstrated the persistence of social anger. This new strike wave, driven largely by younger workers, was not simply a repetition of 1980. It occurred in a changed environment, in which Gorbachev's reforms in the Soviet Union had altered expectations about what Moscow would accept. The government, convinced that it could neither govern effectively without broader consent nor crush another national strike, proposed talks. After a meeting between Interior Minister Czesław Kiszczak and Wałęsa on August 31, 1988, the leaders of both sides moved toward negotiations, and a strategy of bargaining began to appear more attractive than renewed confrontation, though the talks themselves were delayed until early 1989 by disagreements over who would participate.

Section 132.4

The Polish Round Table, the Elections of 1989, and the First Noncommunist-Led Government

The Round Table talks opened in Warsaw on February 6, 1989, and concluded on April 5. They brought together representatives of the government and its allied parties, of Solidarity and the opposition, and of the Church as observers. Rather than a single negotiation, the Round Table operated through working groups on political reform, economic and social policy, and trade union pluralism, with plenary sessions to approve agreements. Each side entered the talks with different calculations. The party leadership hoped to secure the opposition's cooperation in tackling the economic crisis and in sharing responsibility for painful measures, while retaining decisive control of the state. The opposition, led by Wałęsa and advisers such as Mazowiecki, Geremek, and Adam Michnik, sought the relegalization of Solidarity and a pathway toward political pluralism, but it did not expect, at the outset, to win a government.

The agreements reached in April 1989 were a compromise. Solidarity would be legalized, and it was formally registered on April 17. A freely contested Senate of one hundred members would be restored as the upper house of parliament, which Poland had not had since 1946. For the lower house, the Sejm, the agreement reserved 65 percent of the seats, 299 of 460, for the Polish United Workers' Party and its allied parties, the United Peasant Party, the Democratic Party, and several Catholic groups, while the remaining 35 percent, 161 seats, would be openly contested. A new office of president was created with substantial powers, expected to be filled by Jaruzelski. The party saw this arrangement as guaranteeing continued control, since it ensured a governing majority and the presidency, while allowing limited opposition participation that would lend legitimacy to unpopular reforms. The opposition saw it as an opening that could be enlarged.

The summer of 1989 was a period of improvisation, and it produced the first major breach in communist monopoly rule. The Sejm and Senate, meeting as the National Assembly, elected Jaruzelski president on July 19, but only by the narrowest margin, and several of his own supporters abstained or voted against. The Polish experience was a negotiated transition, in which the incumbents and opposition arrived at a settlement by bargaining, not a revolutionary rupture in which one side overthrew the other. This distinction mattered. Negotiation produced continuity in many institutions and a relatively peaceful transfer of power, but it also meant that the settlement's terms, including guarantees for officials and military leaders, were open to later criticism from those who believed that it had protected the old elite too well. The Polish case also provided a model, and a warning, for others. It demonstrated that communist rulers could be pressed into negotiation, and it encouraged oppositions elsewhere, particularly in Hungary, to hold out for similar arrangements. Its main significance was to show that organized social resistance, begun in a workplace dispute, had survived repression and grown into a force capable of negotiating the dismantling of a political monopoly.

Section 132.5

The Soviet System in Crisis: Economic Stagnation and Political Succession

When Leonid Brezhnev died on November 10, 1982, after eighteen years as general secretary, the Soviet Union appeared to outside observers to be a superpower at the height of its military reach. It had achieved rough nuclear parity with the United States, maintained large conventional forces in Europe, and exercised influence from Cuba to Angola to Vietnam. Beneath that surface the system faced mounting difficulties. Economic growth, which had been rapid in the 1950s and respectable in the 1960s, slowed markedly in the 1970s, and by the early 1980s official and unofficial estimates alike registered very low growth in productivity. The reasons were structural. A centrally planned economy, in which ministries set output targets and prices, rewarded quantity over quality, discouraged innovation, and produced chronic shortages alongside wasted resources. Raw materials, especially oil and gas, supplied export earnings that masked these weaknesses, but they could not substitute for efficiency.

Several burdens compounded the problem. Military spending was very large, though how large remains uncertain, since Soviet budget figures were opaque and Western estimates of its share of national output ranged widely, from low double digits to higher. The war in Afghanistan, begun in December 1979, consumed lives and resources without prospect of victory. Subsidies to allies in eastern Europe and to clients elsewhere imposed further costs. The population experienced these strains through consumer shortages, poor housing, declining health indicators, corruption in the supply system, and the widespread practice of relying on personal connections and the informal economy to obtain what the official one could not provide. A cynicism about official promises, evident in private humor and in popular indifference, was widespread, though it coexisted with real national pride in the Soviet Union's wartime achievements and superpower status.

Politically, the leadership had aged. The succession to Brezhnev illustrated the problem. Yuri Andropov, former head of the KGB, became general secretary in November 1982 and attempted an anti-corruption and labor discipline campaign, along with some modest encouragement of economic experimentation, but he was seriously ill and died in February 1984. His successor, Konstantin Chernenko, was elderly and frail, and he died on March 10, 1985. These short tenures left policy largely static, though they also promoted a younger generation. Andropov had favored the advancement of Mikhail Gorbachev, who as a Central Committee secretary for agriculture from 1978 and a Politburo member from 1980 had acquired experience of the difficulties of Soviet administration.

The Politburo chose Gorbachev as general secretary on March 11, 1985, at the age of fifty-four, the youngest member of the leadership by a considerable margin. Born in the Stavropol region and trained as a lawyer, he had risen through the party hierarchy and was known as energetic, articulate, and open to discussion. His allies included Eduard Shevardnadze, who became foreign minister in July 1985, and Alexander Yakovlev, a former ambassador to Canada and future architect of glasnost. Expectations were mixed. Many Soviet citizens welcomed a younger leader and hoped for improved living standards, while the party and state bureaucracies, which held great powers to delay and dilute change, remained deeply entrenched.

It is important not to read the Soviet Union's later dissolution back into this moment. In 1985 the Soviet state was weakened, but it was not on the verge of collapse. It possessed a coercive apparatus capable of suppressing dissent, a functioning if inefficient economy, and a political elite with strong interests in continuity. Historians who emphasize structural economic decline do not claim that disintegration was inevitable, and the possibility of reform, repression, or stagnation along the lines of the previous decades remained real. What Gorbachev's accession changed was the political direction of the leadership, and with it, in the following years, the range of what could be said and done.

Section 132.6

Perestroika, Glasnost, and the Reform of Soviet Society

Gorbachev's initial program, announced in 1985 and elaborated at the Twenty-Seventh Party Congress in early 1986, emphasized acceleration, a faster pace of economic growth through better management and technological modernization. Within a year or two, the language shifted to perestroika, restructuring, a deeper reform of economic and political institutions. Its sister concept was glasnost, openness or publicity, which at first meant a campaign to make officials more accountable by exposing waste and corruption. The two ideas were intended to reinforce one another: criticism would help identify failures, and restructuring would correct them.

Historical Vocabulary
Perestroika and Glasnost

Perestroika (Russian for restructuring) referred to the reform of the Soviet economic and political system. Glasnost (openness or publicity) referred to the relaxation of censorship and the expansion of public debate. Both words had Soviet precedents, and glasnost in particular had been invoked by earlier reformers. In Gorbachev's usage they were meant to renew socialism, not to replace it. He repeatedly described the goal as a better and more democratic socialism, and there is little evidence that he intended in 1985 to abandon the one-party system or to preside over the end of the union.

Their historical importance lies in the gap between purpose and effect. Glasnost permitted public discussion of the Stalinist terror, of Soviet economic failure, of the Chernobyl disaster in April 1986, and of the suppressed histories of national groups. Perestroika dismantled some mechanisms of central control without building new ones. Students should avoid translations such as "reform" or "liberalization" that suggest a program of democratization from the outset, and should also avoid treating the later outcomes as the intended ones.

Economic reform proceeded unevenly. The Law on State Enterprise, adopted in 1987 and implemented in 1988, gave enterprises greater autonomy and encouraged self-financing, while the Law on Cooperatives in 1988 permitted small private-sector activity in the form of cooperatives. These measures created new markets and incomes, but they also disrupted the planned system's supply relationships without replacing them with reliable market institutions. Prices remained largely controlled, shortages worsened, and the budget deficit grew, aggravated by the fall in world oil prices in 1986, which reduced hard currency earnings. Gorbachev hesitated to undertake the painful price liberalization and closure of unviable enterprises that a thorough market transition would have required, partly because of fear of popular unrest and partly because of resistance within the party. The result was a hybrid economy that lost the discipline of the old system without gaining the efficiency of a new one.

Glasnost transformed public life more quickly. Censorship eased, and newspapers, magazines, and television programs began to publish exposés and historical revelations. Writers and filmmakers whose works had been banned reached audiences. Historians reexamined the collectivization of agriculture, the purges of the 1930s, and the secret protocols to the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact. In December 1986 Gorbachev telephoned the physicist and dissident Andrei Sakharov, exiled in the city of Gorky, to invite him to return to Moscow. Political prisoners were released in substantial numbers in 1986 to 1988, and organizations such as Memorial, devoted to documenting the repression, began to operate. Perhaps the most important effect was on public confidence: the discovery that the official history had been systematically falsified weakened the claim of the party to speak for the nation.

Political reform followed. At the Nineteenth Party Conference in the summer of 1988, Gorbachev won approval for a reorganization of the state that would create a new Congress of People's Deputies, a body of 2,250 members, partly elected by competitive vote, and a smaller standing Supreme Soviet. The elections of March 26, 1989, were the first partially competitive national elections in the Soviet Union since 1917. A third of the seats were reserved for public organizations, including the Communist Party, but in the territorial constituencies voters rejected many prominent party officials, and independent reformers, including Sakharov and Boris Yeltsin, were elected. The Congress, meeting from May 25, 1989, and broadcast live, became a national spectacle in which deputies criticized the military, the KGB, and the party. In March 1990 the Congress repealed the article of the constitution that guaranteed the party's monopoly of political leadership and created the post of executive president, to which it elected Gorbachev. That office increased his formal power, but it also separated his authority from the party apparatus on which he had relied.

The consequences were contradictory. Reform weakened the traditional controls of censorship, party discipline, and fear without creating a stable alternative economic or political system. Conservatives within the party, including many who held positions in the ministries, the military, and the security services, regarded the changes as dangerous. Radical reformers, in turn, regarded them as inadequate, and Yeltsin, who had been removed as Moscow party chief in 1987 after criticizing the pace of change, became a rallying point for those who wanted faster transformation. Gorbachev tried to occupy a middle position, balancing between the two, and as the economy deteriorated and the center's authority waned, that position became harder to hold. The most consequential unintended effect, examined in Section 132.17, was the emergence of national movements in the union republics, which used the new freedoms to question the union's structure itself.

Throughout, it is essential to distinguish Gorbachev's intentions from the eventual outcomes. He sought to preserve a transformed Soviet system, with a reformed Communist Party at its center, and he regarded the union as a voluntary federation that could be renewed. The dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 was not a goal of perestroika, and it resulted from developments, including the rise of republican leaderships, the failure of the August 1991 coup, and decisions by Yeltsin and others, that Gorbachev neither planned nor wanted. At the same time, the reforms made those developments possible, and historians debate how far a different sequence of reforms might have produced a different result.

Section 132.7

New Thinking, Arms Control, and the Changing Soviet Foreign Policy

Domestic reform required a changed international environment. The Soviet leadership concluded that its economy could not sustain unlimited competition in armaments and that its security would be better served by reducing confrontation. Gorbachev and his advisers articulated this in the doctrine of new political thinking, which asserted that nuclear weapons had made war irrational, that security was interdependent rather than zero-sum, and that universal human interests took precedence over class interests in international relations. It was a significant departure from the Marxist-Leninist emphasis on struggle between social systems, though Soviet officials continued to describe their policy in socialist terms and the shift was gradual.

Negotiations with the United States intensified. Gorbachev met President Ronald Reagan for the first time at Geneva on November 19 to 21, 1985, and although no agreements on arms were reached, the two leaders produced a joint statement that a nuclear war could not be won and must never be fought, and they developed a working relationship that mattered later. Reagan, who had entered office in 1981 with sharply anticommunist rhetoric and had pursued a major military buildup, including the Strategic Defense Initiative, proved willing to negotiate. At Reykjavík on October 11 and 12, 1986, the leaders came close to agreement on deep reductions in nuclear arsenals, possibly even their elimination, before talks broke down over Reagan's refusal to limit the Strategic Defense Initiative to laboratory research. The meeting failed in its immediate purpose but broke psychological barriers and set the terms for subsequent negotiations.

The first major agreement came on December 8, 1987, when Gorbachev and Reagan signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty in Washington. The treaty eliminated an entire class of weapons: all ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometers. This was not a numerical ceiling but an elimination, accompanied by unusually intrusive on-site inspection, and the roughly 2,700 missiles destroyed under its terms included the Soviet SS-20 and the American Pershing II and ground-launched cruise missiles whose deployment in the early 1980s had caused such tension in Europe, as Chapter 131 described. The Soviet side accepted asymmetrical reductions, since it possessed more of the missiles concerned. For European populations, the treaty carried particular meaning because the weapons in question had been targeted at, and stationed in, Europe itself.

The Soviet war in Afghanistan was another burden Gorbachev sought to reduce. He described it in 1986 as a bleeding wound, and Soviet diplomats negotiated the Geneva Accords of April 1988, under which Soviet forces withdrew in stages. The withdrawal was completed on February 15, 1989. The war had cost the lives of many thousands of Soviet soldiers and many more Afghans, and it had weakened public confidence in the military and the leadership. Its end freed resources and attention for domestic issues and for relations with Europe, and it signaled to the world that Moscow was prepared to accept the limits of its power.

Section 132.8

The End of the Brezhnev Doctrine and the Limits of Soviet Intervention

The Brezhnev Doctrine, introduced in Chapter 129, was an informal principle rather than a treaty obligation, derived from Soviet justifications of the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia. It asserted that the defense of socialism in any country was the common affair of the socialist states and that threats to it could justify intervention. In practice, it formalized the expectation that Moscow would act to prevent the loss of communist governments within the bloc. Soviet troops were stationed in East Germany, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary, in numbers that remained very large; in East Germany alone they amounted to several hundred thousand personnel. These forces were a visible reminder that political change in the region could, in principle, be reversed from outside.

Gorbachev's approach to the allies shifted gradually. In his early years he emphasized reform within the bloc and mutual consultation among equals, criticizing the old pattern of Soviet dictation. By 1988 he was publicly endorsing the right of each nation to choose its own system, as at the United Nations, and in 1989 he and other Soviet officials repeated the point in bilateral meetings and in the Warsaw Pact. A statement by the Warsaw Pact's Political Consultative Committee in July 1989 affirmed the principle of noninterference. A Soviet foreign ministry spokesman, Gennady Gerasimov, later joked that the old doctrine had been replaced by what he called the Sinatra Doctrine, after a popular song about doing things one's own way; the phrase is useful as a summary but is a journalistic label, not a formal policy.

Relations with individual leaders varied. Gorbachev was frank with Jaruzelski about the need for compromise in Poland and did not oppose the Round Table. He was supportive of Hungarian reformers, who in turn viewed him as an ally against conservatives. With East Germany's Erich Honecker, whose government had grown more hostile to glasnost, relations were cooler. Honecker's regime banned the Soviet magazine Sputnik in 1988 because of its revelations about Soviet history, and Gorbachev's visit to East Berlin in October 1989 for the fortieth anniversary of the GDR is widely remembered for its message that leaders who failed to adapt would be left behind. The precise words attributed to him are debated and are often quoted loosely, and students should rely on the substance, not on a supposed slogan. Other leaders, including Nicolae Ceaușescu in Romania, Todor Zhivkov in Bulgaria, Miloš Jakeš in Czechoslovakia, and Honecker, resisted reform and hoped to wait for Gorbachev to fail.

The reduced expectation of Soviet intervention altered the calculations of both governments and oppositions. Communist leaders could no longer assume that opposition could be suppressed by invoking Soviet backing, and some concluded that they had to bargain. Oppositions, long deterred by the memory of 1956 and 1968, became bolder, though the process was gradual and uncertain: each success encouraged others, but many participants in 1989 did not know in advance whether the Red Army would intervene. Soviet restraint was not by itself sufficient. It created an opening, and what happened next depended on local movements, leaders, and circumstances. Without Solidarity's endurance, the Hungarian reformers' initiative, the Leipzig demonstrators' courage, and the organization of Czechoslovak students and dissidents, the withdrawal of Soviet intervention would not have produced the revolutions of 1989.

Historical Debate

Why Did Moscow Refrain from Intervening in 1989?

The question: Soviet forces remained in several allied countries in 1989, and Soviet leaders had used force in 1953, 1956, and 1968 and had considered it in 1980 and 1981. Why did they not intervene to prevent the loss of communist governments in 1989?

Interpretation A: Gorbachev's convictions and priorities. Some historians emphasize Gorbachev's belief in the principles he proclaimed, including the right of peoples to choose, the futility of force, and the priority of domestic reform and relations with the West. On this view, the decision not to use force was a deliberate political choice by a leader who regarded intervention as incompatible with his entire program.
Interpretation B: Constraints and costs. Others stress structural constraints. The Soviet Union needed Western credit and technology, lacked the resources for a prolonged occupation or repression, and faced domestic opposition to costly foreign commitments after Afghanistan. Intervention would also have destroyed the improved relations on which Soviet security and economic hopes depended. In this view, Gorbachev's convictions mattered, but his options were already narrow.
Interpretation C: Local conditions and misjudgment. A third group emphasizes that Soviet leaders did not simply decide to let go. They expected reform communists, such as those in Poland and Hungary, to remain in power and the Warsaw Pact to survive in some form. Events in 1989 moved faster than Moscow anticipated, and by the time the collapse of governments was evident, the political cost of intervening had become prohibitive.

Evidence: Historians draw on Soviet Politburo records and the notes of Gorbachev's advisers, on memoirs by participants, on records of conversations between Gorbachev and eastern European leaders, and on diplomatic reporting from Western and eastern European governments. These sources illuminate intentions but are shaped by the authors' later reputations, and they do not settle counterfactual questions, such as what Soviet leaders would have done if the crises had arisen earlier or in a different order.

Why it matters: The answer affects how much weight to give individual leadership, structural weakness, and local agency in explaining the revolutions of 1989. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and most scholars combine them.

Student task: Identify evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation. Then explain which additional documents or testimonies would help determine whether Soviet restraint was a principled decision, a pragmatic response to limits, or a consequence of events moving faster than expected.

Students should note one further distinction. Gorbachev's public commitment to sovereign choice was real, but its application was uneven and evolving. In 1988 and early 1989 he and his advisers still hoped to see reform communists lead change in the region. They were reluctant to see the Warsaw Pact dissolve and they expected that economic and security ties would persist. Only gradually, in the summer and autumn of 1989, did the implications become clear. As the next division shows, Soviet military forces were still present in Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and East Germany when the communist governments collapsed, but they remained in their barracks.

Division II outcome. Reforms intended to strengthen the Soviet system weakened its coercive and ideological foundations, while a changed foreign policy removed the expectation of intervention that had underpinned communist rule in eastern Europe. These developments created the strategic environment in which the revolutions of 1989 took place.

Section 132.9

Hungary's Negotiated Transition and the Opening of the Iron Curtain

Hungary entered the late 1980s with the most reform-minded communist establishment in the Soviet bloc, and with a growing sense that the established compromise had run its course. Under János Kádár, who had led the party since the suppression of the 1956 revolution, the government had permitted small private enterprise, relatively tolerant cultural policy, and a measure of consumer abundance. In exchange it expected political acquiescence. By the 1980s this arrangement was failing. The country had accumulated one of the highest per capita foreign debts in the region, living standards stagnated, and the economy could no longer finance both the welfare commitments and the imports on which the population relied. Younger party members, economists, and intellectuals pressed for deeper change, and independent groups, such as the Hungarian Democratic Forum, formed in 1987, and the Alliance of Free Democrats and the youth organization Fidesz, formed in 1988, began to operate openly.

The party reacted by changing its leadership. Kádár, by then elderly and ill, was removed as general secretary in May 1988 and replaced by Károly Grósz, and within a year more reform-minded figures, including Imre Pozsgay, Rezső Nyers, and Miklós Németh, who became prime minister in November 1988, had taken the lead. Reform within the Hungarian Socialist Workers' Party proceeded alongside the growth of independent political organizations, and in early 1989 the party accepted in principle the introduction of a multiparty system. In February 1989 the Central Committee endorsed a reassessment of the events of 1956 as a popular uprising rather than a counterrevolution, a historical judgment with immediate political consequences because it reversed the founding justification of the Kádár regime.

That reassessment culminated in a public ceremony. On June 16, 1989, the thirty-first anniversary of his execution, Imre Nagy, prime minister during the 1956 revolution, was reburied in Budapest together with four others executed with him, in a ceremony attended by a very large crowd at Heroes' Square. Some speakers, notably the young Fidesz leader Viktor Orbán, called for the withdrawal of Soviet troops. The reburial did not by itself end communist rule, but it opened a space for public memory that the party could no longer control, and it demonstrated the capacity of the opposition to mobilize large crowds peacefully. National round-table negotiations between the party and the opposition groups, which ran from June to September, agreed on a framework for competitive elections, constitutional revisions, and the legal status of parties. A divided opposition, including those who signed the agreement and those who refused, would challenge parts of the process, and a referendum in November 1989 resolved remaining disagreements on the order of elections. On October 23, 1989, the thirty-third anniversary of the 1956 uprising, Hungary was proclaimed a republic, dropping the word "People's" from its name. Earlier that month the ruling party had dissolved itself at a congress and re-formed as the Hungarian Socialist Party. Free elections followed in March and April 1990.

Hungary's domestic transformation acquired international consequences through the border. The Hungarian government decided in early 1989 to dismantle the technical barrier on its frontier with Austria, which had been fortified under Soviet-era arrangements. Dismantling began on May 2, 1989, for reasons that included the cost of maintaining the system and the government's desire to reduce international isolation, and it did not initially mean free passage across the frontier. Hungarian citizens could by then travel relatively freely, but East Germans, who could travel to Hungary as tourists, were not permitted to leave for Austria. By the summer, thousands of East Germans had gathered in Hungary, many sheltering at the West German embassy in Budapest and at church-run camps, in the hope of reaching the West.

Two events must be distinguished. The Pan-European Picnic, held on August 19, 1989, near Sopron on the Austrian frontier, was an organized gathering of opposition groups, with the support of Austrian and Hungarian politicians, including Otto von Habsburg. For several hours a border gate was opened symbolically, and some six hundred East Germans, who had learned of the event, crossed into Austria. The border guards did not use force, and the event revealed that the frontier regime had weakened. It was not, however, a formal policy change. That came later. On August 25 Prime Minister Németh and Foreign Minister Gyula Horn met West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl in Bonn, and Hungary decided to allow East Germans to leave for Austria. The decision was implemented from midnight on September 10 to 11, 1989, and tens of thousands of East Germans crossed within days, traveling through Austria to West Germany. Hungary thereby suspended a bilateral agreement with the GDR that had obliged it to prevent such departures.

Hungary's decision deepened East Germany's emigration crisis and showed that a reforming communist government could defy an allied regime's demands without Soviet intervention. Its domestic transition thus helped spread the crisis across the bloc.

Section 132.10

East Germany's Peaceful Revolution and the Fall of the Berlin Wall

The German Democratic Republic seemed in the summer of 1989 to be among the most stable states of the bloc. It had the strongest economy in the Soviet sphere, an extensive security apparatus under the Ministry for State Security, the Stasi, and a leadership led since 1971 by Erich Honecker that rejected reform. Yet it faced a legitimacy problem that no other communist state shared to the same degree. East Germans watched West German television, had relatives in the West, and compared their situation continuously with that of a prosperous and democratic state that shared their language. The Berlin Wall, built in 1961 as Chapter 129 recounted, and the fortified inner-German border had been designed to hold the population in. At least 140 people are documented as dying at the Berlin Wall itself, and many more died or were injured elsewhere on the border, according to research by historical institutes in Berlin and Potsdam.

By 1989 several forces converged. A growing number of citizens applied for exit permits, and some left through embassies in Warsaw, Prague, and Budapest. In the Prague embassy of the Federal Republic, thousands of East Germans took refuge in September, and on September 30 West German Foreign Minister Hans-Dietrich Genscher announced from the embassy balcony that they would be allowed to leave. The East German government permitted their departure by sealed trains through East German territory, which made it appear that the state was in effect expelling its own citizens, and when crowds gathered at Dresden station in early October, police violence marred the passage of the trains. Meanwhile the Hungarian route continued to channel others to the West.

Inside the country, opposition organized in unofficial groups, many of them with church connections. The Protestant churches were the one institution with some autonomy under the regime, and they provided meeting space and a degree of protection for peace groups, environmental activists, and human rights networks. In Leipzig, prayers for peace held on Mondays at the Nikolai Church since 1982 became the starting point for demonstrations after services. In September 1989 a group of activists, including Bärbel Bohley, founded New Forum, a civic movement which sought dialogue about reform and applied for official registration, which the authorities denied. Other groups, such as Democracy Now and Democratic Awakening, followed, and these early organizations typically sought a reformed and democratic GDR, not unification with West Germany. The demonstrations grew week by week. On October 9, about seventy thousand people marched around the Leipzig ring road, despite widespread fear that security forces would act as they had in China in June 1989. Local figures, including the conductor Kurt Masur and several party officials, appealed publicly for calm, and the authorities did not intervene with force. The day is generally treated as a turning point because it showed that the state either would not or could not respond with massive violence. The reasons remain debated, including uncertainty at the center about who had authority to order such an action and the absence of any Soviet encouragement.

Section 132.11

Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution and the Restoration of Political Pluralism

Czechoslovakia in 1989 was shaped by the memory of 1968. After the Warsaw Pact invasion ended the Prague Spring, as Chapter 129 recounted, the party under Gustáv Husák carried out a purge of reformers from the party and public life and pursued "normalization," a policy that traded relative material comfort for political passivity and public conformity. Many professionals worked in menial jobs, and the regime relied heavily on its police. Open dissent was limited to a small circle. In 1977 a group of intellectuals, including the playwright Václav Havel, the philosopher Jan Patočka, and the former foreign minister Jiří Hájek, issued Charter 77, a statement calling on the government to respect the human rights provisions of the Helsinki Final Act. Signatories were harassed and often imprisoned, and Havel spent several years in prison in the early 1980s. Charter 77 never became a mass movement, but it created a network of people with moral authority, and it sustained independent cultural life in the form of underground theater, music, and samizdat publication.

In the late 1980s, change in the Soviet Union and the success of reform elsewhere in the bloc began to affect public mood. Husák was replaced as party leader by Miloš Jakeš in December 1987, but the leadership remained resistant to reform. Public demonstrations became more frequent, including commemorations of the self-immolation of the student Jan Palach in January 1969 and the anniversary of the August 1968 invasion, and in 1988 a candlelight demonstration by Catholics in Bratislava for religious freedom was violently dispersed. The Catholic Church, whose primate, Cardinal František Tomášek, had become more outspoken, was another source of dissent. By the autumn of 1989 the regime was isolated. Its neighbors Poland and Hungary were undergoing change, and the East German exodus had passed through Czechoslovak territory.

The spark came on November 17, 1989, International Students' Day, which commemorated the Nazi closure of Czech universities and execution of student leaders in 1939. Students organized an officially sanctioned march in Prague, which turned into a larger demonstration of several tens of thousands of people. On Národní třída, riot police blocked the route and beat many of the participants, producing numerous injuries. A false rumor that a student had been killed spread rapidly. The suppression, rather than frightening people, triggered a rapid expansion of protest. Students and actors went on strike, theaters became centers of discussion, and on November 19 opposition groups established Civic Forum (Občanské fórum) in Prague, with Havel as its leading figure, and Public Against Violence (Verejnosť proti násiliu) in Bratislava. The two groups gave the protest an organizational center and a negotiating body.

Section 132.12

Bulgaria, Romania, and the Different Paths away from Communist Rule

Bulgaria and Romania completed the sequence of 1989, but their experiences differed sharply from those of Poland, Hungary, East Germany, and Czechoslovakia, and from each other. In Bulgaria, Todor Zhivkov had ruled since 1954, longer than any other leader in the bloc, and the regime had been among the most closely aligned with Moscow. In the late 1980s it became notorious for a campaign of forced assimilation of the country's ethnic Turkish minority, who were compelled to adopt Bulgarian names, and in the summer of 1989 some three hundred thousand people left for Turkey in an exodus that damaged both the economy and the regime's international standing. Small dissident groups, including the Club for the Support of Glasnost and Perestroika and the environmental movement Ecoglasnost, which had been mobilized by pollution in the city of Ruse, had begun to organize. Their activities were modest compared with those in Poland or Czechoslovakia.

Change came from within the party. On November 10, 1989, the day after the Berlin Wall opened, a Central Committee plenum removed Zhivkov and replaced him with the foreign minister, Petar Mladenov, who had the support of reform-minded colleagues and, apparently, of Moscow. The new leadership promised reform. Opposition groups, now allowed to organize, formed the Union of Democratic Forces in December 1989, large demonstrations took place in Sofia, and the party agreed to drop the constitutional guarantee of its leading role in January 1990. Round-table talks in early 1990 led to a transitional arrangement, the Communist Party renamed itself the Bulgarian Socialist Party in April, and elections in June 1990 returned the Socialists to power. In Bulgaria, therefore, an internal party change preceded and shaped mobilization from below, and former communist elites retained considerable influence.

Romania's path was the most violent. Nicolae Ceaușescu, in power since 1965, had combined an independent foreign policy, which gained him Western esteem in the 1960s and 1970s, with a personality cult, a pervasive security police known as the Securitate, and, in the 1980s, severe austerity imposed to repay foreign debt, which led to shortages of food, heating, and electricity. His programs of rural "systematization," which threatened to demolish thousands of villages, and the large-scale reconstruction of central Bucharest, caused further resentment. Unlike elsewhere, Romania had no organized dissident movement of comparable scale, and Ceaușescu publicly denounced the changes in neighboring states.

The unrest began in Timișoara in western Romania in mid-December 1989. When the authorities moved to evict László Tőkés, a pastor of the Hungarian Reformed Church who had criticized the regime, parishioners, including both Hungarians and Romanians, gathered to protect him. On December 16 and 17 the crowd grew and turned into a general protest, and security forces and army units opened fire, killing and wounding many. Ceaușescu left for a visit to Iran, returned on December 20, and called a mass rally in Bucharest on December 21 to demonstrate support. The crowd began to jeer him, the broadcast was interrupted, and protests spread through the capital, where troops and police again fired on demonstrators. On December 22 the army command withdrew its support, Ceaușescu and his wife Elena fled the Central Committee building by helicopter, and the regime collapsed. A group of second-rank communists, military officers, and dissidents formed the National Salvation Front, led by Ion Iliescu, a former party official who had fallen out with Ceaușescu, and announced that it had taken power.

Fighting continued for several days, with confused exchanges of fire in Bucharest and elsewhere between army units and forces thought to be loyal to Ceaușescu, and the majority of the roughly one thousand people killed in the revolution, according to commonly cited estimates, died after December 22. The Ceaușescus were captured near Târgoviște, tried on December 25 before a hastily assembled military tribunal on charges that included genocide and the mismanagement of the economy, convicted, and shot the same day. The summary proceedings, which were broadcast in part on television, were criticized for their lack of due process, though they were popular among many Romanians. The nature of the transition remains contested. Some historians and many Romanians argue that the Front's leaders, many drawn from the former party and security apparatus, exploited a genuine popular uprising to take power, and they point to unresolved questions about the role of the Securitate and the identity of those who fired at crowds after December 22. Others emphasize genuine mass participation and the overthrow of a dictator. The Front won the elections of May 1990, and many of the issues surrounding the revolution remained subjects of investigation and political dispute for decades.

Multiple Perspectives

Autumn 1989: Four Perspectives on the Collapse of Communist Authority

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified by historians. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or bear equal responsibility.

Protesters and opposition activists: For demonstrators in Leipzig or Prague, the central experience was the overcoming of fear. Many had been told for decades that opposition was futile and dangerous. They did not know if the army or police would act, and attendance at a demonstration was a personal risk, taken with family, workplace, and career in mind. The sense of collective power, once it appeared, was transformative.
Reform communists: Reformers within the party, such as those in Hungary and Poland, often believed that change was necessary to save the system or to protect their own country's future. Some saw negotiation as a way to share the burden of crisis, while others recognized that they were negotiating the end of one-party rule. Many hoped to retain influence, and many were eventually surprised by the speed with which authority slipped from them.
Hard-line leaders: Figures like Honecker, Jakeš, and Ceaușescu interpreted reform as a betrayal of the principles of the movement and the sources of its stability. They relied on traditional methods of control and expected support from Moscow, or at least that Gorbachev would fail. Their isolation in the late 1980s reflected the loss of the assumption on which their authority rested.
Security forces and soldiers: Police, border guards, and soldiers faced orders that might require violence against fellow citizens, often in an environment of unclear instructions and divided leadership. Their decisions, such as those of the guards at Bornholmer Straße or the army command in Bucharest, were decisive. Their motives included fear, uncertainty, loyalty, personal conviction, and in some cases opportunism, and the results differed from country to country.
Division III outcome. The revolutions of 1989 were a connected continental transformation with different national paths. They were enabled by Soviet restraint, made possible by organized and spontaneous mobilization, and shaped by the particular institutions, histories, and leaders of each state.
Division IV German Reunification and the End of Europe's Postwar Division Sections 132.13 to 132.16 · Principal period: 1989 to 1991
Section 132.13

From the Fall of the Wall to East Germany's Democratic Election

The weeks after November 9, 1989, were marked by uncertainty about what the opening of the border meant. Many of the East German activists who had organized the autumn demonstrations wanted a democratized and independent GDR, and on November 28 a group of intellectuals and opposition figures, including the writer Christa Wolf, published an appeal "For Our Country" that urged East Germans to stay and build a reformed socialist state distinct from the Federal Republic. Such views were sincere and widely held among those who had led the opposition, and they reflected a wish to avoid being absorbed by a much larger and wealthier neighbor. But the departure of citizens to the West continued, the economy was deteriorating, and the pace of events increasingly favored those who wanted rapid unity.

West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl seized the initiative. On November 28, 1989, he presented to the Bundestag, without prior consultation with his allies or with his coalition partner, a ten-point program that outlined a path from immediate aid and cooperation, through a contractual community and confederative structures, to eventual federation and national unity. The proposal did not set a timetable, and it assumed that unification would be a gradual process lasting years. It nevertheless transformed the debate by making unity an explicit goal of the Federal Republic's policy. Governments in Paris, London, Washington, and Moscow reacted with varying degrees of concern. Gorbachev criticized the plan as an attempt to dictate terms, and French and British leaders expressed reservations about a larger Germany, while the United States gave Kohl more consistent encouragement.

Inside the GDR, the structure of government was changing. Hans Modrow, a reformist party official from Dresden, had become prime minister on November 13. The constitutional guarantee of the Socialist Unity Party's leading role was deleted on December 1, Krenz resigned from his posts in early December, and the party, soon renamed the SED-PDS and later the Party of Democratic Socialism, split and lost hundreds of thousands of members. A Round Table of government, party, and opposition representatives began meeting on December 7 and became the principal forum for planning the transition, including the dissolution of the Stasi, which was renamed and then abolished, and the scheduling of free elections. Crowds in January 1990 stormed the Stasi headquarters in East Berlin to prevent the destruction of files, an episode that shaped later debates about the records. Meanwhile, the slogans at the Monday demonstrations changed. The original cry that "we are the people" gave way increasingly to "we are one people," which many observers read as a sign that popular opinion was moving toward unification, though historians caution that slogans reflected the views of those who attended and were not a survey of all East Germans. Kohl's reception by large crowds in Dresden on December 19 strengthened that impression.

Elections to the Volkskammer, the East German parliament, were advanced to March 18, 1990, in response to the continued exodus and instability. They were the first free parliamentary elections in the GDR's history and were contested by a wide range of parties, many with strong support from West German sister parties. The principal groupings included the Alliance for Germany, a coalition of the East German Christian Democrats, the German Social Union, and Democratic Awakening, backed by Kohl; the Social Democrats, who had been re-founded in October 1989; the Party of Democratic Socialism, successor to the SED; the liberal groups; and a bloc of civic organizations, including New Forum, which formed Alliance 90. The campaign took place against the backdrop of the rapid collapse of the economic order and uncertainty over the speed and terms of unification. Turnout was very high, over ninety percent. The Alliance for Germany won about 48 percent of the vote, the Social Democrats about 22 percent, the PDS about 16 percent, and the civic groups that had led the revolution received only a small share. The result was widely interpreted as a vote for rapid unification and for the Deutsche Mark, though the reasons for individual votes were varied and the outcome also reflected the organizational and financial support of West German parties.

Lothar de Maizière, leader of the East German Christian Democrats, formed a coalition government in April 1990 that included the Social Democrats and liberals, and it set itself the task of negotiating the end of the GDR. Its program included both reform of the East German legal and economic system and discussions with the Federal Republic about the terms of integration. The democratic mandate of March 18 provided something that the opening of the Wall had not: the authority of East Germans themselves to decide, through elected representatives, to negotiate their incorporation into the Federal Republic. It also meant that reunification would proceed with legitimacy derived from free elections in both states.

Section 132.14

Monetary Union, the Unification Treaty, and October 3, 1990

Combining two states created practical problems on a scale for which there was no precedent. The two Germanys had different currencies, with the East German mark not freely convertible and valued at very different rates in different transactions. They had different legal systems, social insurance arrangements, property regimes, banking structures, and administrative hierarchies. East Germany's economy was integrated into the Comecon trading system, with enterprises tied to markets in the Soviet Union and eastern Europe, and its industry, though more advanced than those of its neighbors, was far less productive than that of West Germany. The continuing westward migration, which amounted to several hundred thousand people in the first months of 1990, added urgency. West German political leaders, particularly Kohl, concluded that only the prospect of an immediate share in West German prosperity could stem the flow, and the slogan of the moment held that if the Deutsche Mark did not come to the East, East Germans would go to the Deutsche Mark.

The Treaty Establishing a Monetary, Economic, and Social Union was signed on May 18, 1990, and took effect on July 1. It introduced the Deutsche Mark in the GDR and transferred responsibility for monetary policy to the Bundesbank. Wages, pensions, and rents were converted at a rate of one East German mark to one Deutsche Mark, and savings were converted at the same rate up to limits that depended on the age of the holder, with the remainder converted at a less favorable rate of two to one, as were most debts. The treaty also extended West German economic and social law to the East, including the market system and the social insurance schemes. The conversion rates were politically popular and were, in economic terms, generous to East Germans, because the real value of the East mark was much lower, but they raised the cost of East German labor and goods dramatically.

The conversion rules varied by legal category and age: regular wages, salaries, pensions, and rents were generally converted at 1:1; residents could exchange eligible cash and bank savings at 1:1 up to 2,000 East German marks for children younger than 15, 4,000 for people aged 15–59, and 6,000 for those aged 60 and over. Amounts exceeding these ceilings and most other monetary claims and liabilities were generally converted at 2:1, with further special cases. These were negotiated legal rates, not market valuations of relative purchasing power.

The political and legal route to unity was the subject of a parallel debate. The West German Basic Law offered two possibilities. Article 23 allowed "other parts of Germany" to join the Federal Republic by declaration of accession, as the Saarland had done in 1957, while Article 146 contemplated a new constitution, adopted by the German people, that would replace the Basic Law. Advocates of the second route, including many East German activists and some West German intellectuals, wanted a broader constitutional debate. The East German government and most West German politicians favored Article 23 as faster and more predictable, and it was adopted. This choice meant that the Federal Republic's constitutional order, its institutions, and its international commitments, including membership in NATO and the European Communities, would be extended to the East, and the Basic Law would remain in force with some amendments, a point that later critics would highlight when discussing whether East German experience had been incorporated into the new Germany.

The Unification Treaty was signed on August 31, 1990, by Wolfgang Schäuble for the Federal Republic and Günther Krause for the GDR. It specified how West German law would apply in the East, provided for the reestablishment of five East German states, Brandenburg, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt, and Thuringia, and named Berlin as the capital while leaving the location of the government to later decision. It also addressed property claims, the status of the Stasi files, and transitional arrangements in sensitive areas. The Volkskammer voted on August 23 to accede to the Federal Republic on October 3, and both parliaments ratified the treaty in September. On October 3, 1990, the GDR ceased to exist, and Germany was reunified. The first all-German elections followed on December 2, 1990, and Kohl's coalition won. In 1991 the Bundestag voted to move the government to Berlin.

The legal act of reunification was therefore completed in less than a year after the opening of the Wall. The political act should be distinguished from the longer and more difficult process of economic and social integration, which continued for decades, and whose early phase is examined in the next section. The speed of the political process reflected a combination of opportunity, fear that the window of opportunity might close if Soviet politics changed, and the preferences of a population that had voted decisively in March. It also left many questions unsettled, and the treaty's provisions would be contested for years.

Section 132.15

The Two Plus Four Negotiations and Germany's International Settlement

German reunification could not be completed by the Germans alone. Since 1945 the four victorious powers, the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, and France, had retained special rights and responsibilities with respect to Germany as a whole and to Berlin, rights that had never been extinguished by a peace treaty. No such treaty existed. Questions of Germany's borders, military status, alliance membership, and sovereignty therefore required an international settlement, and every party had its own interests. The Federal Republic wanted unity, full sovereignty, and continued membership in NATO and the European Communities. The GDR government under de Maizière sought to protect East German interests and avoid neutrality. The Soviet Union feared a powerful Germany allied with the West, and it wanted security guarantees and economic assistance. The United States, under President George H. W. Bush, supported unification within NATO. Britain and France, whose leaders, Margaret Thatcher and François Mitterrand, were concerned at first about the speed and consequences of unity, ultimately accepted it, with Mitterrand emphasizing deeper European integration as a counterweight. Poland, which feared a revival of German claims to territory, pressed for a binding confirmation of the border.

The framework known as Two Plus Four, two German states and four powers, was agreed at a conference of NATO and Warsaw Pact foreign ministers in Ottawa in February 1990. The first formal meeting took place in Bonn on May 5, and further rounds were held in East Berlin, Paris, and Moscow. Poland participated in the Paris session of July 17, when the border question was discussed. The most difficult issue was the military status of the united Germany. Moscow initially favored a neutral Germany or one belonging to both alliances. Western governments rejected neutrality, and in the course of the spring and early summer the Soviet position moved, influenced by the changing situation in the GDR, by the economic crisis in the Soviet Union, and by the political reorientation of eastern Europe. At a meeting between Kohl and Gorbachev in Moscow and in the Caucasus region on July 15 and 16, 1990, Gorbachev accepted that the united Germany could decide freely on alliance membership and thus belong to NATO. The agreement included the withdrawal of Soviet troops from the former GDR by the end of 1994, a limitation of the German armed forces to 370,000, a German renunciation of nuclear, biological, and chemical weapons, and West German financial assistance to the Soviet Union, including support for the costs of the troop withdrawal.

The result was the Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany, signed in Moscow on September 12, 1990, by the foreign ministers of the six states. It confirmed that the united Germany would consist of the territory of the Federal Republic, the GDR, and Berlin, and that its frontiers were final. It prohibited any claims to other territory. It stipulated that no foreign armed forces or nuclear weapons would be stationed in the territory of the former GDR while Soviet forces remained, with certain exceptions for German NATO units not integrated into the alliance's structures, and it ended Four Power rights upon entry into force. The treaty did not enter into force on signature. It required ratification by all signatories, and it took effect on March 15, 1991, after the final instruments of ratification were deposited. The Four Powers suspended their rights as of the day of reunification on October 3, 1990, pending the treaty's entry into force, which is why Germany regained full sovereignty only in 1991 in the formal sense.

The confirmation of the German–Polish frontier was a matter of particular importance. The Oder–Neisse line had been accepted by East Germany in 1950 and, in effect, by the Federal Republic in its treaty with Poland in 1970, but the formal status of the boundary under a peace settlement had remained open and many in Poland feared that a united Germany might reopen it. Following a resolution by the two German parliaments in June 1990 and Poland's participation in the Two Plus Four talks, a German–Polish border treaty was signed on November 14, 1990, and the issue was settled. The final recognition of the border removed a major source of insecurity in postwar central Europe, and it was an important element in the broader reconciliation between Germany and its eastern neighbors. In November 1990 the Charter of Paris, signed by the states of the Conference on Security and Cooperation in Europe, declared the division of Europe over and set out principles of democracy and cooperation, and the Treaty on Conventional Armed Forces in Europe, signed the same month, limited conventional weaponry from the Atlantic to the Urals.

The agreement resolved questions left unsettled since 1945, and it did so by diplomacy rather than force. It cannot be described simply as a bargain between Kohl and Gorbachev, though their personal relationship was important. The outcome reflected the positions of the United States, which insisted on NATO membership, of Britain and France, of the East German government, of Poland, and of Germany's own institutions, and it depended on Soviet weakness and the unwillingness of Moscow to jeopardize its economic hopes and its relations with the West.

Section 132.16

The Human and Political Consequences of German Reunification

For East Germans, reunification brought freedoms they had demanded and also disruption on a scale few anticipated. West German laws, courts, and administrative institutions were introduced rapidly, and many East German officials were replaced or retrained, particularly in the judiciary, universities, and administration, and by West Germans who moved east to fill positions. The currency union exposed East German enterprises to Western competition and to the collapse of their traditional markets in eastern Europe, and many firms proved unable to survive. Unemployment, which had been officially nonexistent under the GDR, rose rapidly, and many workers in industry, agriculture, and the public sector lost their jobs. A wave of migration to the West, especially of younger and more skilled people, affected many eastern towns.

The institution at the center of this process was the Treuhandanstalt, a trust agency created in March 1990 under the Modrow government and charged after reunification with reorganizing and privatizing the thousands of formerly state-owned enterprises. Its task was to sell, restructure, or close firms that employed millions of workers, in conditions of economic collapse and without domestic capital. It sold most of its holdings, often to West German or foreign buyers, and closed many others. Supporters argued that it prevented a longer decline, while critics held that it undervalued assets, closed viable enterprises, and destroyed jobs at a pace that left lasting resentment. The debate continues, and its full account belongs to the discussion of postcommunist transformation in Chapter 136.

Reunification also required coming to terms with the GDR's past. Property questions were among the most difficult, since many properties had been expropriated in the Soviet occupation zone or under the GDR, and the principle adopted in the Unification Treaty, restitution where possible instead of compensation, created enormous disputes and uncertainty. The files of the Stasi, which had employed approximately 91,000 full-time employees in 1989 and a large network of informal collaborators, raised painful questions. The Stasi Records Act of December 1991 gave individuals the right to see their own files and established an authority to administer the archives. The files revealed that friends, colleagues, and family members had informed on one another, and they prompted debates about guilt, responsibility, forgiveness, and the role of the state. Different people drew different conclusions: some regarded access as essential to justice and truth, while others feared it would poison personal relations or be used for political purposes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reunification and Family Life

Many consequences of reunification were experienced within households. In the GDR, a large majority of women of working age were employed, and the state provided extensive childcare, a maternity leave system, and assurance of employment. After 1990, many of these arrangements were dismantled or restructured, and women faced higher unemployment than men in many regions. Birth rates in the East fell dramatically in the early 1990s, a decline attributed by many demographers to economic uncertainty, though other factors may have contributed. Families were divided by westward migration, with parents and grandparents remaining in the East and younger relatives moving for work. At the same time, other families were reunited after decades of separation, and the freedom to travel, to visit relatives, and to buy goods that had been scarce was immediately and widely valued.

East Germans also confronted a changed sense of social status. Qualifications, titles, and professional experiences acquired under the GDR were reassessed, and many people found themselves treated as outsiders in the new Germany, a feeling sometimes described by the term "second-class citizens." Everyday objects of the old state, from the Trabant automobile to brands of food, took on new meanings, and in the following years a nostalgia sometimes called Ostalgie developed, which historians interpret less as a desire for the dictatorship than as a defense of personal experience against dismissal. Sources for these experiences include surveys, oral histories, memoirs, and the records of local administrations, and they vary according to age, region, occupation, and gender.

These developments illustrate a tension between political unity and the continued existence of economic, social, and cultural differences. The new states of the East had formally the same institutions and rights as those of the West, but they started from unequal economic positions and different experiences. Historians have debated how best to describe reunification. It can be understood as the restoration of national sovereignty and unity after the artificial division of 1945 to 1949, a view that emphasizes the democratic decisions of East Germans in March and the legitimacy of a single German nation. It can also be seen as the accession of one state to another, in which the institutions of the larger and richer partner were extended to the smaller one with limited adaptation, a view that emphasizes asymmetry of power and the costs borne by East Germans. The most defensible conclusion combines both: reunification involved a genuine democratic choice and a real restoration of national unity, within a framework that was structurally asymmetrical.

Division IV outcome. German reunification was a legal, democratic, diplomatic, economic, and social process that unfolded over many months and beyond. The destruction of the Wall was its symbolic beginning, but the settlement of 1990 was a series of negotiated decisions that reshaped the European order.
Division V The Dissolution of the Soviet Union and the End of the Cold War Sections 132.17 to 132.20 · Principal period: 1988 to 1991
Section 132.17

National Movements, Republican Sovereignty, and the Crisis of the Soviet Union

The end of communist rule in eastern Europe did not automatically end the Soviet Union, and the processes that threatened its survival followed a different logic. The union was a federation of fifteen republics, formally sovereign and with a constitutional right of secession that had never been exercised, and in practice a centralized state governed through the Communist Party. Soviet nationality policy had created institutions, languages, and elites in each republic, and these provided frameworks for national identity even as Moscow suppressed political expression of it. Glasnost and political reform removed the restraints. Once censorship eased and elections became partly competitive, national grievances about language, history, the environment, and economic control could be voiced openly, and politicians discovered that republican platforms commanded popular support.

Historical Vocabulary
National Sovereignty in the Soviet Context

Sovereignty in the Soviet republics in 1988 to 1991 did not necessarily mean full independence. A "declaration of sovereignty" typically asserted that republican laws took precedence over union laws on the republic's territory and that the republic controlled its natural resources and economy, while leaving open whether the republic would remain in a renewed union. Declarations of independence went further and claimed separate statehood, as the Baltic republics did on the argument that their incorporation in 1940 had been illegal and that they were restoring independence, not seceding. The distinction matters because many republican leaders, including Yeltsin in 1990, sought greater autonomy within a reformed union before independence became the goal.

Students should avoid assuming that all national movements sought immediate separation or that nationalism was the only force at work. Economic grievances, rivalry between republican and union elites, and the political ambitions of leaders also mattered, and national identity was itself shaped through the process.

The Baltic republics led the way. Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania had been independent states between the world wars. The secret protocols accompanying the German–Soviet agreements of 1939 assigned the Baltic states to the Soviet sphere of influence. Soviet forces then occupied Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania in 1940, and the Soviet Union forcibly annexed them. Soviet authorities long denied the existence of the secret protocols, and many inhabitants regarded Soviet rule as an occupation. Popular fronts, formed in 1988 with the nominal aim of supporting perestroika, quickly turned to demands for sovereignty. Estonia declared sovereignty in November 1988. The movement is often called the Singing Revolution, after the mass gatherings at which participants sang banned national songs. On August 23, 1989, the fiftieth anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, approximately two million people joined hands in a human chain, the Baltic Way, stretching across the three republics for several hundred kilometers, to demand the condemnation of the pact. In December 1989 the Soviet Congress of People's Deputies did condemn the secret protocols. Lithuania's Supreme Council, led by Vytautas Landsbergis, declared the restoration of independence on March 11, 1990, and Moscow responded with an economic blockade and political pressure. In January 1991, Soviet troops attacked the television tower in Vilnius, killing fourteen civilians, and clashes occurred in Riga. The violence provoked international protest and further alienated public opinion, and it was followed by referendums in February and March 1991 in which large majorities in the Baltic republics supported independence.

Other republics differed. In Georgia, the killing of about twenty demonstrators by Soviet troops in Tbilisi on April 9, 1989, radicalized national feeling. In Armenia and Azerbaijan, the dispute over Nagorno-Karabakh, an Armenian-majority region within Soviet Azerbaijan, produced mass demonstrations beginning in 1988, pogroms against Armenians in Sumgait in February 1988, large-scale population displacement, and a Soviet military intervention in Baku in January 1990, creating what became the first serious interethnic conflict of the perestroika years. In Ukraine, the popular movement Rukh, founded in 1989, drew on cultural and ecological concerns, including the Chernobyl catastrophe, and on demands for linguistic and political rights, and the republic's parliament declared state sovereignty on July 16, 1990. In Moldova, language laws and the question of relations with Romania led to conflict, and in Central Asia, where nationalist movements were weaker, local leaders were more often cautious about separation, though economic and ethnic tensions erupted in several places. These movements had different histories and objectives, and they cannot be treated as interchangeable.

A decisive development was the emergence of the Russian republic as an independent political actor. Yeltsin, who had been elected to the Congress of People's Deputies in 1989, became chairman of the Russian Supreme Soviet in May 1990, and on June 12, 1990, the Russian parliament declared the republic's sovereignty. In June 1991, Yeltsin was elected president of the Russian republic by popular vote, the first such election in Russian history. This created a rival center of legitimacy in Moscow itself, and it meant that Gorbachev, whose own position derived from the party and from a vote of the Congress, faced a leader who could claim a direct mandate. Yeltsin used the republic's resources and the language of sovereignty to challenge the union government, and this contest between republican and union authorities was at least as important as the national movements in the periphery. Russia's position also meant that a union without Russia was inconceivable, and a Russia that rejected the union could end it.

Gorbachev tried to preserve the union by redefining it. A referendum on March 17, 1991, asked citizens whether they supported the preservation of the union as a renewed federation of equal sovereign republics. Six republics, the three Baltic states, Armenia, Georgia, and Moldova, boycotted it, and in the nine participating republics, along with localities that voted despite republican-level boycotts, official union-wide returns reported approximately 80.0 percent turnout among eligible voters in the administered poll and 76.4 percent voting yes. Those percentages describe the participating electorate, not an uncontested mandate from every resident of all fifteen republics. The result seemed to support a reformed union, but its wording was ambiguous, and it did not resolve disputes about the powers of the center. Negotiations with nine republican leaders at Novo-Ogaryovo near Moscow in the spring of 1991 produced a draft of a new Union Treaty. Meanwhile, the economy declined further, with shortages, inflation, and falling output, and the breakdown of trade among republics weakened the union's economic coherence.

Eastern European states' escape from Soviet control differed from the Soviet republics' challenge to the union itself. The former was largely accomplished in 1989, while the fate of the Soviet state remained contested until 1991.

Section 132.18

The August 1991 Coup Attempt and the Collapse of Central Soviet Authority

By the summer of 1991, conservative officials in the party, army, and security services regarded the direction of reform as a threat to the state and to their own positions. They were alarmed by the draft Union Treaty, which would have transferred substantial powers from the center to the republics and, in their view, dismantled the country. The treaty, officially the Treaty on the Union of Sovereign States, was to be signed by representatives of several republics on August 20, 1991, in Moscow. Gorbachev planned to leave for a vacation in Crimea before the signing, and a group of senior officials, who had assembled in secret, decided to act while he was away.

On August 18 a delegation visited Gorbachev at his holiday residence at Foros in Crimea and demanded that he transfer authority or declare a state of emergency. He refused, and he was placed under guard, with telephone lines cut. On the morning of August 19 the plotters announced that Gorbachev was unable to perform his duties for health reasons and that power had passed to a State Committee on the State of Emergency. Its members included Vice President Gennady Yanayev, who was named acting president, Prime Minister Valentin Pavlov, KGB chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov, Defense Minister Dmitry Yazov, and Interior Minister Boris Pugo. The committee declared a state of emergency, banned demonstrations, imposed censorship, and ordered troops and tanks into Moscow.

Analysis: The two texts show the competing claims to legitimacy: the committee appealed to order, unity, and the constitution, while Yeltsin appealed to legality and the will of the people. They are both political documents written for effect. The committee's declaration conceals the manner of its seizure of power, including Gorbachev's confinement, and Yeltsin's appeal exaggerates the immediate power of its signatories. Neither text records the hesitations within the security forces, which are known from later testimony and investigation.

Questions for students:

  1. What reasons did the committee give for its action, and what reasons did Yeltsin give against it?
  2. Who created each document, and for what audience?
  3. What can historians learn about the legitimacy claims of both sides?
  4. What can they not establish about how soldiers and security officers decided to act?

The coup failed within three days. Yeltsin and his supporters gathered at the Russian White House, where he made a defiant public appearance, standing on a tank outside the building, an image widely broadcast. Large crowds in Moscow and Leningrad, tens of thousands strong, gathered to resist, and barricades were erected around the parliament. The plotters were indecisive. They did not arrest Yeltsin, did not fully control the media, failed to win the support of a major part of the armed forces, and showed visible disarray at a press conference in which Yanayev's hands were seen to tremble. Elite units commanded by the KGB were reluctant to storm the White House, and several military commanders refused to take part. On the night of August 20 to 21 three civilians were killed in clashes with armored vehicles in a Moscow underpass, an event that strengthened opposition rather than intimidating it. By August 21 the committee's members began to flee or surrender, and on August 22 Gorbachev returned to Moscow.

Cause and Effect

Why the August 1991 Coup Failed

Long-term causes

Years of glasnost had created independent media, a politically aware public, and new republican institutions with their own legitimacy. The security services and the army had lost some of their former prestige and cohesion.

Short-term causes

The plotters acted hastily and without a clear plan. The proposed Union Treaty, which was scheduled for signing on August 20, gave them a deadline and a motive, and Yeltsin's election in June had given the Russian republic a leader with a popular mandate.

Event and immediate effects

The coup collapsed by August 21. Gorbachev returned, but Yeltsin dominated the political scene. The Communist Party was suspended in Russia, its property seized, and Gorbachev resigned as general secretary on August 24.

Long-term consequences

Central Soviet authority ended in practice. Republics declared independence in rapid succession, and the possibility of a renewed union disappeared.

Historical complexity: The coup failed because of a combination of the plotters' irresolution, public resistance, divisions within the security forces, and the political leadership of Yeltsin. No single factor explains the outcome, and historians continue to debate how far the plotters could have succeeded with greater resolve and whether Gorbachev's earlier decisions encouraged them.

The coup's failure did not save Gorbachev's project. Although he was restored to office, his authority had been badly damaged. He had been seen as either a victim of the conspiracy or, to some, as someone whose own appointments of the conspirators had created the danger, and in the days after his return Yeltsin treated him with public contempt, forcing him to read out in the Russian parliament a transcript that implicated his own government. The Communist Party, whose leaders had largely supported or acquiesced in the coup, lost its power. Crowds toppled the statue of Felix Dzerzhinsky, founder of the secret police, outside KGB headquarters. Gorbachev resigned as party general secretary and called for the dissolution of the Central Committee, and activities of the party were suspended. Republics moved to independence: Estonia and Latvia had already declared on August 20 and 21, Ukraine declared independence on August 24, Belarus on August 25, and Moldova on August 27, and the Soviet government recognized the independence of the three Baltic states on September 6, 1991. The immediate collapse of the coup must therefore be distinguished from the later formal dissolution of the union, which occurred in December and is examined next.

Section 132.19

December 1991: From the Belavezha Agreements to the Dissolution of the Soviet Union

After August 1991 the Soviet central government continued to exist but lacked the authority to govern. Union ministries lost funding and staff to republican governments, Russia took over many union functions, and negotiations about a new arrangement dragged on. Gorbachev continued to advocate a looser union, a confederation of sovereign states with a common president, but several republican leaders, including Yeltsin, had begun to doubt that such a body could be created or that Gorbachev should lead it. The decisive factor was Ukraine. On December 1, 1991, Ukrainians voted in a referendum on the declaration of independence adopted by their parliament in August, and about ninety percent of those voting supported it, on a turnout of around 84 percent, with majorities in every region, including those with large Russian-speaking populations. Leonid Kravchuk, a former communist official, was elected president. Without Ukraine, the second most populous and economically important republic, a union of any substantial kind was impractical, and Yeltsin said so publicly.

On December 7 and 8, 1991, Yeltsin, Kravchuk, and Stanislav Shushkevich, chairman of the Supreme Soviet of Belarus, met at a hunting lodge in the Belavezha forest in Belarus. They had come to discuss the future of relations among their republics and, without informing Gorbachev beforehand, drafted an agreement that declared that the Soviet Union, as a subject of international law and a geopolitical reality, ceased to exist, and that established a Commonwealth of Independent States. The agreement, signed on December 8, committed the three states to cooperation in foreign policy, economic matters, and security, and invited other republics to join. Its legal basis was contentious, since the union treaty of 1922 that the three republics claimed to be denouncing was the legal foundation for a union of which they were founding members, and the three leaders did not have authority from the union as a whole. The Russian republic's Supreme Soviet ratified the agreement on December 12.

It shows the signatories' attempt to provide continuity on nuclear weapons, treaty obligations, and borders. It was a decision by three leaders and not a popular vote, and it does not show how other republican leaders, the Soviet parliament, or ordinary citizens regarded it. Translations of its legal wording differ, and students should compare versions where possible.

Gorbachev denounced the agreement as illegal and as a violation of the constitution, but he lacked means to resist. The Central Asian republics, offended at not having been consulted, demanded to be included as founding members, and on December 21, 1991, eleven republics, the three signatories of Belavezha and eight others, signed the Alma-Ata declaration and protocol, which enlarged the Commonwealth, confirmed that the Soviet Union would cease to exist, and set out arrangements for coordination. The Baltic states, which had already been recognized, and Georgia did not take part. The leaders agreed that Russia would take the Soviet Union's seat at the United Nations and that the strategic nuclear forces would be controlled jointly pending their eventual removal to Russia, an arrangement later formalized in agreements involving Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan.

On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev announced his resignation as president of the Soviet Union in a televised address, expressing regret at the disintegration of the state while defending the reforms of his tenure. He transferred control of the nuclear launch codes to Yeltsin. The Soviet flag was lowered from the Kremlin that evening. On December 26 the Council of Republics of the Supreme Soviet adopted a declaration acknowledging the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the creation of the Commonwealth. The United States and many other states had recognized the independence of the former republics, and Russia was accepted as the successor state to the Soviet Union in matters of treaty obligations and United Nations membership.

The three dates, December 8, 25, and 26, represented different political and constitutional acts. The agreement of December 8 was a decision by three republican leaders, the Alma-Ata documents of December 21 widened it, Gorbachev's resignation on December 25 ended the presidency, and the declaration of December 26 was the formal acknowledgment by the remaining union legislative body. Treating these as a single moment obscures the role of different actors and the contested legality of the process. The dissolution also raised urgent questions that the following years addressed: control of the Soviet nuclear arsenal, the division of the armed forces and their assets, responsibility for foreign debt and treaty obligations, ownership of state property, the status of Russians living outside Russia, and the recognition of new states. This chapter concludes the narrative of the union's collapse here and reserves the histories of the successor states, including Russia and Ukraine, for the later chapters, notably Chapter 139.

Section 132.20

Why Did the Cold War End? Historical Explanations and European Consequences

Historians have offered several explanations for the end of the Cold War, and each captures something real. One emphasizes Soviet economic weakness: the command economy's low productivity, the burden of military and imperial commitments, and the inability to keep pace with technological change forced the leadership to seek reform and retrenchment. A related view stresses the arms race and Western pressure, including the American military buildup of the 1980s and economic and ideological competition, which allegedly compelled the Soviet Union to concede. A third emphasizes Gorbachev himself, whose reforms, ideas, and decisions changed the character of Soviet policy at home and abroad. A fourth emphasizes the role of societies: the organized opposition of Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia, the mass movements of East Germany, and the national movements in the Soviet republics, as expressions of popular will that governments could no longer contain. Others stress nationalism, elite decisions by communist officials who chose to bargain or to step aside, and the role of international institutions and norms, including the Helsinki process.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Cold War End?

The question: Which factors best explain why a bipolar European order that had lasted for four decades ended within a few years, without a major war?

Interpretation A: Structural weakness and Western pressure. Scholars in this tradition stress the long-term decline of the Soviet economy and the strains of the arms race, together with Western strategies of firmness and economic strength. In some popular versions, American policy in the 1980s is credited with having brought the Soviet Union to the point of collapse.
Interpretation B: Gorbachev and the politics of reform. Historians such as Archie Brown, in The Gorbachev Factor, emphasize that the Soviet system had the capacity to continue for years in stagnation and repression, and that the decisive change came from a leader's choices and ideas. Without Gorbachev, they argue, a different successor might have maintained the system.
Interpretation C: Societies, oppositions, and contingency. Other scholars emphasize mass mobilization, improvised decisions, and accident, and the way that events in one country altered the options of others. Mary Elise Sarotte's study of 1989 stresses improvisation and interaction among actors, while Stephen Kotkin's Uncivil Society stresses the hollowness of the communist regimes and the weakness of their elites. Vladislav Zubok, in A Failed Empire, combines the Soviet leadership's ideas with institutional and imperial exhaustion.

Evidence: Each interpretation draws on different sources: economic statistics and intelligence assessments for structural explanations, leadership records and memoirs for the Gorbachev thesis, and opposition documents, oral histories, and diplomatic records for the societal and contingent explanations. Western policy records are essential for the first interpretation, though they show Western intentions better than Soviet perceptions.

Why it matters: The answers shape how later generations understand the role of ideas, power, and popular action, and influence political arguments about whether external pressure can transform other states. They also affect assumptions about the inevitability of the outcome. Most current scholarship treats the explanations as complementary, with disagreement about relative weight, and it does not accept a purely triumphalist account that credits one government.

Student task: Choose one event from this chapter and explain how each interpretation would account for it. Then identify a kind of evidence that would help determine which account is most persuasive.

No single explanation is adequate. The Soviet economy was weak, but weak economies had persisted before, and the Soviet state retained powerful coercive instruments in 1985. Gorbachev's choices were decisive, but they were made in response to pressures and constraints that he did not control, and their consequences exceeded his intentions. Western policy contributed through military deterrence, diplomatic engagement, and the appeal of Western prosperity and rights, but the pace and form of change in eastern Europe depended on local actors, and in the Soviet Union on republican and national politics that Western governments did not direct. In sum, the Cold War's European order ended through the interaction of domestic resistance, institutional weakness, political decisions in Moscow, mass mobilization, and negotiation.

The question of when the Cold War ended also has no single answer. The revolutions of 1989 ended the division of eastern Europe and the loss of Soviet control. German reunification in 1990 resolved the central problem of the European settlement and was confirmed by the Charter of Paris in November. The dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 removed the other superpower. This chapter treats the three as distinct stages of a connected transformation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Europe Before and After the Cold War, 1980 and 1992

What changed?

  • Communist governments disappeared in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, and Romania, and the Soviet Union ceased to exist in December 1991
  • The Warsaw Pact's military structures were dissolved in March 1991 and the alliance formally ended on July 1, 1991, and Comecon was dissolved in June 1991
  • Germany was reunified and its borders confirmed, and Soviet troops began withdrawing from central Europe, with the last leaving Germany in 1994
  • Fifteen new states emerged from the Soviet Union, and the Baltic states regained independence

What remained continuous?

  • NATO and the European Communities continued to exist, and both would soon be tested by new questions of enlargement and purpose
  • Economic inequalities between western and eastern Europe persisted, and the legacies of planned economies and one-party rule shaped later politics
  • Nuclear weapons remained, and Russia retained the Soviet arsenal and the Soviet seat at the United Nations
  • Questions about national borders, minorities, and historical memory continued, as the Yugoslav case (Chapter 135) would demonstrate

Who experienced change or continuity most directly?

Citizens of eastern Europe and the former Soviet republics experienced the most direct change, in political rights, economic conditions, and national status. Western Europeans experienced more continuity in institutions, though the end of the division altered security assumptions and the context of integration.

Why the pattern matters: The end of the Cold War was a transformation of the political order, not a sudden resolution of all problems. Distinguishing what changed from what persisted helps explain the opportunities and difficulties of the 1990s.

The European consequences were large. Eastern Europe regained political independence of Soviet direction and began a double transition toward competitive politics and market economies, whose uneven course is examined in Chapter 136. German unity changed the balance within Europe and reinforced the importance of European integration, which Chapter 133 will trace from its origins and Chapter 134 through the Maastricht Treaty. The wars of Yugoslav dissolution, examined in Chapter 135, showed that the end of the Cold War did not guarantee peaceful transformation, and later relations between Russia and its neighbors, examined in Chapter 139, would show that the settlement of 1989 to 1991 remained contested. The end of the division brought a remarkable expansion of political possibilities, including free elections, open borders, and the return of independent states. It brought uncertainty too, over economic institutions, national boundaries, and the distribution of power.

The closing lesson of the chapter is historical contingency. In 1985, the specific events of 1989 to 1991 were not predetermined. A different Soviet leader, a violent decision in Leipzig, a different reaction in Moscow to the Hungarian border, a successful coup in August 1991, or a different outcome in the Ukrainian referendum might have produced other results. They resulted from choices made by people in many countries, and they should be studied as outcomes of history, not as inevitabilities. Part XX changes the analytical focus from the ending of one order to the construction of another, beginning with the origins of European integration.

Division V outcome. Reforms, national independence movements, institutional crisis, and political decisions converged in the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the ending of the European Cold War order, leaving a continent whose future was open.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the European order created after 1945 came to an end between 1980 and 1991. Its central argument is that the Cold War's European division ended through the interaction of domestic opposition, economic and institutional weakness, decisions made in Moscow, mass mobilization, and international negotiation, and that its collapse was neither inevitable nor attributable to a single leader, country, or cause. The chapter also insisted on a distinction among three outcomes that occurred at different times: the collapse of communist governments in eastern Europe in 1989, the reunification of Germany in 1990, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.

The first division traced the Polish case. Economic crisis and a crisis of legitimacy under Edward Gierek, the repression of the 1976 protests, and the creation of the Workers' Defence Committee produced cooperation between workers and intellectuals. The Catholic Church and the election of Pope John Paul II provided additional institutional and moral resources, though they were not the sole cause of what followed. The Gdańsk strikes of August 1980 led to the Twenty-One Demands and the Gdańsk Agreement, and the creation of Solidarity, a trade union that became a national social movement of millions. Its growth created a confrontation that the party and the Soviet leadership could not accept, and General Jaruzelski imposed martial law on December 13, 1981. Martial law dismantled Solidarity's legal organization and killed and imprisoned many, but it did not destroy the underground networks, church support, and popular loyalty that sustained the opposition.

When economic stagnation and renewed strikes persuaded the Polish government to negotiate in 1989, the Round Table produced a compromise that legalized Solidarity, restored a Senate, and reserved most Sejm seats for the governing coalition. The elections of June 4, 1989, overturned the premise of that compromise, since Solidarity won nearly every contested seat, and the formation of Tadeusz Mazowiecki's government in the autumn marked the first noncommunist-led government in the Soviet bloc. This was a negotiated transition that preserved some continuities, including Jaruzelski's presidency and communist control of key ministries, and it provided a model for others.

The second division examined the Soviet side. The Soviet Union under Leonid Brezhnev's successors faced declining growth, shortages, military and imperial burdens, and an aging leadership. Mikhail Gorbachev's accession in 1985 brought a leader who sought to reform and preserve the system through perestroika and glasnost. The economic reforms disrupted the planned economy without establishing a functioning alternative, while glasnost and political reform, including the partially competitive elections of 1989, weakened the instruments of control and encouraged criticism, historical reassessment, and national movements. Gorbachev's intentions and the eventual outcomes must be distinguished: he did not seek the dissolution of the Soviet Union, but his reforms created the conditions in which it occurred.

At the same time, Soviet foreign policy changed. The doctrine of new political thinking, summit meetings with Ronald Reagan, the INF Treaty of December 1987, the withdrawal from Afghanistan completed in February 1989, and Gorbachev's address at the United Nations in December 1988 transformed the strategic environment of Europe. The informal expectation, associated with the Brezhnev Doctrine, that Moscow would intervene to preserve communist governments in the bloc was withdrawn. Historians debate why. Some emphasize Gorbachev's convictions, others the constraints of economic need and the costs of force, and others the speed with which events outran Moscow's expectations. Soviet restraint was necessary but not sufficient, for it created opportunities that local movements and leaders used.

The third division compared the revolutions of 1989. In Hungary, party reform, negotiation, and the public reassessment of 1956 produced a republic and competitive elections, while the decision to open the border to East Germans in September 1989 turned an internal reform into an international crisis. In East Germany, emigration, demonstrations in Leipzig and elsewhere, the failure of the leadership, and the confusion surrounding the announcement of November 9 led to the opening of the Berlin Wall. In Czechoslovakia, the police action of November 17 sparked mass nonviolent protest, the formation of Civic Forum and Public Against Violence, and the transfer of power culminating in Václav Havel's election as president. Bulgaria experienced a change of leadership within the party followed by opposition mobilization, while Romania's revolution was violent, ended with the execution of Nicolae and Elena Ceaușescu, and left a contested legacy. The cases show that the revolutions were not uniform, not uniformly peaceful, and not mechanically directed from Moscow or Washington.

The fourth division showed that the opening of the Berlin Wall did not itself reunify Germany. Reunification involved the democratic mandate of the March 18, 1990, election in East Germany, Chancellor Helmut Kohl's ten-point program, monetary, economic, and social union beginning on July 1, the Unification Treaty signed on August 31, and accession under Article 23 of the Basic Law on October 3. It also required the Two Plus Four negotiations among the two German states and the four wartime Allied powers, in which Gorbachev accepted a united Germany's right to choose its alliance, and the Treaty on the Final Settlement signed on September 12, 1990, which entered into force on March 15, 1991. The final recognition of the German–Polish border removed a source of insecurity. The consequences for East Germans included new freedoms and also unemployment, the privatization process of the Treuhandanstalt, struggles over property and Stasi records, and enduring differences of experience. Historians interpret reunification as both a restoration of national unity and the accession of one state to another.

The fifth division turned to the Soviet Union itself. Glasnost and political reform enabled national movements in the republics, beginning with the Baltic states, whose Singing Revolution and Baltic Way challenged the legitimacy of their incorporation in 1940. Other republics, including Ukraine, Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan, followed different paths, and the emergence of Russia under Boris Yeltsin as a rival center of authority was decisive. The attempted coup of August 19 to 21, 1991, by hard-line officials who opposed the proposed Union Treaty failed because of the plotters' irresolution, public resistance, divisions in the security forces, and Yeltsin's leadership, and its failure destroyed the authority of the Communist Party and the Soviet center, though Gorbachev was restored to office.

The dissolution itself proceeded through distinct acts. The Ukrainian referendum on December 1 removed the possibility of a substantial union, the Belavezha agreement of December 8 among Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus declared the Soviet Union at an end and created the Commonwealth of Independent States, the Alma-Ata declaration of December 21 widened it, Gorbachev resigned on December 25, and the Soviet legislature acknowledged the dissolution on December 26. These were separate political and constitutional actions, and the dissolution raised urgent questions about nuclear weapons, armed forces, debts, and recognition that were resolved over the following years.

The final section considered why the Cold War ended. Explanations emphasizing Soviet economic weakness, the arms race and Western policy, Gorbachev's reforms, popular movements, nationalism, and elite decisions each capture part of the process, and no one is adequate alone. The Cold War also lacks a single end date, because 1989, 1990, and 1991 mark different endings: the end of communist rule in eastern Europe, the settlement of Germany, and the end of the Soviet Union. The consequences included the disappearance of the Warsaw Pact and Comecon, German unity, the independence of the former Soviet republics, and an expansion of political possibilities that also brought uncertainty over economic institutions, borders, and power.

Above all, the chapter emphasizes contingency. The sequence of 1989 to 1991 depended on decisions by individuals and groups, from strikers in Gdańsk to border guards in Berlin and officers in Moscow, who acted without knowing the outcome. Subsequent developments, including democratic institutions, market economies, and European integration, were possibilities opened by the end of the old order and not its predetermined results. Part XX therefore changes the analytical focus, returning to the origins of European integration in Chapter 133 and then examining the post-Cold War order.

Solidarity

Organization founded in Poland in September 1976 by intellectuals to assist workers persecuted after the June protests, creating lasting cooperation between workers and intellectuals.

Self-published, unofficial writing circulated outside state censorship in communist states, including Poland, Czechoslovakia, and the Soviet Union.

A change of political system produced through bargaining between incumbents and opposition, as in Poland and Hungary, as distinct from a revolutionary rupture.

Gorbachev's program of restructuring the Soviet economic and political system, intended to renew socialism rather than abolish it.

Openness or publicity, the relaxation of censorship and expansion of public discussion that began under Gorbachev and had major unintended effects.
New political thinking
The doctrine of Gorbachev's foreign policy holding that security was interdependent, that nuclear war was unwinnable, and that universal human interests took priority over class conflict.

The treaty signed on December 8, 1987, eliminating all American and Soviet ground-launched missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometers.

The informal principle, associated with the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia, that the socialist community could intervene to defend socialism in a member state; abandoned in practice in the late 1980s.

The Soviet legislature created by the reforms of 1988 and partially elected in competitive voting in March 1989.
Pan-European Picnic
A gathering on August 19, 1989, near Sopron on the Hungarian–Austrian frontier, during which several hundred East Germans crossed into Austria; distinct from Hungary's formal border opening in September.
Stasi
The Ministry for State Security of the German Democratic Republic, an extensive secret police and surveillance organization whose files were opened to citizens after 1990.

The term commonly used for the East German mass movement of autumn 1989 that ended the SED's monopoly of power.

The Czechoslovak human rights initiative of 1977 that created a network of dissidents later central to the Velvet Revolution.

The civic movements formed on November 19, 1989, in Prague and Bratislava that negotiated the transfer of power in Czechoslovakia.
Securitate
The Romanian secret police under Ceaușescu, whose role in the events of December 1989 remains contested.

The body led by Ion Iliescu that took power in Romania on December 22, 1989, and won the elections of May 1990.

Chancellor Kohl's proposal of November 28, 1989, outlining stages toward German confederation and unity.

The parliament of the German Democratic Republic, freely elected for the first time on March 18, 1990.

The agency responsible for privatizing or closing East Germany's state-owned enterprises, a subject of lasting controversy.

The human chain of about two million people across Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania on August 23, 1989, protesting the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a continental order that seemed permanent ended, and because it shows how historical change can be rapid, plural, and unplanned. The end of the Cold War involved people at every level: strikers who occupied a shipyard, intellectuals who wrote and printed banned texts, party reformers who negotiated, officers who decided not to fire, crowds that decided to walk through a gate, and leaders who chose or failed to use force. Their actions were shaped by structures, including economic weakness and alliance systems, but they were not determined by them.

The chapter also reveals the layering of the process. The collapse of communist governments, the settlement of Germany, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union were distinct stages with different participants and mechanisms, and understanding them separately prevents the common error of treating one symbolic image, the opening of the Berlin Wall, as the whole story. It connects regions that earlier chapters treated separately: Poland and Hungary, Germany and the Balkans, the Baltic and the Caucasus, and the Soviet center and its republics. In this respect it shows how Europe's eastern and western halves were joined in a single history even while divided.

The legacies of the period extend into every later chapter. The reunification of Germany reshaped the balance of Europe and the agenda of integration. The transitions of eastern Europe set the terms of postcommunist economic and political change, and the disputes over borders, minorities, and memory shaped the Yugoslav wars and later conflicts. The way the Soviet Union ended, through the decisions of republican leaders and with unresolved questions about borders and populations, left issues that would resurface in the relations of Russia with its neighbors. Continuities also survived: NATO, the European Communities, the nuclear balance, and the economic gap between east and west.

For Volume VI, the chapter completes the narrative of Cold War Europe and prepares the transition to integration and the post-Cold War order. Its argument, that the outcomes were contingent and that what followed was not guaranteed, is meant to guard against reading the present back into the past. Chapter 133 now turns to the origins of European integration in the 1950s, a story that began before the end of the Cold War and whose later development was transformed by it.
