Versailles and the New Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On June 28, 1919, German representatives signed a treaty in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo. The treaty was only one document in a much larger peace system, and the Europe it helped to create was shaped as much by what the treaties left unresolved as by what they decided.

The setting of the signing carried layers of meaning that contemporaries noticed and later generations have debated. The Hall of Mirrors had been the place where the German Empire was proclaimed on January 18, 1871, after French defeat in the Franco-Prussian War, as Chapter 100 explained. The Paris Peace Conference had opened on the anniversary of that proclamation, and the Allied governments chose the same palace for the signature of the treaty with Germany. Historians generally agree that these choices were deliberate symbolism, and that the symbolism was received very differently in Paris, where it marked a reversal, and in Berlin, where it deepened the sense that the settlement was an act of humiliation rather than negotiation.

This chapter examines the effort of the victorious powers to construct a political and international order after the collapse of four empires. As Chapter 112 showed, the Habsburg, Hohenzollern, Ottoman, and Romanov orders had ended in 1917 and 1918, leaving armistice lines, revolutionary governments, hungry cities, and competing national movements across central, eastern, and southeastern Europe. The peacemakers did not begin with a blank map. They confronted facts on the ground, including new states that had declared themselves before the conference opened, armies that were still fighting in the east, and populations whose languages, religions, and loyalties could not be separated by any border.

The central argument of the chapter is that the settlements of 1919 to 1923 were a genuine experiment in reconstructing European political authority, and that the experiment combined real innovations with deep contradictions. The victors created or enlarged states in the name of national self-determination while denying the principle to others. They imposed military and financial obligations on defeated states while lacking a reliable means of enforcement. They founded the League of Nations as a permanent institution for collective security while failing to bring the United States, Germany, and the Soviet Union into it at the outset. Understanding Versailles therefore requires moving beyond a single treaty and a single date.

A basic point of organization governs the chapter. The Treaty of Versailles was the agreement with Germany. Separate treaties dealt with Austria (Saint-Germain, 1919), Bulgaria (Neuilly, 1919), Hungary (Trianon, 1920), and the Ottoman Empire (Sèvres, 1920), and the last of these was never ratified and was replaced by a renegotiated peace with Turkey at Lausanne in 1923. Many of Europe's postwar borders were fixed in these other treaties, in plebiscites, in decisions by the Allied Council of Ambassadors, and in wars that continued after the conference, such as the Polish–Soviet War and the Greco-Turkish War. Students should resist the common shorthand in which "Versailles" stands for the entire settlement.

The chapter draws on several kinds of evidence. The treaties themselves survive in authentic English and French texts, and the records of the conference, including the minutes of the Council of Four and the reports of territorial commissions, show how decisions were reached. Diaries and memoirs by delegates, petitions from groups excluded from the conference, newspaper coverage, plebiscite returns, census data, and the later records of the League of Nations supplement the diplomatic record. Each of these sources reflects a particular vantage point, and the chapter asks throughout whose voices are missing, especially those of defeated governments, colonial subjects, minorities inside the new states, and ordinary people who experienced new borders as changes in citizenship, schooling, property, and daily movement.

Within Part XVII, this chapter stands between the account of the war's end in Chapter 112 and the examination of interwar democracy, economic crisis, and political polarization in Chapter 114. Within Volume V, it marks the point at which the catastrophe of the First World War was converted into a political settlement whose weaknesses became part of the background to the second catastrophe examined in Part XVIII. That later outcome was not predetermined, and the chapter does not treat the settlement as a mere prelude to renewed war. It treats it as a historical achievement and failure in its own right.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the difference between the armistices of 1918 and the peace treaties of 1919 to 1923, and describe how the Paris Peace Conference was organized.
  2. Compare the peace aims of Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George, and Orlando, and assess how wartime commitments constrained a liberal peace.
  3. Evaluate the uneven application of national self-determination in Europe and in the colonial world.
  4. Analyze the territorial, military, and financial provisions of the Treaty of Versailles, including the distinction between Article 231 and the 1921 reparations schedule.
  5. Distinguish among the treaties of Versailles, Saint-Germain, Neuilly, Trianon, Sèvres, and Lausanne by signatory, date, and principal terms.
  6. Trace the establishment and contested borders of Poland, the Baltic states, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, and Greater Romania.
  7. Assess the minority treaty system and the limits of League supervision, including the effects of displacement and population exchange.
  8. Describe the structure of the League of Nations and the International Labour Organization, and explain how the mandate system reflected imperial priorities.
  9. Interpret a primary source, the text of Article 231, with attention to wording, purpose, and later memory.
  10. Contextualize competing historical interpretations of the peace settlements and explain how unresolved questions passed into the interwar period.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the victors of World War I attempt to establish a new political and international order, and what resources and constraints shaped their decisions?
  2. Why did the settlements simultaneously create new states, protect some national aspirations, impose obligations on the defeated, and leave serious conflicts unresolved?
  3. In what ways was the Paris settlement a system of several treaties rather than a single agreement, and why does that distinction matter?
  4. How consistently was self-determination applied, and who decided which populations counted as nations?
  5. What was the difference between legal liability for reparations and moral blame for the war, and how did that difference become politically confused?
  6. Why was the Treaty of Sèvres replaced while the Treaty of Versailles was not, and what does the contrast reveal about enforcement?
  7. What was genuinely new about the League of Nations and the minority treaty system, and why were these institutions vulnerable?
  8. How should historians weigh contemporary criticism of the settlement against later knowledge of what followed?

Historical Context

By November 1918, the wartime coalitions of the Central Powers had dissolved in sequence. Bulgaria signed an armistice on September 29, 1918, the Ottoman Empire at Mudros on October 30, Austria-Hungary at Villa Giusti on November 3, and Germany at Compiègne on November 11. Chapter 112 examined this collapse. The result was a Europe in which the political authority of the Habsburg monarchy had vanished, German imperial government had been replaced by a republic, and nationalist councils in Prague, Zagreb, Kraków, Budapest, and other cities were asserting sovereignty over territories whose future had not been agreed.

The war's human and material cost framed every decision. France had lost roughly 1.3 million military dead and endured the devastation of its northern departments, as Chapter 110 described. Britain and its empire had mobilized on an unprecedented scale and accumulated large war debts, many owed to the United States. Italy emerged on the winning side but with a bitter sense that its sacrifices had not been matched by territorial reward. Germany had been defeated without Allied armies occupying its territory, a fact that would shape postwar German memory. In eastern and southeastern Europe, war, revolution, famine, and epidemic disease, including the influenza pandemic, continued into 1919 and beyond.

The peacemakers also inherited the wartime diplomacy of the Allies. Secret and public agreements, including the Treaty of London of 1915 with Italy, the Sykes–Picot understanding about the Ottoman Arab provinces of 1916, and the agreement with Romania of the same year, had promised territorial rewards before the war's outcome was known. Against these commitments stood the declared principles of Woodrow Wilson, who had brought the United States into the war in April 1917 as an "Associated" rather than an "Allied" power and had articulated aims for a settlement in January 1918, as the next sections explain. The Russian Revolution further complicated matters. The Bolshevik government had withdrawn from the war in March 1918 and was excluded from the conference, while civil war and foreign intervention continued, as Chapter 111 examined.

Nineteenth-century precedent was available but of limited use. The Congress of Vienna of 1814 to 1815, examined in Chapter 92, had restored a balance among great powers after Napoleon, but it had been conducted by a few governments in relative secrecy and had given little weight to national claims. By 1919, mass politics, wartime propaganda, and the participation of the British dominions and many smaller states meant that public opinion mattered in a way it had not a century earlier. Nationalism, which Chapters 97 and 103 traced through the nineteenth century, had become a principle of legitimacy, but there was no agreed method for deciding which communities were nations or how their boundaries should be drawn.

The evidence for the settlement is rich but uneven. Treaty texts, conference minutes, and the papers of statesmen are abundant, and they were produced largely by the victorious governments and their experts. The perspective of defeated governments is preserved in their written replies and observations, while the experience of minorities, refugees, and colonial petitioners must often be reconstructed from petitions, press reports, local records, and later memoirs. Historians also distinguish between what the treaties required, what governments claimed the treaties required, and what was actually carried out, because the gap between signature and implementation became one of the settlement's most important features.

Section 113.1

Armistice, Peace Conference, and the Work of Creating a New Order

The armistices of autumn 1918 were military agreements, not peace settlements. The German armistice of November 11, 1918, signed in a railway carriage at Compiègne, required evacuation of occupied territory in the west, the surrender of large quantities of weapons and transport, Allied occupation of the left bank of the Rhine and bridgeheads on the right bank, and the annulment of the treaties of Brest-Litovsk and Bucharest. It also left the Allied naval blockade in force, and the blockade continued to restrict food and raw materials until the German government completed the steps leading to ratification in the summer of 1919. For many Germans, the months between the armistice and the treaty were months of hunger, political violence, and uncertainty about what peace would bring.

The Paris Peace Conference opened formally on January 18, 1919. The Allied leaders initially expected to negotiate a preliminary peace on the most urgent issues, such as the German military and naval terms, and to settle the remaining questions later. In practice the conference expanded into a continuous process of negotiation covering the German treaty, the League of Nations, colonies, the successor states of Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire. Delegations from more than two dozen states and the British dominions attended. The work was done in a structure of plenary sessions, a Council of Ten of principal leaders and foreign ministers, and dozens of expert commissions on subjects such as responsibility for the war, reparation, the League, labour legislation, ports and waterways, and the boundaries of Poland, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Yugoslavia, and Greece.

The conference had to act amid crises outside its control. Revolutionary governments appeared in Bavaria and Hungary in the spring of 1919, and the Russian Civil War continued with Allied intervention. Fighting continued between Poles and Ukrainians in eastern Galicia, between Hungarian and Czechoslovak forces, and between Poland and Soviet Russia. Delegates also acted under pressure from domestic opinion. Governments that had asked citizens to endure four years of sacrifice faced electorates that expected recognition of that sacrifice, and the conference's secrecy about its bargaining produced speculation that increased public resentment on all sides.

The conference also set a pattern of institutional improvisation. It was a diplomatic gathering, a legislature of the victors, and an administrative agency all at once. Commissions drafted treaty articles, resolved technical disputes, and sent investigators into contested regions, but final decisions on the most contentious questions often returned to the top leaders. This arrangement allowed quick compromise on some issues and left many others to be settled by successor bodies such as the Council of Ambassadors, the Reparation Commission, and the League of Nations. The settlement was therefore less a single decision in 1919 than an administrative process that extended for years.

The administrative workload of the conference complicates the familiar image of four men drawing borders in a private room. Technical commissions assembled railway data, food-supply reports, ethnographic memoranda, port statistics, and rival petitions. Delegates had to decide who could speak for communities inside former empires and how claims gathered in wartime should be tested against local conditions. Economic networks had survived under imperial customs regimes: a river, market town, or junction might connect districts that national delegates now wished to place in separate sovereign states. The conference could assign a territory in a legal text without supplying a functioning customs office, school administration, or police force. These were separate acts of political construction, and the interval between them could become a source of conflict.

Moreover, the armistices were not a neutral waiting room for diplomacy. Occupying armies, the continuing blockade of Germany, demobilizing soldiers, and the scarcity of coal and food altered the bargaining position of governments before final terms were settled. Germany still had a recognized central government able to sign; much of eastern Europe contained provisional administrations whose claim to control was contested by neighbors or armed movements. The difference influenced the form of negotiation: one government received a comprehensive treaty prepared by its victors, while other settlements emerged from frontier fighting, diplomacy, and international supervision. This uneven chronology is central to understanding why an instrument signed in 1919 did not always stabilize the places named in its clauses.

Historical Vocabulary
Armistice, Treaty, and Ratification

An armistice is an agreement to suspend hostilities, usually on military terms and for a limited or renewable period. It does not settle the political questions that caused the war. A treaty is a formal agreement between states that creates legal obligations. A peace treaty normally ends the state of war and fixes borders, indemnities, and other conditions. Ratification is the formal approval by a state's constitutional authorities, after signature, that makes it bound by the treaty. In 1919, Germany's representatives signed on June 28, but the treaty entered into force only on January 10, 1920, after the required ratifications were deposited. The distinction explains why a treaty could be signed in one year, ratified in another, and implemented over many years, and why the United States, which signed the Versailles treaty through its delegation, never became bound by it.

Section 113.2

The Council of Four, the Peace Delegations, and the Unequal Conference

Late in March 1919, the unwieldy Council of Ten gave way to a smaller body that came to be called the Council of Four. Its members were Woodrow Wilson, Georges Clemenceau, David Lloyd George, and Vittorio Emanuele Orlando. Japan, which had been a victorious power and a member of the Council of Ten, participated in matters affecting its interests but not in the daily bargaining of the Four. The smaller council made quick decisions on the German settlement, Rhineland security, reparations, and the Adriatic. Its meetings were often informal, and the records, which survive in the minutes kept by interpreters and secretaries, show leaders negotiating directly, sometimes without expert advisers.

Each leader represented specific political constraints. Wilson was a president whose party had lost control of Congress in the elections of November 1918, and who would eventually face a Senate that would not approve the treaty. Clemenceau led a country that had experienced invasion twice in half a century and needed assurance of security. Lloyd George governed a coalition that had won a December 1918 election in a climate of demands that Germany pay, while also being aware that British trade depended on a functioning European economy. Orlando faced a country in which nationalist opinion believed that the secret Treaty of London of 1915 had promised Italy far more than the conference seemed willing to deliver. Understanding the settlement requires seeing these men as representatives of constituencies with limited room to maneuver, not as free creators of a plan.

The conference was unequal in structure. Defeated states were excluded from the drafting of their terms. The German delegation was summoned to Versailles in late April and received the draft treaty on May 7, 1919, with a limited period to submit written observations. Austria, Hungary, and Bulgaria were treated similarly, and Soviet Russia was not invited. Smaller Allied states, including Belgium, Serbia, Greece, and Poland, sat in plenary sessions and on commissions but had limited influence on the final bargaining, which created resentment among states that had borne heavy wartime losses. The British dominions secured separate representation, a significant step in the history of the Commonwealth, though they largely aligned with British policy on major questions.

Orlando's withdrawal from the conference on April 24, 1919, after Wilson publicly appealed to the Italian people over the head of their government in the dispute over Fiume, illustrates how personal diplomacy and public politics intersected. Orlando returned in early May, but Italy's sense of grievance persisted. The episode also revealed that the Four could not impose agreement on a great-power partner, and that the consequences of one issue, such as Italian claims in the Adriatic, could spill into others, including German reparations and Japanese claims in China.

Japan's role demonstrates both the breadth and the limits of the phrase 'Big Four.' Japanese representatives participated as a principal Allied power and pressed questions involving former German possessions in the Pacific and Shandong in China. Italian delegates, meanwhile, periodically withdrew amid disputes about their wartime promises and the Adriatic. The status of a delegation depended not simply on having contributed to victory but on access to councils, chairmanships and the authority to place an issue on the agenda. Smaller states and delegations often presented detailed memoranda while waiting outside the rooms in which the leaders made pivotal decisions. Yet those memoranda were not wholly symbolic: expertise and local facts could modify a proposed line, and disagreements among the great powers sometimes opened diplomatic space for smaller governments.

Section 113.3

Wilson's Fourteen Points, Allied War Aims, and the Limits of a Liberal Peace

On January 8, 1918, Wilson outlined the Fourteen Points before the American Congress. The first five were general principles: open diplomacy, freedom of navigation, removal of economic barriers, reduction of armaments, and an impartial adjustment of colonial claims that would give weight to the interests of the populations concerned. Points six through thirteen dealt with specific territories, including the evacuation of Russia, the restoration of Belgium, the return of Alsace-Lorraine to France, the readjustment of Italian frontiers along recognizable lines of nationality, autonomous development for the peoples of Austria-Hungary, the restoration of Romania, Serbia, and Montenegro, a secure sovereignty for the Turkish portions of the Ottoman Empire and autonomous development for its other nationalities, and an independent Poland with free access to the sea. The fourteenth proposed a general association of nations. Wilson's later statements, notably his address of February 1918, gave prominence to the principle that peoples should not be handed from one sovereignty to another as if they were property.

The Fourteen Points were not a treaty. They were a declaration by one of the belligerents, and their status was settled by the pre-armistice exchange of notes in October and November 1918. The German government appealed to Wilson to arrange an armistice on the basis of the Points. The Allied governments accepted them as the basis for the peace, but Britain made a reservation about the freedom of the seas, and the Allies stipulated that Germany must compensate civilians for damage caused by German aggression on land, sea, and air. Historians continue to discuss how far this exchange constituted a binding contract, and a fair summary is that both sides appealed to it afterward, selectively, when it supported their positions.

The Allied governments had other priorities. France sought security against a Germany that remained more populous and industrially stronger, and French planners wanted a Rhineland separated from German military control and a strong Poland and Czechoslovakia in the east. Britain's priorities included naval supremacy, the disposition of German colonies, imperial communications in the Middle East, and the restoration of trade, and Lloyd George was wary of both a German collapse and French predominance on the continent. Italy sought the territories promised in 1915. Japan sought recognition of its position in Shandong and the Pacific islands north of the equator. Each government had already made commitments or taken positions that narrowed the room for a liberal peace based on general principles.

The Fourteen Points also contained ambiguities that the settlement exposed. Point ten promised autonomy, not independence, for the peoples of Austria-Hungary, a formula overtaken by events when national councils declared independent states in October and November 1918. Point thirteen promised Poland access to the sea but did not specify the territory through which that access would run. Point nine appealed to nationality as a principle for the Italian frontier, while the Treaty of London had promised Italy territories containing large non-Italian populations. The distance between a declared principle and a boundary line became the central problem of the conference.

The Fourteen Points must also be read as a speech delivered while war continued, not as a statute binding the Paris negotiators. Its provisions mixed open diplomacy and economic principles with particular territorial proposals for Belgium, France, Italy, Poland, Austria-Hungary, the Balkans, and the Ottoman lands. Some formulations spoke of autonomous development rather than immediate sovereign independence. This distinction mattered to leaders who later cited Wilson as authorizing a national state or a colonial liberation that the address did not explicitly promise. British and French governments had made other wartime commitments, and the military victory had strengthened their demand for security and restitution. Negotiation therefore tested whether a general principle could survive the detailed adjudication of competing claims.

Section 113.4

Self-Determination, Empire, and the Peoples Who Could Not Decide

The phrase "self-determination" was used by Wilson, but it had also been invoked by Lenin and the Bolsheviks and by many national movements, and it was never given a precise legal definition at Paris. Wilson's own secretary of state, Robert Lansing, privately worried that the phrase could raise expectations that the conference could not satisfy. In practice, the peacemakers treated national self-determination as one principle among several, to be weighed against strategic considerations, economic viability, historic frontiers, wartime obligations, and the convenience of making new states strong enough to resist both Germany and Soviet Russia. As Chapter 103 showed, nineteenth-century nationalism had already combined language, history, religion, and political claims in contested ways, and there was no neutral test for deciding which communities were nations.

Application was uneven. The conference allowed plebiscites in some borderlands, including northern Schleswig, Allenstein and Marienwerder in East Prussia, Upper Silesia, and the Klagenfurt basin in Carinthia. It declined to hold plebiscites in other places where the outcome might have disadvantaged an ally or complicated security, such as Alsace-Lorraine, the Sudeten German districts of Bohemia, South Tyrol, or parts of Transylvania. Germans in Austria were forbidden to unite with Germany, despite declarations by the Austrian provisional assembly in November 1918 that German-Austria was part of the German republic. The comparison is not a simple one, because strategic arguments, local administration, and the preferences of different populations were mixed, but historians generally agree that defeated populations were treated less generously than those of victorious or newly allied states.

Self-determination was also constrained by the existence of empires. The principle was not extended to the colonies of the Allied powers, and petitions from outside Europe were largely ignored. The Egyptian Wafd delegation was refused a hearing, a petition to the conference signed by the Vietnamese nationalist then known as Nguyen Ai Quoc (later Ho Chi Minh) received no response, and Korean activists inspired by the conference's language launched the March First Movement against Japanese rule. When the conference upheld Japanese claims to the former German concessions in Shandong, Chinese students and citizens organized the May Fourth demonstrations, and the Chinese delegation refused to sign the treaty with Germany. A Japanese proposal for a racial equality clause in the League Covenant secured a majority of those voting in April 1919, but Wilson, presiding, ruled that unanimity was required, and the clause failed. The wider consequences of these decisions are examined in Chapter 128.

Finally, borders could not make states homogeneous. Even where commissions studied ethnographic maps, census returns, and language statistics, populations were intermixed in cities, in the countryside, and across regions such as Transylvania, Galicia, the Banat, Silesia, and Macedonia. Census categories were themselves contested, since they reflected the policies of earlier governments. The peacemakers therefore faced a dilemma without a clean solution: smaller states would be more homogeneous but less viable, and larger states would be more viable but contain more minorities. Their choice generally favored larger states, with the promise of minority protections, a choice that placed the burden of reconciliation on institutions that did not yet exist.

The people excluded from final decision-making did not merely wait to be governed. Petitioners circulated manifestos, appealed to public opinion, organized political committees, and sometimes mobilized troops. Arguments over Korea, China, Egypt, India and the Ottoman Arab provinces show the international circulation of Wilsonian vocabulary even though Paris made few concessions to anticolonial equality. Within Europe, Jewish organizations and other advocates for minority protection likewise disputed the assumption that a state's majority could speak on behalf of every resident. A self-determination principle could identify a political aspiration without establishing which geographic unit would vote, who qualified as a voter, how nonvoters would be protected, or what government would enforce a result. The unresolved procedural questions proved as important as the ideal itself.

Section 113.5

The German Settlement: Frontiers, the Saar, Danzig, and Plebiscites

The Treaty of Versailles was a long document. Its first part was the Covenant of the League of Nations, and its remaining parts dealt with Germany's frontiers, political clauses for Europe, colonies, military and naval limits, prisoners, penalties, reparation, finance, economic matters, aerial navigation, ports and waterways, labour, and guarantees of execution. In the west, Germany returned Alsace and Lorraine to France, territories annexed in 1871 and, as Chapter 100 noted, a long-standing source of Franco-German antagonism. The treaty transferred the small districts of Eupen and Malmedy to Belgium and gave Germany's Hlučín district to Czechoslovakia. After a plebiscite administered under Allied supervision in February and March 1920, northern Schleswig voted to join Denmark and the middle zone voted to remain German.

In the Saar, the treaty placed the coal-rich basin under the administration of the League of Nations for fifteen years, gave France ownership of the mines as compensation for the destruction of French coalfields, and provided for a plebiscite at the end of that period to determine its future. The compromise reflected French economic and security demands, which were stronger than British and American preferences. In January 1935, as later chapters will show, the Saar population voted overwhelmingly to return to Germany.

The eastern frontier was the most contentious. The treaty gave the restored Polish state most of the province of Posen, where a Polish uprising in December 1918 had already asserted control, and most of West Prussia, creating what became known as the Polish Corridor, a strip of territory giving Poland access to the Baltic and separating most of Germany from East Prussia. Danzig, a port with an overwhelmingly German-speaking population but essential to Polish trade, became a Free City under the protection of the League of Nations, with a High Commissioner appointed by the League and a customs union with Poland. Memel in the far north was detached from Germany and placed under Allied authority, then seized by Lithuanian forces in January 1923. Plebiscites in the southern East Prussian districts of Allenstein and Marienwerder in July 1920 produced large majorities for Germany.

Upper Silesia, a major industrial and mining region, illustrates the difficulty of reconciling national and economic claims. The May 7, 1919 draft terms proposed cession of Upper Silesia to Poland, but after German protests the signed June 28 treaty instead provided for a plebiscite under Article 88, which took place on March 20, 1921, and produced a majority for Germany overall but strong Polish majorities in many eastern districts. Polish insurrections in 1919, 1920, and 1921 and German paramilitary resistance preceded and followed the vote. The League Council, acting on the recommendation of an expert commission, partitioned the region in October 1921, and a detailed German–Polish convention concluded at Geneva in 1922 regulated the economic life of the divided industrial district for fifteen years. Altogether, Germany lost roughly 13 percent of its prewar territory and a smaller share of its population, as well as all its overseas colonies, which were transferred to mandate administration (Section 113.18). Precise totals vary according to how the plebiscite areas and colonial populations are counted.

Upper Silesia reveals why a treaty boundary and a territorial outcome should not be conflated. The German terms presented in May 1919 proposed cession to Poland, but German objections and Allied reconsideration yielded a plebiscite clause in the signed text. The vote in March 1921 was followed by armed clashes, commission negotiations and League-mediated partition; voting percentages did not translate automatically into a single undivided award. Industrial mines, factories and railway lines crossed linguistic districts, requiring arrangements for commerce after sovereignty changed. Schleswig, the Saar and Danzig represented different legal mechanisms again: zones of voting, temporary international management of resources, and a League-supervised city with protected Polish access. A map marked only in national colors would obscure the institutional arrangements that made these places administratively distinct.

Section 113.6

Security against Renewed War: Disarmament, the Rhineland, and Guarantees

French security concerns drove the military clauses of Versailles. The treaty limited the German army to 100,000 men serving long enlistments, which prevented the training of large reserves, and abolished conscription and the General Staff. It forbade Germany from possessing heavy artillery, tanks, poison gas, military aircraft, and submarines, and limited the navy to a small force of obsolete warships with a ceiling of 15,000 personnel. An Inter-Allied Military Control Commission supervised the disarmament until 1927. The measures were intended to remove Germany's ability to launch a surprise offensive, but they were also a statement that a defeated state's armaments were a matter for international regulation, a principle that the Covenant proposed to extend to all members through general disarmament, which did not follow.

The Rhineland was the main territorial guarantee. The treaty required Germany to demilitarize the entire left bank of the Rhine and a strip fifty kilometers wide on the right bank, forbidding fortifications and the assembly of armed forces. Allied troops would occupy the left bank and the bridgeheads of Cologne, Koblenz, and Mainz for fifteen years, with phased evacuation of the three zones after five, ten, and fifteen years, subject to German compliance. Clemenceau had originally wanted a separate Rhenish state under French influence, but Wilson and Lloyd George rejected that proposal as inconsistent with self-determination. The compromise left the Rhineland politically German but militarily restricted, and left Allied occupation forces as an everyday presence in cities such as Cologne and Mainz until the evacuation of 1930.

The security package was completed by an Anglo-American guarantee. On June 28, 1919, Wilson and Lloyd George signed treaties promising that their governments would come to France's aid in the event of unprovoked German aggression. The British guarantee was conditional on the American one. When the United States Senate declined to approve the Versailles treaty and the guarantee with it, the British commitment lapsed. France was left with the Rhineland occupation, an inadequate League of Nations guarantee that depended on Council unanimity, and a search for alliances in eastern Europe that led to treaties with Poland in 1921 and Czechoslovakia in 1924.

Historians have disagreed about whether the military clauses were too harsh or too weak. German officers and politicians resented them as a permanent inferiority, and the Reichswehr leadership found ways, partly through secret cooperation with the Soviet Union that began in the early 1920s, to preserve military expertise. Others have argued that the restrictions were less severe than German nationalist rhetoric claimed, and that they were never intended to be permanent. What is clear is that the system depended on continued Allied cooperation, which weakened as British and American interest declined and as French and British views of German recovery diverged, themes taken up in Chapter 114.

Versailles also distinguished several forms of restraint that later commentary sometimes merges. Disarmament limited German military forces and weapons; demilitarization restricted fortifications and troops in specified Rhineland zones; occupation authorized Allied forces to remain for treaty-defined periods as a security guarantee. None of these amounted to French annexation of the Rhineland. German acceptance of restrictions could be inspected, but their durability depended on political cooperation, verification and the willingness of other states to act against evasion. An inspectorate could find a prohibited weapon; it could not by itself resolve French insecurity or create a European consensus on enforcement. The most consequential weakness was not simply a missing clause but the gap between international oversight imagined in 1919 and the resources governments were prepared to supply later.

France had also sought outside security assurances to make the Rhineland settlement politically tolerable. British and American guarantees were discussed alongside the treaty but were not interchangeable with its own obligations. The failure of U.S. ratification undermined the associated American undertaking and affected the British arrangement, altering the political balance on which French acceptance had rested. Here the relationship between text and implementation becomes visible: a signed settlement might contain detailed German restraints while leaving the wider coalition that was expected to uphold them less reliable. This is why the guarantee issue must be evaluated alongside French fears of renewed invasion, rather than reduced to either vindictiveness or an unqualified belief in international law.

Section 113.7

Article 231, Reparations, and the Difference between Liability and Blame

The reparation provisions of the treaty are among the most frequently misunderstood. Article 231 asserted the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing the loss and damage suffered by the Allied and Associated Governments as a result of the war. Article 232 acknowledged that German resources were not adequate to make complete reparation, and required compensation for damage to the civilian population of the Allies and their property. Article 233 established a Reparation Commission to fix the amount, to give Germany an opportunity to be heard, and to prepare a schedule of payments. The treaty itself did not contain a final sum. It required an initial payment of 20 billion gold marks by May 1, 1921, in cash and in kind, and left the remaining total to the Commission.

Two historical distinctions are essential. First, Article 231 attributed responsibility to Germany and its allies for causing loss and damage, which was the legal basis for compensation, not a finding that Germany alone caused every aspect of the war. Austria-Hungary, Hungary, and Bulgaria accepted equivalent provisions in their own treaties. Second, the commonly cited figure of 132 billion gold marks did not appear in the June 1919 treaty. It was established by the Reparation Commission in the London Schedule of Payments of May 1921, following Allied pressure on the German government and an ultimatum threatening occupation of the Ruhr towns. The schedule divided the debt into three categories of bonds, designated A, B, and C. The A and B bonds, totaling 50 billion gold marks, represented the effective payment obligation, while the 82 billion in C bonds were contingent and, as most historians now emphasize, served partly to satisfy Allied domestic opinion.

Reparations covered damage to civilians and property and, at the urging of the South African statesman Jan Smuts and with Wilson's acceptance, also included war pensions and separation allowances for soldiers' families. This broadened the claim substantially. The inclusion reflected the political reality that Britain and France had enormous pension obligations, and that a treaty limited to physical destruction would have favored France and Belgium over Britain. The treaty also required deliveries in kind, including coal, ships, livestock, and railway rolling stock, and deprived Germany of its merchant fleet above a certain tonnage.

Estimates of what Germany could pay, and what Germany actually paid, remain contested. Contemporary estimates by economists such as John Maynard Keynes were much lower than Allied claims, and modern scholars differ on how to measure payments, in part because cash transfers, deliveries in kind, occupation costs, and loans that enabled payment were all counted differently. A balanced summary is that the nominal liability was large, that actual transfers were considerably smaller, and that the political effects of the reparations question, including inflation, the 1923 Ruhr crisis, and the later Dawes and Young plans, were more consequential than the sums alone. These developments belong to Chapter 114.

A legal reading separates the successive functions of Articles 231 through 235. Article 231 states a premise for responsibility for damage as defined in the treaty. Article 232 recognizes Germany's limited resources and outlines compensable damage, notably civilian losses; Article 233 gives an inter-Allied Reparation Commission the responsibility to determine the amount and devise a schedule. Article 234 provides for subsequent consideration of Germany's capacity. Article 235 separately stipulates an interim equivalent of twenty billion gold marks during 1919, 1920 and the first four months of 1921, with occupation costs and approved essential imports deducted in the specified manner. These distinct steps explain why it is legally misleading to claim that Article 231 itself fixed either a complete bill or the famous 1921 figure.

The schedule announced on May 5, 1921 expressed an accounting total of 132 billion gold marks, but its structure involved different classes of bonds and contingencies. A nominal figure printed in a settlement cannot be treated as identical to cash transferred, industrial deliveries actually collected, or the net economic burden after loans and other payments. Historians therefore ask several different questions: what amount was authorized, what could realistically be raised, what was paid in each period, and what political meaning the demand acquired. German critics portrayed the obligation as a permanent humiliation; Allied publics often saw compensation as an inadequate answer to devastation. Those incompatible political interpretations cannot be decided by quoting a solitary number.

Section 113.8

Germany's Delegation, Signature, Ratification, and the Politics of an Imposed Peace

The German government had expected a negotiated peace based on the Fourteen Points. When the delegation, led by Foreign Minister Ulrich von Brockdorff-Rantzau, was presented with the draft treaty on May 7, 1919, it found the terms far harsher than anticipated. Brockdorff-Rantzau's response, which according to widely repeated accounts he delivered while remaining seated, rejected the idea that Germany alone bore responsibility for the war, and the German government subsequently submitted extensive written objections. Official German opinion presented the treaty as a Diktat, a dictated peace, a label that persisted in German political culture throughout the interwar years.

The Allies made limited concessions, chiefly the plebiscite in Upper Silesia, and on June 16 they presented a final reply with an ultimatum. The German government split. The Scheidemann cabinet resigned on June 20 rather than sign, and a new government under Gustav Bauer, with Hermann Müller as foreign minister, faced the prospect that the Allies would resume military operations. Senior generals privately advised that resistance in the west was not feasible. On June 21, German officers scuttled the interned fleet at Scapa Flow, a gesture that denied the Allies the ships and added to Allied resentment. On June 23, the National Assembly voted to accept the treaty, and Müller and Johannes Bell signed on June 28.

The treaty also demanded penalties for individuals. Article 227 arraigned former Kaiser Wilhelm II for a supreme offense against international morality and the sanctity of treaties, and Articles 228 to 230 provided for trials of persons accused of violating the laws and customs of war. The Netherlands, where Wilhelm had fled, refused extradition. The Allies eventually allowed a limited number of cases to be tried by the German Supreme Court at Leipzig in 1921, which produced few convictions and widespread Allied dissatisfaction. The episode shows how aspirations for international criminal accountability exceeded the machinery available to realize them, a point that resurfaces in Chapter 123.

The distinction between legal ratification and political acceptance is crucial. Germany's National Assembly approved the treaty, its government signed, and the treaty entered into force on January 10, 1920, when the required ratifications were deposited. Legally, Germany was bound. Politically, a broad spectrum of German opinion, from liberals to nationalists, regarded the settlement as illegitimate, and parties could attack their opponents as "fulfillers" of an unjust treaty. Historians generally agree that this political rejection was more dangerous to the republic than any single clause, while debating how far the treaty was a cause of later German instability, as opposed to a convenient focus for grievances arising from defeat, inflation, and the legacy of the war.

Germany's political decision involved competing institutions and a sharply constrained timetable. Foreign Minister Ulrich von Brockdorff-Rantzau objected to the terms after their presentation, and the government's objections were conveyed through written exchanges rather than full parity at the negotiating table. The cabinet led by Philipp Scheidemann resigned rather than accept the settlement; Gustav Bauer then headed the government that confronted the Allied ultimatum. The Weimar National Assembly voted to authorize acceptance, although German leaders sought to reserve objection to provisions they viewed as dishonorable. Allied refusal to reopen the central terms narrowed the choice to signature or possible renewed hostilities. The treaty was signed by Hermann Müller and Johannes Bell on June 28, not by the politicians who had designed its main provisions.

These details matter because democratic parliamentary authorization is not the same as enthusiastic popular legitimacy. German voters and parties disagreed profoundly over whether acceptance was necessary, whether resistance was possible, and whether responsibility lay with wartime leaders or postwar negotiators. The republic had to defend its institutions while also administering an agreement many citizens associated with defeat. It would be a mistake, however, to represent Germans as having held a single unchanging opinion: social democrats, liberals, conservatives, nationalists and veterans' groups debated the treaty with sharply different purposes. Political memory was subsequently constructed through speeches, campaigns and commemorations, not mechanically imposed by the Hall of Mirrors alone.

Section 113.9

Saint-Germain: The Austrian Republic and the End of the Habsburg Legal Framework

The Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye, signed on September 10, 1919, dealt with the German-speaking remnant of the Habsburg monarchy. The Austrian Provisional National Assembly had proclaimed the state of German-Austria in October and November 1918, declared it a part of the German republic, and replaced the Habsburg dynasty with a republic. The Allies refused to accept either the name or the union. The treaty required the state to call itself the Republic of Austria, recognized the independence of Czechoslovakia, Poland, and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, and prohibited union with Germany without the consent of the League Council. The same prohibition appeared in the German treaty. The Austrian army was limited to 30,000 volunteers, and Austria accepted a reparation obligation, though in practice the Reparation Commission collected little.

Territorially, Austria lost the lands that other successor states claimed. Bohemia, Moravia, and Austrian Silesia went to Czechoslovakia, and the southern Tyrol up to the Brenner Pass, along with Trentino, Trieste, and Istria, went to Italy, which had been promised these territories in 1915. The South Tyrol transfer placed several hundred thousand German-speaking inhabitants under Italian sovereignty without a plebiscite, a decision that created a lasting minority problem. Most of the southern Slavic lands of the monarchy joined the new South Slav kingdom, and Bukovina went to Romania. A plebiscite in the southern zone of Carinthia on October 10, 1920, produced a majority in favor of remaining with Austria, which preserved the area for the Austrian republic. Burgenland, a mainly German-speaking region of western Hungary, was assigned to Austria, though a later referendum in the city of Sopron in December 1921 kept that city in Hungary.

The more fundamental problem was economic. Vienna, the former capital of a state of some fifty million people, was now the capital of a country of roughly six and a half million, with a very large metropolitan population, an industrial base separated from its raw materials, and food supplies that depended on territories now across new borders. Post-armistice shortages, inflation, and the decline of administrative employment fed economic crisis. Austria's survival depended on international loans arranged through the League of Nations in 1922, which attached supervision to financial aid. Many Austrians, across political lines, believed that the republic was not viable, and the combined ban on union with Germany and the sense of economic weakness fed a persistent culture of doubt about the state's future, a theme examined in Chapters 114 and 115.

Saint-Germain also formalized the legal end of the Habsburg framework. The treaty recognized the dissolution of the monarchy and required the successor states to accept shares of the prewar debt, to settle property questions, and to protect minorities. It did not, however, create a framework for economic cooperation among the successor states, even though the former monarchy had been a large customs union with a shared currency and railway network. The successor states instead raised tariffs, created national currencies, and pursued policies of economic nationalism. Historians such as those who have studied the economic geography of central Europe have emphasized this fragmentation, though others have argued that the monarchy's economic integration was less complete than later nostalgia suggested.

The Austrian republic illustrates the difference between inherited administration and new constitutional authority. Vienna continued to possess courts, ministries, civil servants and banks, but its commercial hinterland had been severed by new borders. Public officials who once oversaw imperial-scale systems had to adjust their competencies to a far smaller state. The Allied prohibition on immediate union with Germany was tied to the broader security settlement and made Austrian independence not merely a domestic constitutional issue but an international obligation. Meanwhile, areas claimed by German-speaking Austrians could be assigned elsewhere because strategic, historical and mixed-population arguments competed with a simple language map. An effective study of Saint-Germain therefore requires reading both the territorial articles and the practical record of economic reconstruction.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty and Successor States

Sovereignty is the supreme authority of a state within its territory and its independence from external control, though in practice it is exercised within treaties, alliances, and international law. A successor state is a state that emerges from the territory of a dissolved or reduced state and assumes some of its rights and obligations. After 1918, the term is especially used for Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, all of which took territory from the Habsburg monarchy. The term can obscure important differences. Some states, such as Poland, claimed continuity with a state that had existed before the eighteenth-century partitions. Others, such as Czechoslovakia, were new creations, while Romania and Serbia were enlarged existing states. Treaties required successor states to accept debt shares, administer inherited property, and protect minorities, which meant that sovereignty in 1919 was accompanied by international obligations that earlier nineteenth-century states had not faced in the same way.

Section 113.10

Trianon: Hungary, New Frontiers, and the Human Geography of Loss

The Treaty of Trianon, signed at the Grand Trianon palace at Versailles on June 4, 1920, dealt with Hungary. Negotiations were delayed by the political turmoil that followed the collapse of the Habsburg monarchy. The liberal government of Mihály Károlyi gave way in March 1919 to a Hungarian Soviet Republic under Béla Kun, which fought Czechoslovak and Romanian forces and was overthrown in August 1919, after Romanian troops advanced to Budapest. A conservative regime followed, and in March 1920 the National Assembly chose Admiral Miklós Horthy as regent of a kingdom without a king. The Allied conference gave the Hungarian delegation, led by Count Albert Apponyi, a hearing, but the territorial terms had already been decided on the basis of the reports of the commissions for the successor states.

Hungary lost roughly two thirds of the territory of the prewar Kingdom of Hungary, excluding the autonomous kingdom of Croatia-Slavonia, and a similar share of its population, with exact figures depending on the method of calculation. Slovakia and Subcarpathian Ruthenia passed to Czechoslovakia. Transylvania, most of the Banat, and parts of the eastern Hungarian plain went to Romania. Croatia-Slavonia, the Bačka, and part of the Banat went to the South Slav kingdom, and Burgenland went to Austria. Hungarian military forces were limited to 35,000 volunteers. The treaty imposed reparations and incorporated the minority protections of the successor states. It entered into force in July 1921.

The most significant human consequence was that a very large number of Hungarian-speakers, roughly three million depending on the census and the definitions used, found themselves living in successor states, in Slovakia, Transylvania, and the Vojvodina. These communities were often concentrated in border districts or in large pockets, such as the Székely lands of eastern Transylvania. The question of how many were "Hungarian" is itself politically sensitive, since language, religion, and self-identification did not always coincide, and pre-1918 Hungarian censuses, like those of its successors, reflected the interests of the governments that organized them. The peacemakers drew on ethnographic arguments, notably from Romanian, Czech, and Serbian experts, as well as strategic and economic considerations, including the desire to give new allies defensible frontiers and rail connections.

Trianon entered Hungarian political culture as a national trauma and became the basis for irredentism, the claim for recovery of lost territories. Revisionism was a central theme of interwar Hungarian politics, and the memory of the treaty has remained a significant element of Hungarian public culture. Historians distinguish between the documented human consequences of the treaty, including the displacement of officials, teachers, and railway employees, and the nationalist narratives that presented Trianon as the unjust dismemberment of a historic millennial state. They also stress that the Hungarian kingdom had itself been a multinational state in which Romanians, Slovaks, Serbs, Croats, and others experienced policies of Magyarization, which is why successor-state leaders considered the transfer a liberation and Hungarian leaders considered it a loss, a pattern central to the memory dispute.

Trianon's geography altered the lives of people who had not crossed any border. A teacher might remain in the same classroom but be required to use a different official language or demonstrate citizenship under new rules. A railway clerk could find that the nearest workshop, pay office or customary market now lay abroad; a landowner might hold fields on both sides of a customs frontier. The situation varied substantially between Transylvania, Slovakia, Vojvodina and Burgenland. Census classifications offered useful evidence but recorded responses under different languages, categories and administrative assumptions, making simple numerical comparisons hazardous. Rather than treating the social result as the automatic expression of a single ethnic percentage, historians must pair treaty lines with school regulations, employment rules, petitions and the places where individual households lived.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living across the New Hungarian Borders

For ordinary people, the new frontiers were not abstractions. Towns that had been administrative or commercial centers, such as Pozsony (Pressburg, later Bratislava), Kolozsvár (later Cluj), Nagyvárad (later Oradea), and Szabadka (later Subotica), acquired new names, new official languages, and new governments. Civil servants, teachers, and railway workers who refused or were unable to swear loyalty to the new state often lost their posts, and refugees from the successor states arrived in rump Hungary in the hundreds of thousands, with estimates generally above 300,000. Many were housed for years in railway cars and temporary camps around Budapest.

Institutions moved with them. The Hungarian university of Pozsony relocated to Pécs and that of Kolozsvár to Szeged, as Hungarian authorities sought to preserve the national educational network, while Romanian and Czechoslovak authorities established or expanded their own universities in the transferred cities. Citizenship rules created uncertainty for families who had been long settled in a place, and disputes about landowners who chose Hungarian citizenship while holding property in Romania, known as the optants question, reached the League of Nations in the 1920s. Railways were rerouted to new frontier posts, trade networks that had connected mountain forests, industrial towns, and the Hungarian plain were interrupted by customs barriers, and markets that had supplied cities across the old kingdom found new tariff walls. Minority schooling, church property, and the language of local administration became sources of daily conflict, and also of negotiation, as local communities adapted. The social history of Trianon therefore involves both displacement and continuity, because many Hungarian-speakers remained in their ancestral communities and became minorities there.

Section 113.11

Neuilly, the Balkans, and the Adriatic Problem

The Treaty of Neuilly-sur-Seine, signed on November 27, 1919, settled the peace with Bulgaria. Bulgaria had entered the war in 1915 in the hope of recovering Macedonia lost in the Second Balkan War of 1913, as Chapter 107 described, and its defeat in September 1918 had been followed by the abdication of Tsar Ferdinand in favor of his son Boris III. The treaty reduced the army to 20,000 volunteers, imposed reparations of 2.25 billion gold francs, and transferred several frontier districts, including Strumica and the western border areas around Tsaribrod, to the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes. Bulgaria also lost Western Thrace, which was placed under Allied control and then assigned to Greece at the San Remo conference in April 1920. As a result, Bulgaria lost its direct access to the Aegean, a grievance that shaped Bulgarian foreign policy for decades.

Neuilly also contained a Convention on the reciprocal and voluntary emigration of minorities, under which Greeks in Bulgaria and Bulgarians in Greece could emigrate under the supervision of a mixed commission. In practice, tens of thousands moved, and the arrangement was an important precedent for later exchanges, though it was voluntary in form and the compulsory Greek–Turkish exchange of 1923 was a different instrument. The treaty also placed the Bulgarian state within a broader Balkan settlement in which Romania had gained from the Allied victory, and in which the Macedonian region remained divided among Greece, Serbia (now within the South Slav kingdom), and Bulgaria, leaving an unresolved question that fed political violence and irredentism.

The Adriatic was the area in which Allied commitments and the principles of nationality collided most openly. The Treaty of London of 1915 had promised Italy South Tyrol, Trentino, Trieste, Istria, much of Dalmatia, and islands in the Adriatic, but not the port city of Fiume (Rijeka), which had been claimed by Hungary and was a vital outlet for the new South Slav state. After the collapse of Austria-Hungary, Italian forces occupied parts of the coast, and the South Slav kingdom claimed Dalmatian and Istrian lands inhabited by Croats, Slovenes, and Serbs. Wilson refused to endorse the full Italian claims, Orlando withdrew, and the dispute was left to direct negotiations that took more than a year.

In September 1919, the poet and nationalist Gabriele D'Annunzio led a force of irregular soldiers into Fiume and held the city until Italian government forces expelled them in December 1920. The Treaty of Rapallo between Italy and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, signed on November 12, 1920, resolved the main issues by giving Italy Istria, the city of Zara and a number of Adriatic islands, and creating a Free State of Fiume. Italy annexed Fiume under an agreement of January 1924, after the rise of Mussolini's government, discussed in Chapter 115. The Adriatic settlement left a legacy of bitterness on both sides, including the Italian nationalist claim of a "mutilated victory" and the experience of Slovene and Croat communities who became minorities in Italy.

The Adriatic illustrates how secret wartime commitments competed with arguments about local consent. The 1915 Treaty of London encouraged Italian expectations of extensive eastern Adriatic gains, but those ambitions confronted South Slav state-building and Wilson's objections. D'Annunzio's armed seizure of Fiume in September 1919 was neither a treaty settlement nor simply a domestic literary spectacle: it challenged Italian government authority and invited competing claims to a commercially valuable port. Italy and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes negotiated the November 1920 Treaty of Rapallo in response to this unsettled environment. Because later diplomatic history contains a different German–Soviet Treaty of Rapallo signed in 1922, students must identify parties and date each time the place name appears. Balkan frontier arrangements combined negotiation with coercion, and no single principle explained all outcomes.

Section 113.12

Sèvres and Lausanne: An Unratified Partition Proposal and a Renegotiated Turkish Peace

The Ottoman Empire signed the armistice of Mudros on October 30, 1918, and Allied forces occupied Constantinople (Istanbul) in November. The peace settlement was delayed by disputes among Britain, France, Italy, and Greece, and by American hesitation. Meanwhile, Greek forces landed at Smyrna (Izmir) on May 15, 1919, with Allied authorization, an operation encouraged by the Greek prime minister Eleftherios Venizelos and by British support. The landing, accompanied by violence against local Muslim civilians, helped catalyze the Turkish national movement led by Mustafa Kemal, who established an alternative authority at Ankara. In April 1920, a Grand National Assembly convened there, in open opposition to the sultan's government in Constantinople.

The Treaty of Sèvres, signed on August 10, 1920, by representatives of the Ottoman government of Sultan Mehmed VI, proposed a drastic reorganization. It would have given Greece Eastern Thrace up to the outskirts of Constantinople and an administrative zone around Smyrna with the possibility of a later plebiscite, placed the Straits under an international commission, recognized an Armenian state and offered the prospect of Kurdish autonomy, assigned Arab provinces to Britain and France as mandates, and allocated zones of influence in Anatolia to France and Italy. It also restored financial and legal privileges for foreigners and limited the Ottoman army. The Ankara government rejected the treaty, and neither Turkey nor several other signatories ratified it as a definitive settlement. Sèvres therefore illustrates the difference between a signed treaty and an implemented one.

The treaty's fate was settled by war. The Greco-Turkish War of 1919 to 1922 began with Greek advances into Anatolia, which stalled in 1921, and ended with a Turkish counteroffensive in August 1922 that drove Greek forces from Anatolia. In September 1922, Turkish forces entered Smyrna, and a large part of the city was destroyed by fire amid massacres and mass flight, an event whose responsibility and details remain subjects of contested memory. The Armistice of Mudanya of October 11, 1922, restored Turkish control of Eastern Thrace, and the Ankara Assembly abolished the sultanate on November 1, 1922. The Allies, facing a victorious Turkish government, called a new conference at Lausanne, which met between November 1922 and July 1923.

The Treaty of Lausanne, signed on July 24, 1923, replaced Sèvres. It recognized the sovereignty of the new Turkish state over Anatolia and Eastern Thrace up to the Maritsa river, abolished the capitulations, created no Armenian or Kurdish state, set a regime of demilitarization and international supervision for the Straits, and included provisions for the protection of non-Muslim minorities in Turkey and Muslim minorities in Greece. The Mosul question was deferred and later settled through the League in 1926. Separately, the Convention concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations of January 30, 1923, provided for the compulsory transfer of Greek Orthodox Christians in Turkey and Muslims in Greece, with exceptions for the Greeks of Constantinople and the Muslims of Western Thrace. The exchange affected roughly 1.2 million Orthodox Christians and between 350,000 and 400,000 Muslims, with estimates varying, and many Greeks had already fled in 1922. The criterion was religion, not language or self-identification. It was a major precedent for the later use of population transfer as a tool of ethnic homogenization, taken up again in Chapter 123.

The Ottoman government's position, the Turkish nationalist position and those of Greek and Armenian representatives were not one debate inside a unified Turkish polity. The Ottoman grand vizier Damad Ferid Pasha delivered an appeal to the peacemakers in June 1919 and his delegation subsequently submitted a memorandum defending a particular imperial reorganization. Representatives of the Ankara movement rejected both that delegation's authority and the partition envisioned at Sèvres. Greek prime minister Eleftherios Venizelos pressed territorial claims within the Allied diplomatic process while Greek troops occupied contested parts of western Anatolia. Armenian representatives Boghos Nubar and Avetis Aharonian sought recognition and security for Armenian populations after genocide and displacement; the projected arrangements in Sèvres did not materialize as a stable enforceable state settlement. These were differing legal claims voiced from unequal military and diplomatic positions.

The difference between Sèvres and Lausanne cannot be explained by a simple change of names on a document. Nationalist military success made a new negotiating framework unavoidable, while the treatment of noncombatants became a defining moral dimension of the result. The January 30, 1923 convention ordered a compulsory exchange defined chiefly by religion, subject to specified exceptions for Greek Orthodox residents of Constantinople and Muslims of Western Thrace. It was negotiated before the July peace treaty and was not a voluntary movement of two linguistically homogeneous populations. Armenian survivors and Kurdish communities did not receive the same settlement mechanisms; the wartime destruction of Armenian communities cannot be treated as equivalent to later migration policies. Lausanne supplied a recognized Turkish peace settlement before the republic was proclaimed in October 1923.

Section 113.13

Poland, the Baltic States, and the Contested Eastern Boundaries

Poland re-emerged as a state in November 1918 from territories that had belonged to Russia, Germany, and Austria since the partitions of the late eighteenth century, as Chapters 79 and 91 noted. Józef Piłsudski became head of state, and the new government faced the challenge of integrating three administrative, legal, and economic systems, three currencies, and several railway networks. Its western frontier was fixed at Versailles, and plebiscites determined parts of the Silesian and East Prussian borders (Section 113.5). But the eastern frontier, in a region where Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Lithuanians, and Jews lived in mixed communities, was not settled by the conference. Allied experts proposed a provisional line, later known as the Curzon Line, but the Polish government did not accept it.

The frontier was decided by war. Polish forces defeated Ukrainian forces in a conflict over Eastern Galicia, including the city of Lwów (Lemberg, later Lviv), in 1918 and 1919. In 1920, a Polish offensive into Ukraine was followed by a Soviet counteroffensive that reached the outskirts of Warsaw, until Polish forces halted it in the Battle of Warsaw in August 1920. The Treaty of Riga of March 18, 1921, divided the disputed territory between Poland and Soviet Russia and Soviet Ukraine, leaving Poland with western Belarus and western Ukraine, far east of the Curzon Line. The Council of Ambassadors recognized Polish sovereignty over Eastern Galicia in 1923. Poland thus emerged as a multiethnic state in which Poles made up roughly two thirds of the population, a fact that framed the minorities problem examined below. The war also challenged Soviet expectations of westward revolution, though its detailed treatment belongs to the separate history of Russia.

The Baltic states followed a related path. Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania proclaimed independence in 1918, in the context of German occupation, the collapse of the empire, and Bolshevik advances. Each fought wars against Bolshevik forces and, in Latvia and Lithuania, against German freebooters and local rivals, with varying degrees of British naval and other foreign assistance. Soviet Russia recognized the independence of Estonia in the Treaty of Tartu of February 2, 1920, of Lithuania in July 1920, and of Latvia in August 1920. The principal Allied powers recognized Estonia and Latvia de jure on January 26, 1921, while comparable recognition of Lithuania came on December 20, 1922; all three entered the League of Nations in September 1921. The Finnish and Swedish dispute over the Åland Islands, which have a Swedish-speaking population, was settled by a League decision in 1921 that confirmed Finnish sovereignty while guaranteeing the islands' autonomy, one of the League's early successes.

Vilnius (Wilno, Vilna), a city with a large Polish and Jewish population and a Lithuanian national claim as the historic capital of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, remained a source of friction. In October 1920, General Lucjan Żeligowski, acting in apparent mutiny but with Piłsudski's approval, seized the city, and Poland incorporated the region in 1922. Lithuania refused to recognize the annexation, relations between the two states were severed, and Kaunas served as Lithuania's temporary capital. In January 1923, Lithuania seized Memel, and an international convention of 1924 confirmed Lithuanian sovereignty with local autonomy. The Teschen (Cieszyn) district was divided between Poland and Czechoslovakia in 1920. These disputes show that the borders of east-central Europe were settled by a combination of war, local initiative, and diplomatic recognition.

On the Baltic shore, independence was a process rather than a single Paris decree. Estonia's February 1920 Treaty of Tartu with Soviet Russia provided a bilateral peace and recognition in a setting where the Bolshevik government had been excluded from the Paris deliberations. Estonia and Latvia received de jure recognition from the principal Allied powers in January 1921; Lithuania's corresponding recognition came in December 1922. All three entered the League in September 1921, a distinct institutional milestone that must not be substituted for the recognition dates. Vilnius, claimed by both Lithuania and Poland, reveals that acknowledgment of a state's existence did not remove the territorial conflicts surrounding it. The separate categories of military possession, treaty title, recognition and League membership need to remain visible on dated maps.

The Polish–Soviet War further demonstrates that frontier decisions depended on armed power beyond the jurisdiction of Paris. The Treaty of Riga in March 1921 established a border after military campaigns and negotiations that could not be derived from Wilson's speech alone. For populations near that line, religious affiliation, language, farm ownership and wartime experience were not identical indicators of political preference. Contemporary administrative reports could describe a district in one national vocabulary while its inhabitants used another in markets or schools. Polish diplomacy likewise had to balance Baltic access through the corridor, relations with the Free City of Danzig and economic needs in industrial borderlands. Each posed a different problem of how an acknowledged sovereign state could actually operate.

Section 113.14

Czechoslovakia and the Successor States: Administration, Citizens, and Legitimacy

Czechoslovakia was proclaimed in Prague on October 28, 1918. Tomáš Masaryk, who had spent the war abroad organizing political support and a Czechoslovak legion, became its first president, and Edvard Beneš became foreign minister. The state combined the Czech lands of Bohemia, Moravia, and part of Silesia, which were industrial and urbanized, with Slovakia, a more rural region that had been under Hungarian administration, and, from 1919, Subcarpathian Ruthenia, whose autonomy was promised but long deferred. In the Pittsburgh Agreement of May 1918, Czech and Slovak émigré leaders in the United States had spoken of Slovak autonomy, but the constitution of February 1920 created a centralized state and a nominally unified "Czechoslovak" nation, a conception that Slovak autonomists contested.

The population was diverse. In the census of 1921, those designated Czechs and Slovaks together made up a little over two thirds of the population, though the idea of a single Czechoslovak nation was a political construct, while Germans made up roughly a quarter, Hungarians about 5 percent, and Ruthenes, Jews, and Poles smaller shares. German-speaking inhabitants of the Bohemian borderlands had declared their own provinces and sought union with German-Austria in November 1918, and Czechoslovak troops suppressed demonstrations in March 1919, causing dozens of deaths. The state proved to be among the most stable democracies of the interwar period, with a developed economy and strong political institutions, but its structure left Germans, Slovaks, Hungarians, and Ruthenes with differing degrees of dissatisfaction.

The state also had to translate inheritance into administration. A reception law passed on October 28, 1918, carried over Austro-Hungarian laws until they could be replaced. Officials and officers of the monarchy staffed the new administration and army, and the new state took over the railway and postal systems. In early 1919, the finance minister Alois Rašín organized the stamping of banknotes to separate the Czechoslovak currency from that of the old monarchy, an operation that provided financial independence and contributed to relative monetary stability. Land reform of 1919 broke up large estates, many of them held by German and Hungarian nobles, and thereby changed property relations in ways that were also national in effect. A language law of 1920 designated Czechoslovak as the official language while giving minority languages a legal status in districts where at least 20 percent of residents belonged to a minority, which was more generous than many neighboring states, though minorities still felt disadvantaged.

Czechoslovak legitimacy rested on several claims, including historic Bohemian statehood, the principle of national self-determination, and the contribution of the Czechoslovak legions to the Allied cause. Its critics rejected each claim in turn: Germans pointed to their own wish to join Austria, Slovaks to the unfulfilled promise of autonomy, and Hungarians to the incorporation of Hungarian-speaking districts. Foreign policy built on the alliance system of the Little Entente, concluded with Romania and Yugoslavia in 1920 and 1921, and on a close relationship with France. The experience of Czechoslovakia thus shows that a relatively successful democratic state could coexist with real minority grievances, a theme returning in Chapter 116.

Czechoslovakia drew on substantial imperial-era industrial capacity, skilled officials and transport infrastructure; these advantages did not automatically make its entire citizenry share an identical conception of the nation. Czech and Slovak politicians negotiated cooperation while facing disagreements over centralization and local autonomy. In the German-speaking border districts, many people expected political arrangements different from those ultimately imposed; Hungarian- and Ruthenian-speaking communities had distinct concerns about schools and offices. When the new government revised a railway schedule or school language rule, it was converting a claim to sovereignty into daily practice. Continuity of technical staff could coexist with discontinuity in the official language, electoral framework and administrative jurisdiction. This duality explains why institutional strength and minority grievance could develop together.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Habsburg Administration to Successor States

What Changed

  • Sovereignty passed from a dynastic monarchy to republics and a few monarchies with national legitimacy, and borders that had been internal administrative lines became international frontiers
  • The monarchy's large internal market was divided by tariffs and separate currencies, and railway networks were reoriented toward national capitals
  • Official languages shifted, with German and Hungarian giving way to Czech, Polish, Romanian, Serbo-Croatian, and other languages in schools, courts, and offices
  • Many officials, officers, and teachers lost their posts or were replaced, especially those who had served Hungarian or German administrations

What Stayed the Same

  • Much Habsburg law, administrative practice, and bureaucratic training survived through reception laws and the continued employment of experienced officials
  • Cities, universities, industries, and churches continued, often with the same buildings and sometimes the same personnel
  • Mixed populations persisted, so that the social complexity of the monarchy continued within the new states
  • Local economic structures, including landholding patterns outside the reform areas, persisted for years

Who experienced change most directly? Officials, teachers, minorities, and residents of cities that changed hands experienced the sharpest change. Farmers and workers in regions with local majorities, such as Czech villages, might have felt greater continuity, though inflation, currency change, and land reform also affected them. Why the pattern matters: Recognizing continuity prevents the misleading picture of an instantaneous break, and recognizing change prevents the equally misleading idea that the monarchy's institutions simply continued under new flags.

Section 113.15

Yugoslavia, Greater Romania, Italy, and the Problem of Political Integration

The Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes was proclaimed in Belgrade on December 1, 1918, under the Karađorđević dynasty, uniting the Kingdom of Serbia, Montenegro, which was incorporated after an assembly at Podgorica deposed King Nicholas, and the South Slav lands of the former Habsburg monarchy, including Croatia-Slavonia, Slovenia, Dalmatia, and Bosnia and Herzegovina. The name Yugoslavia was officially adopted only in 1929. As discussed in Chapter 107, the idea of South Slav union had a long history, but its meaning was contested. Serbian leaders, notably Nikola Pašić, tended to see the state as an expanded Serbia, while many Croatian politicians favored federal arrangements, and Slovene leaders sought autonomy within a larger unit.

The state's inhabitants had very different political experiences. Serbia had been an independent state with a parliamentary tradition and its own army, Croatia had been part of the Hungarian crown with its own diet, Slovenes had lived in the Austrian half of the monarchy, and Bosnia had been administered by Austria-Hungary since 1878. Religious differences added further complexity, including Orthodox, Catholic, and Muslim communities and a smaller Jewish population, as well as German, Hungarian, Albanian, and other minorities. The Vidovdan Constitution, adopted on June 28, 1921, established a centralized parliamentary monarchy, and the Croatian Peasant Party led by Stjepan Radić refused to take its seats for several years, protesting the centralization. In Macedonia and Kosovo, administration was authoritarian and colonization policies encouraged Serbian settlers. The experience illustrates the difficulty of making the principle of South Slav unity a basis for common political institutions, themes that return in Chapter 135.

Romania, which entered the war in 1916 and suffered occupation, emerged with extensive territorial gains and new administrative responsibilities. Transylvania, whose union with Romania was declared by an assembly at Alba Iulia on December 1, 1918, parts of Banat, and Bukovina were addressed through differing military, political, and treaty processes. Bessarabia requires a separate legal qualification. Although Romania exercised effective control following a union vote in 1918, a treaty recognizing Romanian sovereignty signed at Paris on October 28, 1920 by Britain, France, Italy, Japan and Romania never entered into force because Japan did not ratify it. Soviet Russia rejected the Romanian claim, and the United States did not join the treaty. Greater Romania included large Hungarian, German, Jewish, Ukrainian and other populations; demographic proportions vary by census year and classification. Romania's land reform and integration policies illustrate that an enlarged state, even where recognized, still had to reconcile inherited legal systems, currencies, land tenure and competing claims of political belonging.

Italy, a winner of the war, also faced difficulties of integration. It gained Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste, Istria, and Zara, and its government pursued policies in the new border provinces that grew harsher after Mussolini took power in 1922. The postwar crisis, including unemployment, labor unrest, and nationalist resentment over a "mutilated victory," contributed to a political climate that proved favorable to the rise of fascism, as Chapter 115 explains. This development warns against assuming that victory guaranteed political stability, and it connects the territorial settlement to the political transformations of the following decade without making those transformations inevitable.

Romania's expansion likewise illustrates the limits of the word 'recognized.' Some disputed regions were governed from Bucharest after military occupation and political union decisions, but the external legal position differed by territory. In Bessarabia, Britain, France, Italy and Japan signed a Paris treaty with Romania on October 28, 1920 recognizing Romanian sovereignty; that instrument never came into force because Japan did not ratify it, and Soviet Russia continued to reject the claim. An official diplomatic note is therefore not evidence that the region was uncontroversially settled by a legally operative treaty. The distinction has broad analytical value: an administration can collect taxes and run schools in a district while international consent remains incomplete. Similar caution should inform descriptions of Yugoslav and Italian borderland incorporation.

Section 113.16

Minorities, Treaty Guarantees, Displacement, and the Cost of New Borders

The conference recognized that the new borders would leave many people in states where they were not part of the majority. The response was a series of minority protection arrangements, the first of which was the Polish Minorities Treaty, signed on June 28, 1919, the same day as the Treaty of Versailles. Similar treaties followed with Czechoslovakia, the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, Romania, and Greece, and equivalent clauses appeared in the peace treaties with Austria, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Turkey. Albania and the Baltic states made declarations upon joining the League. The treaties guaranteed equality before the law, religious freedom, the right to use minority languages privately and, in some cases, in courts and education, and the right to maintain minority institutions at the groups' own expense. They also required the granting of citizenship to residents of the territories concerned.

The League of Nations was assigned the responsibility for supervising these obligations. Minorities, or organizations on their behalf, could submit petitions to the League Secretariat, which examined their admissibility and referred them to the Council, usually through a committee of three Council members. The League's Minorities Section, a small unit of the Secretariat, tried to solve problems quietly through negotiation. A special arrangement, the Geneva Convention of 1922, gave Upper Silesia an unusually detailed system, including a Mixed Commission and an Arbitral Tribunal, to which individuals could appeal directly. Jewish organizations, including the Committee of Jewish Delegations, played a significant role in persuading the conference to include provisions for Jews as a national minority in Poland and Romania, and Jewish communities across east-central Europe frequently used the petition system. Muslim communities in Yugoslavia, Albania, Bulgaria, and Greece were also covered, and the Bosnian Muslim and Albanian populations of Yugoslavia presented particular problems of political incorporation.

The system had serious limits. The obligations applied only to specific new or enlarged states, not to Britain, France, Italy, or other great powers, which led the states concerned to complain that they had been singled out and subjected to a discriminatory standard. The League had no power to impose compliance beyond publicity and diplomatic pressure, and the Council's requirement of unanimity limited action. Many governments interpreted minority rights as a threat to national unity, and some minorities, such as Germans in Poland and Czechoslovakia, found support from Germany, which used the League's procedures for political purposes. In September 1934, the Polish government announced that it would no longer cooperate with the League's minority supervision, an early sign of the system's collapse.

Displacement was the other side of the new borders. Refugees included Hungarians moving from successor states, Russians and Ukrainians fleeing civil war and famine, Armenians who had survived wartime violence, and Greeks and Turks displaced in 1922 and 1923. The League responded by appointing Fridtjof Nansen as High Commissioner for Russian refugees in 1921, and the identity document known as the Nansen passport, introduced in 1922, enabled many stateless persons to travel and work. This humanitarian response was significant, though it did not create a durable right of asylum. Taken together, the minority treaties, citizenship rules, and refugee responses show that the new order was concerned with the human consequences of its borders, even as it failed to prevent displacement.

Minority protection was international in the form of its guarantees, but local in the circumstances that determined whether those guarantees mattered. A minority petition might refer to an expelled teacher, a closed religious school or a citizenship rule applied unevenly by a district official. League procedures could generate diplomatic attention and pressure; they did not function as a uniform appellate court available to every resident of Europe. Signatory governments resented asymmetrical supervision, arguing that larger powers were not subject to equivalent undertakings. Minorities themselves disagreed about whether to pursue integration, cultural autonomy, external protection or border revision. A careful case study begins by asking which treaty bound which state, who had standing to petition, what evidence was submitted and what institution could act upon a complaint.

Displacement also requires distinguishing related but different experiences. Some people fled wartime fighting; others were expelled or were encouraged to leave through political pressure; still others were included in the compulsory Greco–Turkish exchange instrument of 1923. The League's refugee work under Fridtjof Nansen sought practical responses to the loss of papers and recognized nationality, including identity documentation for stateless people. The term 'refugee' itself was not uniformly defined in these years, and retrospective totals may mix departures, returns and resettlements. Thus demographic estimates should specify geographic coverage, period and whether they refer to households, persons registered by officials or all people on the move. Numbers are not a substitute for explaining the legal route by which a person could become a stranger in a familiar home.

Historical Vocabulary
Minority Rights and Population Exchange

Minority rights, in the context of 1919, meant legal guarantees that groups defined by language, religion, or national identity, living within a state dominated by another group, would receive equal treatment and could preserve their institutions. Unlike later conceptions of universal human rights, the treaties applied only to particular states and were supervised by the League as a matter of international concern. A population exchange is the organized, mutual transfer of populations between two states, so that each becomes more homogeneous. The 1919 Greek–Bulgarian convention was voluntary in form, while the Greek–Turkish convention of January 30, 1923, was compulsory and defined affected persons by religion. The two concepts reflect opposite approaches to diversity: one tried to protect it where it existed, and the other treated it as a problem to be resolved by relocating people. Historians debate how far the second approach, which was praised by some contemporaries as a realistic solution, legitimized later forced transfers.

Section 113.17

The Covenant and the League of Nations: Collective Security as an Institution

The Covenant of the League of Nations formed Part I of the Treaty of Versailles and of the other peace treaties, a decision that Wilson insisted upon so that the League could not be separated from the settlement. A League commission chaired by Wilson drafted the Covenant in February 1919. The League was intended to prevent war through a combination of public diplomacy, arbitration, collective pressure, and the reduction of armaments. It was a permanent international organization with its own headquarters in Geneva, which distinguished it from the nineteenth-century Concert of Europe, discussed in Chapter 92, whose diplomacy had been conducted through ad hoc conferences.

The League had three principal organs. The Assembly, in which every member state had one vote, met annually and discussed matters within the League's sphere. The Council, which included permanent members and rotating non-permanent members, dealt with disputes and security questions. The original design envisaged Britain, France, Italy, Japan, and the United States as permanent members, so the American absence left four. The Secretariat, directed by the British official Sir Eric Drummond as first Secretary-General, provided continuity, administration, and expertise. The Permanent Court of International Justice, created under the Covenant and opened in 1922, offered a judicial forum for legal disputes. Important decisions in the Assembly and Council required unanimity, with some exceptions, which protected sovereignty but made action difficult.

The Covenant's central provisions concerned security. Article 10 committed members to respect and preserve the territorial integrity and existing political independence of all members against external aggression, though its wording was ambiguous about what members were obliged to do. Articles 12 to 15 established procedures for disputes, including arbitration or Council inquiry and a three-month cooling-off period before war could be lawfully begun. Article 16 provided that a member resorting to war in violation of those procedures would be deemed to have committed an act of war against all members, and required economic and financial sanctions, while leaving military action to Council recommendation. Article 8 contemplated a general reduction of armaments. The system relied on the willingness of states to act collectively and on their agreement about who was an aggressor.

The League formally began on January 10, 1920, and held its first Council meeting in Paris on January 16 and its first Assembly in Geneva in November 1920. Its first years brought modest successes in settling the Åland dispute, administering the Saar and Danzig, supervising minorities, helping Austrian and Hungarian finances, and organizing refugee relief. Its weaknesses were also evident. The United States did not join. Germany was admitted only in 1926, and Soviet Russia, which regarded the League as an instrument of the victors, joined in 1934. Without these major powers, the League's authority depended heavily on British and French policy, which often diverged. Historians therefore distinguish between the League's institutional innovation, which was real and lasting, and its practical effectiveness in preventing great-power conflict, which was limited.

The Covenant's operation depended on states that retained sovereignty and controlled their own military and fiscal decisions. Its promise of collective security was not a standing international army. Decisions required consultation, procedures and a political willingness to accept costs; unanimity rules and the interests of powerful members could obstruct responses. The League was nevertheless more than an empty aspiration. Its Council and Assembly created recurring forums for diplomacy and its secretariat supported investigations and communications that previous arrangements had handled through ad hoc conferences. In the early years the organization helped supervise certain territorial questions and minority obligations, but its remit was selective. Assessing institutional success consequently requires asking what responsibility it had actually been assigned rather than judging every frontier crisis against an imagined universal government.

Section 113.18

Mandates, Labour Standards, and the International Peace's Imperial Dimensions

Article 22 of the Covenant created the mandate system for the colonies of Germany and the non-Turkish territories of the Ottoman Empire. It declared that the well-being and development of peoples not yet able to stand by themselves formed a "sacred trust of civilization," and that the tutelage of such peoples should be entrusted to advanced nations acting as mandatories on behalf of the League. Mandates were divided into three classes according to the degree of development ascribed to the inhabitants. Class A mandates, former Ottoman territories such as Syria and Lebanon (France) and Iraq, Palestine, and Transjordan (Britain), were considered provisionally independent. Class B mandates, including Tanganyika, parts of Cameroon and Togoland, and Rwanda-Urundi, were administered under tighter control. Class C mandates, including South West Africa and Pacific islands, were administered essentially as integral parts of the mandatory power. The allocation was decided at the conference and confirmed at San Remo in April 1920.

The mandate system involved reporting to the League's Permanent Mandates Commission, which examined annual reports and received petitions. It created a new principle of international accountability for colonial rule. Yet the mandatory powers were the same victorious empires that had taken the territory, the Commission had no power of enforcement, and the system confirmed imperial expansion for Britain, France, Japan, Belgium, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand. Arab, African, and Asian leaders, who had been promised self-government by wartime statements, often experienced the mandates as continued imperial control, and the Palestine mandate, which incorporated the British government's 1917 declaration of support for a Jewish national home, generated conflict between Arab and Jewish communities. These developments tie European peace-making to the colonial world, and the later political consequences are traced in Chapter 128.

The other major institution of the new order was the International Labour Organization, established in 1919 under Part XIII of the Treaty of Versailles. Its founding principles linked lasting peace to social justice, a notion shaped by wartime labour mobilization, socialist and trade-union demands, and the fear of revolution after 1917. The ILO had an unusual tripartite structure in which delegates of governments, employers, and workers took part in its annual International Labour Conference, the first of which met in Washington from October to November 1919 and adopted conventions including one limiting industrial working hours to eight a day and forty-eight a week. The French socialist Albert Thomas became its first director in 1920. The ILO could not compel states to adopt its conventions but could publicize standards, and it became one of the most durable parts of the settlement, surviving the League itself.

Humanitarian cooperation, including refugee relief, health programs, and the suppression of the traffic in women and children, also grew within the League framework, though sometimes on a modest scale. For students, the importance of these developments is that the peace was not only about borders and punishments. It also aimed to create permanent institutions for economic and social cooperation, however limited in practice, and these institutions were intertwined with imperial authority. The first postwar international order was thus both innovative and conservative: it created new forums for accountability and cooperation while preserving the unequal structure of empire.

The mandate system made the contradiction of internationalized imperial power especially visible. Article 22 described supervision as a 'sacred trust of civilisation,' but the powers administering former German and Ottoman territories retained substantial authority and did not offer uniform political participation. The Permanent Mandates Commission reviewed reports and could scrutinize administrative claims; it did not confer immediate independence or erase the relationship between imperial interest and governance. The International Labour Organization presented a different institutional model. Its tripartite representation of governments, employers and workers gave social legislation a place within the peace architecture, acknowledging that unemployment, workplace conditions and industrial conflict were questions of international stability. These innovations should be judged against what they could institutionally do, without confusing the mandates' tutelage with the ILO's labor standard-setting.

Section 113.19

Ratification, U.S. Absence, Implementation, and a Four-Track Treaty Timeline

The difference between signing and implementing a treaty became clear in the case of the United States. Wilson returned home in July 1919 to seek the Senate's consent to the treaty, a requirement of a two-thirds vote under the American Constitution. Opposition focused on the League Covenant, especially Article 10, which some senators believed would commit the United States to wars at the direction of a foreign body. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee, proposed reservations, and Wilson, who suffered a severe stroke in October 1919 during a speaking tour, refused to accept compromises. On November 19, 1919, and again in March 1920, the Senate failed to reach the two-thirds majority needed to approve the treaty with reservations. The United States concluded a separate treaty with Germany in August 1921 and did not join the League, though American officials and private organizations continued to participate in some international work.

The treaty system required machinery to turn commitments into reality. The Reparation Commission calculated and administered German obligations, the Inter-Allied Rhineland High Commission supervised the occupation, and the Inter-Allied Military Control Commission verified German disarmament. The Council of Ambassadors, meeting in Paris, continued to decide boundary questions and treaty interpretation, often more important in practice than the League itself in the early 1920s. Delimitation commissions surveyed borders, and plebiscite commissions supervised votes in disputed areas. The first conference with the German government as a participant took place at Spa in July 1920, and it addressed disarmament and coal deliveries. The administrative work was slow, and quarrels among the Allies about enforcement, especially between France and Britain, allowed Germany and other defeated states to exploit disagreement.

Students should therefore think of the settlement as proceeding along four parallel tracks, which did not move at the same speed or conclude at the same time. The German track began with the treaty of June 1919 and continued with the reparation schedule of 1921. The successor-state track consisted of the treaties of Saint-Germain, Neuilly, and Trianon and the subsequent border adjustments, plebiscites, and wars in east-central Europe. The Turkish track began with Sèvres and ended with Lausanne and the exchange convention. The institutional track included the League, the ILO, minority supervision, and the mandates, which began operating in 1920 and developed through the decade. The sequence in the following study shows how these tracks were intertwined and how the settlement continued to evolve long after the 1919 signatures.

A continuing conference system developed because signature alone could not complete the settlement. Ambassadors, reparation specialists, Allied occupation authorities and League officers interpreted clauses, supervised disarmament, adjudicated claims and renegotiated implementation. Their work sometimes resembled an extended diplomatic afterlife of Paris rather than a clean transfer from conference to international organization. States submitted different questions to different bodies: a reparations dispute was not automatically a League boundary case, and a national parliament's vote on ratification did not decide an international commission's future jurisdiction. The U.S. Senate's refusal to ratify Versailles in November 1919 and March 1920 meant that the country never joined the League, despite Wilson's role in its founding program. American financial and diplomatic influence continued, but the intended collective-security architecture operated without an essential author.

The sequence also demonstrates what 'enforcement' meant in practice. The 1921 reparations schedule, Upper Silesian arbitration, 1923 Ruhr occupation and the renegotiation of the Ottoman settlement all involved different combinations of persuasion, legal interpretation, occupation, economic pressure and military capacity. The Ruhr crisis of January 1923 was a response to disputes about German deliveries; it is best treated here as evidence of coercive implementation rather than as the entire story of interwar economic politics. In contrast, Lausanne resulted from a fundamental shift in the ability of the Allies to impose the earlier Sèvres program. Students should record treaty signature, entry into force, supervision and later change as separate dates. Without those distinctions, a chronology can create the false impression of a coherent settlement arriving at once.

Section 113.20

Was It a New Europe? Achievements, Contradictions, and Historical Debate

The settlement created a Europe that was in some ways new. The empires of the Habsburgs, Hohenzollerns, Romanovs, and Ottomans no longer ruled central and eastern Europe. A belt of states from Finland and Estonia to Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia owed their legal existence to the peace and the national movements it recognized. Governments were more often republican or parliamentary than before the war, and women received the vote in Germany, Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland, and several other states, as Chapter 114 will show. A permanent League, a labour organization, minority treaties, mandates, and a Permanent Court introduced new forms of international accountability. These were not minor changes, and a purely negative assessment would ignore them.

Yet the contradictions were severe. National self-determination was applied inconsistently and produced states that contained large minorities. The treaty system treated defeated states as objects rather than participants, while relying on those states' compliance. Britain, France, and the United States disagreed about German power, reparations, and security, and the withdrawal of the United States and the absence of the Soviet Union and Germany from the League undermined the new institutions. Economic fragmentation in the former Habsburg lands and the problems of reparation and war debts weighed on recovery. The new order depended on the continued cooperation of the great powers, and by the early 1920s that cooperation was fraying.

There were achievements that deserve analytical weight. International recognition protected the existence of several states whose independence had been imperiled under imperial rule; the League established regular machinery for interstate consultation; the ILO made labor conditions part of the formal peace agenda. The existence of minority treaties also represented a partial acknowledgment that populations could possess rights not exhausted by the claims of their state's majority. These measures were neither identical in every region nor fully enforceable. Nevertheless, treating them as meaningless because later crises occurred would erase contemporary actors who used them, sometimes effectively, to obtain political recognition or challenge discriminatory policies.

The weaknesses were just as concrete. The distribution of power at Paris marginalized defeated and colonized peoples, national lines could split mixed communities, and guarantees could be politically hollow when governments refused enforcement. French security expectations, German objections to reparations, Hungarian revisionism, Polish access to the Baltic and the fate of Greek and Armenian communities involved different causal chains. No one grievance by itself accounts for the entire European order. An accurate synthesis therefore compares promises and institutions with who could invoke them, whose objections were heard and which governments had the power to act. A treaty is simultaneously a legal instrument, a diplomatic compromise and a contested public symbol, and these levels need not move together.

Historians have advanced different interpretations using those distinctions. Margaret MacMillan emphasizes the difficult decisions and limited knowledge of peacemakers; Zara Steiner places their choices in the longer context of material weakness and fragile international cooperation; Erez Manela investigates how Wilsonian expectations traveled beyond the diplomats' intended audience. These inquiries overlap rather than establishing a simplistic winner between optimistic and punitive accounts. Counterfactual alternatives can illuminate constraint only when their implementation is tested: a broader German negotiating role might have changed legitimacy but also faced Allied electoral commitments, while a stronger international minority-monitoring mechanism would have required governments to supply money, access and political backing. Neither imagined outcome is a proven path to stability. The chapter's result is a framework for evaluating contingent policy decisions, leaving the economic and political crises examined in Chapter 114 to their own evidence.

Historical Debate

Assessing the Peace Settlements

The question: Were the peace settlements of 1919 to 1923 fundamentally flawed in ways that made later instability and renewed war likely, or were they a reasonable if imperfect response to an impossible situation whose later failure resulted from subsequent choices and weak enforcement?

Economics and contemporaneous criticism — John Maynard Keynes: In The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), Keynes criticized the treatment of Germany and wider European economic reconstruction. His intervention is a contemporary argument that requires comparison with later payment records, rather than a definitive tally of what Germany eventually paid.
Diplomatic choices and contingency — Margaret MacMillan: Paris 1919: Six Months That Changed the World (U.S. ed. 2002) reconstructs negotiations and rival aspirations, resisting the claim that the future was mechanically fixed in the Hall of Mirrors. Compare her emphasis on decisions and political constraints with Zara Steiner’s The Lights that Failed: European International History, 1919–1933 (2005), which develops the longer history of weak cooperation and security.
Global reception and unequal self-determination — Erez Manela: The Wilsonian Moment: Self-Determination and the International Origins of Anticolonial Nationalism (2007) shows how Wilsonian language reached audiences not empowered to settle Europe’s map. This approach asks whose participation and expectations counted; it differs in scope and evidence from Keynes’s economic critique and Steiner’s diplomatic study. Susan Pedersen’s The Guardians (2015) adds a specialist focus on the League mandate system.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on treaty texts, conference records, economic data on German payments and loans, parliamentary debates, minority petitions, and the records of governments in the 1920s and 1930s. They differ over how to measure German capacity to pay, how to weigh the intentions of the peacemakers against the consequences of their decisions, and how much weight to give the postwar behavior of Germany and the Allies.

Why it matters: How historians assess the settlement affects their understanding of the causes of the Second World War, the responsibility of different states for the interwar crisis, and the value of international institutions. It also affects the meaning of terms such as "Versailles," which can denote the German treaty, the full peace system, or a political symbol used by opponents of the settlement.

Cause and Effect

From Wartime Collapse to the New International Order

Long-term causes

Nineteenth-century nationalism, the weakness of multinational empires, great-power rivalry, and the ideal of international arbitration had prepared the ground for a new order. The Balkan crises discussed in Chapter 107 had already shown the limits of the old diplomatic system.

Short-term causes

The military defeat of the Central Powers, revolutions in Russia and Germany, the declaration of independence by national councils, American intervention, Allied wartime promises, and public demands for compensation and security all shaped the specific form of the settlement.

Event or transformation

The Paris Peace Conference and the associated treaties of 1919 to 1923 redrew central and eastern Europe, imposed obligations on defeated states, and created the League and the ILO.

Immediate effects

New states had to build administrations and armies while fighting border wars. Minorities faced new citizenship and language regimes, and refugees moved across new frontiers. Germany and Hungary developed strong movements for revision.

Long-term consequences

The settlement provided the legal and territorial framework for interwar Europe, set up institutions that influenced later international organization, and left unresolved disputes over borders, minorities, reparations, and security that later governments exploited.

Historical complexity

No single cause produced the settlement or its limits. Power, ideology, domestic politics, local events, and the actions of non-European actors all mattered, and later outcomes were contingent on decisions made in the 1920s and 1930s.

Historical complexity: The peace was shaped simultaneously by constraints the peacemakers did not control and by choices they did, which is why historians avoid explanations that treat Versailles either as a simple cause of later catastrophe or as an unavoidable outcome of wartime conditions.

Counterfactual decision alternative A — a broader negotiating role for defeated Germany (not an event that occurred): If the Allies had provided structured German input before presenting the May draft, aspects of the treaty’s reception or technical administration might have differed. Against that possibility stands French and British domestic pressure for security and compensation, the German government’s weakened position and the limited Allied willingness to reopen core conditions. The counterfactual does not establish that ratification or durable legitimacy would have followed.

Counterfactual decision alternative B — internationally resourced minority-rights monitoring (not an adopted universal mechanism): A stronger, symmetrical petition and inspection procedure could in principle have made school, citizenship and religious-rights guarantees more credible across the successor states. Yet it would have required funds, access, binding jurisdiction and sustained cooperation from the very governments whose sovereignty was under supervision. Compare the actual Polish treaty and League petition system with these absent capacities before judging likely outcomes.

The unresolved questions passed into the interwar period. Germany's acceptance of its frontiers in the west but not in the east, Hungarian and Bulgarian revisionism, the grievances of Italy, the status of minorities, the weakness of collective security, and the economic problems of reparations and war debts all shaped the decade examined in Chapter 114. At the same time, successor states built functioning governments and economies, minorities developed political organizations and cultural institutions, and the League and ILO built practices of international cooperation. The settlement of 1919 to 1923 was therefore both the foundation of a new Europe and the setting of its vulnerabilities, and its history must be told without reducing it to either achievement or failure.

Chapter Summary

The armistices of autumn 1918 stopped the fighting but did not decide the political order that would follow. The Paris Peace Conference, which opened on January 18, 1919, was a continuing process, not a single meeting, and it produced separate treaties with Germany, Austria, Bulgaria, Hungary, and, in a form that was later replaced, the Ottoman Empire. Students must therefore distinguish Versailles, the German treaty, from the larger peace system of which it was one part.

The conference was unequal in structure. The Council of Four, consisting of Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George, and Orlando, made the key decisions, defeated states were excluded from drafting their terms, smaller allies had limited influence, and petitions from colonial peoples were largely ignored. The participants brought different aims: Wilson's liberal internationalism, French demands for security, British imperial and commercial priorities, and Italian territorial claims based on earlier secret promises.

Self-determination was a powerful principle but was applied unevenly. Plebiscites were held in some borderlands and denied in others, Austria was forbidden to join Germany, and colonial peoples were excluded from the principle altogether. Because populations were intermixed, the new borders created large minorities, and the peacemakers placed their hopes on minority treaties supervised by the League rather than on population movement.

The Treaty of Versailles returned Alsace-Lorraine to France, created the Polish Corridor and the Free City of Danzig, placed the Saar under League administration, limited the German military, demilitarized the Rhineland, and assigned colonies to mandates. It also asserted, in Article 231, the responsibility of Germany and its allies for loss and damage as the basis for reparation. That article was a legal basis for compensation and not a finding of sole causation of the war, and the reparations total of 132 billion gold marks was set only in 1921, with effective liabilities much lower than the nominal figure.

Germany signed under ultimatum on June 28, 1919, and the treaty came into force on January 10, 1920. Legal acceptance did not equal political acceptance. The idea of a dictated peace, or Diktat, became a political weapon against the Weimar Republic, even though historians continue to debate how far the treaty itself, as distinct from defeat and economic disruption, destabilized German politics.

The other treaties redrew the map of central and southeastern Europe. Saint-Germain recognized the Austrian republic and the successor states and forbade union with Germany. Trianon reduced Hungary to roughly a third of its former territory and left some three million Hungarian-speakers outside its borders. Neuilly cost Bulgaria Western Thrace. Sèvres, signed in 1920, was overturned by the Turkish national movement's military victory and replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, accompanied by a compulsory Greek–Turkish population exchange affecting well over a million people.

New and enlarged states, including Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Romania, and the Baltic states, faced the challenge of integrating regions with different legal systems, languages, and political experiences. Several borders were settled by war, including the Polish–Soviet War and the Greco-Turkish War. Czechoslovakia sustained a democratic system but had grievances among Germans and Slovaks, while Yugoslavia and Romania struggled with centralization and minorities. Borderlands such as Upper Silesia, Danzig, Vilnius, Fiume, and Eastern Thrace were focal points of conflict.

The League of Nations, the Permanent Court of International Justice, the mandate system, the minority supervision procedures, and the International Labour Organization were innovative institutions, though weakened by the absence of the United States, the late entry of Germany and the Soviet Union, the unanimity rule, and lack of enforcement power. The mandates also preserved imperial authority, and the League's humanitarian and economic work, while real, could not compensate for divisions among the great powers.

Historians disagree about the settlement. Critics in the tradition of Keynes emphasize its punitive and economically damaging features, while later scholars stress its limits and the failure of enforcement, and many current historians stress contingency. A balanced view recognizes that the peace created a new Europe of states, institutions, and obligations, and left unresolved conflicts between sovereignty, minority protection, security, and economic obligation. Chapter 114 turns to the way democracies and economies operated within this framework.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a continent that had been shaken by four years of war tried to turn military outcomes into political order. The settlements of 1919 to 1923 produced a map, a set of institutions, and a set of obligations that structured European politics for two decades, and they did so by combining principles such as self-determination and collective security with the power politics of victors and the ambitions of new national movements. Studying them reveals how decisions made under pressure, with incomplete information and conflicting commitments, can create new possibilities while leaving others closed.

The chapter also connects many regions of Europe. The Rhineland, Danzig, Transylvania, Vilnius, Fiume, Thrace, and the Straits were all affected by decisions made in Paris, but their histories were also shaped by local initiatives, wars, and communities. The Greek–Turkish exchange links the Balkans, Anatolia, and the Aegean, while the mandate system links Europe to the Middle East, Africa, and the Pacific. These connections reinforce a theme of this textbook, that European history cannot be written as the history of a few western capitals.

Many later developments grew from this settlement. The politics of revision in Germany and Hungary, the legal and institutional framework of minority protection, the experience of international organization, the use of population transfer, and the meaning of sovereignty in the new states all carried forward. The following chapter examines how fragile parliamentary regimes and unstable economies functioned within this framework, and Part XVIII will show how the failure of the interwar order contributed to a second continental catastrophe, without making that catastrophe inevitable.

For Volume V, the chapter marks the transition from the experience of war to the construction of a postwar order that proved unstable. Its most important lesson may be that peace-making is a continuing historical process in which signatures are only one stage. Treaties mattered, but so did enforcement, legitimacy, and the willingness of governments and peoples to accept the outcome, and the historian's task is to understand all of these, not to reduce the settlement to a single verdict.
