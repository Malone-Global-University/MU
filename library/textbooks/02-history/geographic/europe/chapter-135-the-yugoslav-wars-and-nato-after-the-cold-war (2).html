Chapter Opening

The end of the Cold War changed the institutions through which Europeans organized their security, but it did not end war in Europe. Between 1991 and 2001 the dissolution of Yugoslavia produced a series of distinct armed conflicts, campaigns of mass expulsion, and a genocide, and the responses to them transformed the North Atlantic Treaty Organization from a Cold War defensive alliance into an organization that also fought, policed, and administered in the name of crisis management.

In the Bosnian city of Mostar, a stone bridge with a single arch spanned the Neretva River for more than four centuries. Built in the 1560s under Ottoman rule, the Old Bridge (Stari Most) joined the two halves of a city whose population included Muslims, Catholic Croats, Orthodox Serbs, and others, and it had become an emblem of the region's layered history. On November 9, 1993, it collapsed under fire from Bosnian Croat forces during a war between former allies. It was rebuilt with international funding and expertise and reopened in July 2004. The bridge's destruction and reconstruction carry several of this chapter's themes: the destruction of shared spaces as an instrument of war, the involvement of many outside governments and institutions, and the long, uneven labor of postwar repair.

This chapter asks why the dissolution of Yugoslavia, a socialist federation that was neither a Soviet satellite nor a Western ally, produced a succession of wars rather than a negotiated separation, how European and international institutions responded, and how those conflicts changed NATO's role. Chapter 134 traced the European Community's evolution into the European Union and the creation of a Common Foreign and Security Policy at Maastricht in 1991 and 1992. The same months saw Croatia's war and the first international debates over Bosnia. Chapter 135 therefore shows that European integration and the end of the Cold War did not eliminate warfare, persecution of civilians, or competition over sovereignty within Europe, and that the new security order of the 1990s was tested early and severely.

It would be a serious mistake to treat these events as a single war. The ICTY, the tribunal established to prosecute crimes committed in the former Yugoslavia, organizes its history around a sequence of separate conflicts: Slovenia in 1991, Croatia from 1991 to 1995, Bosnia and Herzegovina from 1992 to 1995, Kosovo in 1998 and 1999, and the armed confrontation in what is now North Macedonia in 2001. Each had different participants, different territorial questions, different forms of violence, and different outcomes. They were linked by a common institutional collapse, by the involvement of overlapping political leaders and armed forces, and by the movement of refugees, weapons, and ideas across newly contested borders, but they were not interchangeable. This chapter keeps them distinct.

It also takes seriously the vocabulary of atrocity. Civilians were expelled from their homes, detained in camps, raped, shelled in besieged cities, and killed in organized mass executions. The chapter distinguishes among ethnic cleansing, a descriptive term for the forcible removal of populations; war crimes and crimes against humanity, which are legal categories with defined elements; and genocide, which requires proof of intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part. Judicial bodies established that genocide occurred at Srebrenica in July 1995. The same bodies also convicted individuals of serious crimes on several sides of the wars, while recognizing that the scale and organization of violence differed from one campaign to another. Criminal responsibility is individual, and no ethnic or religious community as a whole is responsible for the crimes of those who claimed to act in its name.

The chapter's second major subject is institutional. The Yugoslav wars were the first sustained test of the European Community and the European Union in foreign policy, of United Nations peacekeeping in an unfinished war, of NATO in combat, and of international criminal justice since Nuremberg (Chapter 123). They produced the Dayton settlement, which preserved Bosnia and Herzegovina as a state with two entities, and they produced a very different arrangement for Kosovo under United Nations Security Council Resolution 1244. NATO emerged from these conflicts with new operational experience, new partnerships, and a new Strategic Concept in 1999, while its central commitment to collective defense remained. NATO enlargement, a separate process that proceeded in the same years, belongs to Chapter 136.

The evidence for this history is unusually abundant and unusually contested. Historians draw on judgments, indictments, and exhibits from the ICTY; United Nations reports and Security Council resolutions; NATO and OSCE documents; forensic and demographic studies; contemporary journalism and photographs; the diaries and memoirs of participants; and the testimony of survivors. Each category reflects the purposes of the institution that produced it, and all of them are used in the later political struggles over memory. This chapter treats law, diplomacy, and memory as sources to be analyzed rather than as settled conclusions to be repeated. A linked documentary register at the end of the chapter provides evidence trails for its principal claims and makes the difference between documents, editorial paraphrases, and scholarly interpretations explicit.

Within Part XX, this chapter stands between the institutional story of integration in Chapter 134 and the account of post-communist transition and enlargement in Chapter 136. Within Volume VI, it supplies the most violent counterweight to the narrative of European consolidation after 1989. It does not suggest that integration failed, nor that violence was inevitable. It suggests instead that the post-Cold War order was built in the middle of a war, and that its institutions were shaped by what they could and could not do there.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the federal structure of socialist Yugoslavia, including the six republics, two autonomous provinces, and the constitutional settlement of 1974, and distinguish republics, nations, and nationalities.
  2. Analyze how economic crisis, institutional paralysis, the decline of communist legitimacy, and the political choices of republican elites combined to break the federation apart.
  3. Distinguish the wars in Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kosovo, and North Macedonia by participants, territorial issues, forms of violence, and outcomes.
  4. Describe the Siege of Sarajevo, the campaigns of ethnic cleansing in Bosnia, and the fall of Srebrenica, using the evidence and legal findings that support historical reconstruction.
  5. Differentiate ethnic cleansing, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide, and explain why criminal responsibility is individual rather than collective.
  6. Assess the diplomacy of the European Community and European Union, the mandates and limitations of UNPROFOR, and the reasons peace plans failed before 1995.
  7. Trace NATO's operations in Bosnia, from enforcement of the no-fly zone through Operation Deliberate Force, and explain how they changed alliance practice.
  8. Compare the Dayton settlement for Bosnia with the framework established by Resolution 1244 for Kosovo, and explain why they were not equivalent.
  9. Evaluate competing legal, moral, military, and political arguments about NATO's 1999 air campaign without conflating them.
  10. Interpret primary sources from the United Nations, NATO, the ICTY, and the Dayton negotiations, taking account of institutional purpose and limitation.
  11. Connect the Yugoslav wars to the larger post-Cold War transformation of European security, while distinguishing crisis management from NATO enlargement.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. In what ways was nationalism in Yugoslavia the product of political mobilization rather than an automatic expression of ethnic difference?
  3. Why did the separation of Slovenia lead to a brief conflict while the separations of Croatia and Bosnia and Herzegovina led to long and destructive wars?
  4. How should historians distinguish legitimate claims to self-government from the criminal use of violence against civilians?

  8. How did the Yugoslav wars alter NATO's missions and partnerships while leaving collective defense at the center of the alliance?

Historical Context

The first Yugoslav state was proclaimed on December 1, 1918, as the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, and was renamed Yugoslavia in 1929. It emerged from the collapse of Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman retreat from the Balkans, a settlement examined in Chapter 113, and it joined territories with different legal traditions, religious communities, and levels of economic development. Interwar politics turned on the competing claims of centralism and federalism, on the place of Croatia in the kingdom, and on royal authority. The state was dismembered by Germany, Italy, and their allies in April 1941, as described in Chapter 118.

The Second World War in Yugoslavia combined foreign occupation, revolution, and civil conflict. The Independent State of Croatia, an Axis dependency governed by the Ustaša movement, persecuted and murdered Serbs, Jews, and Roma on a large scale, including at the Jasenovac camp complex (Chapters 120 and 121). Chetnik forces loyal to the exiled royal government committed atrocities against Muslim and Croat civilians, and some Chetnik units collaborated with Axis forces. The communist-led Partisans under Josip Broz Tito built a multiethnic resistance movement that eventually controlled the country, and that also carried out violent reprisals and postwar killings. Estimates of Yugoslav wartime deaths, commonly placed at around one million or somewhat more, remain debated, and the numbers attached to particular camps and massacres became political instruments in later decades. The socialist federation that emerged in 1945 rested on a doctrine of "brotherhood and unity" that discouraged open public argument about these crimes. The memory of the war was therefore never fully worked through, and it remained available for political reuse.

Yugoslavia's position in the Cold War was unusual. Expelled from the Soviet-led Cominform in 1948, Tito's government pursued an independent communist path, introduced workers' self-management, accepted limited Western assistance, and helped found the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961. It was a communist single-party state with a political police and limits on dissent, but it was not a Soviet satellite and not a member of the Warsaw Pact (Chapters 124 and 125). Its citizens traveled abroad and many worked in Western Europe as migrant laborers, a degree of openness unusual in eastern Europe. When the communist regimes of eastern Europe fell in 1989 (Chapter 132), Yugoslavia lost the external balance that had long sustained its distinctive position. At the same moment, the European Community was deepening its own institutions (Chapter 134), and its governments were uncertain whether they were prepared to manage conflict on the continent's southeastern edge.

The evidence for the chapter's earliest sections includes constitutional texts, census returns, party congress records, press coverage, parliamentary debates, memoirs, and, after 1991, diplomatic cables and diaries published by participants. Census categories require particular care. Yugoslav censuses allowed respondents to declare themselves "Yugoslavs," "Muslims" in a national sense (recognized as a category in the early 1970s), or members of other communities, and these declarations changed over time. They measure how people chose to identify, not fixed essences, and the data cannot by themselves predict political behavior.

Section 135.1

The Yugoslav Federation: Peoples, Republics, and the Tito Settlement

Socialist Yugoslavia was organized as a federation of six republics: Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, and Macedonia. Within Serbia lay two autonomous provinces, Vojvodina in the north, with a mixed population that included a large Hungarian minority, and Kosovo in the south, with a population that was overwhelmingly Albanian. The federal capital, Belgrade, was also the capital of Serbia. The constitutional language of the federation distinguished "nations" (narodi), which included Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, Macedonians, Montenegrins, and, from the early 1970s, Muslims in a national sense, from "nationalities" (narodnosti), such as Albanians and Hungarians, whose national homelands lay outside Yugoslavia. The distinction mattered, because the right of self-determination and the formal status of a republic belonged in constitutional theory to nations, not to nationalities.

That structure was not ethnic in a simple sense. The republics did not coincide with the settlement patterns of the peoples for whom they were named. About 12 percent of Croatia's population in 1991 were Serbs, concentrated in several regions along the border with Bosnia and in the east. Bosnia and Herzegovina, in the 1991 census, was home to roughly 44 percent Muslims (the term for Bosniaks used in that census), 31 percent Serbs, and 17 percent Croats, with the remainder including declared Yugoslavs. Large Albanian communities lived in Macedonia and Montenegro as well as Kosovo. Religion (Orthodox, Catholic, Muslim, and others), language (Serbo-Croatian in several regional variants, Slovene, Macedonian, Albanian, Hungarian), republic of residence, and declared nationality overlapped but did not coincide, and many people, especially in cities and in Bosnia, lived in mixed neighborhoods and mixed marriages.

Tito's authority rested on the partisan victory in the Second World War, on a political system run by the League of Communists, and on a series of successful choices in foreign policy. The break with Stalin in 1948 exposed Yugoslavia to Soviet pressure and led the regime to imprison suspected Cominform supporters, notably at the Goli Otok camp, while it also freed the leadership to develop its own model. Workers' self-management, introduced from 1950, gave factory councils a formal role in running enterprises within a market-influenced socialist economy. Nonalignment, expressed in the Belgrade summit of 1961, positioned Yugoslavia as a leader among states that rejected membership in either Cold War bloc. Yugoslavia was therefore not a Soviet satellite, and its relations with both Washington and Moscow were shaped by this independent position (Chapters 124 and 125). Internal liberalizing movements, such as the Croatian Spring of 1971, were suppressed, but the regime responded to them in part by decentralizing power.

The result was the Constitution of 1974, a very long document with more than four hundred articles. It strengthened the republics and gave the autonomous provinces rights close to those of republics: their own constitutions, assemblies, and judiciaries, and representation in federal institutions, while keeping them formally within Serbia. It created a collective state presidency, with one member for each republic and each province, to take effect after Tito. It rested on consensus and delegate procedures that gave many actors a veto. Tito died in Ljubljana on May 4, 1980, three days before his eighty-eighth birthday. The collective presidency, with an annually rotating head, then had to govern without an arbiter in a period of economic strain. The Yugoslav People's Army (JNA), the one institution that presented itself as fully federal, retained special prestige, but its officer corps was disproportionately drawn from Serbs and Montenegrins.

Essential distinction: Yugoslavia was a multiethnic federation, but neither its republics nor its ethnic communities were interchangeable. Where an argument in later sections turns on "Serbia," "Serbs," "the Serbian leadership," or the JNA, it refers to different actors whose interests only sometimes coincided.

Historical Vocabulary
Federalism, Autonomy, and Self-Management

Federalism divides governmental authority between a central government and constituent units. Yugoslav federalism divided power among republics, which possessed constitutional recognition as the homes of nations, and the provinces of Serbia, which were called "autonomous" and held powers that varied across constitutions. Autonomy in this context meant self-government within a larger unit, not a right of secession. Self-management (samoupravljanje) was the doctrine that workers should govern enterprises through elected councils. The terms matter because later disputes, over Kosovo's status, over the right of republics to leave, and over who spoke for Serbs outside Serbia, turned on exactly these constitutional distinctions. They should not be confused with the English word "federation" as used in modern discussions of the United States or Germany, where the constituent units and the sources of legitimacy differ.

Primary Source Window

Constitution of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, 1974, selected federal and autonomy provisions

Creator: The Yugoslav federal and republican political leadership, adopted by the Federal Assembly · Date: Promulgated February 21, 1974 · Type: A constitutional text

Provenance and status: The constitution was passed through the Federal Assembly in January 1974, promulgated on February 21, 1974, and published in the Official Gazette of the SFRY (Službeni list SFRJ). Authentic language editions and later English translations exist; students using an English version should identify the translator. Its legal force ended as the federation dissolved in 1991 and 1992.

Document access: Text (translation; check edition); Archives of Yugoslavia overview. Targeted reading: Articles 1–2, 321 and 327; distinguish enactment from promulgation, and verify translation against the published edition.

Context: The constitution followed the crisis of the Croatian Spring and the demonstrations in Kosovo in 1968, and was intended to answer demands for decentralization while preserving the party's leading role and the federal framework.

Editorial paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation Articles 1–2 describe Yugoslavia as a federal state composed of socialist republics and the Serbian autonomous provinces, constituted on the principle of equality of nations and nationalities. It assigns considerable powers to the republics and to the autonomous provinces, which were described as constituent elements of the federation, and Article 321 provides for a collective presidency with members from the republics and provinces and, at that time, the League of Communists’ president ex officio; Article 327 establishes a one-year presidency chair. It also provides for decisions in many federal bodies by agreement among the units.

Analysis: The document shows that the federation's legal design allowed many units to block decisions, which made decisive federal action difficult when the units disagreed. It also shows the ambiguous status of the provinces, which were part of Serbia but possessed federal representation. It does not by itself show how the system worked in practice, because real authority lay with the party and with personal networks. It also does not reflect the preferences of ordinary citizens, whose views are better studied through surveys, press, and local records.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the relationship among republics, provinces, and the federation?
  2. Who created it, and for what purposes?
  3. What can historians learn about the intended balance between unity and decentralization?
  4. What can historians not establish from a constitutional text alone?
Section 135.2

Economic Crisis, Federal Paralysis, and Competing National Political Movements

Yugoslavia's economy in the 1980s combined a legacy of rapid postwar growth with serious structural weakness. Borrowing from Western banks after the oil shocks of the 1970s left the country with a foreign debt that reached roughly twenty billion US dollars by the early 1980s, and repayment required austerity programs negotiated with the International Monetary Fund. Inflation rose through the decade and exceeded a thousand percent on an annual basis by 1989. Real wages fell, unemployment rose well above ten percent in some years, and shortages of imported goods became common. The self-managed enterprise system and the powers of republican banks and ministries encouraged investment in local projects, duplicated industries, and made a coordinated national response difficult.

Regional inequality sharpened political conflict. Slovenia's output per person was roughly double the federal average, and Croatia's also exceeded it, while Kosovo's was well below half of the average, and Bosnia, Macedonia, and Montenegro also lagged. Richer republics contributed to a federal fund for less developed regions and resented the transfers, while the poorer regions argued that the system had not delivered convergence. Republican leaderships increasingly treated control of revenue, banking, trade barriers, and investment as matters of republic interest. Scandals such as the Agrokomerc affair in Bosnia in 1987 damaged public trust in the party. A federal government under Prime Minister Ante Marković introduced a stabilization program in December 1989 that initially reduced inflation, but it was undermined when Serbia's authorities issued money without federal authorization in late 1990, an act revealed in January 1991, and when republican governments pursued their own priorities.

The legitimacy of the League of Communists declined with the economy. Intellectuals and civic movements raised questions that the party could not contain. In Slovenia, the magazine Mladina and the committee formed in defense of four men tried by a military court in 1988 became a focus of democratic opposition. In Serbia, a draft memorandum by members of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts, leaked in 1986, argued that Serbs were disadvantaged within the federation, and it was criticized by the Serbian party leadership of the time and defended by others. In Croatia and elsewhere, historical grievances about the Second World War and the interwar kingdom reentered public argument. Newspapers, television, publishing houses, and veterans' organizations became arenas in which elites contested the meaning of the federation.

Nationalism in these years was politically mobilized rather than automatic. Public dissatisfaction was broad, and surveys in the late 1980s showed that many citizens still identified with Yugoslavia and were more concerned with living standards than with national questions. Political entrepreneurs used media, party organizations, public rallies, and references to wartime suffering to present the crisis as a national one and to frame neighbors as threats. Which leaders chose such strategies, and how many people responded, varied by republic and by time. Distinguishing broad dissatisfaction from the strategies of particular elites is essential, because the historical question is not whether people had grievances but how those grievances were turned into political programs, and which programs resulted in violence.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Breakup of Yugoslavia?

The question: Was Yugoslavia's dissolution and the violence that accompanied it primarily the result of structural conditions, such as economic crisis and institutional design, or of the decisions of political elites who mobilized national grievances?

Interpretation A: Structural and institutional explanations: Scholars such as Susan Woodward, in Balkan Tragedy, stress the combination of debt and austerity, the weakness of the federal government after 1974, the collapse of communist legitimacy after 1989, and the lack of international attention or assistance to hold the federation together. In this view, republican leaders responded rationally to the breakdown of the system, and the wars followed from the failure to manage the dissolution of a state.
Interpretation B: Elite agency and political leadership: Journalists and historians such as Laura Silber and Allan Little, and scholars such as Sabrina Ramet, emphasize that particular leaders chose nationalist mobilization, used state media, and planned or tolerated armed force, and that alternatives, including negotiated confederation, were available and rejected. The role of Milošević, Tuđman, and the JNA leadership is central to this reading.

A rejected interpretation: A third claim, popular in early 1990s journalism, held that the wars were the eruption of "ancient hatreds." Specialists have overwhelmingly rejected this, because it cannot explain the many decades of coexistence, the high rate of mixed marriages in some regions, the timing of the wars, or the wide variation of violence between regions. It should not be presented as a current scholarly position.

Evidence: Economic statistics, constitutional texts, party records, diaries and memoirs of leaders such as Borisav Jović, public opinion polls, the transcripts and exhibits of ICTY trials, and the documents of international mediators.

Why it matters: If structure dominated, the lessons concern institutional design and international support. If elite choices dominated, the lessons concern accountability and prevention. Most current scholarship combines the two and asks how structural pressures made some choices more attractive, and how particular decisions made violence more or less likely.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this section that supports each interpretation, and explain why neither explanation alone accounts for the difference between Slovenia in 1991 and Bosnia in 1992.

Section 135.3

Milošević, Serbian Politics, Kosovo, and the Crisis of Federal Authority

Kosovo was at the center of Serbian politics and of the federal crisis. Kosovo's population was overwhelmingly Albanian (the 1981 census recorded roughly three quarters, and by the 1990s, with a boycotted census, estimates were around 85 to 90 percent), and it had been the poorest part of the federation. In 1981 Albanian students and workers demonstrated for the status of a republic, and the authorities suppressed the protests. For many Serbs, Kosovo was also the site of medieval monasteries and of the Battle of Kosovo of 1389, a historical memory later invoked in politics. Kosovo Serbs complained of discrimination, pressure, and incidents of violence, while the extent to which emigration was driven by intimidation or by the region's poverty is a matter of continuing study. Albanians, for their part, saw their economic marginalization and the limits on their political rights as the main problems.

Slobodan Milošević, a banker and communist official, rose within the Serbian League of Communists in the mid-1980s. In April 1987, when he visited Kosovo Polje to meet local Serbs, he reportedly told a crowd protesting police action that they would not be beaten, a moment later used extensively in political imagery. In September 1987, at the Eighth Session of the Serbian party's central committee, he defeated his former patron, Ivan Stambolić, and took control of the party. In 1988 and 1989, large rallies, described by supporters as the "anti-bureaucratic revolution," pressured the leaderships of Vojvodina (October 1988) and Montenegro (January 1989) to resign and installed allies. Together with the revised constitutional status of Kosovo and Vojvodina, this gave Serbia, directly or through allies, influence over four of the eight votes in the federal presidency. The leaders of Slovenia and Croatia regarded this as a fundamental change in the federal balance.

Constitutional amendments in March 1989, approved in Kosovo's assembly under pressure and amid strikes and demonstrations in which more than two dozen people were killed, reduced the autonomy of Kosovo and Vojvodina. In June 1989, on the six-hundredth anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo, Milošević addressed hundreds of thousands of people at Gazimestan. In July 1990 the Serbian authorities dissolved Kosovo's assembly after its Albanian delegates declared Kosovo a republic, and a new Serbian constitution in September 1990 formalized the loss of provincial autonomy. Tens of thousands of Albanians lost jobs in public enterprises, schools, and media, and Albanian-language institutions were curtailed. In response, Kosovo Albanians built parallel structures: a shadow government, a school system operating in private homes, and health services, led from 1989 by the Democratic League of Kosovo under Ibrahim Rugova. This background is essential for the later conflict (Section 135.17).

The Serbian leadership's relations with other republics deteriorated. Slovenes held rallies in support of Kosovo Albanians and criticized the use of force in 1989, and in late 1989 Serbia imposed economic measures against Slovenia and prevented a planned Serbian rally in Ljubljana. The Serbian leadership argued for a centralized federation based on one citizen, one vote, which would have favored the largest nation, while Slovenia and Croatia argued for a loose confederation. Historical control: The Serbian state leadership, Serbian political parties, Serb communities in Croatia and Bosnia, and the federal institutions were separate actors. Serbs outside Serbia had their own leaders, whose interests and strategies often overlapped with Belgrade's but were not identical, and the JNA had institutional interests of its own.

Section 135.4

The Breakdown of Federal Politics and the Declarations of Independence, 1990–1991

The federation's last all-Yugoslav political institution failed at the Fourteenth Extraordinary Congress of the League of Communists in January 1990. After the Slovenian delegation's proposals for a confederal reorganization were rejected, the Slovenes left the hall on January 22, and the Croatian delegation then withdrew, ending the party as an effective Yugoslav organization. Multiparty elections followed in the republics: Slovenia and Croatia in April and May 1990, Bosnia and Herzegovina and Macedonia in November and December 1990, and Serbia and Montenegro in December 1990. In Slovenia, a coalition won the legislative elections and Milan Kučan became president; in Croatia, the Croatian Democratic Union (HDZ) under Franjo Tuđman won; in Bosnia, three national parties, the Party of Democratic Action, the Serb Democratic Party, and the Croatian Democratic Union, won most seats; in Serbia and Montenegro, former communist parties led by Milošević and by allies remained in power. A plebiscite in Slovenia on December 23, 1990, brought a large majority for independence, and a Croatian referendum on May 19, 1991, brought a large majority for Croatian sovereignty and independence, but most Serbs in Croatia boycotted it.

Croatia's new constitution, adopted in December 1990, defined Croatia as the national state of the Croatian nation and designated Serbs as a minority rather than, as in the previous constitution, one of the constituent nations. Serb leaders, who had begun to organize politically in the Serb Democratic Party around Knin, declared autonomy for Serb-majority areas and held a referendum on Serb autonomy in 1990. Barricades of logs went up in August 1990, in what became known as the Log Revolution. A series of armed incidents in 1991, at Plitvice in March and Borovo Selo in May, were important steps toward war. The Serbian press and Croatian press presented these events in strongly opposed terms, and in both cases historical references to the Second World War were used to evoke fear.

The conflict over the federation's future ran through the presidency and the army. In 1990 the JNA had disarmed the Territorial Defence forces of Slovenia and Croatia, which had been republican reserve forces, and the two republics rearmed secretly. In March 1991 the military leadership sought a state of emergency from the federal presidency and failed, and in the same month there were large protests in Belgrade. In May 1991 the representatives of Serbia, Montenegro, and their allies blocked the scheduled rotation of the presidency to the Croatian representative, Stjepan Mesić, who took office only in July. The positions of the republics diverged: Slovenia and Croatia proposed a confederation of sovereign states, and, failing that, independence; Serbia and Montenegro favored a federation (or the preservation of Yugoslavia in some form); and Bosnia and Herzegovina and Macedonia, whose populations were mixed and whose leaders feared the consequences of dissolution, proposed an intermediate asymmetric model that found little support.

Slovenia and Croatia declared independence on June 25, 1991. The European Community, whose foreign ministers had hoped to mediate (the Luxembourg foreign minister remarked that it was the "hour of Europe"), sent a delegation of foreign ministers, the Troika, which negotiated the Brioni Agreement of July 7, 1991. It established a three-month suspension of the independence declarations, the return of the JNA to barracks, and a monitoring mission of the European Community. After the moratorium ended in October, Croatia and Slovenia severed constitutional ties on October 8. Macedonia held a referendum on September 8, 1991, and declared independence. The JNA withdrew from Macedonia without conflict by early 1992, although Greek objections to the country's name delayed international recognition and entry to the United Nations under a provisional designation. Bosnia and Herzegovina's path was different and much more dangerous, as examined in Section 135.9. Political dissolution and armed conflict were therefore related but not identical: the end of federal authority did not by itself require war, and the war in each republic began for particular reasons.

Division II · Sections 135.5 to 135.8 · 1991–1998 The Wars in Slovenia and Croatia A short war over borders, a long war over territory, and the first test of international intervention
Section 135.5

Slovenia's Ten-Day War and the First Armed Break with Yugoslavia

Slovenia's armed separation began on the days immediately following the declaration of independence. On June 25 and 26, 1991, Slovenian authorities took control of border crossings with Italy, Austria, and Hungary, and with them the revenue and symbols of sovereignty. The federal government in Belgrade, under Prime Minister Ante Marković and the federal defense leadership, ordered the JNA to secure the border crossings and the airport. On June 27 columns of JNA units left their barracks. Slovenia's Territorial Defence and police, which had planned for this situation, blocked roads, surrounded barracks, and attacked JNA positions at several border posts and around Ljubljana airport. Federal aircraft attacked targets in Slovenia, including the airport and television transmitters.

The fighting lasted about ten days and was limited geographically. Slovenia's terrain, with narrow roads and mountain passes, favored defenders who knew the ground, and its small and dense settlement meant that the JNA could be isolated into small groups. Many JNA conscripts came from other republics and had little motivation to fight, and some surrendered. Commonly cited estimates put the number of dead at roughly sixty to seventy on all sides, including JNA soldiers, Slovenian defenders, and civilians, some of them foreign truck drivers. These numbers are considerably smaller than in the later wars, but they represented real losses, and a brief war cannot be described as an entirely peaceful separation.

Slovenia's situation differed from that of Croatia and Bosnia in ways that explain its short war. The population was almost entirely Slovene, with a very small Serb minority, so no large community disputed the republic's borders. The Slovenian leadership had prepared a political and military strategy and used the media effectively. And, as diary entries published by Borisav Jović, then Serbia's representative in the federal presidency, suggest, Serbia's leaders concluded that they had little stake in keeping Slovenia, and increasingly preferred to concentrate on the question of territories with Serb populations. Historians treat this evidence with care, since it is a participant's account, but it fits other documents. The Brioni Agreement of July 7 provided for a ceasefire and a moratorium, and the federal presidency decided in mid-July that the JNA would leave Slovenia, a withdrawal completed in October 1991.

The consequences were large. Slovenia's de facto independence showed that the federation could not hold a republic that determined to leave, and it showed the Croatian leadership that independence might be achievable. It shifted the debate in Belgrade toward a different question: if Yugoslavia could not be preserved as a whole, which territories and populations would be part of a Serb-led state? This question made the conflict in Croatia more dangerous. The failure to rebuild a common federal political authority also left the JNA without a clear political master, a condition that allowed it to evolve from a federal army into a force that increasingly served the interests of one side.

Section 135.6

Croatia's War: Serbian Rebellion, the JNA, Vukovar, and Dubrovnik

Croatia's war was a conflict over territory and the status of a minority. In the elections of 1990 the Croatian Democratic Union won on a program of national statehood, and its leader, Franjo Tuđman, a former partisan general turned historian and dissident, became president. The government's symbols, personnel changes in the police and public service, and the redefinition of Serbs as a minority alarmed many of the roughly 580,000 Serbs of Croatia. The Serb Democratic Party, first under Jovan Rašković and then under more radical leaders such as Milan Babić, organized the Serb Autonomous Regions of Krajina, in the hinterland of Dalmatia, and later in other areas, and in December 1991 proclaimed the Republic of Serbian Krajina. These leaders received political and material support from Belgrade, and Serbian paramilitary groups from Serbia entered Croatia. The Croatian government armed itself despite an arms embargo imposed in September 1991 and created its own forces.

The JNA's role changed in the course of 1991. At first it positioned itself as a buffer between the sides. During the summer and autumn, as fighting spread, it increasingly supported Serb forces, and by autumn it was attacking Croatian towns. Historians differ about the Serbian leadership's strategic aims: some emphasize the project of a "Greater Serbia," while others emphasize the aim of preserving a Yugoslav state in which all Serbs lived together. Both involved redrawing borders by force and both required the displacement of non-Serb populations from the targeted areas. By the end of 1991, Serb forces and local authorities controlled roughly a quarter to a third of Croatia's territory. Tens of thousands of Croats and others fled or were expelled from these regions, and several hundred thousand people were displaced in total over the year.

The siege of Vukovar, a multiethnic city on the Danube in eastern Slavonia, became the war's emblem. From late August 1991, JNA and Serb irregular forces surrounded the city, which Croatian defenders held for about three months against much better-armed attackers while the city was subjected to heavy bombardment. Vukovar fell on November 18. The following days saw the removal of hundreds of people, wounded and others, from the city's hospital, and the killing of nearly two hundred of them at the Ovčara farm. The ICTY later convicted JNA officers of crimes connected with these events. Much of the population had left or been expelled, and the city was largely destroyed. At the same time Croatian forces committed crimes against Serb civilians in some places, such as Gospić in autumn 1991, and the ICTY and Croatian courts investigated some of these crimes in the years afterward.

The bombardment of Dubrovnik, beginning in October 1991 and most intense on December 6, drew international attention for a different reason. The old city, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1979, was struck by shelling from JNA and Montenegrin positions on the heights above, damaging hundreds of buildings. The ICTY convicted JNA General Pavle Strugar of responsibility for the attack on the Old Town. Dubrovnik showed to European publics the cost of the war to a recognized part of the continent's heritage, and it affected the debate on recognition, as examined in the next section. Evidence for the war includes ICTY judgments and exhibits, Croatian and Serbian documentation centers (with different emphases), humanitarian reports, journalism, and exhumations of mass graves. Casualty estimates for the war overall are on the order of twenty thousand dead, depending on the source and the counting method, and they must be treated as estimates.

Section 135.7

Ceasefires, International Recognition, and United Nations Peacekeeping

European Community mediation began with the Brioni Agreement and continued at a peace conference at The Hague under Lord Carrington from September 1991. A plan he proposed in October, which would have created a loose association of sovereign or autonomous republics with protections for minorities, was rejected by the Serbian leadership. The Security Council adopted Resolution 713 on September 25, 1991, imposing an arms embargo on all of Yugoslavia, a measure that in practice favored the better-armed JNA and Serb forces. The United Nations Secretary-General appointed Cyrus Vance as personal envoy, and after many failed ceasefires, the Sarajevo Agreement of January 2, 1992, produced a ceasefire that largely held in Croatia.

The debate over recognition divided European governments. Germany, under Chancellor Helmut Kohl and Foreign Minister Hans-Dietrich Genscher, pressed for early recognition of Slovenia and Croatia, arguing that recognition would deter further Serbian aggression. Britain, France, the Netherlands, and the UN Secretary-General feared that recognition would worsen the situation in Bosnia, where the population was mixed. The Community adopted guidelines in December 1991 that made recognition conditional on commitments about democracy and minority rights and referred applications to an Arbitration Commission under the French jurist Robert Badinter. The commission concluded that Yugoslavia was in a process of dissolution and that republican borders would become international borders unless otherwise agreed, and it said that Bosnia should hold a referendum. Germany recognized Slovenia and Croatia on December 23, 1991, with effect from January 15, 1992, the date on which the other Community members also recognized them. Bosnia was recognized on April 6 and 7, 1992, and Slovenia, Croatia, and Bosnia entered the United Nations on May 22, 1992. Historians continue to argue about whether recognition accelerated or only reflected events, and the evidence does not support a simple answer.

The Vance Plan, accepted by the Croatian government and the Serbian leadership in Belgrade, provided for the withdrawal of the JNA from Croatia, the demilitarization of areas under Serb control that would be designated United Nations Protected Areas, and the return of displaced persons. The Security Council established UNPROFOR in Resolution 743 on February 21, 1992, with an initial headquarters in Sarajevo and deployment in Croatia that began in March. It was a peacekeeping force, meaning that it relied on the consent of the parties, was lightly armed, and could use force only in self-defense. The plan did not resolve the political status of the territories. Serb leaders in the Krajina were reluctant, the process of disarmament was incomplete, and few displaced people returned.

In May 1992 the Security Council adopted Resolution 757, imposing sanctions on the new Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, proclaimed by Serbia and Montenegro on April 27, 1992, which claimed to be the continuation of the old state, a claim the United Nations did not accept. The sanctions contributed to a severe economic crisis, including one of the worst hyperinflations of the twentieth century in 1993 and 1994, although they were also evaded through smuggling and affected the civilian population broadly. The sanctions were suspended after the Dayton negotiations. Croatia's international recognition did not give its government control over all its claimed territory: in 1992, about a quarter of the country remained outside government control, in areas that UNPROFOR was supposed to protect. The restraint of the force, and the continuing division of Croatia, were important elements of the next phase.

Section 135.8

The Croatian War's Final Phase, Operation Storm, and Displacement

Between 1993 and 1995 the situation in Croatia changed through military reorganization and political choices rather than through the international peace plans. A Croatian operation in January 1993 retook territory near Maslenica, and in September 1993 Croatian forces attacked the Medak Pocket, where they were accused of killing Serb civilians and destroying villages. An international proposal known as the Z-4 plan, prepared during 1994 by representatives of the United States, Russia, the European Union, and the United Nations, was formally presented on January 30, 1995. It offered extensive autonomy for Serb-majority areas within Croatia, but the proposal was not accepted as the basis of a settlement; the Krajina leadership refused to consider it at the time, while Croatia rejected elements it viewed as excessive constraints on sovereignty. The Croatian army, enlarged, equipped, and trained in the meantime, was far stronger than in 1991.

In May 1995, Operation Flash retook Western Slavonia in about two days, and many local Serbs left. The Serb forces responded by firing rockets at Zagreb, killing civilians; Milan Martić, the Krajina leader, was later convicted by the ICTY for the attacks. The decisive operation was Operation Storm, launched on August 4, 1995, after a Croatian–Bosnian agreement in July to coordinate action against Serb forces in the Bihać area. Within a few days, the forces of the self-proclaimed Republic of Serbian Krajina collapsed, in part because their command had weakened and because Belgrade did not intervene. Croatian authorities regained control of most of the territory.

The consequences for civilians were severe. Estimates of the number of Serbs who fled the Krajina during and after the operation range from about 150,000 to 200,000, depending on source and definition. Most fled in columns on the roads toward Bosnia and Serbia, some under artillery fire. The Croatian authorities said that the Serbs had left on the instructions of their own leaders, while Serb and international sources emphasized fear and intimidation. A significant number of Serb civilians, many of them elderly, who remained were killed in the following weeks, and houses were looted and burned in many villages. The ICTY indicted three Croatian generals, and in April 2011 a trial chamber convicted Ante Gotovina and Mladen Markač of crimes including persecution and deportation and found that a joint criminal enterprise existed to remove Serbs by force. In November 2012 the Appeals Chamber, by a majority of three judges to two, reversed the convictions and acquitted both men, concluding that the shelling findings did not meet the standard the trial chamber had used and that the existence of the enterprise had not been established. The judgment was welcomed in Croatia and criticized in Serbia, and it divided international lawyers. It did not question that crimes had occurred, and Croatian courts prosecuted some of them.

Eastern Slavonia, still under Serb control, was handled through negotiation. The Erdut Agreement of November 12, 1995, provided for a transitional period, and the United Nations Transitional Administration for Eastern Slavonia (UNTAES) governed the region from January 1996 until its peaceful reintegration into Croatia in January 1998. It is often considered one of the more successful transitional administrations of the decade. The Serb population of Croatia, about 580,000 in the 1991 census, was only about 200,000, or 4.5 percent, in the 2001 census. The war's outcome, a Croatian military victory, thus coexisted with the permanent loss of homes and communities for many people.

Division III · Sections 135.9 to 135.12 · 1992–1995 Bosnia and Herzegovina: War, Ethnic Cleansing, and Genocide The deadliest of the Yugoslav conflicts and the central test of international responses
Section 135.9

Bosnia's Independence and the Outbreak of a Multisided War

Bosnia and Herzegovina, a former Ottoman territory administered by Austria-Hungary after 1878 and incorporated into the first Yugoslav state in 1918, was the most mixed of the republics. In the 1991 census, of a population of about 4.4 million, roughly 44 percent were Muslims (today called Bosniaks), 31 percent Serbs, 17 percent Croats, and the remainder mainly declared Yugoslavs. Few municipalities were homogeneous, and populations of different communities lived in neighboring villages, in the same towns, and often in the same apartment blocks. The republic had a long history of coexistence, but it also had memories of the Second World War, which were reactivated by politicians in 1990 and 1991. The elections of November 1990 returned a legislature dominated by three national parties: the Party of Democratic Action under Alija Izetbegović, the Serb Democratic Party under Radovan Karadžić, and the Croatian Democratic Union of Bosnia and Herzegovina. They formed a coalition that did not survive the breakup of the federation.

The three parties wanted different outcomes. Bosniak leaders, fearing the consequences of remaining in a rump Yugoslavia dominated by Serbia, sought either a reformed Yugoslavia that included all the republics or, if Slovenia and Croatia left, an independent Bosnia. Serb leaders wanted to remain in Yugoslavia and organized Serb autonomous regions in the autumn of 1991, held a plebiscite in November, and in January 1992 proclaimed a Serb republic within Bosnia (later called Republika Srpska). Croat leaders organized the Croatian Community of Herceg-Bosna in November 1991, and some sought links with Croatia. After Serb deputies walked out of the assembly in October 1991, the Bosniak and Croat majority voted for sovereignty. In early 1992 the European Community's mediator, the Portuguese diplomat José Cutileiro, brought the three parties to an agreement in principle on dividing Bosnia into national cantons, but Izetbegović withdrew his assent, and accounts of the reasons are disputed.

The European Community, following the Badinter Commission's opinion, required a referendum. It was held on February 29 and March 1, 1992. The turnout was about 63 percent, and almost all participants voted for independence, but the Bosnian Serb leadership called on Serbs to boycott it. The first deaths occurred days later, and barricades appeared in Sarajevo. The European Community recognized Bosnia and Herzegovina on April 6, 1992, and the United States followed the next day. Large-scale fighting had already begun, with Bosnian Serb forces and paramilitaries attacking Bijeljina in early April and Bosnian Serb artillery shelling Sarajevo. Mass demonstrations for peace in Sarajevo on April 5 and 6, when sniper fire killed some protesters, are often taken as a symbolic starting point for the siege.

The armed forces were unequal and shifting. The JNA, which still had units and equipment in Bosnia, announced that it was withdrawing in May 1992, but the Bosnian-born personnel and most weapons remained in the territory, and on May 12, 1992, the Bosnian Serb assembly created the Army of Republika Srpska under General Ratko Mladić. It was supported by Serbia with finance, logistics, and personnel, and by paramilitary groups from Serbia. The Bosnian government's army, formed from territorial defense units, police, and volunteers, was poorly armed and constrained by the arms embargo. The Croatian Defence Council (HVO), supported by Croatia, initially fought together with the Bosnian government army against Serb forces, and then fought against it in 1993. By the end of 1992 Bosnian Serb forces controlled about 70 percent of the republic's territory. In May 1992 the Bosnian Serb leadership adopted a set of strategic goals, which the ICTY later used in evidence, that included separation from the other national communities. The conflict thus combined a struggle over the sovereignty of a recognized state and the pursuit of territorial control through violence against civilians.

Geography complicated every political solution. Bosnia is mountainous, with rivers such as the Sava on the north, the Drina on the east, and the Neretva in Herzegovina, and with transport routes that run along valleys. Sarajevo lies in a basin surrounded by hills. The northern Posavina area, around Brčko, formed a narrow link between the Serb-held territories to the west and east. The eastern Drina valley included Bosniak enclaves such as Srebrenica, Žepa, and Goražde, and the Bihać region in the northwest was cut off from the rest of the government-held territory. Neither the republican boundaries nor the settlement patterns produced simple territorial solutions, and every proposed division required moving people.

Section 135.10

The Siege of Sarajevo and the War against Civilian Life

The siege of Sarajevo lasted from April 1992 until the end of February 1996, nearly four years, a period often counted as about 1,425 days and longer than the siege of Leningrad. About 350,000 people were trapped in a city that lay in a valley below hills held by Bosnian Serb forces, and the government-held areas were encircled by Bosnian Serb positions. Artillery, mortars, tanks, and snipers fired from the hills and from buildings. The main boulevard became known as Sniper Alley, and crossing open ground could be fatal. On the worst days, United Nations observers recorded thousands of shell impacts. The siege restricted movement, destroyed buildings, and cut off supplies.

The effects on daily life were severe. Electricity, gas, and running water were repeatedly cut off, and residents gathered wood from parks and furniture to heat homes in winter. Water had to be collected from limited sources, often under fire. Food depended on humanitarian aid brought by convoys and by the airlift, organized by the UN High Commissioner for Refugees from the Sarajevo airport, which was controlled by United Nations forces from mid-1992 and which became the longest humanitarian airlift in history. A tunnel dug beneath the airport runway and opened in 1993 provided a way to move people and supplies between the city and government territory. Hospitals operated with shortages and were hit by shelling. The National Library, in the old city hall (Vijećnica), was destroyed by fire after being shelled in August 1992, with a loss of an estimated 1.5 million volumes and manuscripts.

Attacks on civilians were not incidental. Shells struck markets, queues for bread and water, trams, and funerals. The two best-known attacks on the Markale marketplace occurred in February 1994 and August 1995, killing dozens of people in each case, and the second helped prompt NATO's Operation Deliberate Force. UN and ICTY findings attributed these attacks to Bosnian Serb positions, and claims that Bosnian government forces were responsible were investigated and not supported by those findings. The ICTY convicted two successive commanders of the Sarajevo-Romanija Corps, Stanislav Galić and Dragomir Milošević, of crimes including terror against the civilian population. Research by the Sarajevo Research and Documentation Center counted more than eleven thousand dead during the siege, including more than fifteen hundred children, and tens of thousands wounded, figures that are estimates and are still refined. Bosnian government forces also committed some crimes during the war, and Sarajevo's Serb residents, who numbered in the tens of thousands, experienced the siege as well. These facts do not alter the documented pattern of the siege.

The siege was not only a story of destruction. Residents formed neighborhood groups, repaired buildings, shared resources, and maintained schools in basements, theaters, newspapers such as Oslobođenje, and cultural events. The American writer Susan Sontag directed a production of Waiting for Godot in 1993, and the Sarajevo Film Festival began in 1995. Journalists, aid workers, and United Nations personnel were present in large numbers, and the siege was one of the most heavily reported events of the war, which affected the response of international publics and governments. In historical perspective it was an example of urban warfare in which a modern European capital was bombarded for years, and the extended struggle to preserve ordinary life became part of its significance.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Week in Besieged Sarajevo: Reconstructing Routine from Evidence

This feature does not present an invented family's testimony. It describes patterns that survivors, humanitarian organizations, journalists, and city records document, and asks students to reconstruct a week from those sources.

Documented routines included daily decisions about when to cross open streets, which depended on the position of snipers and the timing of shelling. Water had to be fetched from outdoor points, usually carried by children and adults in containers over long distances. Households often rationed food from humanitarian packages and limited vegetable gardens. People cooked with wood, burned books or furniture in cold winters, and were sometimes without electricity for long periods. Many people went to work or school when they could, and adults managed jobs, volunteer duties, and care for relatives. Sick and wounded people depended on hospitals with shortages.

For each source, identify who produced it, what it does and does not show, and how its purpose shapes its account.

Counting casualties during the siege requires the same discipline as interpreting the shelling evidence. A figure commonly commemorated in Sarajevo is 11,541 killed, while an ICTY-linked demographic study used a different definition and produced a higher total for the siege area. The Sarajevo association commemorating murdered children cites 1,601 children, drawing on named records, but scholars should state the list and period behind any child-casualty total. These figures cannot be compared as though each covered identical populations and intervals. This is a methodological caution, not a reason to minimize the civilian death toll, the systematic danger posed by snipers and shelling, or the destruction of daily life.

Section 135.11

Ethnic Cleansing, Detention Camps, Sexual Violence, and the Fragmentation of Bosnia

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnic Cleansing

Ethnic cleansing describes the removal of a population from a territory through coercion, terror, violence, or expulsion, in order to create an area dominated by another group. The phrase became widely used in reports from Bosnia in 1992. It is a descriptive historical and political term, not a separate offense in the ICTY Statute. The acts that make up ethnic cleansing, such as killing, deportation, and torture, may be prosecuted as war crimes, crimes against humanity, or, if the legal elements are proved, genocide. Genocide requires proof of intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part. Ethnic cleansing is therefore not automatically genocide, and the distinction is important for both law and history.

In 1992 and later, forces fighting for control of territory seized towns and villages and expelled or killed those who did not belong to the dominant group. The pattern was documented most extensively in territories seized by Bosnian Serb forces in northern, eastern, and western Bosnia, where Bosniak and Croat civilians were the main victims. A typical sequence involved military takeover, arrests of local leaders, the disarming and detention of men, the expulsion of the population, looting and burning of houses, and the destruction of mosques and Catholic churches. Prijedor, in northwestern Bosnia, was taken by Serb forces at the end of April 1992, and thousands of non-Serbs were held in camps such as Omarska, Keraterm, and Trnopolje, where killings, beatings, and sexual violence were documented. In Foča and elsewhere in eastern Bosnia, women were held and raped, and the ICTY's judgment in the Kunarac case was the first at the tribunal to convict defendants for rape as a crime against humanity and for enslavement.

Sexual violence was widespread, and the victims were mostly Bosniak women, although Serb and Croat women and men were also victims in other places. Estimates of the number of victims vary widely and cannot be established precisely, because many cases were never reported. Cultural destruction was also systematic. The Ferhadija Mosque in Banja Luka, a sixteenth-century building, was destroyed in 1993, and many other mosques, Catholic churches, and some Orthodox churches were demolished. The shelling of the Oriental Institute in Sarajevo in 1992 destroyed archives. The ICTY investigated crimes committed by forces on different sides. Its Čelebići judgment convicted Bosnian Muslim and Croat officials for crimes against Serb detainees, and cases in the 1990s and 2000s dealt with crimes by Bosnian Croat leaders and some Bosniak commanders. Overall, however, available data show that the majority of civilian victims were Bosniaks, and the major campaigns of forced expulsion were carried out against non-Serb populations.

In 1993 and early 1994, the Bosnian government and Bosnian Croat forces, previously allied, fought one another, in a conflict driven by competing claims to central Bosnia and Herzegovina and by the aims of the Croatian leadership and the Herceg-Bosna authorities. The conflict included the killing of civilians at Ahmići in April 1993, the detention of Bosniaks in camps such as Heliodrom, and the siege of East Mostar. On November 9, 1993, HVO artillery destroyed the Old Bridge. In Washington in March 1994, the United States brokered agreements between the Bosnian government, the Bosnian Croats, and Croatia that ended the fighting and created the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina. The ICTY later convicted senior Bosnian Croat leaders in the Prlić case, whose appeal judgment was delivered in November 2017.

Historians and courts distinguish the patterns of violence from generalizations about communities. The ICTY indicted and convicted individuals from the Serb, Croat, and Bosniak communities, but the largest number of convictions concerned Bosnian Serb forces, reflecting the larger scale of their documented campaigns. Equating all armed campaigns, however, obscures differences in scale, organization, and intent, and the claim that one community was collectively guilty obscures the individuals who committed, resisted, or protected victims from crimes. The evidence for the period includes survivor testimony, reports of United Nations rapporteurs, forensic investigations of mass graves, and, from 1995 onward, judicial records. The United Nations General Assembly in December 1992 called ethnic cleansing a form of genocide in a resolution, while the courts later applied the narrower legal definition.

Section 135.12

Srebrenica, July 1995: The Fall of a UN Safe Area and Genocide

Srebrenica, a small town in eastern Bosnia near the Drina River, had about 37,000 residents in its municipality before the war, a majority of them Bosniaks. Serb forces took it in April 1992, and Bosniak forces under Naser Orić recaptured it and the surrounding area in the following months. Raids from the enclave on Serb villages killed civilians and were used in Bosnian Serb propaganda, and Orić was later tried by the ICTY, convicted at trial in 2006 of failing to prevent the mistreatment of Serb prisoners, and acquitted on appeal in 2008. By early 1993, the enclave was crowded with refugees from the surrounding area, and its population, estimated at perhaps 40,000 or more, depended on international aid. After a Bosnian Serb offensive in spring 1993, the Security Council in Resolution 819 on April 16 demanded that Srebrenica be treated as a safe area, and in Resolution 824 in May extended the designation to five other places. UNPROFOR troops were deployed, later from a Dutch battalion, under rules that allowed limited use of force. The enclave was supposed to be demilitarized, but arms were not fully removed, and the Bosnian Serb army deliberately restricted convoys. In March 1995, Karadžić issued a directive that, as ICTY judgments found, ordered operations to create conditions of insecurity and hopelessness for the inhabitants.

On July 6, 1995, units of the Bosnian Serb Army's Drina Corps began an attack. Dutch troops had limited numbers and were unable to stop it, requests for NATO air support were delayed by command procedures, and limited air strikes on July 11 did not halt the advance. General Mladić entered the town on July 11. Between 20,000 and 25,000 residents, mostly women, children, and elderly people, gathered at the UN compound at Potočari. Over July 12 and 13, buses and trucks took them out, and Bosnian Serb soldiers separated and detained men and boys, including some who had come to the compound, beating and killing some. At the same time a column of perhaps 12,000 to 15,000 men and boys tried to reach government territory through the forests toward Tuzla, and it was attacked and ambushed. Thousands were captured or surrendered, and from July 13 to approximately July 16 and afterward they were executed at places including a warehouse at Kravica, schools and fields near Orahovac, the Branjevo military farm, and the dam at Petkovci. Subsequently, Bosnian Serb units dug up the primary graves and moved the bodies to secondary sites in the autumn, in order to conceal the crimes.

The killing was investigated extensively. ICTY teams exhumed mass graves, the International Commission on Missing Persons developed DNA identification techniques, survivors testified, and intercepted communications and aerial images were used. A figure of approximately eight thousand men and boys killed is accepted by the ICTY and the International Court of Justice, and the identification of remains has continued for decades. In its 2001 trial judgment and its appeal judgment in April 2004, the ICTY found that genocide had occurred, and the trial judgment convicted General Radislav Krstić as a participant in genocide. Crucially, the Appeals Chamber in April 2004 confirmed that genocide occurred but revised Krstić’s individual responsibility to aiding and abetting genocide and imposed a 35-year sentence. The ICJ found in its judgment of February 2007 that genocide occurred at Srebrenica, that Serbia had not committed genocide itself or been complicit but had violated its obligation to prevent it and to punish those responsible. The Karadžić and Mladić trials resulted in convictions for genocide at Srebrenica, as examined in Section 135.16. These are established judicial findings, and they are distinct from the question of which persons and states were legally responsible. Responsibility lies with the individuals who planned and carried out the killings, not with a community as a whole.

Srebrenica also became a symbol of international failure. The 2002 report commissioned by the Dutch government led to the resignation of the Dutch cabinet in April 2002. In May 2024 the United Nations General Assembly designated July 11 as an annual day of reflection and commemoration of the 1995 genocide at Srebrenica, a resolution that some governments opposed, and public denial or minimization of the genocide by some political figures continues to be a source of tension. The Potočari Memorial Center, near the former UN compound, holds the graves of those identified, who are buried each July 11.

Cause and Effect

Srebrenica and the Failure of International Protection

Long-term causes

The Bosnian Serb leadership's strategy of territorial control and ethnic separation, the isolation of the eastern enclaves, and the international community's reluctance to commit enough force to defend the places it designated as safe.

Short-term causes

The 1995 directive to squeeze the enclave, the limited strength and mandate of the Dutch battalion, the slowness of the combined UN and NATO command procedures, and the decision of the Bosnian Serb leadership to take the enclave.

Event

The fall of the enclave on July 11, 1995, the removal of women and children, and the organized killing of approximately eight thousand men and boys.

Immediate effects

Mass displacement, the fall of Žepa, a sharp change in international attitudes, and increased willingness to use NATO air power and diplomatic pressure in the following weeks.

Long-term consequences: Judicial findings of genocide, criminal trials of political and military leaders, a decades-long process of identifying victims, political controversies about commemoration and denial, and a continuing debate about the responsibility to protect civilians.

Historical complexity: The failure to protect the enclave arose from mandates, resources, and decisions by international bodies, but those failures did not cause the killings. The responsibility for the crimes lies with those who planned and committed them, and the events cannot be reduced to a single cause.

Division III outcome: The Bosnian war was the deadliest of the Yugoslav conflicts. Its course was shaped by the combination of territorial warfare, expulsions, genocide, international hesitation, and the eventual use of force, and the next division examines how these factors led to the Dayton settlement.

Judicial records: The 2004 Krstić appeal and ICJ Bosnia v. Serbia case record establish distinct individual and state-law findings. Neither supports attributing responsibility to an ethnic population as a whole.

Section 135.13

The United Nations, European Diplomacy, and the Limits of Peacekeeping

The international response to the Bosnian war involved many institutions with different mandates. The European Community, whose members were also expanding their cooperation through the Maastricht Treaty (Chapter 134), led early mediation, convening the London Conference in August 1992 jointly with the United Nations and then the International Conference on the former Yugoslavia, chaired by Cyrus Vance and Lord Owen, and later by Owen and Thorvald Stoltenberg. The Conference on Security and Cooperation in Europe, which became the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) in 1995, played a secondary role. The Western European Union and NATO conducted naval monitoring of the embargo in the Adriatic. The United Nations provided humanitarian relief through the High Commissioner for Refugees and deployed UNPROFOR, whose mission was extended from Croatia to Bosnia and Herzegovina in 1992, first to protect the airport at Sarajevo and then to support humanitarian convoys.

UNPROFOR's difficulties were structural. A peacekeeping force depends on the consent of the parties, on impartiality, and on the use of force only for self-defense, and these principles worked poorly when one party sought to expel or kill civilians. Its mandates were limited, and troop contributors, many of them European, were reluctant to risk casualties. The Security Council created six safe areas in 1993 (Resolutions 819 and 824) and authorized UNPROFOR, in Resolution 836, to use force in self-defense, including to deter attacks on them, and invited member states to provide air support. The Secretary-General estimated that tens of thousands of additional troops would be needed to defend the safe areas, and the Council approved far fewer, so the designation promised more than the force could provide. The "dual key" arrangement, which required both UN and NATO approval for air strikes, delayed decisions. In May and June 1995, after NATO air strikes near Pale, Bosnian Serb forces took hundreds of UN personnel hostage.

The great powers disagreed on strategy. The United States, then under the Clinton administration, favored lifting the arms embargo on the Bosnian government and using air strikes, an approach called "lift and strike." The British and French governments, whose troops were on the ground as peacekeepers, feared that it would endanger them and widen the war. Russia, with historic ties to Serbia, resisted measures that appeared to target it. The Contact Group created in 1994, composed of the United States, Russia, Britain, France, and Germany, tried to coordinate positions. Peace plans failed for similar reasons. The Vance–Owen plan of January 1993 would have divided Bosnia into ten largely autonomous provinces and was rejected by the Bosnian Serb assembly in May 1993 after its leader had signed it. The Owen–Stoltenberg plan of August 1993 proposed a union of three republics, and was rejected by the Bosnian government's side. A Contact Group plan in 1994, which would have divided territory roughly 51 to 49 between a Federation and a Serb entity, was rejected by the Bosnian Serbs. Each plan reflected the parties' territorial claims, and none came with a credible commitment to enforce it.

As reports of camps, expulsions, and siege warfare appeared, the criticism of the international response grew sharply, within governments and in public opinion. Some officials and analysts argued that the arms embargo and the neutrality of the peacekeepers favored the better-armed aggressor. Others argued that stronger intervention would have prolonged the war and endangered the peacekeepers. These debates, which are part of a larger historiography of "humanitarian intervention," became more intense after Srebrenica, and they shaped the decisions of 1995 described in the next section.

Section 135.14

NATO's Military Intervention in Bosnia and the Transformation of Alliance Operations

NATO was created in 1949 to deter and, if necessary, defeat a Soviet attack on its members (Chapter 125). Its Article 5 committed members to treat an armed attack on one as an attack on all. After the revolutions of 1989 and the collapse of the Soviet Union (Chapter 132), the alliance reconsidered its purpose. Its Strategic Concept of November 1991, adopted in Rome, kept collective defense at the center but emphasized crisis management, dialogue, and cooperation with former adversaries, and the North Atlantic Cooperation Council was established the same year. In 1992 NATO ministers agreed that the alliance could support peacekeeping under the authority of the CSCE or the United Nations. Yugoslavia was thus the setting in which these new ideas were first applied.

NATO's operations in Bosnia developed gradually. From July 1992 NATO ships conducted Operation Maritime Monitor in the Adriatic, initially supervising the UN sanctions; Operation Maritime Guard began enforcing restrictions in November 1992, and joint NATO–Western European Union enforcement under Operation Sharp Guard began on June 15, 1993. These were successive missions, not a single operation running from 1992. The Security Council banned military flights over Bosnia in Resolution 781 in October 1992 and authorized enforcement in Resolution 816 in March 1993, and NATO's Operation Deny Flight began in April 1993. On February 28, 1994, NATO aircraft shot down four Bosnian Serb aircraft near Banja Luka in the alliance's first combat engagement in its history. After the February 1994 Markale marketplace attack, NATO issued an ultimatum, and Bosnian Serb heavy weapons were withdrawn from around Sarajevo or placed under UN control. NATO aircraft also conducted limited strikes near Goražde in April 1994 and elsewhere, at the request of UN commanders. The coordination between the alliance and the United Nations was imperfect, and the two institutions had different cultures and objectives.

After the fall of Srebrenica and Žepa in July 1995, the situation changed. A conference in London in July 1995 agreed on a stronger response to further attacks on safe areas, a Rapid Reaction Force of British, French, and Dutch troops was deployed near Sarajevo, and the UN withdrew personnel from vulnerable positions. On August 28, 1995, a second shell struck the Markale market, and on August 30 NATO began Operation Deliberate Force, which continued with a pause until September 20. NATO aircraft flew more than three thousand sorties against Bosnian Serb command, air defense, communications, and ammunition targets, and French, British, and other forces fired artillery from the Rapid Reaction Force. The operation ended when Bosnian Serb forces agreed to withdraw heavy weapons from the exclusion zone around Sarajevo.

Historians differ about the weight of air power in producing the settlement. Many accounts stress that it combined with the Croatian and Bosnian ground offensives of August and September 1995, which shifted territory, with the exhaustion of the Bosnian Serb army, and with the diplomacy led by the American envoy Richard Holbrooke and with Belgrade's willingness to negotiate on behalf of the Bosnian Serbs. NATO's own account, drawing on its documents and operational history, describes Bosnia as the first step in its transformation into a crisis-management organization. Critical distinction: NATO's operations in Bosnia were linked to arrangements established by UN Security Council resolutions, including those concerning the no-fly zone and the safe areas. The legal controversy surrounding NATO's Kosovo air campaign in 1999 was different, as Section 135.18 explains.

Section 135.15

The Dayton Peace Agreement and the Reconstruction of Bosnia

By autumn 1995, the military and diplomatic conditions had changed. Croatian and Bosnian government forces had taken territory in western Bosnia, the Bosnian Serbs had lost ground and were under air attack, and Belgrade, weakened by sanctions, wanted a settlement. Under American leadership, with participation by the European Union envoy Carl Bildt and by Russia and other members of the Contact Group, negotiators agreed in September on basic principles, including a single Bosnia and Herzegovina composed of two entities with a rough 51 to 49 division of territory. A ceasefire took effect in October. Negotiations at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base near Dayton, Ohio, began on November 1, 1995, among Slobodan Milošević (who represented the Bosnian Serbs), Franjo Tuđman, and Alija Izetbegović. The agreement was initialed on November 21, 1995, and formally signed in Paris on December 14, 1995. The two events should be distinguished.

The General Framework Agreement and its eleven annexes preserved Bosnia and Herzegovina as a sovereign state composed of two entities, the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, which was itself divided into cantons, and Republika Srpska. It did not partition Bosnia into independent states. Annex 4 was the constitution: it created a state-level presidency with three members (a Bosniak, a Croat, and a Serb), a bicameral parliamentary assembly, a Council of Ministers, and a Constitutional Court, and left many powers with the entities. Annex 2 defined the inter-entity boundary line, Annex 3 provided for elections, Annex 6 for human rights, Annex 7 for the right of refugees and displaced persons to return, Annex 10 for a High Representative to oversee civilian implementation, and Annex 11 for an International Police Task Force. The status of Brčko was left to arbitration, and in 1999 an arbitral award created a self-governing district. Critics argue that the constitution entrenched ethnic categories, and in 2009 the European Court of Human Rights held in the Sejdić and Finci case that excluding citizens who were not members of the three constituent peoples from the presidency and upper house violated the European Convention.

The military annex authorized a NATO-led Implementation Force (IFOR), which deployed in December 1995 with about 60,000 troops, including contingents from non-NATO states such as Russia, and which took over from UNPROFOR. In December 1996, IFOR was replaced by the smaller Stabilisation Force (SFOR), which remained until December 2004, when a European Union force, EUFOR Althea, took over. Civilian implementation involved the Office of the High Representative, the OSCE, which organized elections, the United Nations, and many agencies, and in 1997 the Peace Implementation Council gave the High Representative powers, known as the Bonn powers, to remove officials and impose legislation. Elections held in 1996 and afterward returned nationalist parties in many areas. Refugee returns were slow at first and then increased, with hundreds of thousands of people returning in the following decade, though many did not or could not. Property restitution, reconstruction, the search for missing persons, and political reconciliation all remained difficult.

Section 135.16

The ICTY, War Crimes Prosecutions, and the Limits of International Justice

The Security Council established the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia by Resolution 827 on May 25, 1993, after a Commission of Experts created in 1992 documented violations and the Secretary-General reported on a proposed statute. The tribunal sat in The Hague. Its jurisdiction covered grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions, violations of the laws or customs of war, genocide, and crimes against humanity committed in the territory of the former Yugoslavia since January 1, 1991. It prosecuted individuals, not states or organizations, and it could impose prison sentences, not the death penalty. Its prosecutors included Richard Goldstone, Louise Arbour, and Carla Del Ponte. The ICTY closed in 2017, with remaining functions transferred to the International Residual Mechanism for Criminal Tribunals. At the ICTY’s closure in December 2017, its statistics recorded 161 individuals indicted and 90 sentenced; subsequent official case statistics, incorporating later residual proceedings, record 93 sentenced. These are different reporting dates, not competing estimates. Other defendants were acquitted, had cases referred to national courts, or died before proceedings could be concluded.

The distinction among legal categories is essential. War crimes are serious violations of the laws and customs of war, such as attacks on civilians and the mistreatment of prisoners. Crimes against humanity are specified acts, such as murder, deportation, and persecution, committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population. Genocide is a specified act committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part. Individual responsibility can arise through direct commission, planning, ordering, aiding and abetting, participation in a joint criminal enterprise, or command responsibility for failing to prevent or punish subordinates' crimes. The tribunal developed the law in these areas, including in cases on sexual violence, and heard thousands of witnesses and received a vast documentary record, including military records, intercepts, and forensic evidence from mass graves.

The most notable cases concerned senior leaders. Slobodan Milošević was indicted in 1999 while serving as president of Yugoslavia, arrested in 2001 after he lost power, and transferred to The Hague, where his trial began in 2002. He died in his cell on March 11, 2006, before a verdict, and the proceedings ended without judgment. Radovan Karadžić, arrested in 2008, was convicted in 2016 of genocide at Srebrenica and other crimes and sentenced to forty years, and in 2019 the Residual Mechanism's Appeals Chamber increased the sentence to life imprisonment. Ratko Mladić, arrested in 2011, was convicted in 2017 of genocide at Srebrenica, crimes against humanity, and war crimes, and sentenced to life, and the judgment was upheld on appeal in June 2021. Both were acquitted of genocide charges relating to municipalities in 1992, while convicted of other crimes there. Other prominent cases ended differently. The Appeals Chamber acquitted Croatian generals Gotovina and Markač in 2012, acquitted the Yugoslav army's chief of staff Momčilo Perišić in 2013, and the tribunal acquitted Ramush Haradinaj of Kosovo, and others, after trials. Each outcome must be described exactly, distinguishing indictment, conviction, acquittal, and proceedings terminated without judgment.

The tribunal has been praised for establishing facts and for helping to end impunity, and criticized for the length of its proceedings, its cost, perceived inconsistencies in its judgments, and its distance from the societies affected. Its findings have been used and contested in the politics of memory. Some nationalist narratives reject the judgments, while others select the findings that suit their positions. National courts, such as the State Court of Bosnia and Herzegovina and war crimes chambers in Croatia and Serbia, also prosecuted many cases, and the tribunal transferred some to them. The International Court of Justice performed a different function from the ICTY: it considered state responsibility in the case brought by Bosnia and Herzegovina against Serbia and Montenegro, and not individual criminal responsibility. A court can establish legal responsibility, but it cannot by itself resolve a political dispute or produce reconciliation.

Section 135.17

Kosovo: Autonomy, Political Resistance, and Armed Conflict, 1989–1999

Kosovo's status was different from that of the republics that declared independence in 1991 and 1992. Under the 1974 Constitution it was an autonomous province of Serbia and a constituent element of the federation, but it was not a republic, and Albanians were classed as a nationality, not a nation. Kosovo Albanians argued that the loss of autonomy in 1989 (Section 135.3) was unconstitutional and that the population's right to self-determination was being denied. Serbian authorities argued that Kosovo was an integral part of Serbia and that the provincial autonomy had been a concession, not a right of statehood. This difference explains why the international community, which recognized the republics within their federal borders on the basis of the Badinter Commission's opinions, did not recognize an independent Kosovo in the 1990s, and why the issue was omitted from the Dayton negotiations, a fact that damaged the credibility of nonviolent politics among Kosovo Albanians.

In the 1990s the dominant Albanian political response was nonviolent. The Democratic League of Kosovo, led by the writer Ibrahim Rugova, built a parallel system of governance, with unofficial elections in 1992 in which Rugova was chosen president of a self-declared republic, a school system, and a health service financed in part by taxes from the diaspora. It sought international recognition and avoided open confrontation. By the mid-1990s, many younger Albanians were skeptical of this approach, and armed groups appeared. The Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA, in Albanian UÇK) claimed its first attacks on Serbian police in the mid-1990s, and its strength grew after the collapse of the Albanian state in 1997, when weapons became widely available from looted depots, and with support from diaspora communities.

Armed conflict escalated in 1998. After Serbian special police attacks in the Drenica region in early March, including the killing of the KLA commander Adem Jashari and many members of his family at Prekaz, support for the KLA increased. During the summer and autumn, Serbian and Yugoslav forces conducted offensives against villages in areas where the KLA was active, using heavy weapons against settlements and driving out civilians, while the KLA attacked police, Serb civilians, and Albanians it considered collaborators, and kidnapped people. The UN estimated that between 200,000 and 300,000 people were displaced by the autumn. The Security Council adopted Resolution 1160 in March 1998 (an arms embargo), Resolution 1199 in September, and Resolution 1203 in October, demanding a ceasefire and withdrawal, without authorizing force. In October 1998 the American envoy Richard Holbrooke reached an agreement with Milošević that provided for withdrawals and for an unarmed verification mission of the OSCE, the Kosovo Verification Mission, of roughly 1,300 to 1,400 monitors, while NATO authorized, but did not launch, air strikes.

The truce broke down. On January 15, 1999, the bodies of 45 Kosovo Albanians were found near the village of Račak, and international monitors and forensic experts concluded that most had been killed by Serbian security forces, a finding that Serbian authorities disputed at the time and that the ICTY later accepted in the Milutinović trial judgment. The Contact Group convened negotiations in February at Rambouillet, France, and in March in Paris. The proposed interim agreement envisaged substantial self-government in Kosovo for three years, with a later mechanism to determine its final status, and a NATO-led force. The Kosovo Albanian delegation, including KLA representatives, signed on March 18. The Serbian delegation objected to the international force and to a military annex that would have given NATO personnel broad access in Yugoslavia, and the Serbian assembly rejected foreign troops while expressing willingness to discuss autonomy. The OSCE monitors were withdrawn on March 20.

Section 135.18

NATO's Kosovo Air Campaign, 1999: Humanitarian Intervention and International Law

On March 23, 1999, NATO Secretary General Javier Solana announced that he had directed the alliance's military commander, General Wesley Clark, to begin air operations, and on March 24 Operation Allied Force began. It lasted 78 days, until June 10. NATO's nineteen members supported the operation, though with different degrees of enthusiasm, and Greece and Italy, among others, had reservations. NATO's stated objectives, restated at its Washington summit in April, were a verifiable end to violence and repression in Kosovo, the withdrawal of Yugoslav military, police, and paramilitary forces, the stationing of an international military presence, the unconditional return of refugees and access for humanitarian aid, and a political framework based on the Rambouillet agreement. NATO leaders had expected that a short campaign would bring the Yugoslav leadership back to the table. It did not.

The campaign was conducted by air, because the alliance did not want to send ground forces, and over time grew from attacks on air defenses and military targets to include infrastructure such as bridges, power facilities, fuel depots, and the state television building in Belgrade, where sixteen people were killed. NATO's aircraft flew more than 38,000 sorties. The alliance reported no combat deaths among its aircrews, though it lost aircraft. At the same time, Serbian and Yugoslav forces carried out a campaign of expulsion and killing against Kosovo Albanians, which sharply intensified after the bombing began. UNHCR counted roughly 850,000 refugees outside Kosovo by June, mostly in Albania, Macedonia, and Montenegro, and several hundred thousand more were displaced within Kosovo. Villages were burned, men were killed in massacres, identity documents were destroyed, and bodies were later moved, as shown by the discovery of mass graves in Serbia itself, including at Batajnica near Belgrade. The KLA was also accused of abuses, including against Serb and Roma civilians and some Albanians, and proceedings against some of its former members continued afterward.

NATO's own conduct was investigated. Its aircraft struck civilian targets in several incidents, including a convoy of Albanian refugees near Đakovica in April, a passenger train on a bridge at Grdelica in April, and the Chinese embassy in Belgrade in May, which NATO said was a targeting error based on outdated maps. Human Rights Watch documented about five hundred civilian deaths from NATO strikes, a figure which is an estimate based on investigations of specific incidents, and Yugoslav authorities gave higher numbers. The prosecutor of the ICTY, after a committee review in 2000, decided not to open an investigation into NATO's conduct. The use of cluster munitions and the targeting of civilian infrastructure and media remained contested.

On June 3, Yugoslavia accepted a peace plan negotiated with the participation of Russian and Finnish envoys, and the Military Technical Agreement was signed at Kumanovo, in Macedonia, on June 9. The Security Council adopted Resolution 1244 on June 10, 1999, and NATO suspended its air operations. The reasons for Milošević's decision to accept are debated: they included the damage from the air campaign, the loss of Russian diplomatic support, the prospect of a ground invasion, and domestic pressure. The legal question was different. The operation did not have an explicit Security Council authorization to use force. Russia and China opposed it, and a Russian draft resolution condemning it was defeated on March 26. Mandatory legal distinction: Resolution 1244, adopted after the campaign, authorized an international civil and security presence and did not give prior authorization to the bombing.

Historical Vocabulary
Humanitarian Intervention

Humanitarian intervention refers to the use of military force by a state or group of states in another state, without that state's consent, to stop or prevent widespread suffering or killing of civilians. The UN Charter generally prohibits the use of force against another state, except in self-defense or when authorized by the Security Council under Chapter VII. Debates after 1999 about whether a doctrine of humanitarian intervention exists outside those exceptions led to the development of the concept of a "responsibility to protect," endorsed in a limited form at the UN World Summit in 2005. The term should not be treated as a legal doctrine that was settled in 1999, because states disagreed about it then and still do.

Historical Debate

Was NATO's 1999 Intervention Legal, Legitimate, or Both?

The question: Did NATO's air campaign against Yugoslavia, conducted without explicit Security Council authorization, violate the UN Charter, and if so, could it nevertheless be justified as necessary to prevent atrocities?

Interpretation A: The campaign was necessary and justified: Supporters argued that Serbian and Yugoslav forces had already displaced hundreds of thousands in 1998, that the Security Council had identified a threat to peace in Resolutions 1199 and 1203, that diplomacy had failed, and that Russia's and China's opposition blocked authorization. Many NATO governments described the action as a response to an imminent humanitarian catastrophe and as necessary to protect people, and some of them made clear that it should not become a precedent.
Interpretation B: The campaign violated the Charter: Critics, including Russia, China, and many legal scholars, argued that the Charter permits force only in self-defense or with Council authorization, that no such authorization existed, and that allowing a regional alliance to decide for itself would weaken the legal order. Some also argued that the campaign worsened the humanitarian situation in the short term and caused civilian casualties.

A middle position: An independent commission led by Richard Goldstone, reporting in 2000, described the intervention as "illegal but legitimate," meaning that it breached formal legal rules while being morally defensible, and proposed criteria for future cases. The International Court of Justice dismissed Yugoslavia's cases against NATO members in 2004 for lack of jurisdiction, so it did not decide the question.

Evidence: Security Council resolutions and debates, NATO statements, the reports of the OSCE mission and UNHCR, ICTY judgments about Serbian forces' conduct, data on civilian casualties from NATO strikes, and legal scholarship.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians interpret NATO's role, the development of the responsibility to protect, and later arguments about intervention. Legal, moral, military, and political judgments are different questions. An action may be considered legitimate and illegal, or lawful and militarily ineffective.

Student task: Separate the legal, moral, military, and political questions in this debate, and identify evidence for each. Explain which additional evidence would most help assess whether the campaign prevented or intensified atrocities.

Section 135.19

Resolution 1244, KFOR, Postwar Kosovo, and the Macedonian Crisis of 2001

Resolution 1244, adopted on June 10, 1999, by fourteen votes with China abstaining, acting under Chapter VII, authorized an international civil presence and an international security presence in Kosovo. The Yugoslav and Serbian military, police, and paramilitary forces withdrew in accordance with the Military Technical Agreement of June 9, and the NATO-led Kosovo Force (KFOR), which entered on June 12 with tens of thousands of troops, took responsibility for security. A Russian contingent had moved to Priština airport on the same day, a tense moment resolved by negotiation. The United Nations Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo (UNMIK) governed the territory under a Special Representative, Bernard Kouchner, with the OSCE responsible for institution-building and the European Union for reconstruction. The resolution reaffirmed the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia while providing for substantial autonomy and self-government in Kosovo pending a political settlement, so it did not itself decide Kosovo's final status.

Security was a central problem. Within weeks, hundreds of thousands of Kosovo Albanian refugees returned, many to destroyed homes. At the same time, Serbs, Roma, and other minorities faced reprisals, kidnappings, and killings, and a large number of them left, with estimates of around 200,000 non-Albanians displaced. Roma were often accused of collaboration, and Roma neighborhoods, such as the one in southern Mitrovica, were destroyed. Mitrovica became divided along the Ibar River. KFOR and the UN police worked to protect minorities with limited success, and in March 2004 widespread anti-minority violence and associated clashes caused nineteen deaths, according to the OSCE and Human Rights Watch, eight Kosovo Serbs and eleven Kosovo Albanians, while attacking Serb and other minority communities and damaging churches and homes. Missing persons were a long-term issue, with thousands unaccounted for in 1999 and more than a thousand still missing decades later. The mix of international administration, local institutions, and unresolved status complicated the establishment of courts, property rights, and criminal accountability.

Armed conflict also spread across the border. In the Preševo Valley in southern Serbia an Albanian armed group fought in 2000 and 2001 and was ended by negotiation. In Macedonia, an Albanian armed group calling itself the National Liberation Army began attacks in early 2001, in the Tetovo area, claiming that Albanians, who made up roughly a quarter of the population, were denied equal rights. The government used its security forces in response, and civilians were harmed and displaced on both sides. With mediation by the European Union and NATO and the United States, the leaders of the main parties signed the Ohrid Framework Agreement on August 13, 2001. It provided for amendments to the constitution, wider use of the Albanian language, increased representation in public institutions, and local government reform, in exchange for disarmament. NATO's Operation Essential Harvest in August and September 2001 collected about 3,800 weapons, and later NATO and European Union missions followed.

Kosovo's status remained disputed. After international negotiations led by Martti Ahtisaari failed to produce an agreed settlement, Kosovo's assembly declared independence on February 17, 2008. A large number of states recognized it, while Serbia, Russia, China, and several European Union members, including Spain, Greece, Cyprus, Romania, and Slovakia, did not. The International Court of Justice, in an advisory opinion in July 2010, concluded that the declaration did not violate international law, without deciding whether Kosovo had become a state. Relations between Belgrade and Priština were later addressed in European Union-facilitated talks. KFOR's continuing mission is based on Resolution 1244. Detailed treatment of those relations is reserved for later chapters.

Section 135.20

The Yugoslav Wars, NATO, Sovereignty, and Europe's Post-Cold War Security Order: Historical Synthesis

The collapse of Yugoslavia differed from the largely peaceful transformations of most other east European states in 1989 to 1991 (Chapter 132). Czechoslovakia divided peacefully, effective January 1, 1993, and the Soviet Union dissolved without a general war among its republics, though it experienced conflicts at its edges. Yugoslavia was a multiethnic federation with mixed populations in several republics, a strong army whose leadership became a political actor, leaders who chose to mobilize national grievances, disputed borders that did not match the distribution of populations, and a federal system that had no mechanism for orderly separation. Slovenia, with a homogeneous population, left after a short war. In Croatia and Bosnia, the questions of territory and of the status of minorities produced long wars. In Kosovo, the issue was the denial of autonomy and the suppression of political opposition. In Macedonia, the settlement came by political agreement after a short armed conflict.

The European response to the early conflicts showed the limits of cooperation. The European Community in 1991 and 1992 had no common military capacity, its members disagreed about recognition, and the Common Foreign and Security Policy created by Maastricht (Chapter 134) was new. The experience contributed to later efforts, in the late 1990s, to create a European security and defense policy, though those developments belong to later chapters. The United Nations committed forces under mandates that could not protect civilians in safe areas, and the United States, hesitant until 1995, became decisive in the diplomatic endgame. By 1999, the NATO allies acted together without a Security Council mandate, and the legitimacy of that action remained contested.

NATO's role changed significantly. Its Strategic Concept of 1991 placed new emphasis on crisis management and partnership, and the alliance created the Partnership for Peace in January 1994 to build cooperation with non-members. In Bosnia it conducted its first combat operations and then its first large ground deployment, IFOR, and in Kosovo it fought its first sustained air war and deployed KFOR. The Strategic Concept adopted at the Washington summit in April 1999 recognized non-Article 5 crisis response operations as an alliance task, while reaffirming collective defense as its core purpose. NATO's crisis-management operations were different from its enlargement, which brought Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic into the alliance in March 1999, in the middle of the Kosovo crisis, and which is examined in Chapter 136.

The wars also advanced international criminal justice, produced the Dayton settlement and the Resolution 1244 framework, and revived debates about the balance of sovereignty, self-determination, and civilian protection. Sovereignty was invoked by states resisting intervention, self-determination by communities seeking independence or autonomy, and humanitarian responsibility by those seeking to protect civilians. The conflicts showed that these principles could conflict, and that an international order could be built on the end of the Cold War without settling how to reconcile them. The settlements themselves were also very different. Dayton preserved a single state with two entities, while Resolution 1244 created a temporary administration whose political outcome was left open.

Continuity and Change Over Time

NATO from 1949 to the Post-Cold War Period

What Changed

  • NATO moved from preparing to deter a Soviet attack to taking part in combat, peace-support, and crisis-management operations in the Balkans.
  • It developed partnership programs, such as Partnership for Peace, and worked with non-members, including Russia, in operations.
  • It acted outside the territory of its members and, in 1999, without an explicit Security Council authorization.

  • Article 5 collective defense remained the alliance's central commitment.
  • Decisions required consensus among members, and American military capacity remained crucial.
  • Political questions about the relationship with Russia and the alliance's boundaries continued, to be examined in Chapter 136.

Who experienced change most directly: Military planners, peacekeeping troops, and the populations of Bosnia and Kosovo experienced the new operations most directly. Why the pattern matters: It shows that institutions can adopt new functions without abandoning their original purpose, and that the legitimacy of those functions was contested.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the breakup of Yugoslavia produced distinct armed conflicts that tested European security institutions, international humanitarian law, and the meaning of sovereignty after the Cold War. Yugoslavia was a multiethnic federation of six republics and two autonomous provinces, held together by a party, an army, a founding wartime narrative, and the personal authority of Josip Broz Tito. It was not a Soviet satellite, and its independent position between the blocs gave it a distinctive international role, which the end of the Cold War removed.

The constitutional settlement of 1974 decentralized authority and gave republics and provinces many powers of veto, which left the federal system dependent on consensus. After Tito's death in 1980, collective leadership faced a severe economic crisis marked by debt, inflation, unemployment, and regional inequality. These conditions weakened the legitimacy of the League of Communists and encouraged republican leaderships to defend their own interests. The economic crisis did not make war inevitable, but it created the conditions in which political movements could present national grievances as the solution to a general crisis.

Political choices turned the crisis into a struggle over the state. Slobodan Milošević's consolidation of power in Serbia, the reduction of Kosovo's autonomy in 1989, the collapse of the League of Communists in 1990, the multiparty elections won by national parties, the blocking of the federal presidency, the rearming of republican forces, and the unilateral declarations of independence on June 25, 1991, all reflected decisions by identifiable actors. Historians debate the relative weight of structural conditions and elite agency, and have rejected the claim that the wars were the product of ancient hatreds.

The wars were separate. Slovenia's brief war in 1991 ended with the JNA's withdrawal, in part because Slovenia was almost ethnically homogeneous and Serbia's leaders had little interest in keeping it. In Croatia, a Serb rebellion, supported by Belgrade and the JNA, led to the siege of Vukovar, the bombardment of Dubrovnik, and the displacement of many people, and the war ended with Croatian offensives in 1995, the exodus of Serb civilians, and the peaceful reintegration of Eastern Slavonia. The international community recognized Croatia in 1992 without that recognition giving its government control of its territory.

The Bosnian war, which began in April 1992 after a referendum and international recognition, was the deadliest. It combined a struggle over the sovereignty of a recognized state with campaigns of territorial control, ethnic cleansing, detention camps, sexual violence, and the destruction of cultural heritage. The siege of Sarajevo lasted nearly four years, and the conflict between Bosnian Croat and Bosniak forces in 1993 and 1994 was ended by the Washington Agreement. Documented crimes were committed by forces on several sides, but the major campaigns of forced expulsion and the largest numbers of civilian victims were associated with attacks on Bosniak and Croat populations by Bosnian Serb forces. Ethnic cleansing is not in itself genocide.

At Srebrenica in July 1995, the Bosnian Serb Army killed approximately eight thousand Bosniak men and boys and forcibly removed the town's women, children, and elderly. The ICTY and the International Court of Justice found that genocide occurred. Criminal responsibility is individual: it lies with those who planned and carried out the crimes, and not with a community. The fall of the enclave also exposed the limits of a safe-area policy that had promised protection without providing the forces to defend it.

International responses to the wars involved the European Community, the United Nations, NATO, the OSCE, and others. European diplomacy produced the Brioni Agreement and the Badinter opinions, but could not stop the fighting. UNPROFOR operated under mandates that assumed the consent of the parties and could not protect civilians. Peace plans failed because they could not be enforced. In 1995, a combination of Croatian and Bosnian ground offensives, NATO's Operation Deliberate Force, the revulsion after Srebrenica and the second Markale attack, and American diplomacy led to the Dayton Agreement, initialed on November 21 and signed in Paris on December 14. It preserved Bosnia and Herzegovina as one state with two entities and created a heavy international presence.

The ICTY, created in 1993, prosecuted individuals for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide, and helped establish the historical record through thousands of witnesses and extensive documents. Its record included convictions of leaders such as Karadžić and Mladić, the death of Milošević before a verdict, and acquittals such as those of Gotovina and Markač on appeal. It could not produce reconciliation, and its findings remain contested in political memory. The ICJ's different role in considering state responsibility should be distinguished from that of the ICTY.

In Kosovo, the loss of autonomy in 1989, a decade of nonviolent Albanian resistance, the emergence of the KLA, and Serbian counterinsurgency led to a humanitarian emergency in 1998 and 1999. After the failure of the Rambouillet talks, NATO began Operation Allied Force on March 24, 1999, without explicit Security Council authorization, and the campaign lasted 78 days. It was accompanied by the mass expulsion of Kosovo Albanians by Serbian and Yugoslav forces, and it caused civilian casualties and damage to infrastructure. Resolution 1244, adopted afterward, created a UN administration and KFOR, and did not retroactively authorize the bombing. The legal, moral, military, and political questions surrounding the campaign are different and remain debated.

The conflicts transformed NATO. The alliance, whose 1991 Strategic Concept had already stressed crisis management and partnership, conducted its first combat operations in Bosnia, deployed IFOR and SFOR, fought the air campaign in Kosovo, and deployed KFOR, while its 1999 Strategic Concept confirmed collective defense as its central purpose. These developments are distinct from NATO enlargement, which is examined in Chapter 136. The Yugoslav wars show that the end of the Cold War changed Europe's security institutions without ending violent conflict, and that the post-Cold War order was shaped by what institutions could and could not do in the face of mass violence.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Federation of republics
The constitutional structure of socialist Yugoslavia, with six republics and two autonomous provinces, in which republics were recognized as the homes of nations.
Autonomous province
A unit of Serbia, either Kosovo or Vojvodina, that had its own institutions and federal representation but was not a republic.
Nations and nationalities
The Yugoslav constitutional distinction between peoples with a republic as a homeland and peoples, such as Albanians and Hungarians, whose national states lay elsewhere.
Self-management
The Yugoslav doctrine and system in which workers' councils participated in managing enterprises.
Nonalignment
Yugoslavia's Cold War policy of not joining either bloc, which distinguished it from the Soviet satellites.
League of Communists of Yugoslavia
The ruling party, organized through republican branches, whose collapse in January 1990 ended all-Yugoslav political organization.
Yugoslav People's Army (JNA)
The federal armed forces, whose role changed from federal institution to the military support of one side in the wars.
Territorial Defence
The republican reserve forces that Slovenia and Croatia used as a basis for their defense in 1991.
Brioni Agreement
The July 7, 1991, agreement mediated by the European Community that suspended the independence declarations for three months.
Badinter Commission
The Arbitration Commission of the Conference on Yugoslavia, which advised on recognition and held that Yugoslavia was dissolving.
UNPROFOR
The United Nations Protection Force, created in 1992 and extended to Bosnia, with limited mandates.
Safe area
A UN-designated zone, such as Srebrenica, that was meant to be protected from attack, but without forces sufficient to defend it.
Ethnic cleansing
The forcible removal of a population from a territory through coercion, terror, violence, or expulsion, which is a descriptive term and not a separate offense in the ICTY Statute.
Genocide
Specified acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part.
Crimes against humanity
Acts such as murder, deportation, and persecution committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population.
Command responsibility
Criminal liability of a superior who knew or had reason to know of subordinates' crimes and failed to prevent or punish them.
Joint criminal enterprise
A mode of liability used by the ICTY for participation in a common criminal plan.
Operation Storm
The Croatian offensive of August 1995 that retook most of the Krajina and led to the flight of many Serbs.
Washington Agreement
The March 1994 agreement that ended the Bosniak–Croat war and created the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina.
Operation Deliberate Force
NATO's air campaign against Bosnian Serb forces from August 30 to September 20, 1995.
Dayton Agreement
The General Framework Agreement for Peace in Bosnia and Herzegovina, initialed on November 21 and signed on December 14, 1995.
Entity
One of the two constituent units of postwar Bosnia and Herzegovina, the Federation and Republika Srpska.
High Representative
The international official responsible for civilian implementation of the Dayton Agreement, with powers expanded in 1997.
IFOR and SFOR
The NATO-led forces that implemented the military aspects of Dayton from 1995 and 1996, until the EU's EUFOR Althea replaced SFOR in 2004.
ICTY
The tribunal established by Resolution 827 to prosecute serious violations of humanitarian law in the former Yugoslavia since 1991.
Kosovo Liberation Army
The Albanian armed organization that emerged in the late 1990s and fought Serbian and Yugoslav forces.
Operation Allied Force
NATO's air campaign against Yugoslavia, March 24 to June 10, 1999, conducted without explicit Security Council authorization.
Resolution 1244
The June 10, 1999 Security Council resolution establishing UN administration and an international security presence in Kosovo.
Ohrid Framework Agreement
The August 2001 political agreement that ended the armed conflict in Macedonia and expanded minority rights.
Strategic Concept
NATO's public statement of its purposes, revised in 1991 and 1999, which reflected the move toward crisis management.
Humanitarian intervention
The use of force to prevent mass suffering in another state without that state's consent, a concept whose legality is contested.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the post-Cold War European order was built during a war and was shaped by what its institutions could and could not do. The same years saw the Maastricht Treaty, the creation of the European Union, and the opening of eastern Europe to the West, and also the siege of Sarajevo, the genocide at Srebrenica, and mass expulsions in Croatia, Bosnia, and Kosovo. A history of the period that considers only integration, or only violence, would be incomplete.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. The collapse of a multiethnic federal state, the mobilization of national identity by political elites, the use of violence to change borders and populations, and the role of international institutions as both actors and limits are themes that connect to the collapse of empires after the First World War (Chapter 112) and to the population movements after the Second World War (Chapter 123). It differs from those earlier episodes in the presence of international courts, television coverage, and a continuing international presence that persisted after the fighting.

The consequences extended beyond the 1990s. NATO's operational experience, the development of international criminal justice, the debate over humanitarian intervention and the responsibility to protect, the unresolved status of Kosovo, and the postwar constitutional arrangements of Bosnia and Herzegovina all remained subjects of controversy. The migration of refugees across Europe, a subject for Chapter 137, and the eastward expansion of NATO and the European Union, examined in Chapter 136, were connected to these events, though they had their own causes. The security tensions of the twenty-first century, examined in Chapter 139, also draw on the memory of these wars.

For Volume VI, the chapter contributes a necessary counterweight to narratives of steady consolidation. It reminds students that contemporary Europe is a historically contingent political order, formed by decisions and failures, and that the memory of the Yugoslav wars is still contested by the societies that lived through them. Understanding them requires attention to evidence, to the differences among the conflicts, and to the responsibility of individuals, and it requires resisting both ethnic generalization and the temptation to treat the outcomes as predetermined.
