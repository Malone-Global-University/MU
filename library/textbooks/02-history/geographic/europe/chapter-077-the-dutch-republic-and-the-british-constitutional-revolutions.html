The Dutch Republic and the British Constitutional Revolutions

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 1581 the representatives of a group of northern provinces declared that their prince had forfeited his right to rule them. In 1649 a court sitting in London condemned its own king to death. In 1688 a Dutch army landed in England and a reigning monarch left his kingdom. Each event was a different answer to a single question: who has the authority to govern, and on what terms?

Historians often group these events together because they occurred within little more than a century and involved two neighboring societies connected by trade, religion, war, and dynastic marriage. The grouping is useful but dangerous. It can suggest that the Dutch Republic and the Stuart kingdoms were two stages in a single march toward representative government, or that both were early drafts of the liberal democracies of later centuries. Neither was true. The Dutch Republic was governed by urban oligarchies, provincial estates, and a confederal assembly whose members carried instructions from their home provinces, while a princely house, the House of Orange-Nassau, repeatedly claimed leadership. The Stuart kingdoms passed through monarchy, civil war, regicide, republic, military rule, restoration, and a second change of dynasty, and the settlement that emerged after 1688 kept the monarchy, an established church, and a narrow political nation.

This chapter therefore begins from a comparative problem rather than from a presumed destination. Why did these two polities, which resisted or rejected the model of centralized royal authority associated with Louis XIV (examined in Chapter 76), arrive at such different constitutional arrangements? How did religion, commercial wealth, taxation, military organization, and political bargaining shape what each government could do? And how did the two systems become entangled, through the Anglo-Dutch Wars, the marriage of William of Orange and Mary Stuart, and the Dutch expedition to England in 1688, so that the history of one cannot be understood without the other?

The chapter's evidence is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Dutch historians can use the resolutions of the States General and the provincial estates, municipal records, notarial and commercial archives, church registers, pamphlets, paintings, and the records of the chartered companies. British and Irish historians can use parliamentary journals, statutes, state papers, assize and quarter-sessions records, parish registers, newsbooks and pamphlets, diaries, army records, and the extraordinary notes taken at Putney in 1647. Almost every document, however, was produced to argue a case. Pamphlets praised or denounced regents, stadtholders, kings, and generals, and later generations remembered these struggles through national myths that the chapter will repeatedly try to set aside.

The chapter also insists on a vocabulary of precision. Before 1707 England and Scotland were separate kingdoms that shared a monarch from 1603, and Ireland was a third kingdom with its own parliament and a population divided by religion and by centuries of conquest and settlement. The Dutch Republic was not a nation-state in the later sense. Its sovereignty was claimed by provinces, which acted together through the States General. The terms stadtholder, regent, prerogative, privilege, and constitutional monarchy all have specific meanings that differ from modern popular usage, and three of them receive dedicated Historical Vocabulary boxes.

Within Part XII, this chapter occupies a particular place. Chapter 74 introduced the early modern state in general terms, Chapter 75 examined the dispersed Habsburg inheritance from which the Dutch Republic broke away, and Chapter 76 examined French royal power. Chapter 77 now examines two alternatives to that French model, a confederal republic and a parliamentary monarchy, and asks how each combined fiscal strength with limits on rulers. Chapter 78 will turn to Austria, Prussia, and central Europe, and Chapter 80 will place both Dutch and British power within the eighteenth-century balance of power.

Within Volume III, the chapter marks a transition. The Volume began with Renaissance cities and religious reformation and has moved through overseas empire and dynastic states. Here the themes of religion, commerce, empire, and state building converge on questions of political authority. At the same time, the chapter prepares the Volume's successor, Volume IV, in which the Enlightenment, the Atlantic revolutions, and the French Revolution would draw selectively on the memory of 1581, 1649, and 1688. Those later uses of the past were not the same as what seventeenth-century actors intended, and a recurring task of this chapter is to distinguish the two.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the transformation of resistance to Habsburg rule into a confederal republic, from the provincial grievances of the 1560s to the Act of Abjuration of 1581 and the recognition of independence in 1648.
  2. Explain how the Dutch Republic was governed, distinguishing provincial sovereignty, municipal regent oligarchy, the States General, the stadtholderate, and the office of Grand Pensionary.
  3. Analyze the relationship among Dutch commerce, public finance, religious diversity, and political authority, including the contradictions created by overseas commerce and coerced labor.
  4. Assess the crises of 1618–1619, 1650, and 1672 as conflicts between competing claims of provincial, regent, and Orangist authority.
  5. Distinguish the personal union of the Stuart kingdoms from a unified state, and explain why governing England, Scotland, and Ireland created recurring political and religious crises.
  6. Evaluate competing explanations for the British Civil Wars, connecting fiscal, constitutional, religious, and inter-kingdom causes without reducing them to one.
  7. Compare the political programs of Parliament, the New Model Army, Presbyterians, Independents, and Levellers, using the Putney Debates as evidence.
  8. Interpret the Commonwealth, Protectorate, and Restoration as successive attempts to define legitimate authority, and explain why neither republic nor restored monarchy resolved the underlying disputes.
  9. Assess the constitutional settlement of 1688–1689 and its different consequences in England, Scotland, and Ireland, including the limits of toleration and political rights.
  10. Connect public credit, taxation, and war finance to the military and commercial capacities of the Dutch Republic and post-1688 Britain.
  11. Compare the Dutch and British cases and explain why limits on rulers must be distinguished from political equality.
  12. Interpret primary documents from both polities as arguments produced by particular groups for particular purposes.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a revolt against a distant ruler become the foundation of a republic that lacked a monarch, and why did the Dutch provinces not simply choose another prince?
  2. In what sense was the Dutch Republic a republic, and in what senses did oligarchy, provincial particularism, and Orangist loyalty qualify that description?
  3. How did commercial wealth and public credit translate into political and military power, and what tensions did that wealth create within the Republic?
  4. Why did conflicts over taxation, religion, and prerogative escalate into civil war in the Stuart kingdoms, and what role did Scotland and Ireland play in that escalation?
  5. What did the successive regimes of 1649 to 1660 reveal about the difference between constitutional theory and the practical foundations of authority?
  6. Was 1688–1689 a revolution, a dynastic coup, a foreign invasion, a constitutional settlement, or all of these, and for whom?
  7. How did the Dutch and British political systems influence one another through trade, warfare, dynastic connection, and finance?
  8. Why should historians separate the limitation of rulers from the extension of political rights, and what exclusions persisted in both polities?

Historical Context

By the middle of the sixteenth century the Low Countries were among the most urbanized and commercially active regions of Europe. Seventeen provinces, differing in language, law, and institutions, had been assembled by the Valois dukes of Burgundy and the Habsburgs, and Charles V, as examined in Chapter 75, had transmitted them to his son Philip II of Spain in 1555 and 1556. Each province kept its own estates, its own privileges, and its own customs, and cities such as Antwerp, Ghent, Bruges, and Amsterdam defended charters that limited what a ruler could demand. Philip II's government, with its taxation, its pursuit of heresy, and its attempts at administrative centralization, collided with these arrangements. The ensuing revolt, whose military course belongs to Chapter 66, is the starting point of the Dutch story in this chapter.

The British Isles in 1603 were also composed of distinct legal and political entities. England and Wales shared one parliament and legal system, Scotland had its own parliament, church, and law, and Ireland, nominally a kingdom attached to the English crown, had its own parliament dominated by a Protestant minority while most inhabitants were Catholic or, in the case of Ulster Scots, Presbyterian. The accession of the Scottish king James VI to the English throne as James I created a union of crowns, not of states. Governing three kingdoms with separate churches and institutions would prove far harder than governing any one, and the later crisis cannot be explained without that structural fact.

Both societies were shaped by the confessional politics of the Reformation, examined in Part X. The Dutch Reformed Church, shaped by Calvinist theology, became the privileged public church of the Republic, but it never encompassed the whole population. In England, the Elizabethan settlement had produced a Protestant national church with bishops and a prayer book, criticized by Puritans who wanted further reform and by Catholics who rejected it altogether. In Scotland, the Reformation produced a Presbyterian church that valued assemblies over bishops. These differences meant that religious policy was never simply a matter of doctrine; it was a matter of who governed the church, who paid for it, and whether the crown could impose uniformity.

Finally, both societies faced the pressures of war and fiscal-military competition described in Chapter 74. Armies grew, fortifications became more elaborate, and navies became expensive. States that could not raise money by consent had to find other expedients, such as sales of office, forced loans, or reliance on bankers. The Dutch Republic solved the problem through provincial taxation, urban credit, and the commercial capital of Amsterdam. The Stuart monarchy, which collected relatively little from its subjects by European standards and depended on Parliament for extraordinary taxation, struggled to do so before 1640. Later regimes, from the Commonwealth to the post-1688 monarchy, found different solutions. The fiscal history is not a background to the political history; in large part it is the political history.

Students should also note a technical point about dates. In the seventeenth century England and Scotland used the Julian calendar (Old Style), which ran ten days behind the Gregorian calendar by the 1600s, while most of the Dutch Republic, including Holland and Zeeland, had adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1582. William of Orange's landing at Torbay on 5 November 1688 (Old Style) was 15 November in the Dutch reckoning. This chapter gives English and Scottish dates in Old Style, with the year taken as beginning on 1 January, and notes the difference where it matters. Monetary units also differ: Dutch figures appear in guilders, English figures in pounds sterling, and the two cannot be combined without explicit conversion and context.

Section 77.1

The Habsburg Netherlands, Provincial Liberties, and the Origins of Resistance

The Low Countries that Philip II inherited in 1555–1556 were not a state but a collection of lordships, duchies, counties, and cities, brought under Burgundian and then Habsburg rule by dynastic marriage, purchase, and conquest. Their wealth was extraordinary. Antwerp was the leading commercial and financial center of northwestern Europe in the mid-sixteenth century, the Flemish and Brabantine cities produced textiles, and the northern maritime provinces, especially Holland and Zeeland, dominated Baltic shipping and herring fishing. For a ruler seeking revenue for the wars described in Chapter 75, the region was among the most valuable parts of the Habsburg inheritance.

That wealth rested on the protection of local rights. Each province had an assembly of estates, usually composed of nobles, clergy, and towns, which had to consent to taxes. Cities held charters that exempted citizens from outside jurisdiction and regulated trade. Rulers swore to respect these privileges at their formal inauguration, and the language of privilege and contract formed the ordinary vocabulary of political bargaining. Resistance in the 1560s, and even in the early 1570s, was largely expressed as defense of ancient liberties against innovation, not as a demand for a new political order.

Several grievances converged. Philip II's government in Brussels, under his regent Margaret of Parma and her adviser Cardinal Granvelle, sought tighter administrative coordination and a reorganization of bishoprics, which threatened noble and urban influence over church appointments. Taxation to support Spanish wars fell heavily on commercial economies already strained by harvest failures and trade disruption in the mid-1560s. The persecution of heresy, carried out through inquisitorial edicts, alarmed Catholics as well as Protestants who feared its effect on trade and civil order. Calvinist congregations grew rapidly in the southern and northern cities alike.

Loyalties were not fixed. High nobles such as William of Orange and the counts of Egmont and Horn initially sought to reform royal policy from within the Council of State, not to break with the king. Many urban elites were Catholic, and many Protestants were not Calvinists. Mennonites, Lutherans, and Calvinists disagreed about doctrine and about resistance. The Revolt did not begin as a unified national movement, and no modern Dutch or Belgian identity then existed. The later division between a Protestant north and a Catholic south was the outcome of the war, not a boundary present in 1566.

Historians reconstruct these origins from the correspondence of the regent and Philip II, the records of the provincial estates and city councils, the confessions and consistory records of Reformed congregations, inquisitorial trial records, and a large body of pamphlets. Each type of source reflects a perspective. Royal correspondence emphasizes order and heresy, noble petitions emphasize liberties, and Calvinist records emphasize persecution, so a balanced account must set them against one another.

Section 77.2

The Dutch Revolt, William of Orange, and the Struggle for Political Authority

In August 1566 attacks on church images, known as the Iconoclastic Fury, spread across the Low Countries, beginning in Flanders and reaching Antwerp and many northern towns. Image-breaking was carried out by organized groups, often Calvinist in inspiration, and was widely condemned by both Catholics and moderate Protestants, but it convinced Philip II that his Netherlandish government could not maintain order. He sent the Duke of Alba with an army in 1567. Alba established the Council of Troubles, which tried thousands of people, executed Egmont and Horn in 1568, and imposed new taxes, including a tenth penny on sales that broke the principles of consent by estates.

William of Orange, a high noble with estates in the Low Countries and the principality of Orange in southern France, went into exile in Germany. His early military efforts failed, but resistance revived in 1572 when the Sea Beggars, privateers who sailed under Orange's authority, seized the port of Brill on 1 April. Towns in Holland and Zeeland, angered by Alba's taxation and encouraged by the weakness of the garrisons, declared for Orange. In July 1572 the estates of Holland met at Dordrecht and recognized William as stadtholder, or lieutenant, of the province. The act was significant because it was an act of provincial authority, not a decision of an abstract nation. The cities and nobles of Holland were claiming the right to appoint their own executive.

Spanish military pressure, financial collapse, and mutinies by unpaid soldiers transformed the situation. After Spanish troops sacked Antwerp in November 1576, in an episode remembered as the Spanish Fury, delegates from the provinces concluded the Pacification of Ghent, which sought to end the fighting, remove foreign troops, and suspend persecutory edicts. For a moment it appeared that the seventeen provinces might unite against what they saw as military misrule. William of Orange, whose objective was a coalition of provinces tolerant enough to embrace Catholics and Protestants, worked to maintain that unity.

It did not last. The Calvinist regimes that took control of some cities, such as Ghent, alarmed Catholic nobles and cities in the south and east. Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, who became governor-general in 1578, exploited these divisions by offering reconciliation to the southern nobility. Orange's project of a broad and religiously mixed union was undermined by confessional hostility and by competing local interests. The conflicts of these years show that political authority in the Revolt was contested not only between rebels and the Habsburgs but among the rebels themselves.

Section 77.3

The Union of Utrecht, 1579, and the Formation of a Political Confederation

The Union of Utrecht, signed on 23 January 1579, was concluded by provinces that did not wish to rely on the fragile unity of the Pacification of Ghent after the southern reconciliation with Parma. The original signatories were Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, and Gelderland, together with the Ommelanden, the rural districts around Groningen; Groningen city itself did not join as an original signatory; Friesland and Overijssel joined in the following months, and several cities in Flanders and Brabant also adhered, but most were later reconquered. The Union was therefore a defensive treaty among particular provinces and cities, not a founding act for a unified state.

Its provisions are best read as a compact for mutual defense and common finance that preserved provincial authority. The signatories agreed to act as if they were one province for purposes of defense, to share the costs through common taxes and levies, and to refrain from making separate treaties or alliances with foreign powers without common consent. Decisions on war, peace, and taxation required unanimity or agreement among the provinces, rules that would later make federal decision-making slow but also protected each province from being outvoted on its vital interests. Other matters, such as local government and religious arrangements, stayed with each province.

The religious provision is especially important and frequently misunderstood. The Union allowed Holland and Zeeland to maintain their existing arrangements, which favored the Reformed Church, and left other provinces free to adopt their own arrangements consistent with the Religious Peace. It also stated that individuals should not be persecuted for their religion, a principle often described as freedom of conscience. This provision should not be read as modern religious equality. It did not guarantee public worship for every confession, and in practice Catholic worship was progressively restricted as the Reformed Church became the public church of the Republic. The Union expressed a political desire to avoid inquisition and persecution while accepting that public religion would remain under provincial control.

The Union of Utrecht became in later centuries a quasi-constitutional text, regularly cited in disputes about provincial and federal powers. It is important, however, to avoid reading it as a drafted constitution. It was a treaty among sovereign or semi-sovereign entities written in the middle of a war, and it contained gaps and ambiguities that later political practice filled. The most significant ambiguity concerned where sovereignty lay, a question that the Act of Abjuration and later practice would answer in favor of the provinces.

Section 77.4

The Act of Abjuration, 1581, and the Rejection of Philip II

In 1580 Philip II issued a ban against William of Orange, effectively outlawing him and offering a reward for his death. Orange responded with a printed Apology, and the States General, dominated by the northern provinces and a few southern allies, moved toward formally renouncing Philip. On 26 July 1581 they issued the Act of Abjuration, declaring that Philip had forfeited his sovereignty over the provinces. The act did not proclaim a republic. It declared the king deposed and left open the question of who should govern.

The act's argument was legal and political. It maintained that a prince is appointed by God to protect his subjects, and that when a prince oppresses them, violates their rights, and treats them as slaves, they may abandon him and choose another. The statement drew on medieval ideas of contractual kingship, on the practice of inaugural oaths, and on the argument that Philip had broken the privileges he swore to uphold. The provinces thus justified their action in terms of restoring legitimate government, not of creating something new. The act circulated widely in print, in several languages, as part of an effort to win legitimacy from Protestant and other European powers.

Having abjured Philip, the provinces looked for another prince. The States General invited François, Duke of Anjou, the brother of Henry III of France, who accepted sovereignty in 1581 but proved a disastrous ruler; his attempt to seize Antwerp in January 1583, known as the French Fury, ended his authority. William of Orange was assassinated in Delft on 10 July 1584 by Balthasar Gérard, a Catholic acting on Philip's ban. The provinces then offered sovereignty to Henry III of France, who declined, and to Elizabeth I of England, who refused the crown but agreed in the Treaty of Nonsuch in 1585 to send troops under the Earl of Leicester.

The Leicester episode (1585–1587) is revealing. Leicester accepted a title as governor-general, but he alienated the regents of Holland by favoring Calvinist and popular factions and by trying to centralize authority. His failure showed that the province of Holland, led by the Land's Advocate Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, would not accept an outside ruler who threatened its autonomy. By default, the provinces became a republic in which sovereignty was exercised by provincial estates and their delegates in the States General. Historians such as the Dutch constitutional scholars of the later twentieth century stress that this outcome was less a planned republican design than the result of the failure of alternatives.

Section 77.5

The Consolidation of the United Provinces, 1588–1648

After the departure of Leicester, the northern provinces entered a phase of consolidation in which the States General, the Council of State, and provincial estates assumed practical sovereignty. Under Oldenbarnevelt's political leadership and the military leadership of Maurice of Nassau, William's son, who became stadtholder of Holland and Zeeland in 1585 and later of other provinces, the Republic reorganized its army, took the fortified towns of the eastern and southern frontier, and stabilized its borders. The military reforms associated with Maurice and his cousin William Louis, including drill, standardized training, and fortification, are examined in Chapter 66 and Chapter 74; here the point is that the Republic could maintain a disciplined professional army financed by taxation and credit.

Spain's own financial crises and its commitments elsewhere made a negotiated pause attractive. The Twelve Years' Truce, concluded in April 1609, recognized the Republic's de facto independence for the duration and was followed by conflict over the practical meaning of this independence, including trade with the Indies and Reformed worship. When the Truce expired in 1621, war resumed as part of the wider struggles of the Thirty Years' War (Chapter 67), in which the Republic's naval strength and commercial interests were prominent. By the 1640s, both sides were exhausted; Spain was fighting France and revolts in Catalonia and Portugal, while the Republic's regents feared that Frederick Henry's military successes would strengthen the House of Orange more than their own provinces.

The Peace of Münster, signed on 30 January 1648 and ratified in May of that year, recognized the United Provinces as a free and sovereign state and was concluded separately from the wider settlement at Westphalia. It settled borders, trade questions, and the status of the Generality Lands. It also confirmed the closure of the Scheldt to ocean-going traffic, an outcome that favored Dutch ports and would damage Antwerp and the southern Netherlands for generations. The peace secured diplomatic recognition, but it also removed the immediate military threat that had justified Orange leadership, so that tension between regents and the stadtholder would intensify in the following years.

The consolidation of the Republic shows that political forms often follow from circumstance. Dutch republican institutions were practical arrangements for managing a war in which no prince had succeeded in uniting the provinces. Only gradually did writers, preachers, and artists begin to describe the Republic as a distinctive form of political life. The invention of that tradition, with its heroic accounts of the Revolt, belongs to the seventeenth-century culture examined later in this chapter.

Section 77.6

The Seven Provinces, Provincial Sovereignty, and the Constitutional Structure of the Republic

The Republic comprised seven provinces: Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, Overijssel, Friesland, and Groningen. Drenthe, a sparsely populated territory, was associated with the Republic but had a more limited role. Each province had its own estates, its own tax system, its own courts, and its own claims to sovereignty. The provinces differed greatly in wealth and structure. Holland and Zeeland were dominated by towns and maritime commerce, while Gelderland, Overijssel, and Friesland had stronger landed noble and rural elements and Groningen was dominated by its city and surrounding territory.

Alongside the provinces were the Generality Lands, territories conquered from Spain or acquired during the war, including Staats-Brabant, Staats-Vlaanderen, and Staats-Limburg. These lands had no representation in the States General and were governed directly by it as a collective possession. The Generality Lands were important strategically, since they formed the frontier against the Spanish Netherlands, and economically, since they were sometimes treated as subordinate zones, with a largely Catholic population that had fewer political rights than inhabitants of the sovereign provinces. They illustrate that the Republic exercised imperial-style control within its own borders.

The central constitutional doctrine of the Republic was provincial sovereignty. Each province claimed to be a sovereign entity that cooperated with others through the Union. This did not mean that provinces were equal in practice. Holland's wealth, population, and share of federal expenditure gave it commanding influence, and its Land's Advocate, later called the Grand Pensionary, became the most important political official of the Republic. But it did mean that formal authority was dispersed. No central government could impose taxes, appoint officials, or change religious arrangements in a province without provincial consent.

The lack of a single constitutional document means that historians reconstruct the system from the Union of Utrecht, the resolutions of the States General, practice, and the writings of contemporary jurists and political commentators. The Dutch system was never formally codified until the end of the eighteenth century, so interpretation depended heavily on precedent and on political power.

The formal structure within each province differed considerably, and these differences shaped how provinces behaved in the common assembly. In Holland the estates consisted of the nobility, with a single vote, and the eighteen enfranchised towns; the clergy, who had sat in the medieval estates, had disappeared with the Reformation. Zeeland's estates were dominated by a handful of towns together with a single noble represented in practice by the Prince of Orange as First Noble, a status that gave the House of Orange a direct constitutional foothold in the province. In Utrecht the estates were composed of representatives of the ecclesiastical chapters, the nobility, and the towns, so that even after the Reformation the province retained institutional traces of its medieval church. Gelderland was divided into quarters, each with its own assembly and its own claims, and Overijssel and Friesland combined noble, rural, and urban representation in different proportions. Groningen was shaped by the long rivalry between the city of Groningen and the rural Ommelanden, whose uneasy partnership the Union of Utrecht had already revealed. A single phrase such as "the provinces" therefore conceals seven distinct political systems.

The consequences of this variety were practical. Because each province controlled its own taxation, its own justice, and its own militia companies, a decision taken at The Hague had no force until provincial estates had adopted and implemented it. The States General itself could issue resolutions, instructions, and placards, but the officials who enforced them were provincial and municipal. The confederation had a small number of bodies that were genuinely common: the army and the Council of State that supervised it, the admiralty colleges, the Generality Lands, and the diplomatic service. Everything else depended on cooperation. For this reason the Dutch Republic is often described by historians not as a failed unitary state but as a successful composite one, whose members had every incentive to cooperate against a shared external threat and whose principal difficulties arose when that threat receded.

Section 77.7

The States General, Collective Defense, and the Problems of Federal Decision-Making

The States General met at The Hague and consisted of delegations from the provinces. Each province had one vote, regardless of size, and each delegation carried instructions from its home estates. Delegates could not simply bind their provinces; on important matters they had to refer back for new instructions, a practice that made decisions slow but ensured that provincial interests were protected. The States General appointed ambassadors, received foreign envoys, and handled war, peace, and treaties. It also governed the Generality Lands and supervised the common army and navy.

Important executive functions were handled by other bodies. The Council of State, composed of representatives of the provinces, oversaw military administration and finance, and a Chamber of Accounts audited expenditures. Five admiralty colleges, located in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, Zeeland, North Holland, and Friesland, managed naval affairs and collected customs duties that financed convoys and warships. The decentralization of the navy reflected the commercial interests of the ports and complicated naval coordination.

Foreign observers often mocked this system as slow and cumbersome, and sometimes it was. When provincial delegates disagreed, negotiation could take months, and a single province could block a decision. Yet the system also had advantages. Consensus gave decisions legitimacy, because provinces that had agreed were bound to implement them, and the practice of negotiation among regents who knew and often were related to one another created a certain political cohesion. The Republic also relied on informal coordination, especially through the leading official of Holland, who in practice managed the agenda of the States General.

For the early modern period, the Dutch system was neither unusually inefficient nor unusually effective. It reflected the practical needs of a maritime and commercial federation whose members insisted on retaining their rights. The fact that it sustained a large war effort for decades shows that decentralization did not necessarily mean weakness, an important point for the comparison with monarchical states that this chapter develops.

The mechanics of decision-making explain both the Republic's slowness and its durability. Each province sent a delegation to the weekly sessions of the States General, and the number of delegates varied by province, but each province cast one vote. The presidency rotated weekly among the provinces, so that no single province chaired permanently. Delegates carried written instructions from their estates, and on any question outside those instructions they had to consult their principals, a procedure known as ruggespraak, or referral back. On matters of war, peace, alliances, and taxation, practice required that the provinces agree unanimously, whereas lesser administrative questions could be settled by a majority. The practical effect was that every province held a veto over the most consequential decisions, and that every important policy had to be negotiated province by province.

Coordination was supplied by institutions and by people. The Council of State, which sat permanently at The Hague with deputies from the provinces, prepared the annual estimate of military expenditure known as the Staat van Oorlog, which set out the size and cost of the army and fortifications and, by dividing that cost according to the agreed quotas, determined what each province owed. The Chamber of Accounts audited the Generality's finances. The Raadpensionaris of Holland, and in practice the Grand Pensionary in the stadtholderless periods, drafted proposals, conducted negotiations with the other provinces, and managed foreign correspondence, so that the leading official of one province exercised a coordinating function for all. The Griffier, the secretary of the States General, kept the records and the continuity of business. Provinces that fell behind in their payments were pressed through negotiation and, in principle, through the threat of coercion by the common army, but arrears were a chronic problem and in practice the States General depended on the persuasion of the regents of the contributing provinces.

The system's defenders and critics both appealed to it. Critics, among them foreign diplomats and Orangist writers, complained that decisions took months and that provinces could paralyze the whole. Defenders, notably regent writers, argued that unanimity protected the Republic from the rash decisions of a single ruler and bound every province to commitments it had freely accepted. Both claims contained truth. The seventeenth-century Republic mobilized for war, concluded treaties, and sustained a large navy without an executive in the modern sense, but it did so by repeated negotiation among men who knew one another and who shared, in most periods, an interest in the security of commerce.

Section 77.8

The States of Holland, Amsterdam, and the Political Weight of Commercial Wealth

The States of Holland, which met at The Hague, had a distinctive composition: the nobility, the Ridderschap, had one vote, and eighteen towns had one vote each. Because the towns had eighteen of nineteen votes, Holland's political life was shaped primarily by urban interests. This was a striking contrast to most European representative assemblies, in which nobles and clergy held most seats. The States of Holland also controlled most of the Republic's wealth. Holland provided roughly three-fifths of the federal budget, a proportion that gave it great leverage in the States General.

Within Holland, Amsterdam held a special position. By the middle of the seventeenth century it was the leading commercial and financial center of Europe, the home of the Exchange Bank, the stock market, and the headquarters of a large VOC chamber. Its city council, composed of leading merchant families, could influence the votes of the States of Holland through its financial importance and the alliances of its regents, even though each of the eighteen towns formally had the same single vote. Amsterdam's regents often pursued policies of peace, commerce, and low military expenditure, which brought them into conflict with those who valued military strength.

This situation created a tension between formal constitutional equality and practical economic weight. Smaller towns resented Amsterdam's dominance, and other provinces resented Holland's. The Dutch system worked in part because Holland's leaders understood that the other provinces had formal rights and could not simply be ignored, and because informal bargaining reconciled different interests. When it failed, as in 1650, the result was constitutional crisis.

Holland's position also shaped Dutch political thought. Writers such as Pieter de la Court, in works published in the 1660s, argued that the interests of Holland and of its trade were best served by a government by regents without a stadtholder or monarch, and that war, which enriched princes and soldiers, damaged merchants. This was an argument by and for a particular elite, and it must be read as political advocacy, not as a neutral description.

Holland's estates also had a standing executive committee, the Gecommitteerde Raden, which handled business between sessions, and a powerful legal and political officer in the Raadpensionaris, or Land's Advocate before the title changed. The Land's Advocate was a salaried official appointed for a term, usually five years, who sat in the assembly without a vote but drafted resolutions, advised on law, and led Holland's delegation to the States General. This gave the officeholder, from Oldenbarnevelt to Johan de Witt and Anthonie Heinsius, an influence that depended less on formal powers than on knowledge, continuity, and the confidence of the leading towns. Because Holland's resolutions on foreign policy and defense largely set the course of the Republic, the officeholder functioned in practice as its chief minister, though without a constitutional title that said so.

The towns of Holland did not carry equal weight. Dordrecht claimed precedence as the oldest town and spoke first in the assembly by custom, and Haarlem, Delft, Leiden, Amsterdam, Gouda, and Rotterdam ranked among the leading voices, but the weight of Amsterdam's money, shipping, and credit far exceeded its single vote. Amsterdam's regents could and did block measures they disliked by threatening to withhold the city's share, and in cooperation with allied towns they shaped the formation of majorities. At the same time, the smaller towns often resented being treated as satellites, and the nobility, though limited to one vote, held a distinctive position because many of its members held military offices and looked to the stadtholder for patronage. These alignments meant that a stadtholder seeking support in Holland could find allies among the nobility and some towns, while regent opponents could rely on Amsterdam's wealth. Much of the politics of the seventeenth century takes the form of attempts by one side or the other to rearrange those alliances.

Section 77.9

Urban Regents, Municipal Oligarchies, and the Boundaries of Republican Participation

The basic unit of Dutch political life was the city, governed by a council (the vroedschap) and magistrates (burgomasters and aldermen) drawn from a narrow circle of wealthy families known as regents. The councils were usually self-perpetuating: when a member died, the remaining members chose a successor, often from related families, a practice called co-optation. In some cities and periods the stadtholder had the right to select magistrates from nominations, which gave him a means of influence. Offices were held for life or long terms, and family networks, kinship, and patronage shaped appointments.

The regents were not a hereditary nobility, and wealth, not birth, was the basis of entry, though over time the leading families became more exclusive and acquired country houses and noble-style status. They controlled municipal finances, courts, charitable institutions, the militia companies (schutterij), and appointments to the local Reformed consistory and to many provincial offices. They also appointed deputies to the provincial estates. In this way a few thousand men governed a population of some two million.

Participation was not wholly absent. Guilds and militia companies had traditions of petitioning, and urban crowds sometimes pressed their views, notably in 1672 and in later disturbances. Pamphlets, newspapers, and sermons created a public sphere in which political questions were discussed by many who held no office. Yet the highest political institutions were closed to most inhabitants. Women could not hold office. Catholics and other religious minorities were generally excluded from public positions. Most male residents were not citizens (poorters) with full rights. The Republic, in short, was a government by a limited urban elite, and modern terms such as democracy do not describe it.

This oligarchic character explains why historians often call the Republic a "regent republic." It also explains its political vulnerabilities: the regents could be accused of pursuing family and commercial interests, and Orangist writers frequently appealed to popular opinion against them. That appeal would be decisive in 1672.

The recruitment of regents illustrates how oligarchy operated in practice. In Amsterdam the city council, the vroedschap, had thirty-six members who held office for life, and when a seat fell vacant the remaining members chose a successor, usually from among the sons, sons-in-law, and allies of existing members. Each year the council and the outgoing magistrates selected four burgomasters, who ran the city's daily government, from among the members. Within a few families, notably the Bickers, De Graeffs, and Hoofts in the early and mid seventeenth century, political office was passed through kinship, marriage alliances, and shared commercial interests. The result was a political class that was open at the margins to men who had acquired wealth and connections but closed to the great majority.

The role of the stadtholder in municipal appointments was one of the sharpest points of contention. In several provinces, the stadtholder had the right to select magistrates from lists of nominees, or to renew the town councils, and the use of this prerogative gave him a means of rewarding friends and disciplining opponents. The practice of changing the composition of councils by outside authority was called a Wetsverzetting, and its use by Maurice in 1618 and by William III in 1672 transformed local politics in many towns. Regents defended the principle of co-optation as a guarantee of municipal liberty, while Orangist writers accused regents of turning their offices into hereditary property. Both claims described real features of the system.

Beneath the regents, participation took other forms. Guilds, militia companies, and neighborhood organizations had traditions of petitioning, and the militia officers, drawn from prosperous burghers, formed a recognized political constituency. Amsterdam's burghers could take part in the civic militia and in some church offices, though not in the council. Printed political commentary expanded after 1650, and the political pamphlet became an instrument in contests among regent factions and between regents and the House of Orange. This was a public sphere, but it was not a representative one: those who petitioned and published could shape opinion without holding office, and the exclusion of women, most workers, and religious minorities from the governing circle remained intact.

Section 77.10

The House of Orange, Stadtholders, and the Military Authority of the Republic

The stadtholder was originally the lieutenant of the prince in a province, appointed by the ruler to represent him. After the rejection of Philip II the provincial estates continued the office and appointed stadtholders themselves, usually from the House of Orange-Nassau. The stadtholder was not a king. He was a provincial officer, appointed and paid by the estates, with powers that varied from province to province but often included command of the provincial troops, the right to influence appointments to city councils, the authority to settle disputes among towns, and a role in justice and pardon.

The great importance of the Orange stadtholders came from the combination of these provincial offices with the offices of captain-general and admiral-general of the Union, which gave them command of the army and navy. In addition, the House of Orange had European prestige: it held the principality of Orange, was linked by marriage to Protestant dynasties, and was identified in popular memory with the Revolt. This combination gave Orange stadtholders a quasi-monarchical status. Frederick Henry, stadtholder from 1625 to 1647, had a court at The Hague, a policy of arranging prestigious marriages, and an influence over foreign policy that sometimes rivaled that of the regents.

Tension between regents and stadtholders arose from different priorities. Regents, especially in Holland, favored low taxes, commerce, and a defensive military policy, and feared that a victorious Orange general might seek sovereignty. The stadtholders, and the military and church groups close to them, favored active warfare, strong armies, and a more cohesive government. Popular support for Orange, rooted in memory of William the Silent and in Reformed preaching, offered the House an alternative power base to the regent councils. This was not a struggle between democracy and monarchy, since both sides were elites. It was a conflict between two models of leadership within a republic.

The stadtholderate also involved a distinct dynastic politics. The marriage of William II to Mary, the daughter of Charles I, in 1641 connected the House of Orange to the Stuarts, with consequences that would become dramatic in 1688. The Orange family's connections explain why events in England mattered to the Dutch, and why Dutch regents suspected that Orange loyalty to the Stuart cause could draw the Republic into dangerous commitments.

The authority of the stadtholder depended heavily on the combination of offices he held. Provincial stadtholderships were conferred separately by each province's estates, so that a member of the House of Orange might be stadtholder of Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, and Overijssel while a cadet branch, the Nassau-Dietz family, held Friesland and Groningen. Beyond the provinces, the States General commissioned the Prince of Orange as captain-general and admiral-general of the Union, commanding the army and navy and nominating a large share of military officers. These appointments yielded a network of patronage, since officers, the holders of fortress commands, and many civil servants owed their positions to the stadtholder. The Orange family also had substantial private resources, including estates and, until its seizure by France, the principality of Orange, which supported a princely court at The Hague.

Because the stadtholderate was a creation of provincial estates, it could be limited or left vacant by them, and this is what regent politicians did in 1650 to 1651 and again after William III's death. But the Orange tradition had strengths that regents could not match. Orange princes were military commanders in a state that spent most of its first century at war; they were symbols of the Revolt and of Protestant leadership; and they commanded the loyalty of many Reformed ministers and of urban crowds. The Frisian stadtholders, whose province had its own tradition of noble landholding, added a further dimension, since their position meant that the Orange cause was never entirely dependent on Holland. The tension between these two sources of authority, the provincial estates and the princely house, defines the constitutional history of the Republic.

Historical Vocabulary
Stadtholder and Regent

Stadtholder (Dutch stadhouder, "place-holder") originally meant a prince's lieutenant in a province. In the Republic it became a provincial office, held by members of the House of Orange-Nassau or, in some provinces, of the Frisian branch of the family, combined with military command at the federal level. It is often wrongly translated as "king" or "governor-general." It was neither: the stadtholder was appointed by provincial estates and could be removed or left vacant, as happened in the stadtholderless periods.

Regent (regent) designated a member of the ruling urban or provincial elite who held offices in city councils, provincial estates, courts, and charitable institutions. The term does not refer to a person ruling on behalf of a monarch, the usual English sense. Regents were an oligarchy of wealth and connection, not a legally closed nobility. Understanding the contrast between stadtholder and regent is essential to interpreting the political struggles of the Republic.

Section 77.11

Public Finance, Provincial Contributions, Military Expenditure, and Government Credit

The Republic's capacity to fight a long war against the greatest European power depended on finance. Revenue came from a mixture of sources. Provinces raised taxes, including excises on consumption goods such as beer, grain, and peat, as well as levies on property and other transactions, and contributed to the common treasury according to a quota system that allocated shares of the federal budget among the provinces. The admiralties collected customs duties, and the Generality Lands provided additional income. These taxes, heavy by European standards and falling heavily on consumption, were collected by local authorities and generally accepted because they had been voted by the estates in which the taxpayers' regent elites sat.

The key to the Republic's financial strength was credit. Because the provinces and cities were regarded as reliable debtors that paid interest regularly, they could borrow at lower rates than many monarchies. Cities and provinces sold annuities and bonds to wealthy investors, many of them in Amsterdam, and the interest was secured by specific tax revenues. The legitimacy of the system, in which the lenders were often the same people who voted the taxes, made repudiation unlikely. Confidence in public obligations made it possible to mobilize large sums quickly in an emergency without the forced loans and defaults that damaged many monarchies' credit.

This financial structure also created political consequences. The burden on Holland caused resentment among its taxpayers and strengthened the regent argument that the military establishment should be limited. The dependence of the state on the creditors gave bondholders an interest in peace and in stable government. And the connection between commercial capital and public finance meant that the fiscal capacity of the Republic could not easily be separated from its political structure.

Dutch fiscal government rested on a sequence of processes that can be described step by step. The Council of State prepared the Staat van Oorlog, an estimate of the military expenditure of the Union for the coming year, and the States General approved it. The total was then divided among the provinces according to a fixed quota, known as the repartition, in which Holland carried approximately 58 percent, with the remaining provinces responsible for much smaller shares. Each province raised its contribution through its own taxes, and its receivers paid the sum to the Receiver-General of the Union. Admiralty colleges had their own income from convoy duties and licence fees on trade. Exceptional needs, such as the emergency of 1672, required extraordinary taxation and special levies voted by the provinces.

Provinces and cities raised revenue through a range of taxes. The excise, collected on staples such as beer, wine, salt, peat, grain, and milling, and on many manufactured goods, produced the bulk of urban revenue, which is why contemporaries complained of the high cost of living. Land and house taxes, including the verponding, fell on property. Occasional extraordinary levies on wealth or on households supplemented them, and tax farming, by which collection rights were auctioned to private contractors who advanced the money, was widely used. These taxes were generally heavier than those of neighboring monarchies when measured per head, though they were levied with the consent of the regents who sat in the estates and who, as the principal holders of public bonds, had an interest in their regular payment.

Borrowing was equally important. The States of Holland and the other provinces issued long-term annuities and obligations, secured on specified tax revenues, which paid interest to holders and could be traded. Cities issued similar instruments. The interest rate that these governments paid fell over the first half of the seventeenth century, from the high rates of the early revolt to about four percent by mid century, as lenders judged the Republic a reliable debtor. The 1672 emergency demanded unusually heavy borrowing and taxation, and the debt of Holland rose steadily through the wars of William III. The point for this chapter is that the Dutch system combined consent, specific tax assignments, and regular interest payments in a way that made default less likely, and that this credibility gave the Republic access to money when other states had to resort to forced loans or sale of offices. Its structural weakness was the reverse of its strength: the burden fell most heavily on Holland, whose regents regularly resisted the increases in military spending that the stadtholders and their allies demanded.

Historical Debate 1

Was the Dutch Republic a Polity of Unusual Liberty, an Oligarchic Republic, or Both?

The question: How should historians characterize the political system of the Republic in comparison with contemporary monarchies?

Interpretation A: Unusual liberty. Some historians emphasize the Republic's limits on rulers, its pluralistic religious practice, its lively press, and its relative freedom from royal absolutism, arguing that these features distinguished it among European states and attracted foreign admirers and refugees.
Interpretation B: Oligarchy. Others stress that a small circle of regents controlled political offices, that most inhabitants were excluded, that toleration was limited and varied by place, and that the apparent liberty of the Republic served the interests of an urban elite.

Evidence: Both draw on the same city-council records, pamphlets, and legal documents. They differ in the standard of comparison, whether the Republic is measured against contemporary monarchies or against later democratic ideals.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether the Republic is treated as a precursor of liberal politics or as an early modern oligarchy with its own logic. Most current scholarship accepts elements of both: it was freer than many contemporaries and also oligarchic.

Division III · Sections 77.12–77.16

Dutch Commerce, Society, Religion, and Culture. This division explains how economic resources and social institutions sustained republican government, and confronts the exclusions and contradictions of the Dutch Golden Age.

Section 77.12

The Dutch Commercial Economy and the Foundations of the Golden Age

The prosperity of the seventeenth-century Republic rested on a long development of commerce in the northern Low Countries. In the late Middle Ages, towns in Holland and Zeeland had developed herring fishing, shipbuilding, and the carrying trade between the Baltic and western Europe. Dutch shippers bought grain, timber, and naval stores in the Baltic and carried them to western and southern Europe, and returned with salt, wine, and manufactured goods. This bulk trade, known as the "mother trade," provided steady employment and capital, and Dutch shipbuilders developed the fluyt, a cheaply built cargo vessel requiring a small crew, which lowered freight costs.

Amsterdam became a staple market, a place where goods from all over the world were stored, sorted, and resold. Merchants could find in Amsterdam information, credit, insurance, and warehousing, and they could obtain a wide variety of commodities. The decline of Antwerp after the Spanish recapture in 1585 and the closure of the Scheldt brought migrants, capital, and skills northward, an inflow of merchants, artisans, and refugees from the southern Netherlands that boosted Holland's urban economy. Dutch merchants also extended their trade to the Mediterranean, the Levant, West Africa, the Caribbean, and Asia.

The economy was not limited to commerce. Urban manufacturing included textiles at Leiden, shipbuilding along the Zaan, sugar refining, brewing, printing, and the finishing of imported goods. Agriculture was highly commercialized, with dairying, market gardening, and industrial crops such as flax and hops, supported by drainage and land reclamation, as noted in Chapter 2. Peat provided fuel, and windmills powered drainage, sawing, and milling. Many rural households combined farming with fishing, spinning, or seasonal labor, and rural wages and urban wages were relatively high, though so were prices.

The Republic's economic advantage should not be treated as natural or automatic. It depended on specific institutions, on access to cheap water transport, on the openness of markets, and on a political system that protected property and merchants' interests. It also depended on the weakness of competitors, since civil war in France and the Thirty Years' War in Germany disrupted rival commerce. By the later seventeenth century, when England, France, and others adopted protective policies and developed their own shipping, Dutch commercial leadership faced growing pressure.

Section 77.13

Amsterdam's Financial Institutions, Banking, Insurance, and Public Credit

Amsterdam developed a financial infrastructure that supported commerce and state finance. The Amsterdam Exchange Bank (Wisselbank), established in 1609 by the city, accepted deposits of coin and allowed merchants to settle transactions by transfer of bank money between accounts, which reduced the problems of coin of varying weight and quality that plagued international trade. Bank money often traded at a premium over circulating coin, because it was reliable. The Bank was a municipal institution, tied to the city's credit.

Merchants used bills of exchange to move funds across Europe, and Amsterdam brokers specialized in arranging credit and discounting bills. Marine insurance, which protected shipowners and cargo owners against risk, was widely available, with underwriters often grouped in chambers. The shares of the VOC, founded in 1602, were traded on the Amsterdam exchange, creating one of the earliest continuously active markets in transferable shares. Speculative activity, including trading in options and futures-like contracts, was recorded by contemporaries and criticized by some, as in the famous account by Joseph de la Vega in 1688.

The links between private and public finance were close. The same wealthy investors who held VOC shares and merchant credit also purchased provincial and municipal bonds. Because interest on those bonds was secured by specific taxes, and because the investors often were regents, the system supported public credit. Financial institutions also facilitated the extension of credit to foreign governments, so that Amsterdam capital supported foreign wars, including in later periods the wars of the British monarchy. In the eighteenth century, when relative commercial decline coincided with continued financial strength, Dutch investment in foreign debt became an important feature of the economy.

Students should avoid anachronistic language here. These institutions were not "capitalism" in some final form, nor did they eliminate crises. The Republic experienced financial panics, and the speculative boom in tulip contracts in the 1630s, though often exaggerated in later retellings, was a reminder that markets could be volatile. The institutions are significant because they combined commercial and fiscal functions in a way that supported the Republic's political and military capabilities.

Section 77.14

Religious Diversity, the Reformed Church, and the Limits of Toleration

The Dutch Reformed Church, Calvinist in doctrine, was the privileged public church of the Republic. Its ministers were paid by public funds, its churches occupied former medieval buildings, and public offices were generally reserved for its members or those willing to attend. Yet only a minority of the population were members, and many inhabitants adhered to other confessions or to none. Catholics remained a large minority, perhaps a third or more in the mid-seventeenth century in some estimates, and were especially numerous in the Generality Lands, Utrecht, and parts of Gelderland and Overijssel. Mennonites, Lutherans, Remonstrants, and later Jews also formed communities.

Dutch practice combined official preference with practical tolerance. Public worship by non-Reformed groups was generally prohibited, but private worship in houses or buildings that did not look like churches, often called "hidden churches" (schuilkerken), was tolerated, sometimes in return for payments to local officials. Enforcement varied from town to town and province to province, depending on the regents' attitudes and the pressure from the Reformed clergy. Amsterdam, for example, was relatively accommodating, while other places were stricter. Legal privilege and informal toleration should not be confused. Toleration meant that authorities refrained from persecuting conscience, not that all religions enjoyed equal rights.

Jewish communities, composed of Sephardic refugees from Iberia and later Ashkenazi migrants from central and eastern Europe, settled in Amsterdam from the late sixteenth century onward and built synagogues, schools, and a network of commerce and printing. They were allowed to practice their religion, though they were excluded from guilds in many trades and from public office, and the Reformed Church sometimes pressed for restrictions. Huguenot refugees from France after 1685 also found a home in the Republic. The Republic's reputation as a haven for dissenters attracted thinkers and printers, and Amsterdam's presses produced works that could not appear elsewhere, though censorship was not absent.

Religion and politics were closely entangled. The Remonstrant controversy of 1610 to 1619, discussed in Section 77.17, began as a theological dispute about predestination but became a conflict over whether the church or the provincial authorities controlled religion. The outcome confirmed the Reformed Church's doctrinal position at the Synod of Dort and marginalized Remonstrants, but it also confirmed that civil magistrates retained substantial authority over the church. Dutch religious life was thus a balance between a confessional public church and a plural society.

Section 77.15

Urban Society, Households, Women, Poverty, and Cultural Production

Dutch urban society was hierarchical. At the top were regents and wealthy merchants. Below them came lesser merchants, shopkeepers, master craftsmen, and professionals, and at the bottom were laborers, sailors, domestic servants, and the poor. The guilds regulated crafts and often provided welfare for members. The relatively high level of urbanization in Holland meant that a large part of the population lived in towns of different sizes, and Amsterdam's population probably reached some 200,000 by the later seventeenth century, though estimates vary.

Women's roles were significant but constrained. Under Dutch law, married women had limited legal capacity, but many worked in trade, shops, markets, and as spinners, seamstresses, and servants. Wives of sailors and merchants often managed household finances and businesses during their husbands' long absences. Women did not hold political office or vote, and the household, as in most of early modern Europe, was understood as a patriarchal unit. Contemporary writers such as Jacob Cats offered advice literature on marriage and domestic order, which reveals ideals rather than practice.

Poverty was visible. Cities developed institutions of charity, including orphanages, almshouses, and workhouses, run by the church or by municipal and private governors, often regents themselves. The famous Amsterdam spinning house and rasp house disciplined the idle and criminal through work. Charity reflected both religious obligation and social control. High food prices and unemployment could hit workers hard, and the Republic's prosperity coexisted with considerable inequality.

The wealth and civic institutions supported remarkable cultural production. Painters such as Rembrandt, Frans Hals, Johannes Vermeer, and Jan Steen worked in a market in which civic bodies, merchants, and households purchased portraits, landscapes, still lifes, and scenes of daily life, not simply commissions from a court and church. Print shops and publishers produced books, maps, and pamphlets, and literacy was relatively high. Yet the artistic and intellectual achievements of the Golden Age were produced by and for a particular social stratum. They reveal the values and anxieties of urban elites, including a concern about wealth and moral order, but cannot be taken as direct evidence of the lives of the poor. Historians use paintings as sources only with attention to genre, patronage, and purpose.

Section 77.16

Overseas Commerce, Chartered Companies, Coerced Labor, and Republican Contradictions

The VOC, chartered by the States General in 1602, held a monopoly of Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and the authority to make treaties, wage war, build forts, and administer justice in its territories. The WIC, chartered in 1621, held comparable privileges for the Atlantic. Their capital was raised from investors, including many regents, and their governing boards, the Heren XVII (Gentlemen Seventeen) for the VOC and the Heren XIX for the WIC, were composed of representatives of regional chambers. These companies were quasi-sovereign enterprises that exercised power beyond the Republic's own constitutional structure. Their full histories belong to Chapter 71, with Atlantic slavery in Chapter 70.

The overseas system was built on violence and coercion. In the Indonesian archipelago the VOC used force to secure monopoly over spices, including the destruction of the Banda Islands' population and economy in the 1620s. Elsewhere it relied on treaties, alliances with local rulers, and trading posts. In the Atlantic, the WIC participated in the slave trade, conquered parts of Brazil, and established plantations and trading forts, while Dutch merchants supplied capital and ships for the transport of enslaved Africans to the Americas. In the Cape Colony and in Asian ports, the VOC used enslaved labor as well. These facts are central to the Republic's wealth and cannot be set aside in celebratory narratives.

The link between global commerce and metropolitan political institutions is intricate. Profits from the VOC and other trades enriched regents and merchants, who invested in land, public bonds, and cultural patronage, and the companies' customs revenue contributed to the state. Commercial elites also supported the defense of trade routes through the navy. The companies were governed by the same circles that governed cities and provinces, so that political and economic power overlapped. Meanwhile, the principles of rights and liberty invoked in 1581 against Philip II were not extended to colonized peoples or to enslaved Africans.

This contrast is an example of the contradictions of the age. The Republic combined limits on political authority with the exercise of coercive power overseas, and the combination was not accidental. Historians debate how much overseas commerce, as opposed to European trade, contributed to Dutch prosperity, with many arguing that Baltic and European commerce remained larger in the seventeenth century. The point for this chapter is that the political system and the overseas system were linked and must be examined together.

Section 77.17

Maurice of Nassau, Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, and the Confessional-Political Crisis

During the Twelve Years' Truce, a theological dispute at Leiden University between two professors, Jacobus Arminius and Franciscus Gomarus, about predestination and divine grace spread into the churches and the political arena. In 1610 followers of Arminius presented a Remonstrance to the States of Holland setting out their positions, and they became known as Remonstrants, while their opponents, who presented a Counter-Remonstrance, were called Counter-Remonstrants. The Remonstrants, supported by Oldenbarnevelt and many regents, favored a church subordinate to civil authority and a less rigid doctrine. Counter-Remonstrants wanted a national synod and strict Calvinist orthodoxy.

The conflict became constitutional. Oldenbarnevelt and Holland insisted that each province had authority over religion within its borders, while Counter-Remonstrants, backed by many Reformed ministers and by popular opinion in some cities, wanted the States General to call a national synod. Maurice of Nassau, who disagreed with Oldenbarnevelt over the Truce and the direction of policy, sided with the Counter-Remonstrants in 1617. In 1618 he used troops to depose Remonstrant magistrates in several towns, and the States General arrested Oldenbarnevelt and others.

A national synod met at Dordrecht (Dort) from November 1618 to May 1619 and confirmed Calvinist doctrine against the Remonstrants, expelling their ministers from the church, with delegates from other Reformed churches attending. Oldenbarnevelt was tried by a special court and beheaded in The Hague on 13 May 1619. The episode shows how deeply theology and political authority were intertwined. It strengthened Maurice and the Orange-aligned and Reformed groups, and it settled the confessional character of the public church. But it did not eliminate regent power, which revived after Maurice's death in 1625.

The memory of 1619 persisted in Dutch politics. For later regents it was a warning about the danger of a stadtholder allied with popular and clerical forces. For Orangists, it was proof that regents might threaten the Republic's unity. Both interpretations shaped the later crises of 1650 and 1672.

The sequence of the crisis of 1617 to 1619 shows how theological dispute became a constitutional contest. In 1617 the Reformed congregations of several towns, supported by Maurice, demanded a national synod to settle the doctrinal question, while the States of Holland, guided by Oldenbarnevelt, insisted that doctrine within Holland was a matter for the provincial authorities. On 4 August 1617 the States of Holland passed the Sharp Resolution, which authorized towns to recruit paid guard companies, called waardgelders, to maintain order, and which placed the authority of the provincial estates above that of the States General in this matter. Maurice and the Counter-Remonstrant party regarded this as an attempt to create independent provincial armies and as a threat to the Union.

Maurice's response was decisive. Having secured a resolution of the States General in July 1618 that authorized him to disband the waardgelders, he moved through the provinces, replacing magistrates, replacing the Remonstrant-leaning regents in Utrecht and other towns, and disarming the guards. On 29 August 1618 Oldenbarnevelt, together with Hugo Grotius, the Pensionary of Rotterdam, and Rombout Hogerbeets, was arrested at The Hague. A special tribunal of judges drawn from several provinces, which Oldenbarnevelt denounced as illegal, tried him and sentenced him to death, and he was beheaded on 13 May 1619, aged seventy-one. Grotius and Hogerbeets were sentenced to life imprisonment; Grotius escaped in 1621, reportedly in a chest of books, and went into exile, where he became one of the leading writers on the law of nations.

The Synod of Dort, which sat from November 1618 to May 1619, issued the doctrinal rulings that defined orthodox Reformed teaching on predestination and expelled Remonstrant ministers. It also showed the limits of the Orangist triumph, since the synod met under the authority of the States General and the civil authorities retained control over the church's finances and appointments. The legal question of where authority lay was left unsettled: the provinces remained sovereign, and the army and the House of Orange had demonstrated their capacity to intervene. For regents, the memory of 1618 was a warning about the dangers of an alliance between a stadtholder, a militant church, and popular opinion; for Orangists, it was a reminder of the Republic's vulnerability to regent factionalism. The Truce expired in 1621, and war with Spain again made Maurice indispensable.

Section 77.18

William II, the Crisis of 1650, and the First Stadtholderless Period

After the Peace of Münster, the Republic's regents wanted to reduce the army, which had grown during the war and was expensive. William II, who succeeded his father Frederick Henry in 1647, opposed disbanding troops, in part because a strong army underpinned his authority and his hopes of aiding his Stuart in-laws in England after the execution of Charles I. The conflict with Holland, led by Amsterdam, over military expenditure and foreign policy escalated. In July 1650 William had six Holland regents arrested and sent troops to seize Amsterdam, an attempt that failed when the city resisted, and he negotiated a compromise.

Then William died of smallpox on 6 November 1650, a few days before his son, the future William III, was born. The sudden vacancy reshaped Dutch politics. In 1651 a Great Assembly of delegates from the provinces met at The Hague and agreed to reduce the army and to leave the stadtholderate vacant in Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, and Overijssel, while Friesland and Groningen maintained their own stadtholder from the Frisian branch of the family. The result was the first stadtholderless period, which lasted until 1672.

During these years Johan de Witt, who became Grand Pensionary of Holland in 1653, led the Republic. The Grand Pensionary was the salaried legal adviser and chief official of the States of Holland, who prepared its agenda and conducted its diplomacy, and in practice dominated the States General. De Witt's regime, which contemporaries called True Freedom, was based on the doctrine of provincial sovereignty and the control of the army and offices by regents. It was not a democratization. It shifted power from the House of Orange to the regent elite, who excluded Orangists from office and supported commerce and a cautious foreign policy.

The regime also defended itself by political means. In 1654, under pressure from Cromwell's England at the end of the First Anglo-Dutch War, Holland adopted a secret Act of Seclusion pledging that it would not appoint a member of the House of Orange as stadtholder, and in 1667 the Perpetual Edict abolished the stadtholderate in Holland altogether and separated military command from provincial office. These measures reveal how much the regents feared a revival of Orange power, and also how much the survival of the regime depended on European circumstances.

William II's confrontation with Holland was a struggle over the army and over the direction of foreign policy. After 1648, the States of Holland, led by Amsterdam, wanted to reduce the army, which cost far more than the Republic's income in peacetime would support. William, who had succeeded in 1647, resisted, and he sought to use the army in support of the exiled Stuarts, whose cause, after Charles I's execution in 1649, linked him to his wife's family. He also quarreled with Amsterdam over the proposed disbanding of regiments. In June 1650 the States General, under his influence, sent a delegation to Amsterdam to examine the city's resistance to the common policy, and the city's regents, led by Andries Bicker and Cornelis de Graeff, refused to give way.

On 30 July 1650, William had six leading Holland deputies, including Jacob de Witt of Dordrecht, the father of Johan, arrested at The Hague and imprisoned at Loevestein castle. At the same time he sent troops under his cousin William Frederick of Nassau-Dietz toward Amsterdam. The city, warned by a courier, closed its gates and prepared to defend itself, and William's advance stalled before the city could be taken. In the negotiations that followed, the prisoners were released, two of the Amsterdam regents most hostile to William accepted a change in office, and a compromise reduced part of the army. The stadtholder had demonstrated the limits of his power, and the regents had demonstrated their determination, but the compromise settled little. Four months later, on 6 November 1650, William died of smallpox, and his son was born eight days afterward.

The constitutional arrangements of the first stadtholderless period grew out of a Great Assembly of the provinces that met at The Hague from January to August 1651. Rather than appointing a successor, the provinces of Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, and Overijssel left the stadtholderate vacant, while Friesland and Groningen retained William Frederick. The Assembly reaffirmed provincial sovereignty and the principle that each province controlled its own troops and appointments. In practice the provinces regained control over the appointment of town magistrates and over the officers of their own forces, and the office of captain-general was left empty, so that military command was exercised by committees of deputies in the field, a cumbersome arrangement that would be tested in the wars of the 1650s and 1660s. The Republic, in short, was governed by the committees, councils, and officials that the regents controlled.

Johan de Witt, who became Grand Pensionary of Holland in 1653 at the age of twenty-seven, presided over this system for nearly two decades. Reappointed every five years, he conducted foreign policy, managed the finances of Holland, and built a network of regent allies, relying on his own mastery of detail and on the cooperation of Amsterdam's leading families. His regime defended itself against Orangism by legislation. In 1654, as the price of peace with Cromwell, Holland adopted the secret Act of Seclusion, pledging never to appoint a prince of Orange as stadtholder, though the act was repudiated after the Restoration. In 1667 the Perpetual Edict abolished the office in Holland and declared it incompatible with the post of captain-general of the Union. De Witt and his supporters called their system True Freedom, but their opponents denounced it as the government of a faction, and the weaknesses of the regime, the underfunded army and the dependence on diplomacy, became disastrous in 1672.

Section 77.19

The Anglo-Dutch Wars and the Competition for Maritime Power

The Anglo-Dutch Wars were commercial wars between two Protestant states that had been allies against Spain. The English Navigation Act of 1651 required that goods imported into England and its colonies from Asia, Africa, and America be carried in English ships or ships of the producing country, and that European goods be carried in English ships or those of the country of origin. It was aimed at Dutch carrying trade, though its enforcement and effects varied. Disputes over the salute to the English flag in the Channel, fishing, and colonial trade added to tension.

The First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654) was fought chiefly at sea between the Commonwealth and the Republic. It ended with the Treaty of Westminster, in which the Dutch accepted the Navigation Act and the secret clause on the House of Orange. The Second Anglo-Dutch War (1665–1667), begun under Charles II, saw English capture of New Amsterdam in 1664, which became New York, and ended with the Treaty of Breda in 1667, after a Dutch raid on the English fleet in the Medway in June 1667 caused a humiliating loss to English naval prestige. The Third Anglo-Dutch War (1672–1674), fought in alliance with France, is discussed in Section 77.20 and ended with the Treaty of Westminster in 1674.

The wars showed how different political systems could mobilize resources. The Dutch Republic, with its fiscal credit and strong ports, could fund large fleets, but its decentralized admiralties sometimes complicated coordination. England, with a smaller peacetime revenue and dependence on parliamentary grants, found war costly, and Charles II's financial difficulties were a persistent theme. The wars also influenced English political debate, as critics of the court cited naval failures and the dependence on French subsidies.

For the purposes of this chapter, the wars link the two histories by showing that commercial competition did not prevent ideological and dynastic connection. The republican regime of De Witt and the Commonwealth and Stuart regimes in England were at once rivals and parts of a shared Protestant maritime world. Naval operations are left to the relevant maritime and diplomatic chapters.

The origins of the Anglo-Dutch Wars lay in the collision of two commercial systems. The Dutch carrying trade, with its large fleets of cheap cargo ships, dominated the movement of Baltic and European goods and challenged English merchants in the Atlantic, the Mediterranean, and Asia. English complaints centered on Dutch trade in the English colonies, on the Dutch fisheries off the English coast, and on the VOC's behavior in the East Indies, where the execution of English traders at Amboyna in 1623 remained a grievance in English memory. The Navigation Act of 1651, passed by the Rump Parliament, was framed to restrict the carrying of goods in foreign ships and thereby to deprive the Dutch of their advantage, and its enforcement, together with the stopping of Dutch ships and disputes over the flag, led to hostilities in 1652.

The First Anglo-Dutch War (1652 to 1654) was fought primarily in the narrow seas, with engagements such as Dungeness, Portland, the Gabbard, and Scheveningen, where the Dutch admiral Maarten Tromp was killed in August 1653. The English navy, expanded by the Commonwealth and led by Robert Blake, George Monck, and others, had the better of the exchanges, and Dutch trade suffered severely from blockade and from losses of merchantmen. The Treaty of Westminster of April 1654 ended the war on terms that favored England and included the secret clause on the House of Orange. The war had also shown that two republics could fight bitterly over commerce, despite proposals in 1651 for a closer political union.

The second and third wars followed the Restoration. The Second Anglo-Dutch War (1665 to 1667) began with Dutch and English clashes in Africa and North America, including the English seizure of New Amsterdam in 1664, and included the battles of Lowestoft in 1665 and the Four Days' Battle in 1666. In June 1667, while peace talks were under way, a Dutch squadron under Michiel de Ruyter sailed up the Thames and the Medway, burned ships at Chatham, and towed away the English flagship Royal Charles, an episode that damaged English prestige and showed the effect of financial shortage on the Royal Navy. By the Treaty of Breda in July 1667, the English retained New Netherland, which became New York, while the Dutch kept Suriname and strengthened their position in the Spice Islands. The Third Anglo-Dutch War is treated in the next section because it was part of the larger crisis of 1672.

Section 77.20

The Rampjaar of 1672, Johan de Witt, and William III's Rise

In 1672, called the Rampjaar (the disaster year), the Republic was attacked by France, England, and the German bishoprics of Münster and Cologne. Louis XIV's army, crossing the Rhine in June, overran Gelderland, Overijssel, and Utrecht with astonishing speed, and French troops came close to Amsterdam, while the Dutch fleet fought off an English and French attack at sea. The regents' policy of limiting the army had left the Republic poorly defended, and critics blamed them for the catastrophe. The Dutch opened the dikes to flood the approaches to Holland, creating the Waterline defense that held the French at bay.

The crisis was also political. Popular and Orangist agitation demanded the appointment of William III, then twenty-one, as stadtholder and captain-general. In July the States of Holland named him stadtholder, and other provinces followed. Johan de Witt, wounded by an assassination attempt, resigned as Grand Pensionary, and on 20 August his brother Cornelis, who had been accused of plotting against William, was seized by a crowd in The Hague, and both brothers were murdered. The killings were carried out by an armed mob with the apparent complicity of some authorities, and Orange supporters benefited. They remain one of the most disturbing episodes in Dutch history and a reminder that early modern republican politics could be violent.

After 1672, William replaced many regents in city governments, as stadtholder he had the right to do in several provinces, and strengthened Orange networks. The Republic survived. Alliances with Spain, Brandenburg, and the Emperor, and the French failure to take Holland, led to the withdrawal of England (Treaty of Westminster 1674) and eventually to the Peace of Nijmegen in 1678. The regime had changed, but the structures of provincial sovereignty and urban oligarchy continued. The Rampjaar thus shows both how fragile the Republic was and how resilient its institutions proved.

The origins of the crisis lay in Louis XIV's decision to punish the Republic for its role in the Triple Alliance of 1668 and for its commercial policy. In the Treaty of Dover of 1670 Charles II committed England to support France, and France also secured the alliance of the Prince-Bishop of Münster, Bernhard von Galen, and of the Elector of Cologne. By early 1672 the Republic faced a coalition of France, England, Münster, and Cologne. The regents, who had reduced the army and neglected the fortresses, were unprepared. In February 1672, under growing pressure, the States General appointed William III captain-general for a single campaign, and Zeeland and then Holland moved to give him the stadtholderate.

The military collapse came in June. At sea, the Dutch fleet under De Ruyter fought the combined English and French fleets to a standstill at Solebay on 7 June, which prevented a landing. On land, a French army of well over 100,000 men crossed the Rhine near Lobith on 12 June, took the fortress towns of the Rhine and the IJssel line one after another, overran Gelderland, Overijssel, and Utrecht, and reached the borders of Holland, while Münster and Cologne attacked from the east. The Dutch field army, outnumbered, withdrew behind the water defenses. To protect Holland, the authorities opened sluices and flooded the land between Muiden and Gorinchem, creating the Hollandic Water Line, which the French could not cross. The Dutch envoys offered peace on terms of extensive territorial and religious concessions, which Louis demanded and which William and many of the people refused.

The political explosion followed the military one. In Dordrecht, Rotterdam, and other Holland towns, crowds demanded that the regents be removed and that William be given full authority, and in July the States of Holland appointed him stadtholder, while the other provinces confirmed him. William used his new authority to dismiss many regents and replace them with his supporters in a wave of Wetsverzettingen in the Holland towns. Johan de Witt, wounded in a street attack in June, resigned in August. His brother Cornelis, who had been arrested on a charge, based on the testimony of a barber, that he had plotted to kill William, was visited in prison by Johan on 20 August, and a crowd gathered at the Gevangenpoort in The Hague. Militia and civic guards withdrew or stood aside, and the brothers were dragged out, killed, and mutilated. Historians debate whether Orangist leaders instigated the lynching or merely tolerated it; William did not punish those responsible and rewarded some of them.

The Republic recovered. The French advance stalled before the flooded land, and William built a coalition. The Elector of Brandenburg, the Emperor, and Spain joined the war against France, and French forces in the Republic were stretched thin. In 1673 the Dutch fleet defeated English and French attempts at landing and William recovered Naarden and, with imperial help, took Bonn. England, whose Parliament had grown hostile to the war, made peace at Westminster in February 1674, and Münster and Cologne withdrew. The war continued until the Peace of Nijmegen in 1678 and 1679, which left the Republic intact. The Rampjaar thus created a new political order in the Republic, dominated by William, and it created an Orange myth of the deliverance of the fatherland. It also demonstrated that the regents' political system could not easily withstand military emergencies.

Section 77.21

William III, the Defense of the Republic, and the Anglo-Dutch Political Connection

William III's principal political objective was to check the power of Louis XIV, whose armies and diplomacy threatened the independence of the Republic and the balance of Europe, as discussed in Chapter 76. He built coalitions, through the States General and through his personal connections to German princes and the Habsburgs. His position in the Republic rested on a combination of stadtholderate, army command, and alliance with parts of the regent elite, whose factions in Amsterdam and other cities shifted over time. Amsterdam's regents sometimes opposed his policies, because they feared expense and wanted trade with France.

His marriage on 4 November 1677 to his cousin Mary, the elder Protestant daughter of James, Duke of York, the brother and heir of Charles II, gave him a claim, through Mary, to the English throne, in addition to his own Stuart descent through his mother, Mary Stuart, the daughter of Charles I. The marriage was part of a diplomatic strategy to detach England from France. It also bound the House of Orange to the question of the English succession, which became explosive during the Exclusion Crisis (Section 77.42). William maintained contacts with English political figures, including opponents of James, while avoiding open commitment.

The relationship between William and England combined cooperation and rivalry. English merchants and politicians regarded the Dutch as commercial competitors, and the memory of the Anglo-Dutch Wars was recent. At the same time, English and Dutch Protestants shared a concern about French power and Catholic monarchy. When James II's policies in the later 1680s alarmed Protestant England, and when the birth of a male heir in June 1688 threatened to produce a Catholic dynasty, William saw an opportunity and a necessity, because an England allied with France could threaten the Republic's survival. The intervention described in Division IX was the result of these converging interests.

William died on 8 March 1702, after a fall from his horse, leaving no children. In the Republic, the death of the stadtholder was followed by a second stadtholderless period, in which the regents again governed without an Orange stadtholder in the major provinces, a period that lasted until the crisis of 1747. The Dutch regents' political system continued, but the Republic's power declined relative to Britain and France in the following decades, as discussed in Chapter 80. The rest of this chapter turns to the Stuart kingdoms, from which William's intervention had profoundly altered the political order.

William's position after 1674 combined formal and informal powers. He was stadtholder in Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, and Overijssel, captain-general of the Union, and the dominant influence in the Council of State and in the appointment of magistrates. His chief collaborator in Holland was the Grand Pensionary Gaspar Fagel, who served from 1672 to 1688 and shaped Dutch policy with William, and who was succeeded in 1689 by Anthonie Heinsius, a skillful diplomat who remained in office until 1720. William's relations with Amsterdam remained tense, because some of its regents favored peace with France and trade with the French market, and because they feared that he wanted a monarchy. They did not oppose him on every issue, however, and in the critical decisions of 1688 the city agreed to support the expedition.

In the Dutch-English relationship, William pursued a consistent line: to prevent England from allying with France. After the Third War ended, he cultivated English political connections. His marriage to Mary in November 1677, arranged with the reluctant consent of Charles II and the Duke of York, was meant to secure English support, and he used his English contacts, including Whig and Tory politicians who feared Catholicism, to follow the Exclusion Crisis. During the 1680s he preferred to avoid committing himself to one English faction. He declined to involve himself in the Monmouth Rebellion of 1685, though Monmouth had been his guest in the Netherlands, and his envoys in London, among them Dijkvelt, maintained contact with leading men of different opinions.

The interests of the Republic and of William sometimes diverged, and the diplomacy of 1687 and 1688 illustrates the difference. In 1687 and 1688 James II was drawing closer to France and building a larger army and navy, which threatened the Dutch, and Louis XIV was imposing tariffs on Dutch goods and pressing on the Rhine frontier. The Dutch regents, who feared both a French attack and an English alliance with France, agreed that William should intervene, in part because the alternative, to wait for hostile action, seemed worse. The decision was therefore taken by the States of Holland and the States General, not by William alone, which is why Dutch historians describe the expedition as a Dutch undertaking as well as an Orange one. The Dutch role in 1688 and the consequences for the English settlement are examined in Division IX.

William's death on 8 March 1702 ended the succession of Orange stadtholders in the Republic's central provinces, and in the following weeks the States of Holland, Zeeland, and other provinces decided to leave the office vacant. This second stadtholderless period lasted until 1747. It was dominated by Heinsius until 1720 and then by a succession of regent leaders, and it coincided with a transformation of the Republic's position. The Dutch carried heavy burdens in the War of the Spanish Succession, and their debts rose, while Britain, whose Parliament now controlled a larger and more flexible fiscal system, took the lead in the alliance. The relationship that William had fostered between the two states persisted, but it was now one in which Britain was the senior partner.

Section 77.22

James VI and I, the Union of Crowns, and the Problem of Multiple Kingdoms

When Elizabeth I died in March 1603, her nearest Protestant relative, James VI of Scotland, succeeded as James I of England and Ireland. This created a union of crowns, a personal union in which one man ruled three kingdoms that retained their own parliaments, laws, courts, churches, and officials. It was not a unified state. Scotland and England did not share citizenship in law until a court decision in 1608 (the case of the post-nati) addressed the status of Scots born after the accession, and attempts at a fuller legal and parliamentary union failed in the early years of the reign, in the face of English fears of Scottish competition and Scottish fears of absorption.

James had considerable experience of government in Scotland, where he had managed a turbulent nobility and a strong Presbyterian church, and he brought a conviction that kings ruled by divine right, a theory he set out in writings such as The True Law of Free Monarchies. Yet he also understood the limits of royal power in practice, and he governed England through a combination of patronage, favorites, and bargaining. His court spent lavishly, which created fiscal problems, and his promotion of Scottish and favored courtiers aroused resentment.

The Irish kingdom was governed by a Lord Deputy in Dublin and a parliament dominated by a Protestant minority. After the defeat of Gaelic lords in the Nine Years' War, and the departure of Hugh O'Neill and others in the Flight of the Earls in 1607, the crown encouraged the plantation of Ulster with Scottish and English Protestant settlers, creating a new society and new grievances among the dispossessed Gaelic Irish and the Old English Catholics of the Pale. These tensions made Ireland a persistent source of insecurity.

The union of crowns thus created a structural problem that would shape the entire century. Decisions made in one kingdom, such as religious policy or taxation, could trigger resistance in another, and a monarch who tried to impose uniformity risked provoking all three. Historians have called this the "problem of multiple kingdoms," a framework developed in the late twentieth century that has transformed the study of the period by showing that the so-called English Civil War was also a war of three kingdoms.

The three kingdoms differed in their institutions, and the differences explain why a measure acceptable in one could be explosive in another. England and Wales had a bicameral Parliament, in which the Lords Spiritual and Temporal sat in one House and elected members for counties and boroughs in the other, and a common law administered by royal judges and by local justices of the peace drawn from the gentry. Executive authority was exercised through the Privy Council, and prerogative courts such as Star Chamber and the Court of High Commission handled political and ecclesiastical offences. Scotland had a single-chamber Parliament of nobles, commissioners of the shires, and burgesses, in which the committee called the Lords of the Articles had long allowed the crown to manage the agenda. Scotland had its own law, its own Privy Council in Edinburgh, and a Kirk whose General Assembly claimed spiritual independence. Ireland had a Parliament in Dublin whose legislation could, under Poynings' Law, be initiated only with the approval of the English Privy Council, and an executive led by a Lord Deputy appointed in London.

James tried to bring these arrangements closer together. In October 1604 he proclaimed that he would henceforth use the style King of Great Britain, a title with no basis in law that was meant to prefigure a union. Commissioners from both kingdoms met in 1604 and 1606 to discuss a closer union, and the English House of Commons, concerned about the position of Scots in English trade and law, refused to go beyond limited measures. The status of Scots born after 1603 was settled by a judicial decision in Calvin's Case in 1608, which held that they were subjects of the English crown and could inherit English land, a decision resting on allegiance to the king, not on any merger of the two realms. In Ireland, James confirmed the plantation of Ulster after 1609, settling English and Scottish Protestants on land that had been confiscated from Gaelic lords who had fled or been outlawed, a policy that created a new society in the north and left deep resentments among the dispossessed and among the Old English Catholics who felt excluded from royal favor.

Section 77.23

James I, Parliament, Religion, and the Fiscal Problems of Monarchy

The English crown's fiscal position was weak. Ordinary revenue from crown lands, customs duties, and feudal dues was inadequate for the expenses of a court and for war, and taxation required the consent of Parliament, which met only when summoned. Inflation eroded the real value of subsidies, and the assessment of taxable wealth had not kept up with changes in the economy. James inherited debts from Elizabeth's wars, and his court's expenditure increased them. He sought additional revenue through impositions, additional customs duties imposed on the crown's authority, which the courts upheld in Bate's Case in 1606 but which Parliament challenged as taxation without consent.

This dispute illustrates a broader constitutional problem. The royal prerogative, the powers belonging to the crown by custom and law, was recognized, but its limits were disputed. Parliament claimed privileges, including freedom of speech and the right to be consulted on taxation, which kings regarded as grants rather than rights. Efforts to settle the issue, such as the Great Contract of 1610, which would have exchanged feudal dues for a fixed revenue, failed. Parliament was dissolved in 1611, and again after the "Addled Parliament" of 1614, and James governed for most of the period without it.

Religion added tension. At the Hampton Court Conference in January 1604, James heard Puritan demands for further reform of the Church of England and largely rejected them, although he agreed to a new translation of the Bible, published in 1611. The Gunpowder Plot of 5 November 1605, a conspiracy by a group of Catholics to blow up Parliament and the king, intensified anti-Catholic feeling and led to harsher penal laws. Many Protestants distrusted any royal leniency toward Catholics, an anxiety that became acute when James pursued a Spanish marriage for his son Charles in the early 1620s, at a time when the Thirty Years' War seemed to threaten Protestantism in Europe.

These tensions did not make civil war inevitable. James's court politics were often skillful, and parliamentary opposition was frequently about specific grievances, not about a different constitution. But the disputes created precedents and habits of mistrust. The reign left to Charles I a monarchy with unresolved fiscal problems, a church divided between Calvinist and emerging anti-Calvinist currents, and a Parliament whose members believed that their liberties were under threat.

The history of James's Parliaments displays a recurring pattern of fiscal demand and political bargaining. Parliament met in 1604 to 1610, in the Addled Parliament of 1614, which passed no legislation, and then not again until 1621, when the king needed money for the defense of the Palatinate after his son-in-law Frederick V lost Bohemia and the Rhine territories in the opening phase of the Thirty Years' War. In 1621 the Commons, while voting subsidies, revived the process of impeachment and used it against the Lord Chancellor, Francis Bacon, who was convicted of taking gifts from litigants. They also asserted in a Protestation that their privileges were their birthright, and James, who regarded the claim as presumptuous, tore the page from the Commons Journal and dissolved Parliament. The affair showed that the House of Commons was prepared to defend privileges that the crown regarded as concessions.

Religion and foreign policy converged in the early 1620s. James's pursuit of a Spanish marriage for Prince Charles, supported by his favorite Buckingham for a time, was widely feared in Protestant opinion, which also believed that a Spanish alliance would endanger the cause of the elector Palatine. When the negotiations collapsed in 1623, Parliament pressed for war with Spain, and the Parliament of 1624 voted subsidies and supported the break. The result, which James did not live to see through, was a war for which the crown had inadequate resources. He died in March 1625, leaving his son a war, a debt, a favorite who had become the object of parliamentary hostility, and a church in which Calvinist and anti-Calvinist currents were beginning to diverge.

Historical Vocabulary
Royal Prerogative and Parliamentary Privilege

Royal prerogative refers to the powers and rights that belonged to the crown by the common law and custom of the realm, such as the right to summon and dismiss Parliament, to conduct foreign policy, to command the armed forces, and to appoint officials and judges. Seventeenth-century lawyers and kings disagreed about its limits, especially whether the crown could in an emergency raise money or imprison subjects without parliamentary approval. It is not synonymous with "absolutism," and English monarchs generally claimed to rule under the law.

Parliamentary privilege refers to the rights claimed by Parliament and its members, including freedom of speech in debate, freedom from arrest during sessions, and the right to petition and to control its own proceedings. Kings often described these as concessions, while members described them as ancient inheritance. The disputes between prerogative and privilege underlie much of the seventeenth-century conflict, and neither term should be translated into modern ideas of executive and legislative power.

Section 77.24

Charles I, the Petition of Right, and the Crisis of Parliamentary Consent

Charles I succeeded in March 1625. Shy, devout, and committed to an ordered, ceremonial church, he relied heavily on his favorite, George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham, who had dominated his father's court. Charles inherited a war with Spain and soon became involved in a war with France as well. The military campaigns, including an expedition to Cadiz in 1625 and a failed relief attempt at La Rochelle in 1627, were costly and unsuccessful, and Parliament, which blamed Buckingham, granted insufficient funds and attacked the favorite.

To raise money without parliamentary consent, Charles imposed a Forced Loan in 1626–1627, a demand that wealthy subjects lend money to the crown, and imprisoned those who refused. In the Five Knights' Case in 1627 the judges declined to order the release of prisoners held by the king's special command. Soldiers and sailors were billeted on civilian households, and martial law was applied in some areas. These measures were widely regarded as violations of the rights of subjects to property and liberty.

In 1628, Parliament, led by lawyers and gentry such as Sir Edward Coke, drafted the Petition of Right, which asserted that no one should be compelled to pay any tax, loan, or gift without common consent by act of Parliament, that no free man should be imprisoned without cause shown, that soldiers should not be billeted on subjects against their will, and that martial law should not be used in time of peace. Charles accepted it in June 1628 in return for a grant of subsidies, though he later claimed that it did not limit his essential prerogatives. Buckingham was assassinated in August 1628.

Relations collapsed in 1629. In March, when Charles ordered adjournment, some members held the Speaker in his chair while Parliament passed resolutions against religious innovation and against taxation not authorized by Parliament. The king dissolved Parliament and imprisoned several members. The crisis of 1629 was not a rejection of monarchy, but a conflict over whether the crown could act without parliamentary consent in matters of money and liberty. It prepared the eleven years during which Charles would govern without calling Parliament.

The political significance of the measures of 1626 and 1627 lay in their combination. Charles's first Parliaments, those of 1625 and 1626, refused to grant tonnage and poundage for more than a year and attacked Buckingham, who was impeached by the Commons in 1626, and Charles dissolved them to protect him. To raise funds for the war with Spain and later with France, the government imposed the Forced Loan of 1626 to 1627, assessed on subjects according to the subsidy rolls, and imprisoned or impressed into the army those who refused. Five knights who contested their detention without cause shown sought a writ of habeas corpus, and in the Five Knights' Case of 1627 the judges held that the king's special command was a sufficient cause for imprisonment, a ruling that alarmed lawyers and gentry. Soldiers raised for the expeditions to Cadiz and to La Rochelle were billeted in private households in coastal counties, and martial law was used against soldiers and sailors, with disorders and complaints multiplying.

The Parliament of 1628 met in this atmosphere. Led by Sir Edward Coke, Sir Thomas Wentworth, and Sir John Eliot, the Commons first proposed a bill and then, when the Lords sought a compromise that would leave the king's discretion intact, a Petition of Right. Charles first gave an evasive answer, and only after the Commons turned to a remonstrance against Buckingham did he give the formal royal assent in June, and Parliament then granted five subsidies. The king's later conduct, in particular the collection of tonnage and poundage without parliamentary grant, led to the quarrel of the session of 1629. On 2 March the Speaker, Sir John Finch, was held in his chair by Denzil Holles and Benjamin Valentine while Eliot's three resolutions were read, declaring that anyone who introduced religious innovation or who advised or paid tonnage and poundage without parliamentary consent was an enemy of the kingdom. Charles dissolved Parliament and imprisoned Eliot and other leaders; Eliot died in the Tower in 1632.

Section 77.25

The Personal Rule, 1629–1640, and Government without Parliament

Charles governed from 1629 to 1640 without summoning Parliament, a period known as the Personal Rule or, by its opponents, the Eleven Years' Tyranny. Calling a Parliament was a royal prerogative, and in itself ruling without one was not illegal, but it deprived the crown of the principal means of raising extraordinary taxation. Charles made peace with France and Spain in 1629 and 1630, which reduced military costs, and he relied on existing revenue and expedients such as fines for knighthood, extension of forest laws, monopolies, and customs duties collected without parliamentary approval.

The most important expedient was ship money. This was a levy traditionally imposed on coastal towns to supply ships for defense. In 1634 and 1635 Charles extended it to inland counties as well, collecting funds for the navy that were in effect an annual tax. Most subjects paid, and collection was efficient in the early years, which suggests the relative competence of Charles's government. But resistance grew, and in 1637 to 1638 the gentleman John Hampden contested the levy in court. The judges decided narrowly, seven to five, in the king's favor, a result that publicly raised doubts about the legality of taxation without consent.

Religious policy generated even more resistance. William Laud, who became Archbishop of Canterbury in 1633, promoted ceremonial uniformity, the placing of altars at the east end of churches, rails around them, and the authority of bishops, and he opposed Calvinist predestinarian teaching. Critics regarded these reforms as popery by stealth. Church courts and the Court of High Commission enforced conformity, and the Court of Star Chamber punished critics, as in the mutilation of William Prynne, Henry Burton, and John Bastwick in 1637 for writings against the bishops. Many godly Protestants emigrated, notably to New England.

The Personal Rule was neither a modern dictatorship nor an absolute monarchy on the French model. It operated through local gentry as justices of the peace and sheriffs, whose cooperation was essential, and it had no standing army. Its strengths were competence in finance and order, its weaknesses the lack of consent and the dependence on cooperation of local elites, who could withdraw it. When a crisis came, Charles had no reserve of money or force.

The Personal Rule was not only a fiscal regime but a program of government. Charles governed through a small circle of ministers: Richard Weston, Lord Treasurer until 1635, who restored the crown's finances by economy and by the expedients of the time; William Laud, who became Archbishop of Canterbury in 1633; and Thomas Wentworth, who became Lord Deputy of Ireland in 1633 and pursued a policy he called Thorough, aimed at making royal government efficient and incorruptible. In Ireland, Wentworth obtained subsidies from the Irish Parliament in 1634 to 1635 by promising the Graces, concessions to Catholic landowners, which he then failed to confirm, while pursuing inquiries into land titles that threatened the holdings of Old English and Gaelic proprietors and of the Protestant New English. He raised an army of about nine thousand men in 1640 that would later cause deep alarm in England.

In England, the administration of local government also tightened. The Book of Orders of 1631 required justices of the peace to report regularly on poor relief, the regulation of markets, and the maintenance of roads, and the sheriffs collected ship money in their counties. Ship money was first levied on port towns in 1634, in an attempt to fit out a fleet to protect shipping, and extended to the entire kingdom in 1635, with writs issued each year until 1639. Collection was initially efficient, producing on the order of two hundred thousand pounds a year in the early years, but by 1639 resistance had increased and arrears had accumulated. The judgment in Hampden's Case in 1638, in which seven of twelve judges upheld the king's right to demand the levy in time of danger, was a technical victory that damaged the crown's reputation, because the narrow majority and the arguments of the dissenting judges suggested that the king's claim was dubious.

Religious policy accounted for much of the unpopularity of the regime. Laud's insistence on ceremony, uniformity, and clerical authority, his attacks on Calvinist predestinarianism, and his use of High Commission and Star Chamber against critics aroused fears of popery. In 1637, Prynne, Burton, and Bastwick, a lawyer, a minister, and a physician, were sentenced in Star Chamber to the pillory, the loss of their ears, and imprisonment for publications against the bishops, and the crowds that watched their punishment in London expressed sympathy with them. A sizable emigration, perhaps in the order of twenty thousand people in the 1630s, carried English Puritans to New England and the Caribbean. These events suggest that the regime provoked opposition less by tyranny in the modern sense than by a combination of religious innovation and financial expedients that appeared to threaten the rule of law and the Protestant church.

Section 77.26

Scotland, the National Covenant, and the Bishops' Wars

Charles visited Scotland in 1633 for his coronation and alienated Scottish opinion by his ceremonial preferences and by plans to restore church lands to the crown, which threatened Scottish nobles who had acquired them. In 1637 he and Laud introduced a new Book of Common Prayer for Scotland, issued by royal authority without consultation of the General Assembly or Parliament. On 23 July 1637, the reading of the new service in St Giles' Cathedral, Edinburgh, provoked a riot, and resistance spread across the kingdom.

In February 1638, Scottish nobles, ministers, burgesses, and others signed the National Covenant, a document which reaffirmed the Protestant faith, rejected innovations in worship not authorized by the Kirk's assemblies and parliament, and committed signatories to defend religion and the king. It was not a rejection of the king, but of his religious policy. A General Assembly met at Glasgow in November 1638, defied the king's order to dissolve, and abolished bishops. The Covenanters organized an effective government through committees known as the Tables and mobilized an army, helped by officers who had served in the Thirty Years' War.

Charles resolved to use force. In the First Bishops' War (1639), he marched north with an inadequately funded army and made a truce, the Pacification of Berwick. In 1640 he summoned a Parliament in England, the Short Parliament, in April to obtain funds, but dissolved it in May when members insisted on addressing grievances first. The Second Bishops' War followed in August 1640, when a Scottish army crossed the Tweed, defeated an English force at Newburn, and occupied Newcastle and the northeast. By the Treaty of Ripon in October, Charles agreed to pay the Scottish army's costs, an expense he could meet only by calling another Parliament.

The crisis demonstrates the structure of the three kingdoms problem. A religious policy designed for uniformity provoked resistance in the kingdom with the strongest independent church, and that resistance made the king dependent on his English subjects, who also had grievances. The Scots were not seeking to overthrow monarchy or to impose Presbyterianism on England at this stage, but their actions shifted the balance of power and created an opportunity for English critics.

The Scottish crisis began as a response to religious policy but became a constitutional revolution. Before 1637 resentment had already been fueled by the Act of Revocation of 1625, which threatened the titles of nobles who held former church lands, and by the Five Articles of Perth, imposed by James in 1618, which required practices such as kneeling at communion that many Presbyterians rejected. In 1637 the new Prayer Book, drawn up by Scottish bishops under Laud's influence and imposed by royal proclamation without consultation of either the General Assembly or Parliament, provoked a series of protests, of which the riot at St Giles' on 23 July was the most celebrated. Later tradition attributes the first throwing of a stool to Jenny Geddes, but the contemporary evidence for the story is thin and it should be treated as part of the legend of the Covenant.

The leaders of the opposition were skilled organizers. Nobles such as the Earl of Rothes and Lord Loudoun, the minister Alexander Henderson, and the lawyer Archibald Johnston of Wariston drafted the National Covenant, which was signed at Greyfriars Kirk in Edinburgh at the end of February 1638 and then circulated through the country. It combined a Protestant confession, a list of statutes against popery, and a bond to defend religion and the king's person. The Covenanters' administration worked through committees known as the Tables, which collected funds, appointed officers, and mobilized supporters. When the king sent the Marquess of Hamilton to negotiate, the Covenanters demanded a free General Assembly and Parliament, and at the Glasgow Assembly of November and December 1638 they abolished the episcopate, repudiated the Prayer Book, and deposed the bishops, ignoring the royal commissioner's attempt to dissolve the meeting.

The wars that followed exposed the weakness of the king's position. In 1639, Charles assembled an army at York with insufficient funds and unwilling troops, and the Covenanters under Alexander Leslie, a veteran of the Swedish service, held strong positions on the border. The Pacification of Berwick in June left the issues unresolved. Wentworth, now Earl of Strafford, urged a firm response, and in April 1640 the king summoned an English Parliament, the Short Parliament, which refused supplies until its grievances had been heard and was dissolved after three weeks. In August 1640, the Scottish army crossed the Tweed, routed an English force at Newburn on 28 August, and occupied Newcastle, cutting London's coal supply. By the Treaty of Ripon in October, the king agreed to maintain the Scottish army at his own expense until a settlement was reached. Since he could not do so without parliamentary money, he had to call Parliament again, and the Long Parliament, which met in November 1640, was the result.

Section 77.27

The Long Parliament, the Irish Rebellion, and the Collapse of Political Settlement

The Long Parliament assembled in November 1640 in a mood of broad agreement that the policies of the Personal Rule must be reversed. Leaders such as John Pym directed attacks on the king's ministers. Thomas Wentworth, Earl of Strafford, Charles's chief adviser in Ireland and later in England, was impeached, then condemned by a bill of attainder and executed in May 1641, with the king's reluctant assent. Laud was imprisoned. Parliament abolished the Court of Star Chamber and High Commission, declared ship money and other levies illegal, and passed a Triennial Act requiring Parliament to meet at least every three years, and a measure preventing the dissolution of the current Parliament without its own consent.

On these reforms there was substantial consensus, including among future royalists. The harder issue was the church. Many members wished to abolish or reform episcopacy, a proposal presented in the Root and Branch petition, while others defended bishops and the Prayer Book. This divided Parliament, and the division mattered because it produced the party that would rally to the king in 1642.

In October 1641, a rebellion broke out in Ireland. A group of Gaelic Irish leaders in Ulster, joined by Old English Catholics, rose against the plantation settlement and the Protestant government in Dublin. Many Protestant settlers were killed or driven from their homes, and exaggerated reports of massacre, spread in print in England and Scotland, inflamed opinion and created fear that Catholics were conspiring with the king. Modern scholarship has shown that the number of Protestants killed was far lower than early propaganda claimed, though the violence and displacement were real, and Catholic civilians suffered terribly in the subsequent reprisals. The rebellion raised an acute question: who should control the army to suppress it?

The Grand Remonstrance, a long list of grievances against the king's government, was passed by the House of Commons in November 1641 by a narrow margin of 159 to 148 and published to the public, an unprecedented step. It reflected the radical wing's distrust of Charles and demanded parliamentary approval of royal ministers and church reform. Its narrow passage, and the opposition of members who thought it went too far, shows that Parliament was already splitting. Public opinion, mobilized by petitions, crowds, and a rapidly growing print culture, added to the uncertainty. By the end of 1641 trust between the Crown and Parliament had broken down, and the quarrel over control of the armed forces could not be settled by negotiation.

The Long Parliament's first months were dominated by consensus on the punishment of ministers and the repeal of the instruments of the Personal Rule. The Commons impeached Strafford on 11 November 1640 and later Laud. When the impeachment proved legally difficult, the Commons turned to a bill of attainder, which the Lords passed under the pressure of crowds and of revelations of Strafford's advice to the king to use the Irish army in England. Charles, who had assured Strafford that he would not allow his execution, assented on 10 May 1641 and Strafford was beheaded on 12 May. The Triennial Act of February 1641 required Parliament to meet at least once every three years even without a royal summons. A further statute, passed in May, forbade the dissolution of the existing Parliament without its own consent. Acts of 1641 abolished the Court of Star Chamber and the Court of High Commission, declared ship money illegal, and limited the crown's power to raise distraint of knighthood and forest fines.

The religious question divided the consensus. A petition of December 1640, said to have been signed by about fifteen thousand Londoners, demanded the abolition of episcopacy root and branch, and in May 1641 a bill to that effect was introduced in the Commons. Many members, including the future royalist Edward Hyde and Lord Falkland, supported reforms of ecclesiastical abuses and limits on the bishops' secular power but not the abolition of episcopacy. The Lords, in which the bishops sat, resisted. In the autumn, the issue of control of religion combined with distrust of the king's intentions. In the summer, Charles visited Scotland to concede almost all that the Covenanters wished, partly in the hope of gaining their support, and the discovery of the Incident, a supposed plot against Scottish leaders, increased suspicion that the king was plotting against his critics.

The Irish Rebellion transformed the crisis. On 23 October 1641, Gaelic Irish leaders in Ulster, including Sir Phelim O'Neill and Rory O'More, launched a coordinated rising, aiming to seize Dublin Castle and the major strongholds and to secure redress of grievances over land and religion. The attempt on Dublin failed when it was betrayed, but in Ulster the insurgents drove out Protestant settlers, and violence, expulsions, and killings spread. Old English Catholics of the Pale, initially hesitant, joined the rising by the end of the year, and in 1642 the Confederation of Kilkenny was formed. In England and Scotland, accounts of massacres were printed in pamphlets with fantastic estimates of the dead, and thousands of depositions were later collected from survivors. Modern scholarship reduces the number of Protestant settlers killed by direct violence to the order of several thousand, with many more dying of exposure and disease after being driven out, but the event convinced many in England that a popish conspiracy menaced the kingdom, and it made the control of the army that would suppress the rising the most urgent question in politics.

The Grand Remonstrance, introduced by Pym and his allies in November 1641, summarized the grievances and demanded that the king employ only councilors approved by Parliament and that a synod of divines reform the church. It passed on 22 November by 159 to 148, after a stormy debate that lasted through the night. The royalist opposition, which was now organized around Hyde and Falkland, regarded the document as an attack on the monarchy itself. Charles returned from Scotland to a London in ferment and responded with a measured reply, but the appointment of a controversial officer as Lieutenant of the Tower, the presence of bishops being jostled by crowds, and the bishops' protest of December that parliamentary votes taken in their absence were invalid all deepened the crisis. By the end of the year, a part of the political nation had concluded that Pym's party was trying to destroy the constitution, and another part that the king would use force to destroy Parliament. The Long Parliament had restored a measure of constitutional government in 1641, but it had not restored trust.

Division VI · Sections 77.28–77.33

The Civil Wars, Parliament, and Constitutional Revolution. The British Civil Wars involved competing constitutional claims, several theaters of fighting, religious disputes, new military institutions, and unprecedented challenges to monarchy. They must not be reduced to a contest between a democratic Parliament and an absolutist king.

Section 77.28

The Outbreak of the Civil Wars and the Competing Claims of King and Parliament

On 4 January 1642, Charles entered the House of Commons with armed men to arrest five members of the Commons, including Pym and Hampden, together with a peer, on a charge of treason. The members had been warned and had withdrawn to the City of London, and the king's action, a violation of parliamentary privilege, convinced many that he could not be trusted. He left London on 10 January and would not return until he was a prisoner.

The conflict turned on control of the militia, the county forces that could be raised for defense. In March 1642, Parliament passed a Militia Ordinance, without the royal assent that the constitution required for a bill, claiming that in an emergency the two Houses could act for the kingdom's safety. Charles responded by issuing commissions of array, the traditional royal authority to raise forces. Each side thus claimed legal authority to command the armed forces, a question that neither law nor precedent clearly resolved.

Both sides appealed to the public in print. In June 1642 Parliament sent the Nineteen Propositions, demanding parliamentary control over the great officers of state, the militia, and the church, and the king answered that he would not reduce himself to a figurehead like the Doge of Venice, arguing that the English constitution was a mixture of king, Lords, and Commons and that Parliament's demands would destroy that balance. The royalist argument thus defended the ancient constitution, while Parliament argued that it was protecting law and religion from a king misled by evil counselors. Neither side initially sought a republic.

The war began in August 1642, when Charles raised his standard at Nottingham on 22 August. The first major battle, at Edgehill on 23 October, was inconclusive. People divided for many reasons, including religion, local loyalties, family ties, economic interest, and fear of disorder, and many tried to stay neutral. The war did not begin as a conflict between social classes or as a clear confrontation between two programs.

The decisive escalation of 1642 can be followed in a series of short steps. The king's attempt to arrest the Five Members on 4 January, a breach of privilege that lent credibility to parliamentary fears, was followed by his departure from London and by the withdrawal of the Five Members to the City, where the London authorities protected them. In the following weeks, Parliament demanded control of the Tower and of the fortress of Hull, and when in April the governor of Hull, Sir John Hotham, refused the king entry, Charles's cause in the north suffered a symbolic and practical blow. The Militia Ordinance of 5 March claimed that the two Houses could, in an emergency, command the trained bands without the king's assent, and Parliament appointed the Earl of Essex as lord general in July. Charles, for his part, issued commissions of array to royalist magnates to raise forces in the counties.

Behind the legal arguments stood an unresolved question about where authority lay. Parliament's Nineteen Propositions of June 1642 demanded the right to approve royal councilors, the control of the militia, and a reform of the church, and the king's Answer of that month, drafted with the help of Falkland and the lawyer Sir John Colepeper, described the English constitution as a mixture of monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy, in which each element checked the others, and argued that the Propositions would reduce the king to a cipher. Parliament's counterclaim, expressed in declarations of the spring and summer, was that the two Houses could declare the law and could act for the safety of the kingdom against a king misled by evil counselors. Each side thus claimed to defend the constitution against the other, and each could point to precedents. The moderate royalists and moderate parliamentarians who tried to avert war, through petitions and attempts at mediation, found their room for maneuver diminishing.

When war began, loyalties were not mapped neatly by region or class. The king raised his standard at Nottingham on 22 August 1642 with a small force. The first major battle, at Edgehill on 23 October, ended without a clear result, and Charles's army advanced toward London, only to be confronted at Turnham Green on 13 November by the trained bands of the City and Essex's army, and to withdraw to Oxford. Many people tried to remain neutral, and local treaties of neutrality in several counties in the winter of 1642 and 1643 had to be overridden by Parliament and the king. The war therefore began as a contest between two claims to legitimacy that both sides regarded as constitutional, and neither side at that point wished to abolish monarchy.

Section 77.29

The Political, Religious, and Social Geography of the Wars

Parliament controlled London, the southeast, the ports, and most of the navy, which gave it greater financial resources, including customs revenue and the credit of the City. The king's strength lay in the north, the west, and Wales, and he made Oxford his headquarters. This division was not absolute, and many counties were divided, with local committees and garrisons on either side. Towns such as Bristol, Hull, and Gloucester changed hands or endured sieges. Clubmen movements arose in 1645 in parts of the south and west, in which local people tried to defend their districts against both armies.

War financing and supply imposed heavy burdens. Parliament developed new taxes, including the excise on goods and the monthly assessment on property, and established county committees to collect them and to sequester royalist estates. The king relied on contributions, loans, and plate donated by supporters, and on assessments at Oxford. Soldiers were quartered on households, and local people were subject to requisitions, plunder, and the destruction of buildings and crops. Disease, especially typhus and dysentery, killed many, and mortality was high relative to population. Recent scholarship has estimated that a higher proportion of the English population died in these wars than in the First World War, though the figures are uncertain and depend on how deaths from disease are counted.

Women were affected in many ways. Some managed households and estates when men were away or killed, some petitioned Parliament, some defended houses, as did Lady Brilliana Harley at Brampton Bryan, and some followed the armies or, as camp followers, provided labor. Women also played prominent roles in religious sects and petitioning movements. The experience of war displaced families and communities. The wars were fought in Scotland and Ireland as well, with distinct dynamics: in Ireland, the Confederation of Kilkenny, founded in 1642 by Catholic leaders, established a quasi-government and allied variously with the king and against Protestant forces, while in Scotland the Covenanters, then a royalist faction led by Montrose, fought for control.

Religious difference also shaped allegiance. Many supporters of Parliament wanted further reformation, often called Puritan, though they differed over what shape the church should take, while many royalists defended the Prayer Book and bishops, and others supported the king as the lawful ruler or out of loyalty to law and order. Generalizations about the "causes" of allegiance should therefore be treated with caution.

The military and administrative organization of the two sides reveals what the war required of local society. Parliament created regional associations of counties, of which the Eastern Association of Norfolk, Suffolk, Essex, Cambridgeshire, Hertfordshire, and later Lincolnshire, formed in 1642 to 1643 and commanded from 1643 by the Earl of Manchester, was the most effective, pooling taxation and troops under a committee system. County committees assessed the property of inhabitants, collected the weekly or monthly assessment, and sequestered the estates of royalists, selling or leasing them for the war effort. In July 1643, at Pym's urging, Parliament imposed an excise on commodities such as beer, which was unpopular but productive and which, as the later sections show, outlasted the war. The king's administration at Oxford relied on contributions, forced loans, the sale of honors, and a system of county commissioners, and its capacity to collect was weaker than Parliament's.

The experience of civilians varied widely. Garrison towns and besieged cities suffered the most. Gloucester resisted a royalist siege in 1643, and Bristol, Leicester, and Basing House were taken by storm with significant civilian suffering. Armies requisitioned food, horses, and fodder, billeted soldiers on households, and levied contributions in cash. Epidemic disease, especially typhus and dysentery, accompanied armies and spread among civilians. Historians estimate that deaths from the fighting and from war-related disease in England and Wales reached the low hundreds of thousands, a larger proportion of the population than in some later wars, though the figures are uncertain and depend on assumptions about disease. In 1645, groups of villagers in the southern and western counties, calling themselves Clubmen, organized to protect their neighborhoods from both armies, a reminder that many ordinary people wanted an end to the fighting more than the victory of either side.

The wars in Scotland and Ireland were not merely extensions of the English conflict. In Scotland, the Covenanters, who had signed the Solemn League and Covenant, sent an army to England in 1644, while a royalist rising led by the Marquess of Montrose in the Highlands, supported by Irish troops under Alasdair MacColla, won victories at Tippermuir in 1644 and Kilsyth in 1645 before it was defeated at Philiphaugh in September 1645. In Ireland, the Confederation of Kilkenny, which met at Kilkenny in 1642 as a general assembly of Catholic clergy, nobles, and gentry, established an executive, a system of taxation, and an army, and it sought both religious toleration for Catholics and the recognition of the Irish Parliament's independence. It negotiated with the king through the Earl of Ormond, and it divided between those who wished to settle quickly with the king and those, supported by the papal nuncio Rinuccini after 1645, who demanded greater guarantees for the Catholic church. These divisions meant that Irish politics could not be treated as a mere appendage of the English question.

Historical Debate 2

Were the British Civil Wars Principally Constitutional, Religious, Fiscal, or Inter-Kingdom Conflicts?

The question: How should historians weigh and connect the causes of the war that began in 1642?

Interpretation A: Constitutional and religious causes dominated. Historians in this tradition stress disputes over prerogative, consent, and the nature of the church, and argue that members of Parliament and others sincerely believed that law and Protestantism were at stake.
Interpretation B: Structural and inter-kingdom pressures were decisive. Others stress the fiscal weakness of the crown, the problem of governing multiple kingdoms, and the effects of the Scottish and Irish crises, arguing that without them the English disputes might have been contained.

Evidence: Both rely on parliamentary journals, state papers, letters, sermons, and pamphlets. They differ in emphasis, and many historians combine the factors, treating the war as the result of a convergence of long-term strains and short-term contingencies.

Why it matters: Treating the war as only an English constitutional dispute would hide the role of Scotland and Ireland, while treating it only as a structural crisis would minimize the role of ideas and choices.

Section 77.30

The New Model Army, Military Reform, and Parliament's Victory

The early years of the war were indecisive. The royalists had initial advantages in cavalry and leadership, notably through Prince Rupert, but could not take London, and Parliament's armies, often locally organized, suffered from divided command and uneven discipline. The alliance with the Scots in 1643 (Section 77.31) shifted the balance, and at Marston Moor on 2 July 1644, a combined parliamentary and Scottish army defeated a royalist force and gained control of the north of England. Cromwell's cavalry played an important role.

Yet the parliamentary command seemed unable to exploit its victories, and quarrels among commanders led to a reorganization. In 1645, Parliament passed the Self-Denying Ordinance, which required members of both Houses to resign military commands, and created the New Model Army, a single force of professional soldiers, financed by regular taxation, under Sir Thomas Fairfax, with Cromwell as commander of cavalry by exception. Its officers were promoted partly on merit, and the army was paid and supplied more regularly than its predecessors, though pay arrears later became a grievance.

At Naseby on 14 June 1645, the New Model Army defeated Charles's main army, capturing his baggage and papers, which included correspondence showing his negotiations with Irish Catholics and foreign powers, and Parliament published these. The royalist cause collapsed in the following year, and Charles surrendered to the Scots at Newark in May 1646. Oxford fell in June.

The New Model Army was important not only as a military instrument but as a political force. Many of its soldiers and officers were religious Independents or sectarians, who believed in liberty of conscience and distrusted Presbyterian uniformity, and they had endured years of danger. After victory they expected their service to be rewarded with a settlement that protected their religious freedom and their arrears of pay. The Army's emergence as an independent actor transformed the politics of the revolution.

The creation of the New Model Army combined political and military reorganization. In December 1644, after quarrels between Manchester and Cromwell over the conduct of the campaign following the second battle of Newbury, Parliament's leaders introduced the Self-Denying Ordinance, which required members of both Houses to resign their commissions within forty days. A companion ordinance of February 1645 created a single national army, with a planned strength of about twenty-two thousand men, consisting of foot, horse, and dragoons, under Sir Thomas Fairfax as commander in chief, Philip Skippon as major-general of foot, and with the cavalry command left vacant at first. The Lords accepted the Self-Denying Ordinance in April 1645, in a form that allowed reappointment, and Cromwell, who was exempted from resignation, was given the command of cavalry at the time of Naseby.

The Army's strength lay in its organization. Its units were financed from regular assessments assigned to specific counties, and regiments were standardized and drilled. Soldiers were paid more reliably than in the old armies, at least in the first years, and discipline, though not perfect, was stricter. Many officers were promoted for ability and zeal, and a considerable number of soldiers and officers were religious Independents or sectarians. The cavalry, trained by Cromwell and Fairfax, was especially effective in the charge and in rallying after it, a quality that mattered at Naseby on 14 June 1645, when the royalist army under the king and Prince Rupert was defeated and its baggage train captured. The captured royal correspondence, published by Parliament as The King's Cabinet Opened, revealed Charles's dealings with Irish Catholics and foreign powers and damaged his reputation.

The campaigns that followed showed the Army's ability to conduct sieges and to move. At Langport on 10 July 1645, Fairfax destroyed the royalist western army, and in September he stormed Bristol, where Prince Rupert surrendered. Clearing garrisons in the west, taking Exeter and Dartmouth in the winter, and then defeating Hopton at Torrington and forcing his surrender in Cornwall in March 1646 ended organized royalist resistance in England. The king, with Oxford besieged, left the city in disguise in April and surrendered himself to the Scottish army at Newark on 5 May 1646. Oxford fell on 24 June. The first Civil War, in England, was effectively over, but the question of what settlement should follow, and who should enforce it, remained.

Section 77.31

The Solemn League and Covenant and the Problem of the Three Kingdoms

In 1643, facing military difficulty, Parliament sought Scottish help and agreed to the Solemn League and Covenant, signed in September, which committed the signatories to preserving the reformed religion in Scotland and reforming religion in England and Ireland in accordance with scripture and the example of the best reformed churches. That formula left ambiguous whether England would adopt Presbyterianism. The Scots intended uniformity of religion in the three kingdoms on their model, a goal that would cause conflict with English Independents and others.

A Scottish army entered England in January 1644. Its contribution at Marston Moor was crucial. In return, Parliament summoned the Westminster Assembly of Divines, which drafted a Directory of Worship, a confession of faith, and catechisms, and moved toward a Presbyterian church government. But in England many MPs and army officers resisted a rigid national Presbyterian system, preferring more congregational independence, and the Assembly's recommendations were only partly implemented.

Ireland presented another dimension. After 1641, Protestant forces, Confederate Catholic forces, and royalist and parliamentary agents struggled for control. Charles's attempts to obtain Irish Catholic aid through concessions, including the Cessation of 1643, fed the parliamentary claim that he was allied with Catholics, though the actual results were limited. The Irish settlement became a bargaining issue in all negotiations.

The three-kingdom problem prevented any single settlement. Scots wanted the English Parliament to enforce a covenanted church and security guarantees, English Parliamentarians were divided, the king bargained with everyone, and Irish factions pursued their own aims. The attempt to find a settlement in 1646 and 1647, the Newcastle Propositions of 1646 and the later Treaty of Newport negotiations of September to November 1648 among them, repeatedly stalled, because the parties could neither agree on religion nor trust the king.

The alliance with the Scots shaped parliamentary religious policy but also exposed its tensions. The Solemn League and Covenant of September 1643 was drafted partly by Scots, who envisaged the uniformity of religion in three kingdoms on the Presbyterian model, and partly by English negotiators such as Vane, who inserted the phrase about the example of the best reformed churches, leaving room for interpretation. The Westminster Assembly of Divines, which met from July 1643 and included Scottish commissioners without votes, produced a Directory of Public Worship, a Confession of Faith, and catechisms, and it recommended a Presbyterian church structure. Parliament adopted the Directory in 1645 in place of the Prayer Book and passed ordinances to set up presbyteries in some areas. But the House of Commons, jealous of its authority over the church, insisted on its own control, and Independents in Parliament and the army, who wanted the freedom of congregations, resisted the imposition of a national system. The English church settlement thus remained partial.

The 1646 and 1647 proposals illustrate the difficulty of reaching a settlement. In July 1646 the two Houses sent the Newcastle Propositions, to which the Scots assented, to the king at Newcastle. They required him to take the Covenant, to accept the abolition of episcopacy, to surrender control of the militia for twenty years, and to accept the exclusion of leading royalists from pardon. Charles refused to accept them, and he hoped to profit from the division among his opponents. In January 1647 the Scots, having received a payment toward their arrears, handed the king over to the English Parliament's commissioners and withdrew from England. In the meantime, in Ireland, Ormond had concluded a peace with the Confederates in 1646, which was repudiated by the clerical party, so that the Irish situation remained fluid and the king could not rely on it.

The negotiation of 1648 shows that the problem remained after the Second Civil War. When Charles was a prisoner at Carisbrooke Castle on the Isle of Wight, he concluded the secret Engagement with the Scottish commissioners in December 1647. After the defeat of the Engagers, Parliament in September 1648 reopened negotiations with him at Newport on the Isle of Wight, offering terms that were less demanding than those of Newcastle. The negotiations lasted from September to November, and Charles, who played for time, made concessions on the militia and the church while hoping for a rescue. The Treaty of Newport, which Parliament by a majority regarded as an acceptable basis for settlement, was overtaken by the army's decision to remove the members who supported it. The three-kingdom problem, in short, was not solved by any of the offers made to the king, and each attempt to secure a settlement exposed the divergence of aims among Scots, English Presbyterians, Independents, the army, and the king himself.

Section 77.32

Army Politics, the Levellers, and the Putney Debates of 1647

After the first Civil War ended, Parliament, dominated by Presbyterians, sought to disband the army without paying its arrears and to send part of it to Ireland. The soldiers protested, elected representatives called Agitators, and in June 1647 a junior officer, Cornet George Joyce, took the king into the army's custody. The army issued declarations and a proposed settlement, the Heads of the Proposals, and it marched on London in August. Meanwhile, the Levellers, a movement centered on London and on the army, led by John Lilburne, Richard Overton, and William Walwyn, published pamphlets demanding that political authority derive from the people's consent, religious toleration, and legal reform. They did not use the label "Levellers" for themselves, and it was an insult from opponents who accused them of wanting to level property.

In autumn 1647 a draft constitutional proposal, the Agreement of the People, was debated in the army council meeting in the church at Putney, near London, between 28 October and 1 November, with Cromwell and his son-in-law Henry Ireton presiding or leading for the officers, and soldiers and civilian radicals speaking for the Agreement. Notes were taken by the secretary William Clarke and later published as part of the Clarke Papers. Ireton argued that the vote should be limited to those with a permanent, fixed stake in the kingdom, meaning primarily property holders, because those without property might vote to dismantle it. Colonel Thomas Rainborough replied that the poorest man in England had a life to live as the richest and that every man who was to live under a government should have a voice in choosing it.

The debate was not only about voting. It dealt with consent, the meaning of the army's engagements, the authority of the king, and the extent to which an agreement among the people could override existing law and property. Participants disagreed about whether the franchise should extend to all adult males, to all except servants and beneficiaries of charity, or to those with a stake in the realm. The Levellers' views on the franchise were themselves variable, and historians dispute whether they intended to include all men or exclude servants and wage-earners. Women did not figure in the proposals, though women petitioned in the Leveller cause.

The Putney Debates mattered because they revealed that the victorious coalition had no shared constitutional program. Officers, soldiers, and radicals disagreed about property, authority, and revolution. The army grandees, fearing the spread of radical ideas and mutiny, suppressed a mutiny at Corkbush Field in November 1647. Thereafter the radical movement lost its institutional foothold in the army. The debates remain among the most important sources for early modern ideas about political equality, but they should not be read as a modern democratic movement; their participants argued in the language of scripture, natural law, and English liberties.

The Putney Debates arose from a particular conflict between the army and Parliament. In the spring of 1647, Parliament voted to disband most of the New Model, to send part of it to Ireland, and to give the remaining soldiers inadequate guarantees of arrears and indemnity for acts done in the war. The soldiers elected Agitators, representatives of each regiment, and in June, the Army seized the king, issued a Declaration, and demanded the impeachment of eleven Presbyterian leaders. In July, the army council, led by Ireton and Lambert, proposed the Heads of the Proposals, a moderate constitutional settlement that would limit the king's powers, require biennial Parliaments, and provide religious toleration, without proposing a republic. The king rejected it, hoping that the divisions among his opponents would increase, and in the autumn the more radical Agitators, influenced by London Levellers, produced The Case of the Army Truly Stated, a manifesto of 15 October.

The debates in the General Council of the Army at Putney Church, beginning on 28 October 1647, took place with representatives of the Agitators and with civilian radicals such as John Wildman. The chairman was Cromwell, and the officers, including Ireton, defended the army's previous commitments and the principle that property rights were the foundation of the constitution. The soldiers and their civilian allies, among them Edward Sexby, Thomas Rainborough, and Maximilian Petty, argued that the army had fought for the freedom of the people and that the franchise should be extended. Rainborough's statement that the poorest man in England had a life to live as the greatest, and Ireton's reply that the right to vote should belong to those with a permanent stake in the kingdom, are the best known exchanges. But the debate also ranged over the obligations that the army had undertaken in earlier declarations, the meaning of conscience, and whether the king could be trusted. Cromwell, who was concerned to prevent a rupture in the army, sought compromise and referred matters to committees.

The record of the debates is imperfect. William Clarke, the secretary of the council, took notes in shorthand, which were later transcribed, and they preserve the speeches but may omit or abbreviate some. The meetings continued intermittently into early November, and sessions beyond the principal discussions of 28 October to 1 November took place until 11 November, the day on which the king escaped from Hampton Court to the Isle of Wight. The army's leaders, now convinced that the king was plotting, and worried by mutinous behavior in some regiments, reasserted discipline. On 15 November, at a rendezvous at Corkbush Field, Cromwell and Fairfax suppressed mutinous regiments wearing copies of the Agreement in their hats, and one soldier, Richard Arnold, was shot. The Levellers continued to publish and to organize in London, and Lilburne in particular remained a thorn in the side of the regime, but the movement's connection with the army was broken.

Section 77.33

The Second Civil War, Pride's Purge, and the Trial and Execution of Charles I

In December 1647 Charles escaped from army custody to the Isle of Wight and made a secret Engagement with the Scots, promising to establish Presbyterianism for three years in return for military aid. In 1648, royalist risings in Wales, Kent, and Essex, a mutiny of part of the fleet, and a Scottish invasion under the Duke of Hamilton created the Second Civil War. Fairfax suppressed the English risings, and Cromwell defeated the Scots at Preston in August 1648. The army concluded that the king, by starting a second war after being treated generously, had shown himself unfit to be trusted.

Parliament, however, was still negotiating with the king, and a majority voted in December that his concessions were a basis for settlement. On 6 and 7 December 1648, soldiers under Colonel Thomas Pride excluded or arrested over a hundred members, leaving a remnant known as the Rump Parliament. This was a military intervention in the legislature, undertaken by officers who believed that the nation's security required it. The Rump voted to try the king.

The House of Commons passed an ordinance establishing a High Court of Justice, though the House of Lords rejected it and the Commons declared that the people were, under God, the original of all just power and that the Commons had supreme authority. Charles was tried in Westminster Hall in January 1649 and refused to recognize the court's authority, arguing that no earthly power could try a king and that the court represented only a fragment of the people. The court sentenced him as a tyrant, traitor, and murderer, and he was beheaded outside the Banqueting House on 30 January 1649. Fewer than sixty commissioners signed the death warrant, and many named as judges did not attend.

The execution was an unprecedented act. A reigning monarch was tried and put to death under a claim that political authority derived from the people and that rulers were accountable. Yet it was also a minority act, carried out by a purged legislature and an army. Many contemporaries, including many who had opposed the king in war, were horrified, and it divided opinion in all three kingdoms and in Europe. In Scotland, the Covenanters proclaimed Charles II as king. The regicide thus opened a new period of constitutional experimentation but not of consensus.

The Second Civil War of 1648 was a series of related conflicts that revealed the extent of the discontent with the parliamentary regime. In Wales, Colonel Poyer and other parliamentarian officers declared for the king in the spring, and Pembroke Castle held out until Cromwell took it in July. In Kent and Essex, royalists rose and a force under Lord Norwich (George Goring) and Lord Capel seized Colchester, where Fairfax besieged them from June to August, ending with the shooting of the royalist commanders Lucas and Lisle after the surrender. A section of the navy mutinied. In July the Duke of Hamilton led a Scottish Engager army into England, only to be defeated by Cromwell in the three-day battle of Preston on 17 to 19 August. The Scottish Kirk party, hostile to the Engagement, took power in Edinburgh, and Cromwell was welcomed there in October. The war convinced the army leaders that Charles was a man of blood who had started a second war while negotiating, and a series of army prayer meetings concluded that he should be called to account.

The political events that followed are among the most dramatic of the seventeenth century. Parliament, which had passed a Vote of No Addresses in January 1648, reversed it in August and began negotiations at Newport. The army, stationed around London, issued a Remonstrance on 20 November demanding the king's trial and a new constitutional settlement based on an Agreement of the People. When the Commons, on 5 December, voted by 129 to 83 that the king's answers were a sufficient basis for settlement, the army acted. On 6 December, Colonel Thomas Pride's regiment stood at the door of the House and, with a list supplied by the radical Parliamentarians, arrested some forty members and turned away many others. In all, well over a hundred members were excluded. The Rump that remained, of some eighty active members, passed the measures desired by the army.

The legal and constitutional steps toward the trial were improvised. On 4 January 1649, the Commons resolved that the people were, under God, the original of all just power, that the Commons of England, being chosen by and representing the people, had the supreme power in the nation, and that whatever the Commons enacted had the force of law without the consent of king or Lords. This was a claim of sovereignty for the Commons that no previous English assembly had made. On 6 January, they passed an act establishing a High Court of Justice, with 135 named commissioners, of whom fewer than seventy attended. Judges of the common law courts declined to take part, and the Lords rejected the ordinance. John Bradshaw, a lawyer, was chosen as president. The trial in Westminster Hall began on 20 January. Charles refused to plead, challenging the authority of the court, and maintained that he had been brought there by force and that a king could not be tried by his subjects. The court found him guilty, and on 27 January sentenced him to death as a tyrant, traitor, murderer, and public enemy. Fifty-nine commissioners signed the death warrant.

The execution was carried out on 30 January 1649 on a scaffold before the Banqueting House in Whitehall. In the following weeks, the Rump passed acts abolishing the monarchy on 17 March and the House of Lords on 19 March. The execution raised the question that would shape the next decade: could a regime resting on the force of an army and on the authority of a Commons purged of its opponents claim to represent the people? The regicides themselves argued that they were enforcing the law against a king who had made war on his people. Their critics, including many who had fought against the king, regarded the trial as an act of usurpation. The publication of Eikon Basilike, a book claimed to be the king's own meditations, within days of his death, helped create a royalist martyrology that would shape the political culture of the following decade and the Restoration.

Section 77.34

The English Commonwealth, 1649–1653, and Government without a King

In March 1649 the Rump abolished the office of king as unnecessary, burdensome, and dangerous, and abolished the House of Lords, and in May it declared England a Commonwealth and Free State, governed by the representatives of the people in Parliament. Executive authority passed to a Council of State of about forty members, chosen annually by the Rump, with Cromwell, Fairfax, and civilian leaders among its members. An Engagement of loyalty to the Commonwealth was required of officeholders, and it provoked refusals, including that of Fairfax, who resigned his command in 1650 and was replaced by Cromwell.

The Commonwealth justified itself by arguments that government depends on the consent of the governed and that rulers are accountable, and writers such as John Milton defended the regicide in print. Yet its practical foundation was narrower than its theory. The Rump was a remnant of a Parliament elected in 1640, purged of those hostile to the regicide, and it had no electoral mandate. It was sustained by the New Model Army, to which it owed its existence, and by the committees and officeholders who benefited from the revolution. Its leaders resisted calls to dissolve and hold new elections, in part because they feared that free elections would return royalists and Presbyterians.

The regime faced opposition from several directions. Royalists regarded it as illegitimate and plotted risings. Levellers, whose leaders were imprisoned in 1649 and whose military supporters were crushed at Burford in May, denounced it as a new tyranny. Presbyterians resented the toleration of sects. Radical groups such as the Diggers, who cultivated common land at St George's Hill in 1649 and 1650, challenged property relations. The Commonwealth responded with censorship, the suppression of opposition, and the use of the army.

The constitutional claim of the Commonwealth, that sovereignty resided in the representatives of the people, and its practical dependence on military force illustrate the gap between theory and practice. Contemporary observers noted that a government that had overthrown a king for ruling without consent now ruled without an election. This tension, between republican principle and the realities of power, would continue throughout the 1650s.

The institutions of the Commonwealth were improvised from those of the Long Parliament. The Rump, which met with a quorum of forty, retained legislative authority and exercised it through a large number of standing committees, while the Council of State, created in February 1649 and consisting of forty-one members elected annually by the House, handled executive business, foreign policy, and military affairs. It included army officers such as Cromwell, Fairfax, and Henry Ireton alongside civilian lawyers and gentry such as Bulstrode Whitelocke, and its president was chosen from among its members. The Great Seal was replaced by a seal of the Commonwealth, the royal courts continued with new judges, and a Hale Commission of 1652 examined the reform of the law, though most of its proposals were not enacted. In 1649 and 1650 the Rump introduced an Engagement, an oath of loyalty to the Commonwealth as established without a king or House of Lords, which was required of officeholders and, for a time, of adult males more generally, and which divided Presbyterians and former royalists from supporters of the regime.

The new regime sought legitimacy through argument and propaganda. John Milton, who became Secretary for Foreign Languages to the Council, defended the trial and execution in The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates (February 1649) and attacked the Eikon Basilike in Eikonoklastes. Marchamont Nedham, a journalist who turned from royalist to republican, edited the weekly Mercurius Politicus from 1650 with the Council's support, and John Hall, Henry Parker, and others wrote in defense of government by consent. Republicans differed among themselves, however, about whether the Commonwealth should be a settled constitution or an interim arrangement leading to a fuller reform, and the Rump's refusal to set a date for new elections, despite repeated pressure from the army, deepened mistrust. Contemporaries such as Lilburne, who published England's New Chains Discovered in 1649, accused the rulers of establishing a tyranny of the officers and the Rump.

The opposition was both radical and conservative, and the government's reaction to it showed the character of the regime. Lilburne and other Leveller leaders were arrested in March 1649, and in May, when regiments mutinied for Leveller principles, Fairfax and Cromwell suppressed the mutineers at Burford, shooting three. In April 1649, Gerrard Winstanley and a small group began digging common land at St George's Hill, Surrey, in the name of the Diggers, who held that the earth was a common treasury and who were harassed by local landowners and eventually driven out. The Rump also passed legislation on moral and religious questions, including the Blasphemy and Adultery Acts of 1650, and in the same year it repealed the laws that required attendance at parish churches, creating a measure of freedom of worship for peaceable Protestant dissent while excluding Catholics and those who used the Prayer Book. The Commonwealth was thus not simply repressive or libertarian, but a government of a particular set of Protestant elites attempting to secure a precarious position.

Section 77.35

The Commonwealth Wars in Ireland and Scotland

The Commonwealth's immediate security problem was that Ireland and Scotland had not accepted the regicide. In Ireland, an alliance of Confederate Catholics, Old English, and royalists, brought together by the Duke of Ormond, controlled most of the country, and the Commonwealth feared that it would serve as a base for a Stuart restoration. Cromwell, appointed lord lieutenant, landed at Dublin in August 1649 with a large and well-supplied army.

At Drogheda on 11 September 1649, Cromwell's forces stormed the town after its refusal to surrender, and killed most of the garrison and a number of civilians, including clergy. At Wexford in October a similar massacre took place. Cromwell justified these actions as a just judgment on those responsible for the killings of 1641 and as a means of discouraging further resistance. The extent and character of the killings have been disputed, as has the question of whether civilians were deliberately targeted, but there is no doubt that the violence was extreme by the standards even of its time, and it became a lasting element of Irish historical memory. Cromwell left Ireland in May 1650, and his successors completed the conquest by 1653.

The political consequences were enormous. Parliament passed an Act for the Settlement of Ireland in 1652, confiscating the lands of those who had taken part in the rebellion or served in arms against Parliament, and allocating land to soldiers and creditors who had financed the reconquest. Many Catholic landowners were transplanted to Connacht and Clare, and Catholic ownership of land fell sharply, though the transplantation was incompletely carried out. War, famine, and plague caused massive mortality, with scholars estimating very large losses, though the precise figures remain uncertain and disputed. The conquest therefore transformed the political and social structure of Ireland, and it was not merely a campaign of military pacification.

In Scotland, the Covenanters proclaimed Charles II in 1649 on condition that he accept the Covenants, and Charles agreed. Cromwell, who had succeeded Fairfax, invaded in 1650 and defeated a larger Scottish army at Dunbar on 3 September 1650. Charles was crowned at Scone in January 1651, led a Scottish army into England, and was crushed at Worcester on 3 September 1651 before escaping to the continent. General Monck completed the conquest of Scotland. Scotland was incorporated into the Commonwealth, with representation in a united parliament, though the union was imposed and short-lived.

The Irish campaign began in the context of a war that had been under way since 1641. By 1649 the Confederate Catholics, much of whose leadership had come to terms with the royalist Ormond, controlled most of the island apart from Dublin and Derry. Ormond's defeat at Rathmines on 2 August 1649 by the Parliamentary garrison of Dublin under Michael Jones secured the capital, and Cromwell landed two weeks later with a force of about twelve thousand men and a siege train. He saw his task as both military and religious, and the declarations that he issued presented the campaign as retribution for the massacres of Protestants in 1641 and as a defense of the reformed religion, though they also invited those who were willing to lay down arms to return to their homes. His army was well paid and supplied, which enabled it to operate in the winter.

At Drogheda, which was held by a garrison of about two thousand, including English royalists and Irish Catholics, Cromwell summoned the commander, Sir Arthur Aston, to surrender, and when he refused, took the town by storm on 11 September 1649. The garrison, with some priests and townspeople, was killed. In his report to Parliament, Cromwell described the killings as the just judgment of God on those who had shed so much innocent blood, and argued that they would spare bloodshed in future. At Wexford on 11 October, a similar assault, which came during negotiations for surrender, led to the deaths of some two thousand soldiers and civilians. Historians dispute how far the killings were authorized and how many civilians died, but they agree that the sacks of Drogheda and Wexford were far beyond the routine violence of seventeenth-century sieges, that they were remembered in Ireland as a signal atrocity, and that they achieved the military end of persuading other garrisons to surrender. Others, as at Clonmel in 1650, resisted fiercely, and Cromwell's forces suffered heavy losses.

The reconquest was completed under Ireton and Edmund Ludlow by 1652 and 1653, through sieges at Limerick and Galway and a campaign of attrition against guerrilla forces known as tories. The war was a catastrophe for the civilian population. Famine, plague, and the destruction of crops and livestock caused massive mortality. William Petty, who surveyed the confiscated lands, later estimated that more than half a million people had died between 1641 and 1652, a figure that modern historians treat with caution but which suggests a loss on the order of a fifth or more of the population. Tens of thousands of soldiers left for service on the continent, and a number of prisoners and civilians were shipped to the West Indies as laborers.

The political settlement transformed the ownership of land. The Adventurers' Act of 1642 had promised Irish land to those who financed the war, and the Act for the Settlement of Ireland, passed by the Rump in August 1652, declared that those who had taken part in the rebellion, or who had not shown constant good affection to Parliament, forfeited some or all of their estates. Landowners who were not pardoned were to be transplanted to Connacht and County Clare, west of the Shannon, a program carried out under pressure in 1654. The confiscated lands in the rest of the island were allocated to the Adventurers, to soldiers in lieu of arrears of pay, and to creditors of the state. The Catholic share of land fell sharply, and the Protestant landed class that resulted, together with the new Protestant officials, would be the core of the Ascendancy that governed Ireland after 1690. In Scotland, the campaign was a different kind of conquest. Cromwell crushed the Covenanting army at Dunbar on 3 September 1650, taking several thousand prisoners, and the royalist army that crowned Charles II at Scone on 1 January 1651 and invaded England was destroyed at Worcester on 3 September 1651. Monck stormed Dundee in the same month. A Tender of Union in 1652 and an Ordinance of Union in April 1654 incorporated Scotland into the Commonwealth, with thirty seats in the English Parliament, but the arrangement was maintained by an army of occupation and was disbanded at the Restoration.

Section 77.36

The Commonwealth, Navigation Act, Fiscal Administration, and Maritime Competition

The republican regime inherited an expensive state. It maintained a large army and navy, financed through assessments on property, the excise, customs, and the sale of confiscated royal, church, and royalist lands. Parliament's war finances had already created a more effective tax system than the early Stuarts had possessed, and the Commonwealth extended it. The monthly assessment, collected by local committees, produced regular revenue, and the excise on consumer goods broadened the fiscal base. The regime also borrowed from London merchants and officials.

The navy was a particular success. The republican government expanded the fleet, increased the number of warships, and promoted capable officers such as Robert Blake. These ships were used against royalist privateers, in the Mediterranean, in the West Indies, and in the war with the Dutch. The Navigation Act of 1651 (Section 77.19) was designed to protect English shipping and, by implication, to weaken Dutch commercial dominance. The First Anglo-Dutch War of 1652 to 1654 tested the new navy and led to a Dutch defeat, though the war was costly for both sides.

The Commonwealth's fiscal-military state shows an important point for this chapter: a regime born of resistance to arbitrary taxation developed a heavier fiscal system than its predecessors, because war required money, and consent by a purged Parliament could be easier to obtain than the consent of a freely elected one. The financial burdens, especially the assessment and the excise, were unpopular, and sale of land created new interests. These fiscal structures would survive in altered form after the Restoration, a reason why historians speak of the 1650s as a period of state building.

The war with the Dutch also mattered ideologically. Some republicans hoped for union between the two republics, and in 1651 an English embassy proposed a close political and commercial alliance, which the Dutch regents rejected. Rivalry, not fraternity, prevailed, and the experience illustrates how shared Protestant and republican affinities could be overridden by commercial interests.

The Commonwealth's fiscal system extended the machinery created by the Long Parliament. The monthly assessment, a tax on property apportioned among the counties and collected by local commissioners, was the principal direct tax, and it was levied at varying rates throughout the 1650s. The excise on beer, ale, and other goods, introduced in 1643, was continued and expanded, and customs duties were collected by commissioners appointed by Parliament. Beyond these taxes, the regime raised large sums by selling the lands of the crown, bishops, deans and chapters, and royalists. Acts of 1649 and 1650 provided for the sale of Crown lands and ecclesiastical lands, and the Acts of 1651 and 1652 for the sale of the estates of named royalists, which allowed purchasers, among them officers and creditors, to acquire large holdings. These sales created a body of men with a stake in the permanence of the republic, and they also meant that the Restoration would require a settlement of disputed titles.

The naval buildup was the most conspicuous expression of the regime's capacity. The Admiralty Committee and the Navy Commissioners, working with generals-at-sea such as Blake, Richard Deane, and Monck, increased the size of the fleet substantially, built new frigates, and improved supply and administration. The navy's tasks included suppressing royalist privateering, which had been supported by Prince Rupert's squadron, securing the Irish Sea, and projecting power to the Mediterranean, where Blake demanded satisfaction from the Barbary states and Portugal. In the First Anglo-Dutch War, the Commonwealth fleet fought the Dutch to a successful conclusion, and later the Protectorate fitted out the Western Design, an expedition to the Caribbean that failed to take Hispaniola but captured Jamaica in 1655. The state spent far more on the army and navy in the 1650s than the early Stuart crown had spent on its military functions, which helps explain the burden of taxation that later generations remembered.

The diplomacy of the Commonwealth shows its ambivalence. In 1651, an embassy of Oliver St John and Walter Strickland went to The Hague to propose a close union or alliance of the two Protestant republics, in the expectation that a shared commitment to reformed religion and republican government would override commercial rivalry. The Dutch regents, who were mindful of English demands on trade and of the proposal's implications for their autonomy, declined, and the embassy was received with hostility by Orangist crowds. Within a year the Navigation Act and the war made the point that economic interests overrode ideological affinities. The episode is instructive because it shows republicans in both states recognizing a common political form, but also the limits of that recognition when it conflicted with the commercial and fiscal needs of governing classes.

Section 77.37

The Protectorate, the Instrument of Government, and Cromwell's Constitutional Order

By 1653, the army and the Rump were in conflict. Army officers wanted reforms, including a more godly government, legal reform, and a settlement of arrears, and they distrusted a Parliament that appeared to be perpetuating itself. When the Rump prepared a bill to dissolve itself but to allow existing members to retain their seats in the next Parliament, Cromwell dissolved it by force on 20 April 1653. He then summoned a Nominated Assembly, known by its critics as Barebone's Parliament after one of its members, composed of men chosen by army officers and godly congregations. It attempted reforms of law and religion, including proposals to end tithes, and was divided between moderates and radicals. In December moderates resigned their authority to Cromwell.

The army officers, led by John Lambert, then drafted the Instrument of Government, adopted on 16 December 1653, which established the Protectorate. It vested executive power in a Lord Protector, Cromwell, assisted by a Council of State, and legislative power in the Protector and a single-chamber Parliament, elected on a reformed franchise that redistributed seats from small boroughs to counties and included members for Scotland and Ireland, and excluded Catholics and those who had fought against Parliament from voting. It provided for religious toleration for most Protestants, though not for Catholics or for episcopalian worship in public, and it limited the Protector's power to dissolve Parliament and to maintain a standing army without consent.

The constitution was an attempt to combine monarchical, parliamentary, and military elements in a single order. It was a written constitution, unusual for the time, but it was drafted by army officers and not ratified by the people. Cromwell was an effective but contested head of state, who used the army and a system of major-generals who governed districts in 1655 and 1656, imposing moral reform, security measures, and a tax on royalists. The regime was unpopular, and the major-generals were abandoned in 1657. Protectorate Parliaments were difficult to manage, with members questioning the legitimacy of the army-made constitution.

In 1657, Parliament presented the Humble Petition and Advice, a revised constitution that offered Cromwell the crown, restored a second chamber, and modified the franchise. Cromwell, after long deliberation, refused the title of king, in part because of army opposition, but accepted an enhanced Protectorate with the right to name his successor. The episode indicates that many of the political elite wanted a return to familiar forms, and it shows the persistence of monarchical political culture even in a republic.

The dissolution of the Rump on 20 April 1653 followed a conflict over a bill for the election of a new Parliament. The army's officers, led by Cromwell, wanted a genuine reform before elections, and they feared that the Rump's bill, which would have allowed sitting members to retain their seats and to vet new members, was a device for perpetuating itself. Cromwell entered the House with soldiers and ordered the members out, an act that echoed Pride's Purge and the king's attempt to arrest the Five Members, and that was widely noted at the time. The Council of Officers appointed a Nominated Assembly of about 140 members, chosen from lists supplied by the congregations of the godly, which met in July 1653 and became known as Barebone's Parliament. Its members, who ranged from social radicals to conservatives, debated the abolition of tithes, the reform of the Court of Chancery, and a code of law. In December, after the moderates voted to dissolve the Assembly and hand power back to Cromwell, the army's officers drafted the Instrument of Government.

The Instrument, adopted on 16 December 1653, was a written constitution for England, Scotland, and Ireland, an unusual instrument for its time. Executive power was vested in a Lord Protector and a Council of State of between thirteen and twenty-one members. Legislative power was held by the Protector and a single-chamber Parliament of four hundred members from England and Wales and thirty each from Scotland and Ireland. The franchise in the counties was based on ownership of property worth two hundred pounds, which excluded most small freeholders but enfranchised substantial tenants and merchants, and smaller boroughs were disfranchised in favor of more populous counties and towns. Those who had fought against Parliament and Catholics were excluded from voting and from sitting. Parliament was to meet at least every three years and could not be dissolved for five months after its first meeting. A standing army of thirty thousand was provided for and a revenue of two hundred thousand pounds a year for the civil government. Article 37 secured toleration for those who professed faith in God by Jesus Christ, though not for popery or prelacy, and not for those who abused liberty to the civil injury of others.

The first Protectorate Parliament, which met in September 1654, challenged the basis of the new constitution, with republican members led by Sir Arthur Haselrig and others demanding that the Instrument be subject to parliamentary revision. Cromwell insisted that certain fundamentals, including government by a single person and Parliament, the control of the militia, and liberty of conscience, were not open to change, and he dissolved the Parliament in January 1655 at the earliest permitted date. A royalist rising under Colonel John Penruddock at Salisbury in March 1655 was crushed, and it provoked the creation of the major-generals. From August 1655 to January 1657, England and Wales were divided into districts under ten major-generals, who commanded local militias, enforced security, supervised the suppression of alehouses and other disorders, and collected a tax of ten percent on the incomes of royalists, the decimation. The system aroused deep hostility among the gentry, who objected to the rule of soldiers, and it was abandoned after Parliament rejected a militia bill in January 1657.

The Second Protectorate Parliament of September 1656 excluded about a hundred members whom the Council considered unfit. It then considered the Humble Petition and Advice, drafted by civilian lawyers, which was presented in 1657. The Petition offered Cromwell the title of king, to restore a second chamber, to regularize the franchise, and to specify that the Protector's powers, including the choice of a successor, should be defined by Parliament. Cromwell debated the offer for six weeks, and the army's senior officers, including Lambert, who then fell from favor, objected to the kingship. On 8 May 1657 he declined the crown but accepted the revised constitution, and on 26 June he was reinstalled with ceremonies that resembled a coronation. A newly appointed Other House of Lords was convened in 1658 and led to quarrels that ended in the dissolution of Parliament in February 1658. The episode shows how the Protectorate drifted toward a monarchy in all but name, and how the weakness of its constitutional foundations led its supporters to seek legitimacy in familiar forms.

Section 77.38

Radical Politics, Religious Experimentation, the Protectorate's Collapse, and the Road to Restoration

The 1650s witnessed an extraordinary flowering of religious and political radicalism. Baptists, Fifth Monarchists, who expected the imminent reign of Christ, and Quakers, whose emphasis on inward light and refusal to defer to social superiors alarmed authorities, gained followers. The Protectorate tolerated many Protestant sects, and in 1655 Cromwell allowed Jews to resettle in England informally, after a conference at Whitehall, though no formal legal act of readmission was passed. But toleration had limits: Catholics and Anglicans, and those who denied the Trinity, were persecuted, and the Quaker James Nayler was savagely punished for blasphemy by Parliament in 1656.

Political opposition came from royalists, who staged an unsuccessful rising in 1655, from republicans who objected to rule by a single person, and from Leveller veterans. The regime depended on Cromwell, and his death on 3 September 1658 removed its center. His son Richard, who succeeded as Protector, lacked the army's confidence and Cromwell's authority. Divisions among army officers and between the army and Parliament led to his resignation in May 1659.

The years 1659 and 1660 were a period of near anarchy. The Rump was recalled, then expelled by the army under Lambert, and a Committee of Safety attempted to govern. In Scotland, General George Monck, commander of the army there, declared for a free Parliament, marched south in January 1660, and entered London in February. He restored the secluded members of the Long Parliament who had been excluded by Pride's Purge, and the Long Parliament voted to dissolve itself and call new elections.

The Restoration was not inevitable. It resulted from the collapse of alternative constitutional settlements, the unpopularity of military rule, and the desire of the gentry and many in the army for stability and civil government. Republicanism had not lacked ideas, as the writings of James Harrington and others show, but the experience of the 1650s convinced many that the monarchy, with its familiar institutions, offered a more secure basis for order. The 1650s thus left a double legacy: a memory of regicide and military rule that made many fear a repeat, and a fiscal and administrative apparatus on which later governments built.

The radical and religious ferment of the 1650s was one of the most important legacies of the revolution. Baptists and Independent congregations flourished, and Fifth Monarchists such as Thomas Harrison and John Rogers believed that Christ's kingdom was imminent and criticized Cromwell for failing to establish it. The Quakers, led by George Fox, preached a doctrine of the inner light, refused to doff their hats to social superiors, and attracted both support and persecution. James Nayler, who entered Bristol in 1656 in a manner that imitated Christ's entry into Jerusalem, was tried by Parliament for blasphemy and punished by flogging, branding, and imprisonment, a demonstration that toleration had limits. Cromwell also permitted Jews to reside in England informally after the Whitehall Conference of December 1655, which heard the appeal of Menasseh ben Israel, though no statute or ordinance authorizing readmission was passed.

Cromwell died on 3 September 1658, and the Council and army accepted his son Richard as Protector. Richard lacked military experience and the loyalty of the army, whose senior officers were divided between a party based at Wallingford House and others. His Parliament, which met in January 1659, included republicans who challenged the Other House and the right of Scottish and Irish members to sit. On 22 April 1659 the army council forced him to dissolve Parliament, and in May he was set aside. The officers then recalled the Rump, which resumed power on 7 May, and quarreled with it over the army's pay and its constitutional role. In October, Lambert expelled the Rump by force and formed a Committee of Safety, while resistance developed in the provinces and in the fleet.

The decisive step came from Scotland. George Monck, the commander of the army there, who had served the Commonwealth and the Protectorate, declared for a free Parliament and marched south in January 1660, crossing the Tweed on 2 January. By the time he reached London, in February, the Rump had been restored by a revolt of the garrison and the fleet, but it was unpopular, and Monck compelled it to readmit the members who had been secluded at Pride's Purge. The Long Parliament, which met with its Presbyterian members restored on 21 February, voted to dissolve itself on 16 March and to summon a new Parliament without conditions on the franchise. In the meantime, Monck had been in contact with Charles II, whose advisers, notably Edward Hyde, advised him to issue a declaration promising a moderate settlement.

The republican alternatives that were still being proposed in 1659 and 1660 show that the outcome was not inevitable. James Harrington's Oceana of 1656 had set out a model of a republic with a balance of land and with rotation in office, and in 1660 John Milton published The Ready and Easy Way to Establish a Free Commonwealth, arguing for a perpetual council. The failure of such schemes to attract support reflected the discrediting of the Commonwealth by the dominance of the army, the burdens of taxation, and the experience of instability. Many of the gentry who had supported Parliament in the 1640s were willing to accept a king if he could guarantee their property and the church, and they did so in 1660 on the basis of a settlement that left many questions unresolved.

The Restoration Monarchy and Renewed Constitutional Conflict. The Restoration was not the restoration of the pre-1640 monarchy. The institutions, political fears, fiscal practices, and religious divisions produced by the preceding decades remained consequential.

Section 77.39

The Restoration of Charles II and the Constitutional Settlement of 1660

From exile at Breda in the Dutch Republic, Charles II issued the Declaration of Breda on 4 April 1660, a carefully drafted appeal to the nation, promising a general pardon for those who returned to allegiance, except those whom Parliament should exempt, liberty to tender consciences, a settlement of disputed land titles, and payment of the army's arrears. It left most of the details to Parliament. The Convention Parliament, elected without a royal writ because there was no king, met in April 1660 and invited Charles to return, and he entered London on 29 May 1660, his thirtieth birthday.

The settlement of 1660 restored the monarchy, the House of Lords, and the Church of England, but with significant limits. The Indemnity and Oblivion Act pardoned most but excepted those directly responsible for the regicide, of whom a number were executed and others imprisoned, and the bodies of Cromwell and others were exhumed and desecrated in 1661. The army was largely disbanded, though a small force of guards was retained. Land settlements were left partly to the courts, and royalists who had sold land to pay fines often failed to recover it, a source of lingering resentment.

Finance reflected the compromise. Parliament abolished feudal tenures and the Court of Wards, in exchange for a revenue from the excise, and granted the king customs and other duties estimated, optimistically, at £1.2 million a year. The actual revenue fell short, and Charles faced persistent financial shortfall, which made him dependent on Parliament and open to foreign subsidies. The Triennial Act was modified in 1664 to remove the guarantee that Parliament would meet.

The constitution restored in 1660 was thus the pre-1642 constitution, modified by the abolition of prerogative courts such as Star Chamber, by the end of ship money and arbitrary taxation, and by the memory of civil war. Fundamental questions, including the control of the militia, the relation between Crown and Parliament, and the religious settlement, remained unresolved. The Restoration was a return to monarchy, but not a return to the monarchy of 1640.

The negotiations of spring 1660 were conducted through letters, messengers, and Monck's mediation. The Declaration of Breda, issued on 4 April 1660 and drafted by Hyde, was accompanied by letters to the two Houses, the army, the fleet, and the City of London, and it was received by the Convention Parliament on 1 May. By its terms, the king offered a general pardon to those who returned to their allegiance within forty days, except those whom Parliament might exclude, liberty to tender consciences in religion to the extent that Parliament would agree, the settlement of disputed property titles in Parliament, and the payment of the army's arrears and the retention of its soldiers in service. On the same day, the Houses resolved that according to the ancient fundamental laws of the kingdom, the government was by king, Lords, and Commons, and they invited Charles to return. He landed at Dover on 25 May and entered London on 29 May.

The Convention Parliament enacted the settlement of 1660 in a series of measures. The Act of Free and General Pardon, Indemnity, and Oblivion, which received royal assent in August 1660, pardoned most but excepted those who had been the king's judges and some others. About a dozen regicides were hanged, drawn, and quartered in the autumn, others were imprisoned for life, and in January 1661 the bodies of Cromwell, Ireton, and Bradshaw were exhumed and hanged at Tyburn. The army was paid off from the proceeds of a poll tax and the assessment and largely disbanded by early 1661, though Monck's regiment, the Coldstream Guards, and a few other units were retained as the nucleus of a standing force. Parliament abolished feudal tenures and the Court of Wards in the Tenures Abolition Act, in return for an excise on beer and other drinks, and it granted the king for life customs and a further revenue intended to produce 1.2 million pounds a year. This was less than he needed, because the yield was lower than expected, and it left the monarchy dependent on parliamentary supplements.

The restoration of the crown's authority over the armed forces was an important constitutional decision. The Militia Acts of 1661 and 1662, passed by the Cavalier Parliament, declared that the sole supreme government and command of the militia belonged to the king, and they thereby settled in his favor the question that had caused the break in 1642. The Act of 1664 which replaced the Triennial Act of 1641 required Parliament to meet every three years but removed the provisions for automatic assembly, so that the calling of Parliament remained a royal prerogative. At the same time, the settlement did not restore the prerogative courts of the 1630s, and the principle that taxation required parliamentary consent was not challenged. The settlement also left unresolved the questions of land, since the Convention left many disputes over confiscated and sold estates to be decided by the courts or by private bills, with the result that many royalists who had sold property to pay fines failed to recover it, while the lands of the crown, the bishops, and the cathedral chapters were returned. The Restoration thus satisfied many but disappointed others, and it was a compromise among the interests of the gentry, the church, the crown, and the army.

Section 77.40

The Restoration Church, the Clarendon Code, and Religious Exclusion

The Cavalier Parliament, elected in 1661 and dominated by royalists and Anglicans, restored the Church of England with bishops and the Book of Common Prayer, in a pattern that disappointed those who had hoped for comprehension of moderate Presbyterians. A series of laws, known as the Clarendon Code after the king's chief minister, the Earl of Clarendon, though Clarendon was not its sole author, enforced conformity. The Corporation Act of 1661 required municipal officeholders to take Anglican communion and renounce the Covenant. The Act of Uniformity of 1662 required all clergy to accept the revised Prayer Book, and about two thousand ministers who refused were ejected from their livings in what is called the Great Ejection. The Conventicle Act of 1664 banned religious meetings of more than five people outside the Anglican church, and the Five Mile Act of 1665 forbade ejected ministers from living within five miles of a corporate town.

The effect was to create a category of Protestant dissent, Presbyterians, Independents, Baptists, and Quakers, who were excluded from public office and university education and subject to fines and imprisonment. Enforcement varied. In some areas local magistrates were zealous, in others they turned a blind eye, and the dissenting communities developed their own chapels, schools, and networks. Quakers, who refused oaths, suffered particularly. Catholics, a small minority, were subject to older penal laws, and the fear of popery remained strong.

These laws shaped political identity. Dissenters tended to ally with those suspicious of the court, and Anglican defenders of the established church formed the basis of what became the Tory interest. Religious exclusion thus structured political life, and it created a pool of resentment that would be significant in 1688. The king himself favored toleration, partly from conviction and partly because of his Catholic sympathies, but Parliament resisted his attempts.

For Scotland, the Restoration reestablished episcopacy, in 1662, which many Presbyterians resisted, leading to open-air conventicles and a rising in the southwest in 1679 that was defeated at Bothwell Bridge. The "Killing Time" of the 1680s saw severe repression. Religious policy in the three kingdoms thus continued to create conflicts.

The religious settlement was the work of the Cavalier Parliament rather than of the king. At the Savoy Conference of 1661, Presbyterian ministers led by Richard Baxter met bishops to discuss a revision of the Prayer Book that would permit comprehension, but the bishops conceded little, and the Convocation of 1661 and 1662 prepared a revised Prayer Book that was in some respects less accommodating than that of 1604. The Corporation Act of December 1661 required members of town corporations to take communion according to the Church of England, to renounce the Solemn League and Covenant, and to swear that resistance to the king was unlawful. The Act of Uniformity of May 1662, which took effect on 24 August (St Bartholomew's Day), required all clergy and teachers to use the revised Prayer Book, to be episcopally ordained, and to declare their assent. About two thousand ministers, perhaps a fifth of the clergy, refused and were ejected from their livings in the Great Ejection.

Further statutes aimed at nonconformist worship. The Quaker Act of 1662 penalized the refusal of oaths and meetings of Quakers. The Conventicle Act of 1664 made it an offense to attend a religious meeting of five or more persons over sixteen, other than members of one household, if it did not conform to the Prayer Book, with penalties of fines, imprisonment, and, for repeated offenses, transportation. The Five Mile Act of 1665, passed while Parliament was at Oxford to avoid the plague, forbade nonconformist ministers who had not taken an oath of non-resistance to come within five miles of any corporate town or of the place where they had formerly served. A second Conventicle Act in 1670 strengthened the penalties and encouraged informers. Together these laws are called the Clarendon Code, though Clarendon himself did not support all of them and Charles II sought more than once to moderate them, as in the Declaration of Indulgence he issued in December 1662, which Parliament rejected.

Enforcement varied. In some boroughs and counties, magistrates enforced the laws vigorously, imprisoning ministers and fining congregations, while in others, local sympathy and the sheer number of dissenters made prosecution difficult. Dissenters also adapted, meeting in private houses, establishing academies for the education of their children, and developing networks of ministers and congregations. The exclusion of dissenters from corporations and offices and from the universities created a body of educated and commercially active Protestants with a grievance against the established church, a group that would be important in the Exclusion Crisis and in 1688. The penal laws against Catholics, meanwhile, were enforced sporadically, and fears of popery fed the anxieties that surfaced in 1678.

In Scotland, the Restoration reversed the revolution of 1638. The Rescissory Act of 1661 annulled all legislation since 1633, and the following year episcopacy was restored, with the bishops again sitting in Parliament and controlling the Kirk. Several hundred ministers who refused to accept the authority of the bishops and patronage were ejected from their parishes, and in the southwest of Scotland, where Presbyterian sentiment was strong, they held conventicles in the open air, attended by thousands. A rising in 1666, defeated at Rullion Green, and the murder of Archbishop Sharp in 1679, followed by the defeat of an army of Covenanters at Bothwell Bridge in June 1679, led to severe repression, including executions, transportation, and the billeting of troops, remembered by Presbyterians as the Killing Time. The religious policy of the Restoration thus laid the foundation for the Presbyterian reaction of 1689 and 1690.

Section 77.41

Charles II, Parliament, Finance, and the Anglo-Dutch Wars

The Restoration court was lively and expensive. Charles's income fell below his needs, especially in wartime, and he turned to Parliament for supplies and, secretly, to Louis XIV for subsidies. The Second Anglo-Dutch War (1665–1667), accompanied by plague in 1665 and the Great Fire of London in 1666, ended in the humiliating Dutch raid on the Medway, and Parliament blamed Clarendon, who was impeached in 1667 and fled abroad. The scandal increased suspicion of the court and led Parliament to examine the use of public money.

In the Treaty of Dover of 1670, Charles secretly agreed with Louis XIV to join France in war against the Dutch in return for subsidies, and he promised to declare his conversion to Catholicism at an appropriate time. Only a few advisers knew its Catholic provisions. In 1672, Charles issued a Declaration of Indulgence suspending penal laws against Catholics and Protestant dissenters, and declared war on the Dutch, in the Third Anglo-Dutch War. At the same time he stopped payment on some of the debts owed to bankers, the Stop of the Exchequer, a measure that damaged royal credit.

Parliament resisted. It forced Charles to withdraw the Declaration in 1673, arguing that the crown could not suspend statutes, and passed the Test Act of 1673, which required all officeholders to take Anglican communion, abjure transubstantiation, and take oaths of allegiance and supremacy. The Duke of York, Charles's brother James, resigned as Lord High Admiral, revealing that he was a Catholic. Parliament also pressed for peace with the Dutch, and in 1674 England withdrew from the war.

The reign thus saw the development of a court party, centered on ministers such as Danby, and a country opposition, suspicious of French influence and Catholicism. The war on the Dutch had been unpopular, and the later shift to alliance with the Dutch, symbolized by William's marriage to Mary in 1677, reflected changes in English opinion. The system depended heavily on Parliament's control of money, and this link between finance and political legitimacy remained central.

The financial stringency of Charles II's government shaped its political choices. The revenue settled in 1660 proved inadequate even in peacetime, and war added large costs. For the Second Anglo-Dutch War, Parliament voted record subsidies, including a grant of 2.5 million pounds in 1665, and in 1665 and 1666 it introduced the principle of appropriation, by which money was to be used only for the purposes for which it was voted, and a commission of public accounts to examine expenditure. The plague of 1665, which killed perhaps a hundred thousand people in London, and the Great Fire of September 1666, which destroyed much of the City, disrupted trade and revenue. After the Medway raid in 1667, Parliament, convinced that corruption and mismanagement had caused the defeat, attacked Clarendon, who fled to France, and the government was reorganized under the Cabal of ministers, a group that included Arlington and Buckingham.

Charles's secret diplomacy aimed at reducing his dependence on Parliament. In the Treaty of Dover of June 1670, negotiated by his sister Henrietta Anne, he agreed to support Louis XIV in a war against the Dutch and to announce his conversion to Catholicism when circumstances allowed, in return for French subsidies. A sham version of the treaty, without the religious clause, was shown to the Protestant ministers. In January 1672, the government suspended payments on the debts it owed to bankers in the Stop of the Exchequer, a drastic measure that damaged credit. In March it issued the Declaration of Indulgence, suspending the penal laws against Catholics and Protestant dissenters on the basis of the royal prerogative, and declared war on the Dutch. The measures were part of a program to strengthen royal authority, and they were perceived as such.

Parliament resisted both the Declaration and the war. When it met in February 1673, the Commons resolved that the king could not suspend statutes in matters ecclesiastical without parliamentary consent and refused further supplies unless the Declaration was withdrawn. Charles withdrew it and gave his assent to the Test Act of March 1673, which required all holders of civil and military office to take the oaths of allegiance and supremacy, to receive communion according to the Church of England, and to declare against transubstantiation. The Duke of York and the Lord Treasurer Clifford resigned their offices, and James's refusal to take the Test revealed that he was a Catholic. The Treasurer who succeeded in 1673, Thomas Osborne, later Earl of Danby, pursued a policy of cooperation with the Anglican majority in Parliament and hostility to France, which led to the marriage of Mary to William of Orange in 1677 and to a Dutch alliance. But Danby's own secret dealings with Louis XIV, revealed in 1678, led to his impeachment.

Section 77.42

The Popish Plot, Exclusion Crisis, and the Emergence of Whig and Tory Politics

In 1678, Titus Oates and Israel Tonge alleged a Catholic conspiracy to assassinate the king and install the Duke of York. The Popish Plot was largely fabricated, but the murder of the magistrate Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey, who had taken Oates's deposition, and the discovery of correspondence by the duke's secretary, lent it credibility. A wave of panic led to the execution of several Catholics, the exclusion of Catholic peers from the House of Lords, and a political crisis. Modern historians regard the plot as false, though the fears it exploited were real and rooted in decades of anti-Catholic sentiment and distrust of Charles's French connections.

Between 1679 and 1681, three Parliaments considered bills to exclude James from the succession. The Whigs, led by the Earl of Shaftesbury, argued that a Catholic king would threaten Protestantism and liberty, and that Parliament had the authority to alter the succession. Their opponents, the Tories, argued that hereditary succession was a fundamental law and that exclusion would undermine the monarchy and invite civil war, since it would revive the memory of 1642. The names were originally insults, Whig from Scottish Presbyterian rebels and Tory from Irish Catholic outlaws, but the groups they designated became lasting political tendencies.

The crisis was fought in Parliament, in petitions, mass demonstrations, pamphlets, and coffeehouses. It produced the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679, strengthening protection against arbitrary imprisonment. Charles dissolved Parliaments to prevent the Exclusion Bill, and in 1681 the last of them met at Oxford and was swiftly dissolved. The king, aided by French subsidies and a Tory reaction, governed without Parliament for the last four years of his reign. He punished the opposition, partly through quo warranto proceedings that remodeled borough charters in the Tories' favor, and after the Rye House Plot of 1683, a conspiracy to assassinate the king and his brother, executed Whig leaders such as Algernon Sidney and Lord Russell.

The Exclusion Crisis is significant for several reasons. It shows the rise of organized parties and public politics. It revealed that Parliament claimed authority over the succession, a claim that would be realized in 1689 and 1701. And it showed the limits of that claim, since Charles and the Tories could mobilize loyalty to hereditary monarchy. The result was that James succeeded peacefully in February 1685, though under conditions that left his opponents waiting.

The Popish Plot and the Exclusion Crisis were the product of a political situation already tense because of the Catholicism of the heir and the king's French connections. On 6 September 1678, Titus Oates, a former Anglican clergyman who had become a Catholic and been expelled from a Jesuit college, swore a deposition before the magistrate Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey alleging a Jesuit plot to assassinate the king. Godfrey was found dead in a ditch on Primrose Hill on 17 October with his own sword through his body, and the circumstances were never explained. Public alarm was heightened by the discovery of letters from Edward Coleman, the Duchess of York's secretary, to the confessor of Louis XIV, which revealed Catholic hopes of securing toleration with French help. Parliament, meeting in October, passed a second Test Act excluding Catholics from both Houses, with an exception for the Duke of York, and a series of trials led to the execution of about two dozen Catholics, including priests and lay people, on the basis of perjured testimony. Oates and his collaborators were rewarded until the credibility of the plot began to collapse in 1681.

The Exclusion Bills of 1679, 1680, and 1681 aimed to bar James from the throne, and they became the center of a national controversy. The first bill passed its second reading in the Commons in May 1679 before Charles dissolved Parliament. The second Exclusion Parliament, which met in October 1680 after a long delay, passed a bill in the Commons, but the Lords rejected it in November after a speech by the Marquess of Halifax, who argued that other safeguards could limit a Catholic king. The third Parliament, which met at Oxford in March 1681, was dissolved after a week. In these years the Whigs, led by the Earl of Shaftesbury, organized mass petitions for Parliament to sit, and their opponents organized addresses abhorring such petitions, so that the factions came to be known as Petitioners and Abhorrers, and later as Whigs and Tories. The controversy produced a flood of print, including Robert Filmer's Patriarcha, published in 1680, which defended divinely sanctioned hereditary monarchy and which John Locke and Algernon Sidney would answer in works written in these years.

The crown's counterattack in 1681 to 1685 was effective. Charles secured French subsidies, which freed him from the need for Parliament, and he used the courts and the Tory gentry and clergy against his opponents. Shaftesbury was tried for treason in 1681, but a London grand jury refused to indict him, and he fled to the Netherlands in 1682, dying there in January 1683. The crown brought quo warranto proceedings against the charters of London and of many boroughs, forcing corporations to surrender their charters and accept new ones that gave the king the power to appoint officials. After the discovery of the Rye House Plot, an alleged conspiracy to assassinate the king and the Duke of York in June 1683, Lord Russell and Algernon Sidney were executed. Nonconformists were prosecuted under the penal laws. By the end of Charles's reign, the Tories were in a dominant position, and the Whig leadership was dead, in exile, or silenced. This domination was possible because the crown had the support of the Church of England and the gentry, who feared a repetition of the 1640s, and it left a legacy of resentment among those who had been persecuted.

Section 77.43

James II, 1685–1688, and the Crisis of Royal Prerogative

James II (James VII of Scotland) came to the throne with considerable support from Tories and the Church of England, which hoped that he would respect it despite his Catholicism. He was in his early fifties, had no surviving sons, and his daughters Mary and Anne were Protestants, which reassured many that his reign would be temporary. Parliament granted him a generous revenue, and in 1685 the Monmouth Rebellion, led by Charles II's illegitimate son, the Duke of Monmouth, in the southwest, and Argyll's rising in Scotland, were crushed. Monmouth's defeat at Sedgemoor on 6 July 1685 was followed by the Bloody Assizes under Judge Jeffreys, in which many rebels were executed or transported.

James then built a larger standing army, and used it to overawe opposition, and appointed Catholic officers in violation of the Test Act. In Godden v. Hales (1686), judges supported the king's claim to dispense with the Test Act in individual cases. He established an Ecclesiastical Commission to discipline the Church of England, expelled Protestant fellows of Magdalen College, Oxford, and appointed Catholics to the Privy Council, the army, and the universities. In 1687 he issued a Declaration of Indulgence, suspending penal laws, and sought to build an alliance with Protestant dissenters against Anglicans by promising toleration, while remodeling the corporations to secure a compliant Parliament.

In April 1688 he reissued the Declaration and ordered it read in churches. Seven bishops, led by Archbishop Sancroft, petitioned against it, were tried for seditious libel, and acquitted on 30 June 1688 amid popular celebration. The same month, on 10 June, the queen, Mary of Modena, gave birth to a son, James Francis Edward, raising the prospect of a Catholic succession. Some rumored that the baby was supposititious, a story with no evidence, but its currency shows the depth of mistrust.

The result was an alienation of the elite that James had needed. Tories, who defended hereditary monarchy and the established church, found their church attacked, while Whigs and dissenters distrusted a toleration offered by prerogative and not by statute. The king's use of dispensing powers and the army raised the question of whether he was ruling by law. Some leading men, including Danby, Compton, and Devonshire, began to open communication with William of Orange. Historians stress that James's policies were not irrational. He was pursuing toleration for Catholics and a strong monarchy, but he misjudged the opposition his measures would generate and the resources of his opponents.

James's early reign was marked by firmness and apparent success. Parliament, which met in May 1685, granted him a revenue for life that exceeded that of his brother, and it was dominated by Tories who were eager to support him. Two rebellions in the summer tested this support. In Scotland, the Earl of Argyll landed in May with a small force and was captured and executed in June. In England, the Duke of Monmouth, Charles II's illegitimate son and a Protestant claimant, landed at Lyme Regis on 11 June, drew support from the southwest, and was defeated at Sedgemoor on 6 July, the last pitched battle on English soil, and executed on 15 July. The Bloody Assizes of Judge Jeffreys in the autumn condemned several hundred rebels to death and transported more than eight hundred to the West Indies. The suppression of the rebellion enabled James to increase the army, which he soon enlarged to some twenty thousand men, and to commission Catholic officers.

The policy of promoting Catholics collided with the Test Act and with the loyalty of the Anglican elite. When Parliament reconvened in November 1685 and its members objected to Catholic officers in the army, James prorogued it and never allowed it to meet again. In 1686, in the case of Godden v. Hales, a collusive action brought to test the question, the judges upheld the king's power to dispense with the Test Act in individual cases, and he used this power to appoint Catholics to the Privy Council, the army, the judiciary, and the universities. He established an Ecclesiastical Commission in 1686, which suspended the Bishop of London, Henry Compton, for refusing to discipline a preacher, and in 1687 he forced the fellows of Magdalen College, Oxford, to accept his nominee as president and expelled those who refused. He also appointed Catholic bishops, known as vicars apostolic, and sought to build an alliance with Protestant dissenters on the basis of toleration, issuing a Declaration of Indulgence in April 1687 that suspended penal laws and religious tests.

To secure a Parliament willing to repeal the Test and penal laws, James began in 1687 to remodel the boroughs, removing Tory magistrates and replacing them with dissenters and Catholics, and he questioned the lords lieutenant and justices about how they would vote. This policy caused dismay among the Anglican gentry who had supported the crown in 1681 to 1685. In April 1688, James reissued the Declaration of Indulgence and ordered the clergy to read it from their pulpits on two successive Sundays in May. On 18 May, seven bishops led by Archbishop William Sancroft of Canterbury petitioned the king, arguing that the dispensing power was unlawful and that they could not read the Declaration. James had them tried for seditious libel in the Court of King's Bench, and on 30 June 1688, the jury acquitted them to scenes of public rejoicing. On 10 June, the queen had given birth to a son, James Francis Edward, Prince of Wales, whose birth altered the prospect of a succession by Protestant daughters, and which led to fears of a Catholic dynasty.

The crisis of 1688 shows how James's policies had united opponents who had divided in 1679 to 1681. Tories, who regarded the Church of England as the foundation of political order, and Whigs and dissenters, who feared Catholic absolutism, were disturbed by the army, the dispensing power, and the remodeling of the corporations. The revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 and the flight of Huguenot refugees to England provided a vivid example of what Protestants might expect from a Catholic monarchy allied with Louis XIV. James's own religious policies were not aimed at establishing absolutism in a French sense, but the combination of Catholic appointments, the use of prerogative to override statute, and a large standing army appeared to many to threaten the liberties and religion of the kingdom, and in the summer of 1688, many of the political nation were prepared to consider an alternative.

Division IX · Sections 77.44–77.49

The Glorious Revolution and the Constitutional Settlement. The events of 1688 and 1689 were both a domestic constitutional transformation and an Anglo-Dutch military and political intervention, with very different consequences in England, Scotland, and Ireland. They were not uniformly peaceful.

Section 77.44

William of Orange, the Invitation of 1688, and the Dutch Intervention in England

William III's decision to intervene in England was driven primarily by European strategy. Louis XIV's revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, his pressure on the Republic's trade, and his territorial ambitions made war likely, and an England allied with France would threaten the Republic's survival. The birth of a Catholic prince in June 1688 threatened to make James's policy permanent. On 30 June 1688, the day the bishops were acquitted, seven prominent Englishmen, including the Earl of Danby, the Bishop of London Henry Compton, and Admiral Edward Russell, signed an invitation to William, assuring him that many of the nobility and gentry would support him if he landed with an army. The Invitation was a conspiracy by a few, not a mandate from a nation, but it reflected broader alienation.

The Dutch expedition was a major military and naval operation. The States General and the States of Holland, after debate and with Amsterdam's reluctant agreement, authorized the assembly of about fifty warships and several hundred transports and troops, in part because Louis XIV, preoccupied with the Rhine, chose to attack in the Empire and not in the Netherlands. William issued a Declaration explaining that he came to secure a free Parliament and to protect the Protestant religion, and he did not initially claim the crown. After an aborted first sailing, the fleet sailed in November and landed at Torbay, in Devon, on 5 November 1688 (Old Style), with perhaps twenty thousand men, a mixed force including Dutch, English, Scottish, German, and Huguenot soldiers.

James's army, larger than William's, failed to fight. Prominent officers defected, among them John Churchill, and the king's daughter Anne left London to join the rebels. James lost his nerve, and after attempting to negotiate, he sent his queen and son to France and fled in December, was captured at Faversham and brought back, then allowed to escape and left for France on 23 December. William entered London, and in the absence of a ruler asked an assembly of former MPs and peers to advise on government, then called a Convention. The speed of the collapse reflected the king's isolation and the effectiveness of William's preparation.

It is important to avoid two simplifications. The revolution was not simply a spontaneous English uprising, since it depended on a Dutch invasion. And it was not a simple conquest, because William's success rested on the support of English elites, and he took pains to present himself as a deliverer. Historians have compared the characterizations, "invasion" and "invitation," and most conclude that both were part of the event.

The preparations for the expedition were conducted in an atmosphere of secrecy and diplomacy. In the autumn of 1687, William's agent Everard van Weede Dijkvelt visited England to meet leading figures, and William's position on the Test Act and the penal laws was set out in a letter from Grand Pensionary Fagel, which was widely published and which stated that William and Mary would support toleration for Protestant dissenters and Catholics in worship but not the repeal of the Test. This reassured both Anglicans and dissenters. In the summer of 1688, after the acquittal of the bishops and the birth of the prince, the invitation signed by seven noblemen and gentlemen reached William. The States of Holland, and in particular Amsterdam, were persuaded by arguments that France would attack the Republic and that an England controlled by James was dangerous, and by the French king's decisions in the late summer to send his armies against the Rhineland. On 24 September, France declared war on the Empire, and the French attack on Philippsburg, followed by the movement of French forces away from the Dutch border, removed the immediate threat to the Republic.

William published a Declaration of Reasons, drafted with the help of Gilbert Burnet and distributed in thousands of copies, that set out his purposes: to secure a free Parliament, to investigate the birth of the Prince of Wales, and to defend the laws, liberties, and Protestant religion. The fleet, assembled at Hellevoetsluis, sailed in mid-October but was driven back by a storm. It sailed again on 1 November (New Style) and, aided by an east wind that kept the English fleet in the Thames estuary and later became known as the Protestant wind, passed through the Channel. It landed at Torbay on 5 November Old Style, 15 November New Style, with some fifteen thousand soldiers, a fleet of several hundred vessels, and a retinue that included English exiles, Huguenots, and Scots. William chose to land in the southwest, away from James's main forces, in a region with a tradition of support for Protestant causes, and moved to Exeter, where support came slowly at first.

The collapse of James's position was due to a series of defections and failures of nerve. James moved his army to Salisbury, but several officers, including Lord Cornbury and later John Churchill, then commander of the royal army's cavalry, deserted to William in November. Prince George of Denmark, Anne's husband, left the king's camp, and his daughter Anne fled from London on 26 November. Risings in the north, in Yorkshire and Nottinghamshire, and in Cheshire, led by Danby, Devonshire, and Delamere, further undermined the king's authority. James, who was ill and distrustful of his officers, retreated to London and, after talks at Hungerford, tried on 11 December to flee, was caught by fishermen at Faversham, and was brought back. William, who did not want the king as a martyr, allowed him to leave, and on 23 December he left for France. William then entered London, called together the surviving members of Charles II's Parliaments and the aldermen and Common Councilmen of London for advice, and on 26 December, he accepted their request that he assume the administration of the government and issue writs for a Convention. The invasion was therefore both a military operation, which depended on Dutch ships, soldiers, and money, and a political one that relied on English acquiescence and the unpopularity of the king.

Section 77.45

The Convention Parliament, the Declaration of Rights, and the Bill of Rights of 1689

The Convention that met in January 1689 faced a constitutional problem: there was no king, and no royal writ had summoned it. It debated whether James had abdicated by leaving, and whether the throne was vacant. The House of Commons, with its Whig majority, resolved that James had broken the original contract between king and people, had abdicated the government, and that the throne was vacant. The Lords, with a Tory majority, hesitated and debated alternatives, including a regency for James or a regency for his daughter Mary alone. William made clear that he would not serve as regent or as consort to Mary, and the Houses agreed to offer the crown jointly to William and Mary, with executive power to be exercised by William alone.

On 13 February 1689, the two Houses presented the Declaration of Rights to William and Mary, listing the illegal acts of James II, such as suspending laws, levying money without Parliament's consent, keeping a standing army in time of peace without consent, and prosecuting petitioners, and declaring the rights of subjects, including frequent Parliaments, freedom of speech in Parliament, free elections, and the right to petition. As the Parliamentary Archives record, it was read when the crown was offered but was not formally imposed as a condition of acceptance. In December 1689 its provisions were enacted as the Bill of Rights, with additions including a Protestant succession barring Catholics and those married to Catholics from the throne.

The Bill of Rights restricted the exercise of prerogative power. It declared the suspending and dispensing powers, as exercised by James, illegal, condemned the levying of taxes without parliamentary grant, and required parliamentary consent for a standing army in peacetime. In practice, an annual Mutiny Act, passed from 1689, made military discipline depend on Parliament's yearly renewal. The Bill also confirmed that elections should be free and that parliamentary debates should not be questioned outside Parliament. It did not abolish the prerogative, which remained substantial: the king chose ministers, conducted foreign policy, commanded the armed forces, summoned and dissolved Parliament, and vetoed legislation.

The settlement is best understood as a limitation of monarchy, not as a transfer of sovereignty to the people or to a democratic Parliament. It left the franchise narrow, the church established, and Catholics excluded. It also reflected a compromise among Whigs, who emphasized contract and resistance, and Tories, who stressed continuity and avoided saying that the king had been deposed, using the euphemism of abdication. Ideas of John Locke, whose Two Treatises of Government were published in 1689 though largely composed earlier, are often connected to the revolution, but their role is debated and they belong to Chapter 83, where Locke's thought receives full treatment.

The Convention that met on 22 January 1689 had to resolve a question to which seventeenth-century constitutional law gave no clear answer. In the Commons, which had a Whig majority, a resolution of 28 January declared that James II, having endeavored to subvert the constitution of the kingdom by breaking the original contract between king and people, and having withdrawn himself out of the kingdom, had abdicated the government and that the throne was thereby vacant. The Lords, in which Tory and Anglican peers were strong, resisted the language of contract and the declaration of vacancy. Some wanted to declare a regency in James's name, others to place Mary on the throne alone on the ground that James's baby son had been supposititious. The Earl of Danby proposed that Mary be made queen. These possibilities were closed by the refusal of William to be a mere regent, and by his statement that he would not be his wife's gentleman usher, together with Mary's loyalty to her husband. On 6 February, the Lords agreed to a compromise by which the throne was declared vacant, with the word abdicated used in the sense of having abandoned the government, and the crown was offered jointly to William and Mary, with executive power to be exercised by William alone.

The Declaration of Rights was drawn up by a committee of the Commons, whose chair was John Somers, and was designed to set out the grievances against James and the rights of the subject. It was presented to William and Mary on 13 February when they accepted the crown, though it was read at the same time and was not a legal condition of their acceptance. It listed acts of James, including the suspension and dispensation of laws, the Ecclesiastical Commission, the levying of money without grant of Parliament, the raising and keeping of a standing army in time of peace without consent, the prosecution of the bishops, and the violation of the freedom of elections, and it declared them illegal. It also asserted rights, including the subject's right to petition, the right of Protestants to have arms for their defense suitable to their conditions and as allowed by law, freedom of speech in Parliament, and the demand that Parliament should be held frequently. William and Mary were crowned on 11 April 1689, and the Convention declared itself a Parliament on 23 February.

The Bill of Rights, which received royal assent in December 1689, converted these declarations into statute and added provisions on the succession, barring Catholics and those who married Catholics from the throne and providing that the crown should pass to the heirs of Mary, then to Anne, and then to William's own heirs. Other legislation completed the settlement. The Mutiny Act, passed in March 1689 after a mutiny of Scottish regiments, authorized the discipline of the army for a limited time, so that the army could not lawfully exist for more than a year without parliamentary renewal, and in practice it was renewed annually. The Coronation Oath Act required the monarch to promise to govern according to the statutes in Parliament agreed on and the laws and customs of the same, and to preserve the Protestant reformed religion. An oath of allegiance to William and Mary replaced the previous oaths, and the refusal of Archbishop Sancroft and several hundred clergy, who regarded themselves as bound to James, led to the creation of the nonjuror church. The Revolution Settlement thus combined constraints on royal action with provisions to safeguard the Protestant succession.

Contemporary and later commentary has emphasized different aspects of the Settlement. Whigs interpreted it as a contract in which the people, through their representatives, had resisted a tyrant, an interpretation that drew on the writings of Locke and others. Tories emphasized that the crown remained hereditary, that William and Mary had been chosen from the royal line, and that the deposition of a king by subjects was not a precedent. Both views existed in the Convention, and the language of the documents, abdication, vacancy, and the original contract, reflects the compromise between them. Historians such as Jonathan Israel have emphasized the importance of the Dutch military presence and William's role, while others, such as Steve Pincus, have stressed the transformative aims of the revolution and its modernizing ambitions. These interpretive questions are developed in the Historical Debate below.

Historical Vocabulary
Constitutional Monarchy and Parliamentary Sovereignty

Constitutional monarchy is a modern term for a monarchy whose powers are limited by law and custom. In the later seventeenth century, writers spoke instead of a "limited" or "mixed" monarchy, in which the king shared authority with Lords and Commons. After 1689 the Crown retained substantial executive power, and the modern idea of a monarch who reigns but does not govern did not yet exist. Treating the settlement of 1689 as the beginning of a ceremonial monarchy would be anachronistic.

Parliamentary sovereignty refers to the doctrine that Parliament, meaning King, Lords, and Commons together, can make or unmake any law and that no other body can override it. It developed gradually in the eighteenth century and was formulated by later jurists. In 1689, the Convention's actions suggested that Parliament could alter the succession and limit the crown, but contemporaries did not yet describe the constitution in those terms, and cabinet government and prime ministerial responsibility were later developments.

Section 77.46

The Scottish Revolution Settlement and the Transformation of Royal Authority

In Scotland, the revolution took a different course. James VII had alienated Presbyterians by persecution and Protestants of all kinds by his Catholic policy, but he still had supporters, including many Episcopalians, Highland clans, and nobles who held office under him. When James fled, Scottish leaders met in a Convention of Estates in March 1689, summoned by William. It declared that James had forfeited the crown, a stronger formulation than England's abdication, and adopted the Claim of Right in April 1689, listing his illegal acts and declaring that prelacy was a grievance, which was an implicit demand to abolish bishops.

The Convention offered the crown to William and Mary on terms set out in the Claim of Right and a list of grievances. The Scottish Parliament abolished episcopacy and, in 1690, restored Presbyterian church government, with the Church of Scotland as the national church. It also abolished the Lords of the Articles, the committee that had allowed the crown to control the Parliament's agenda, a significant constitutional change that gave the Scottish Parliament greater independence than before.

The settlement did not command universal support. A Jacobite rising led by John Graham of Claverhouse, Viscount Dundee, won a victory at Killiecrankie on 27 July 1689 but collapsed when Dundee was killed, and was defeated at Dunkeld. Highland loyalty to James persisted, and the government demanded oaths of allegiance from the clans, leading to the Massacre of Glencoe on 13 February 1692, in which soldiers, quartered among the MacDonalds, killed about thirty of their hosts for tardiness in taking the oath. The event, which provoked public outrage, shows the coercive side of the settlement.

For the Scots, the Revolution Settlement meant greater parliamentary authority and a Presbyterian church, but it also meant that Scotland's monarch, William, was primarily preoccupied with England and the continent, and that Scottish interests might be neglected. These tensions would shape the argument for union two decades later.

The Scottish settlement was more radical in its constitutional language and more partisan in its consequences. Following James's flight, a group of Scottish nobles and others in London invited William to take over the administration, and in January 1689 he issued a letter summoning a Convention of Estates to meet at Edinburgh on 14 March. When it assembled, supporters of the Revolution, led by the Duke of Hamilton, who was elected president, had a majority over those who supported James, whose leading advocate was Viscount Dundee. After a stormy debate, and after Dundee left Edinburgh with a small body of horsemen, the Convention resolved on 4 April that James had forfeited the crown by his misgovernment. On 11 April 1689, it adopted the Claim of Right, which listed the illegal acts by which James had violated the constitution of the kingdom, declared that prelacy was an insupportable grievance and ought to be abolished, and offered the crown to William and Mary. William and Mary accepted it in May, and the Convention was turned into a Parliament.

The Claim of Right claimed that James had assumed the crown without taking the coronation oath, a claim that was difficult to substantiate, but the legal point was less important than the political one. The Scottish Parliament abolished the Lords of the Articles, the committee that had enabled the crown to control the legislative agenda, in the session of 1690. In the same year it restored the Presbyterian church, ratifying the Westminster Confession of Faith and the form of church government that had been in force before 1660, and it restored the ministers who had been ejected in 1662. The Episcopalian clergy, including the bishops, were removed or forced to accept the new order, and many Episcopalians, particularly in the northeast, remained loyal to the Stuart dynasty. In 1689, in the southwest, Presbyterian crowds had expelled Episcopalian ministers from their parishes in a series of violent incidents known as rabbling. The settlement thus brought a kind of reversal of fortunes. Presbyterians, who had suffered in the Killing Time, gained control of the church, and Episcopalians became a persecuted minority and a source of Jacobite support.

Armed resistance to the settlement in the Highlands showed that its authority was not universal. Dundee raised the clans for James, and on 27 July 1689 his force, charging downhill, routed the government army under General Hugh Mackay at Killiecrankie, but Dundee himself was killed in the battle, and the Jacobite army was defeated a few weeks later at Dunkeld by a regiment of Cameronian Presbyterians. In 1690, a further Jacobite defeat at Cromdale ended the rising, but Highland loyalty to James persisted. In 1691, the government offered pardons to Highlanders who took the oath of allegiance by 1 January 1692, and the chief of the MacDonalds of Glencoe, Alasdair MacIain, arrived at the place of swearing a few days late. The Secretary of State for Scotland, Sir John Dalrymple of Stair, who disliked the clan, obtained a royal order to deal with the MacDonalds. On the morning of 13 February 1692, a company of soldiers who had been quartered among the clan for some days, under the command of a Campbell officer, killed some thirty-eight men and women, and many others died of exposure after fleeing. The massacre aroused public indignation, and an inquiry in 1695 blamed Dalrymple but not the king.

For most Scots, the more significant problem was not the Highland rising but the relationship with England. William's attention was directed to the war against France, and Scottish interests were not represented at the English court. The Navigation Acts excluded Scots from English colonial trade, English tariffs hurt Scottish exports, and during the years of dearth in the later 1690s, often called the Seven Ill Years, harvest failures led to famine and heavy mortality in parts of the country, which have been estimated by some historians at a tenth or more of the population in the worst-affected districts, though the figures are uncertain. These grievances formed the background to the colonial adventure at Darien and to the debates about union.

Section 77.47

The Williamite War in Ireland and the Uneven Consequences of Revolution

In Ireland, James II had a base of support among Catholics, who had benefited from the policies of his Lord Deputy, the Earl of Tyrconnell, who had promoted Catholics to the army, judiciary, and offices. James landed at Kinsale in March 1689 with French support and summoned an Irish Parliament that repealed the Restoration land settlement. Protestants in Ulster, fearing massacre, closed the gates of Derry in December 1688, and the city withstood a siege from April to late July 1689, in which many inhabitants died of starvation and disease before relief ships arrived.

William came to Ireland in June 1690 with a large multinational army, including Dutch, Danish, German, French Huguenot, and English troops. On 1 July 1690 (Old Style) at the River Boyne, near Drogheda, his army defeated James's, and James fled to France. The war continued. The decisive battle was at Aughrim on 12 July 1691, where the Williamite general Ginkel defeated the Jacobite army, and Limerick surrendered. The Treaty of Limerick, signed in October 1691, promised Catholics the religious freedoms they had enjoyed under Charles II, and allowed Jacobite soldiers to go to France, which many did, an exodus remembered as the Flight of the Wild Geese.

The consequences were harsh. Parliament in Dublin refused to ratify the treaty's civil articles in full. Land confiscations transferred much of the remaining Catholic-owned land to Protestants, and by the early eighteenth century Catholics, though the majority of the population, owned a small proportion of the land. A series of penal laws, enacted mainly from 1695, restricted Catholic education, arms, landholding, and political participation. A Protestant Ascendancy, composed of members of the Church of Ireland, dominated Ireland's Parliament, church, and economy, with Presbyterians and Catholics excluded from power in different ways.

The war in Ireland, with Derry and the Boyne commemorated by Protestants and the Treaty of Limerick remembered by Catholics as broken, became a basis of lasting political memory. For Ireland, the Revolution brought not liberty but a harder subordination, and an account that concentrated on London would badly misrepresent it. The war was also part of a European conflict, since French and Dutch forces fought in it as part of the Nine Years' War.

The Irish phase of the Revolution has to be understood as a war between rival claimants and as a conflict over the structure of Irish society. Under James II, Richard Talbot, Earl of Tyrconnell, who became Lord Deputy in 1687, replaced Protestant officers with Catholics, remodeled the army, and appointed Catholics to the judiciary and to the corporations. These measures alarmed Protestants and encouraged Catholic hopes of recovering lands lost in the seventeenth century. When James landed at Kinsale in March 1689 with French officers and arms, he held a Parliament in Dublin in May that is sometimes called the Patriot Parliament. It declared the independence of the Irish Parliament from the English, repealed the Cromwellian and Restoration land settlements, passed an Act of Attainder against some two thousand Protestants who were absent or supporting William, and affirmed liberty of conscience for all. Its legislation showed the Catholic gentry's priorities, and it ensured that Protestants, fearing the loss of their property, would fight.

The northern Protestants resisted. In December 1688, apprentices in Derry shut the gates against a Catholic regiment sent to garrison the city, and in the spring of 1689, after James's army approached, the city's governor, Robert Lundy, was replaced by officers who were determined to hold out. The siege lasted from 18 April to 28 July 1689, during which the inhabitants and refugees, who numbered many thousands, suffered severely from hunger and disease, until relief ships broke the boom across the River Foyle. In the same period, the Protestant garrison at Enniskillen defeated a Jacobite force at Newtownbutler. Schomberg, William's general, landed in August with a force that suffered heavily from disease in its camp near Dundalk. In June 1690, William himself came to Ireland with an army of about thirty-six thousand men, drawn from several nations, and met James's army of some twenty-five thousand, including French troops, at the Boyne on 1 July (Old Style). William's victory, in a battle in which both kings were present, was strategically less decisive than it was later remembered, since the Jacobite army withdrew in good order, but James returned to France, and Dublin fell.

The war continued for another year. William failed to take Limerick in August 1690 and returned to England, leaving the command to Ginkel, and Marlborough captured Cork and Kinsale in autumn 1690. In 1691, Ginkel crossed the Shannon and defeated the Jacobite army at Aughrim on 12 July (Old Style), where its commander St Ruth was killed, in the bloodiest battle fought in Ireland. Galway surrendered, and Limerick, besieged for a second time, capitulated in October. The Treaty of Limerick, signed on 3 October 1691, had military and civil articles. The military articles allowed Jacobite soldiers to go to France with their arms, and some twelve thousand did so, in what was called the Flight of the Wild Geese. The civil articles promised to Catholics the exercise of their religion as in the time of Charles II and protection of the property of those who submitted. The Irish Parliament, dominated by Protestants, did not ratify all of these articles in full.

The consequences for Irish society were severe. Williamite confiscations affected more than a million acres, and the Catholic share of Irish land, which was already low after the Cromwellian settlement and had recovered somewhat under Charles II, fell by commonly cited estimates from about a fifth of the land in 1688 to about one seventh by 1703. From 1695 a series of penal laws, passed by the Irish Parliament, disarmed Catholics, prohibited their children from being educated abroad and Catholics from teaching, and restricted their ownership and inheritance of land, so that a Catholic could not purchase land or lease it for a long term and, under an act of 1704, the estate of a Catholic was to be divided among his sons unless one conformed. An act of 1704 also required officeholders to take the sacrament according to the Church of Ireland, which excluded Presbyterians as well as Catholics. These laws were not systematically enforced in all places and times, but they formalized the dominance of the Protestant landed elite. The Williamite victory was thus a revolution of a different character in Ireland, one that consolidated a settler regime.

Section 77.48

The Toleration Act of 1689, Religious Exclusions, and the Boundaries of Political Rights

The Toleration Act of 1689, formally an act exempting Protestant dissenters from the penalties of certain laws, granted Protestant dissenters who took oaths of allegiance and supremacy and subscribed to a declaration against transubstantiation the right to worship publicly in licensed meeting houses, and exempted them from the penalties of the Conventicle and Five Mile Acts. Dissenting ministers were required to subscribe to most of the Thirty-Nine Articles. Quakers were allowed to make an affirmation in place of an oath.

The act did not establish religious liberty in the modern sense. It did not repeal the Test and Corporation Acts, so dissenters remained barred from office unless they practiced "occasional conformity," taking Anglican communion once a year, a practice that provoked controversy under Queen Anne. It excluded Catholics and non-Trinitarians, and a separate act of 1698 imposed severe penalties for denial of the Trinity. The Church of England remained established, financed by tithes, and its bishops sat in the House of Lords. Universities remained largely closed to dissenters, who developed their own academies.

The act's principal significance was to end the legal persecution of most Protestant dissenters and to establish that a plurality of Protestant worship could coexist with an established church. That was a significant change from the 1660s, and it contributed to the development of a more diverse religious culture. Yet it reflected a pragmatic recognition of dissenters' strength and their role in the revolution, and not a principle of conscience. Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration, also published in 1689, argued for broader toleration but excluded Catholics and atheists, a reminder of how limited contemporary arguments were.

The boundary of political rights was even narrower than that of religious toleration. The franchise in England depended on property and varied by borough, and the vast majority of adults, including all women, were excluded. Oaths of allegiance and abjuration required of officeholders excluded Catholics and others. Jews were tolerated in practice but without full legal rights. In Ireland, the penal laws deprived the majority of political rights. Toleration and representation were thus extended to a limited group, and the settlement's reliance on Protestant identity defined those who were outside the political nation.

The Toleration Act received royal assent on 24 May 1689, as part of a package of measures meant to unite Protestants behind the new regime. Alongside it, the government and Parliament attempted a more ambitious Comprehension Bill, which would have eased the terms of conformity so that moderate Presbyterians could rejoin the Church of England. The bill failed in the House of Commons, where Anglican members, and in the Convocation of the clergy, were unwilling to alter the Prayer Book, and so the Toleration Act, which had been conceived as a stopgap, became the lasting legal framework for dissent. Its wording is revealing: it did not repeal the penal laws, but exempted those who met its conditions from their penalties. Those conditions included taking the oaths of allegiance and supremacy and making the declaration against transubstantiation, and, for ministers, subscribing to most of the Thirty-Nine Articles, except those on church government and ceremonies, with Baptists being permitted to refuse the article on infant baptism.

The practical operation of toleration depended on registration. Dissenting meeting houses had to be certified at the bishop's court or at quarter sessions, and their doors could not be locked during services. Thousands of such certificates were issued in the following decades, which allows historians to map the growth of dissent. But the dissenters' disabilities continued, and the Test and Corporation Acts continued to exclude them from office except through the practice of occasional conformity, which Anne's Tory ministers tried to stop through bills in 1702 to 1704 and in 1711, when an Act against Occasional Conformity was passed, which was repealed in 1719. The Act of 1698, for the suppression of blasphemy and profaneness, made it an offense to deny the Trinity, and it was enforced to a limited extent. The theologian Thomas Aikenhead was executed for blasphemy in Edinburgh in 1697, and the Trinitarian restriction in the Toleration Act remained in force until the nineteenth century.

The political rights of the population were narrower than the legal privileges of the dissenting Protestants. The English parliamentary electorate was determined by a complex combination of property qualifications in the counties and varying franchises in the boroughs, which included freemen, householders, and in some cases only members of the corporation. By common estimate the electorate in early eighteenth-century England was a few hundred thousand men, a minority of the adult male population, and all women were excluded. Elections remained expensive and often uncontested. The statutes of 1689 improved the security of Parliament as an institution, but the Convention and the Bill of Rights did not alter the franchise. The Licensing Act, which had required the licensing of printed works, lapsed in 1695 and was not renewed, which opened the way to a freer press without any declaration of freedom of the press as a principle. The result was a constitutional order in which some Protestants were protected while Catholics, Jews, non-Trinitarians, and the poor were excluded from the political nation.

Section 77.49

Parliamentary Government, Public Credit, and the Financial Revolution after 1688

William's priority was war with France, which he fought as part of the Nine Years' War (1688–1697), and war required money on a scale the Stuart monarchy had never raised. The new regime found it through a combination of higher taxation, notably the land tax first imposed in 1692, and borrowing. The crucial innovation was long-term borrowing guaranteed by Parliament. Because Parliament, representing the landowners and merchants who would pay, authorized the taxes pledged for interest, lenders had greater confidence than in the king's personal promise, which Charles II had broken in the Stop of the Exchequer.

The Bank of England, founded in 1694, formalized the system. A group of subscribers lent the government £1.2 million, secured by taxes, at 8 percent interest, and in return received a charter to operate as a corporate bank. The Bank issued notes, discounted bills, and managed government debt. Other innovations included the development of a national debt, funded by taxes, annuity schemes, and later, the creation of joint-stock companies and a market in government securities. The Triennial Act of 1694 required elections at least every three years, and the principle that Parliament must meet annually in practice, because money had to be voted each year, became fixed.

Historians call this set of changes the financial revolution. It enabled Britain to borrow far more than France, with lower interest rates, even though France had a larger population and economy. Scholars such as John Brewer have described the resulting entity as a fiscal-military state, one capable of mobilizing resources for war through taxation, borrowing, and administrative expertise, especially the excise service. The Dutch experience of public credit, which William knew well, influenced these developments, though their extent is debated, and the English system developed through local political conflicts and was also shaped by Tory and Whig disputes about the "monied interest."

The connection between constitutional limits and fiscal strength was thus direct. A monarch dependent on Parliament for funds had to maintain its confidence, but a Parliament that supported the war could mobilize resources unavailable to a king who claimed unlimited authority yet lacked credit. This is the central insight of the comparison with France, discussed in Chapter 76, and it should not be oversimplified: France had its own financial instruments, and Britain's debt created tensions that would shape politics in the eighteenth century.

The financial revolution followed from the demands of the Nine Years' War, which England entered in 1689 as a member of the Grand Alliance against Louis XIV. The war required an army that, on the English establishment, reached on the order of eighty thousand men in the later years and a navy that was heavily engaged against French fleets and privateers, and it quickly exhausted the revenue that Parliament had voted to the new regime. The first response was higher taxation. In 1692 Parliament introduced a land tax, which taxed the income from land at four shillings in the pound, assessed by local commissioners, and the excise and customs were increased. The second response was borrowing. In the early 1690s, the government raised money through a series of devices, including a lottery loan known as the Million Adventure of 1693, annuities, and loans secured on specific taxes, with Parliament appropriating funds for particular purposes and requiring that accounts be examined by commissioners. The Tonnage Act of 1694 imposed duties on shipping to provide the revenue to pay interest on a new loan.

The Bank of England was created by the same act. In exchange for a loan of one million two hundred thousand pounds to the government at eight percent interest, together with an annual payment for management, the subscribers were incorporated as the Governor and Company of the Bank of England in July 1694, with the power to issue notes and to deal in bills and bullion. The subscription was filled in about twelve days. The Bank was supported by Whig financiers and merchants, who saw it as a means of tying the interests of the monied men to the new regime, and opposed by Tories, who feared that it would increase the dependence of the landed gentry on financiers. In 1695, Scotland created its own Bank of Scotland by act of its Parliament. The Great Recoinage of 1696, in which the worn silver coinage was withdrawn and reminted under the supervision of the Treasury and Isaac Newton at the Mint, produced a severe monetary contraction and a crisis in credit, which was managed with the help of Exchequer bills and other expedients.

The political effects of the new system were as significant as its economic ones. The Triennial Act of 1694 required Parliament to be elected at least every three years, and the need to vote supplies each year meant that sessions became annual. The Nine Years' War ended with the Treaty of Ryswick in 1697, in which Louis recognized William as king, and Parliament then turned to the question of the standing army, which in 1698 and 1699 it reduced to seven thousand men born in England and Ireland, requiring the dismissal of William's Dutch Guards, a decision that angered the king. In the same period, Parliament settled a Civil List of seven hundred thousand pounds a year on William for the expenses of his household and civil government, separating these from the cost of war, which Parliament voted separately. The national debt, which stood at a few millions in 1688, was on the order of sixteen to seventeen million pounds by the end of the war, according to commonly cited estimates, and it continued to grow in the following decades.

The system was contested. Tory writers, such as Charles Davenant, regarded the new financial institutions with suspicion, arguing that the war was being fought at the expense of the landed interest for the benefit of financiers, and in the decade after 1690 the debate over the monied interest and the landed interest was a central theme of party politics. Whigs, by contrast, argued that public credit was the guarantee of the Revolution settlement, since the creditors of the state had an interest in its survival. The comparison with the Dutch system is instructive. English financial innovators, some of them Dutch or Huguenot in origin, borrowed ideas about annuities, public banks, and joint-stock finance from the Dutch Republic, but their application depended on distinctive English conditions: a Parliament that could vote taxes for the long term and could be trusted by lenders to keep faith, a large and efficient administration for the excise and customs, and a commercial economy that generated a growing tax base. The financial revolution did not make Britain richer overnight, but it enabled the state to sustain a series of large wars over the next century.

Historical Debate 3

Did the Glorious Revolution Establish Parliamentary Sovereignty?

The question: Was 1688 to 1689 a decisive constitutional revolution, or a more limited dynastic and confessional settlement whose constitutional consequences developed afterward?

Interpretation A: A constitutional turning point. Historians in the Whig tradition and many later scholars argue that the settlement subordinated the crown to Parliament in matters of taxation, army, law-making, and succession, and that fiscal dependence made regular parliamentary sessions permanent.
Interpretation B: A limited settlement. Others argue that the revolution was primarily about preserving the Protestant succession and the established church against a Catholic king, that William retained great control of foreign policy and appointments, and that parliamentary sovereignty and cabinet government developed gradually through eighteenth-century practice. Some also stress the violence of conquest in Ireland and Scotland.

Evidence: Both draw on the Bill of Rights, the debates of the Convention, the Mutiny Act, the financial acts, and the later practice of ministers. They differ about whether to read the settlement in light of its text and immediate context or of its long-term consequences.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether 1689 is treated as the origin of parliamentary government or as one stage in a longer development, and whether Whig narratives of liberty can be accepted.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Households: Amsterdam, England, and Ireland or Scotland

An Amsterdam household under the regents. A merchant family of middling wealth might live in a narrow brick house on a canal, with trade goods stored in the attic and a ground-floor office. The wife often managed accounts and household servants, and the family attended the Reformed church or, if Mennonite, Lutheran, or Catholic, a private congregation. Taxes on beer, peat, and other goods raised the cost of daily life, and the family's savings might be invested in municipal annuities or company shares. A poor household, by contrast, might depend on a laborer's wages, a wife's spinning, and charity.

An English household during the Civil Wars. A gentry or yeoman family might face billeting, assessment payments, the requisition of horses and grain, and the absence or death of men in service. Wives sometimes managed estates alone, petitioned committees for relief, or defended property, and they faced the risk of plunder and disease. Neighbors might be divided by allegiance, and religious services could change with the local control of the parish.

An Irish or Scottish household in the later Stuart conflicts. In Ireland, a Catholic farming family in the 1650s might be transplanted to Connacht, or in the 1690s lose land or face the penal laws, while Protestant settlers in Ulster endured sieges and hunger in 1689. In the Scottish southwest, a Presbyterian family might attend illegal conventicles and risk fines or worse in the 1680s, and a Highland family might face demands for oaths in 1692.

These examples illustrate the same developments from very different positions. The evidence for ordinary households is fragmentary, drawn from probate inventories, parish and church records, petitions, diaries, and legal records, and it often survives better for the literate and propertied.

Section 77.50

The Act of Settlement, Queen Anne, Political Parties, and Dynastic Succession

When William's wife Mary died in 1694 and William's own health worsened, the succession question returned. The death in 1700 of Anne's last surviving child, the Duke of Gloucester, left no Protestant heirs in the direct line. Parliament responded with the Act of Settlement of 1701, which settled the succession on Sophia, Electress of Hanover, granddaughter of James I, and her Protestant heirs, excluding the Catholic descendants of James II. It also set conditions on future monarchs, including that they must join the Church of England, that foreign policy commitments to defend foreign territories required parliamentary consent, and that judges would hold office during good behavior and be removable only by address of both Houses.

Anne, who succeeded in March 1702, reigned until 1714. Her reign saw the War of the Spanish Succession, discussed in Chapter 80, in which the Duke of Marlborough, whose wife Sarah was the queen's confidante, won major victories such as Blenheim in 1704, and the growth of party conflict. Whigs and Tories competed in frequent elections, with differing views on war, religion, and the monied interest. Controversies over occasional conformity, the impeachment of the High Church preacher Henry Sacheverell in 1710, and the ministry of the Tory Robert Harley and Henry St John, who negotiated the Peace of Utrecht in 1713, show a politics of organized factions, press campaigns, and electoral contests.

Anne was the last monarch to veto a bill, in 1708, and her reign saw ministers increasingly depend on parliamentary majorities, but she chose ministers and could shift them, as in the Tory victory of 1710 that she encouraged. The relationship between monarchy and representative government remained unsettled. Jacobites, supporters of the exiled Stuarts, remained a threat, and the possibility of a Stuart restoration shaped politics until 1745.

These developments show how the 1689 settlement operated as a framework of competition, not as an end to conflict. Parties, press, and parliament created a political culture that was far more participatory than in 1640, although it was still confined to a small electorate, perhaps a few hundred thousand men in England, a matter of estimate and dispute.

Party conflict was intense in the reign of Anne. Elections took place frequently, in 1702, 1705, 1708, 1710, and 1713, and the two parties competed over religion, war, and finance. Tories, who drew support from the gentry and the Anglican clergy, emphasized the defense of the Church of England and were wary of continental commitments and the monied interest. Whigs, who were allied with the dissenters and many financiers and merchants, supported the war against France, the Hanoverian succession, and toleration for Protestants. These were not parties in the modern sense, but loose and shifting groupings with leaders such as the Whig Junto, Lords Somers, Wharton, Halifax, Orford, and Sunderland, and Tory leaders such as Robert Harley and Henry St John. The queen, who preferred to govern through moderate ministers of both parties, nonetheless found herself forced to rely on the Whigs in the middle years of the war and on the Tories after 1710.

Church issues dominated the political agenda in the early years. The Tories introduced bills against occasional conformity in 1702, 1703, and 1704, and in 1704 an attempt to attach the bill to a money bill, the Tack, failed. In 1710 the Whig government's impeachment of the High Church preacher Henry Sacheverell, who had preached against dissenters and the Revolution Settlement, resulted in a conviction with a very light sentence and in widespread popular demonstrations in his favor, which contributed to the Tory landslide in the general election of 1710. The Tory ministry of Harley and St John then made peace with France in negotiations that led to the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713, a policy that angered the Whigs and the allies, including the Dutch, who felt abandoned. The queen also established Queen Anne's Bounty in 1704 to augment the incomes of poor clergy, which showed her attachment to the Church.

The succession was the central long-term problem. After the Duke of Gloucester's death in 1700, the Act of Settlement of 1701 named the Electress Sophia of Hanover and her Protestant heirs. It provided that future monarchs should join the communion of the Church of England, that England should not be obliged to go to war to defend foreign territories of the monarch without parliamentary consent, that foreign-born persons should not hold office or sit in Parliament, that judges should hold office during good behavior and be removable only on an address of both Houses, and that a royal pardon should not be pleadable against impeachment. Some of these provisions, notably those about foreigners and the privy council, reflected the experience of William's reign and were later repealed or modified. In 1705, Parliament passed a Regency Act and naturalized the Hanoverian family, in an effort to ensure that Hanoverian succession would occur smoothly. In Scotland, the Jacobite cause attracted Episcopalians and Highland chiefs, and in March 1708, a French fleet carrying the Pretender approached the Firth of Forth but retreated without landing. In response to the invasion scare, Anne vetoed the Scottish Militia Bill, the last royal veto of a bill, an act that reflected her concern about arming Scottish subjects.

Section 77.51

The Union of England and Scotland in 1707 and the Creation of Great Britain

The union of 1707 was not the natural continuation of the union of crowns of 1603. Throughout the seventeenth century, the two kingdoms had remained separate in law and institutions, and attempts at closer union in 1604, in 1650s Cromwellian incorporation, and in 1667 to 1670 had failed. The Revolution Settlement left Scotland with a more independent Parliament and a king who rarely visited. Scots felt neglected, and they were excluded from English colonial trade by the Navigation Acts.

The Darien Scheme, a Scottish colonial venture in Panama organized by the Company of Scotland, ended in disaster in 1698 to 1700, with the settlement abandoned after disease, Spanish hostility, and lack of English support, and with heavy losses for Scottish investors, including many in the elite. It left bitterness against England and a sense of vulnerability. When the English Act of Settlement of 1701 chose the Hanoverian succession without consulting the Scots, the Scottish Parliament responded in 1704 with the Act of Security, asserting the right to choose a different successor unless Scotland received trade and constitutional guarantees, and England responded in 1705 with the Alien Act, threatening economic sanctions against Scotland unless it entered negotiations or accepted the Hanoverian succession.

Commissioners from both kingdoms negotiated the Articles of Union in 1706. They provided for a single kingdom of Great Britain, a single Parliament, the Hanoverian succession, free trade within the kingdom and access to colonial trade, and a common coinage and customs and excise. Scotland would send 45 members to the House of Commons and 16 representative peers to the House of Lords, a number small relative to population and criticized by opponents. Scotland retained its own legal system, courts, and Presbyterian national church, and its educational and local institutions, guarantees included in the Treaty. Financial provisions, the "Equivalent," compensated Scotland for taking on part of the English national debt and compensated Darien investors.

The Union was intensely controversial in Scotland. There were riots and petitions against it, and many suspected that bribes and promises had influenced votes, and indeed some Scottish nobles received payments, though historians debate their importance. Nonetheless the Scottish Parliament ratified the Articles in January 1707, and the Acts of Union took effect on 1 May 1707. The Union created a new state, but it did not create a uniform nation: Scotland preserved distinct law, church, and education. Ireland, by contrast, remained a separate kingdom with its own Parliament, dominated by Protestants and subordinate to English legislation, a status that would change only in 1801.

The origins of the Union lay in the interaction of the succession question and the economic situation of Scotland. After the Revolution, the Scottish Parliament had acquired greater independence, and Scottish politicians pursued a variety of aims. The Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies, created by act of the Scottish Parliament in 1695, raised a large sum from Scottish investors, commonly estimated at about four hundred thousand pounds sterling, which represented a very high proportion of the country's liquid capital. The Company planned a colony at Darien, on the isthmus of Panama, and the first expedition sailed in July 1698 with about twelve hundred settlers in five ships. The colony, called New Caledonia, was beset by disease, supply problems, and the hostility of Spain, and it was abandoned in 1699, as was a second expedition in 1700. The English East India Company and the English government, which did not wish to offend Spain, had withheld support, and the failure was blamed on English hostility. Investors, many of them members of the Scottish elite, lost substantial sums, and the crisis left a legacy of resentment.

The immediate cause of the negotiations was the confrontation of 1704 and 1705. In 1703 the Scottish Parliament had passed an Act anent Peace and War, which asserted its right to decide on war and peace after the queen's death, and in 1704 an Act of Security that provided that unless Scotland received satisfaction on trade and constitutional questions, the Estates would choose a successor to Anne different from the person who succeeded to the English throne. The English Parliament responded with the Alien Act of 1705, which declared that Scots would be treated as aliens in England unless Scotland began negotiations for union or accepted the Hanoverian succession by Christmas 1705, and which also prohibited the import of Scottish coal, linen, and cattle. The measure threatened serious economic damage and strengthened the hand of those in Scotland who favored negotiations. Thirty-one commissioners from each kingdom met in London in the spring and summer of 1706 and agreed the Articles of Union on 22 July 1706.

The Articles set out the terms of a union that incorporated both kingdoms into one. Article 1 declared that the kingdoms would be united into one kingdom by the name of Great Britain, Article 2 provided for the succession under the Act of Settlement, and Article 3 for a single Parliament. Article 4 gave Scots freedom of trade and navigation within the united kingdom and its dominions and plantations, an economic benefit that had been denied by the Navigation Acts. The articles on taxation and finance provided that Scotland would bear the same customs and excise duties as England in most respects, with some adjustments, and that Scotland would receive a sum known as the Equivalent, of about four hundred thousand pounds, as compensation for taking on a share of the English national debt, which was used in part to repay investors in the Darien Company and to pay arrears owed to Scottish officials. Article 22 gave Scotland sixteen representative peers in the House of Lords and forty-five members of the House of Commons, a representation that reflected Scotland's taxable capacity and not its population. Scottish law and courts, the royal burghs, and the heritable jurisdictions were preserved, and the security of the Church of Scotland was guaranteed in a separate Act of Security.

The Articles were fiercely debated in the Scottish Parliament in the autumn of 1706. There were riots in Edinburgh, Glasgow, and elsewhere, and petitions from burghs, shires, and presbyteries opposed the Union. Opponents, including the Duke of Hamilton and Fletcher of Saltoun, argued that it would end Scottish independence and subject the country to English interests, while supporters, including the Earl of Seafield, the Duke of Queensberry, and the Earl of Stair, argued that it would bring peace, security, and trade. Money and patronage also played their part, since the English government distributed payments to some Scottish nobles, a fact that gave rise to the Jacobite and nationalist claim that Scotland was bought and sold for English gold. Historians have emphasized that the principal motives were the security of the Protestant succession, the fear of English economic sanctions, and the opportunities of trade. The vote on the first Article, on 4 November 1706, was 116 to 83, and the Scottish Parliament ratified the treaty on 16 January 1707 by 110 to 67. The English Parliament passed its own Act, and the Union took effect on 1 May 1707.

The Union created the Kingdom of Great Britain and a new Parliament, which for several decades after 1707 functioned much like the English Parliament with the addition of the Scottish members. Scottish institutions that had been guaranteed, the legal system, the church, the universities, and the burghs, continued to function, and the preservation of these institutions has been described as the key to the Union's durability. The first decades were marked by tensions, particularly over taxation, including the extension of the malt tax in 1713, which provoked a motion in the House of Lords to dissolve the Union, and by Jacobite risings in 1715 and 1719. Ireland, by contrast, remained a separate kingdom with its own Parliament, which was subordinated to the English by Poynings' Law and, after the Declaratory Act of 1720, by explicit British legislative supremacy. The distinction between the personal union of 1603 and the parliamentary union of 1707 should therefore be kept clear: the first joined two states in one monarch, the second merged the two kingdoms, though not Ireland, into a single state.

Section 77.52

The Political and Social Limits of Dutch and British Constitutional Government

The Dutch Republic and post-1688 Britain both limited the arbitrary power of rulers and both supported unusually effective fiscal and commercial systems, but neither offered equal representation. In the Republic, the highest institutions were closed to all but a narrow set of urban regents and provincial nobles, and Catholics, Jews, and other minorities were excluded from office. The Generality Lands had no voice. In Britain, the franchise was limited by property and varied wildly among boroughs and counties, with rotten boroughs and disenfranchised cities. Women could not vote or sit in Parliament, though some exercised influence through property, patronage, or writing, and a few women such as the queen held the crown.

Religious disabilities structured both societies. In the Republic, Reformed Church membership was a condition of many offices. In Britain, the Test and Corporation Acts excluded dissenters from many offices, the penal laws in Ireland excluded Catholics from nearly all public life, and Scotland's Presbyterian establishment excluded Episcopalians from some roles. The idea that toleration meant equality was foreign to the period.

Both systems also depended on subordination of other territories and peoples. The Dutch Generality Lands, the VOC's possessions, and slavery in the Atlantic trade supplied wealth and labor. England's wealth increasingly depended on the Atlantic slave system and the colonies, as discussed in Chapters 70 and 72, and Ireland was treated as a conquered kingdom. The constitutional liberties celebrated in later narratives were thus bound up with unfreedom elsewhere, and the freedoms of some depended on the exclusion of others.

The key analytical distinction is between limits on rulers and political equality. A constitutional limit means that a ruler cannot tax without consent or imprison without cause. Political equality means that all members of society have comparable rights to participate. Early modern constitutional change achieved the former for a narrow group, not the latter. Treating the first as the beginning of the second risks reading seventeenth-century events as stages in a march toward democracy, a teleology this chapter rejects.

The comparison of exclusions should be made concrete. In the Dutch Republic, the number of people who held political power was small. A few thousand regents in the cities and a few hundred nobles in the provincial estates governed a population that is commonly estimated at about two million. Citizenship in the cities, the burgher right, was held by a minority of residents, and it was hereditary or purchasable. Women could own and manage property but could not hold office. Catholics, who may have been a large minority of the population, could not hold public office and worshipped in private churches. Jews could not hold office and were excluded from most guilds. The Generality Lands, with their Catholic populations, were governed directly by the States General without representation. Outside Europe, VOC and WIC possessions were governed by company officials, and slavery was an important institution in Dutch colonies in the Atlantic and Asia.

In the British case, the exclusions were similarly consequential, though differently arranged. The English county electorate was defined by a forty-shilling freehold, a qualification that had been fixed in 1430 and that, because of inflation, enfranchised a large proportion of the substantial farmers, while borough franchises varied from a handful of voters to thousands. Parliamentary seats were unevenly distributed, with many small boroughs under the influence of patrons. Scotland's franchise, even narrower, was restricted largely to landowners holding a certain valuation, so that a very small number of voters elected Scottish members. In Ireland, the Protestant minority controlled Parliament and office. The Navigation Acts, the Royal African Company, which had a monopoly of the slave trade until 1698, and, after 1713, the asiento that gave Britain the right to supply enslaved people to the Spanish colonies, tied English prosperity to the institution of slavery. Enslaved Africans formed a rapidly growing population in Barbados, Jamaica, and the American colonies, and they had no political rights at all.

The two systems differed in the kinds of limits they placed on rulers. In the Republic, the limit was the dispersion of sovereignty among provinces and cities, and the ruler, the stadtholder, was an officer of the estates. In Britain after 1689, the limit was the dependence of the monarch on Parliament for taxation and legislation, together with statutory rules about the church and succession. Both reduced the capacity of a single ruler to impose his will, but they did so in the interest of particular elites, the regents and the landed gentry and merchants. Recognizing this should not lead to a cynical dismissal. The limitation of arbitrary power, the protection of property from arbitrary seizure, and the recognition of a plurality of Protestant churches were real achievements. They were also partial, and their beneficiaries and victims should both be recorded.

Section 77.53

Two Constitutional Alternatives: Republican Government, Parliamentary Monarchy, and European State Capacity

The two systems developed differently but shared important features. Both arose from resistance to a composite monarchy's attempts to impose uniform religion and heavier taxation, both depended on representative bodies of property-holding elites, and both developed credit-based fiscal systems that allowed them to mobilize resources beyond what their taxable populations alone might suggest. Both also faced internal conflict over the role of a dynastic figure: the stadtholder in the Republic and the king in Britain.

They differed in where sovereignty lay. In the Republic, it was dispersed among provinces and cities, with the States General acting as a federal body of delegates, and the House of Orange as a recurrent focus for centralization. In Britain, after turbulent experiments, sovereignty came to be exercised through the Crown in Parliament, with the monarch retaining executive power but depending on Parliament for money and legislation. The Dutch system was more decentralized and slower to act, while the British system, with a single parliament and growing administrative apparatus, proved more able to concentrate resources in the eighteenth century. This partly explains why Britain's power rose while the Republic's relative position declined after 1700.

The two cases demonstrate that the French model of royal absolutism was not the only path to state capacity, as discussed in Chapter 76. A government with regular legislative authorization of taxation, credible public debt, and access to commercial capital could mobilize large military resources, and could do so with greater credit than a monarch claiming broader personal authority. But it would be wrong to conclude that parliamentary or republican arrangements were inherently stronger. Their success depended on specific circumstances, including commercial wealth, naval geography, and elite cohesion, and both systems were vulnerable, as the Rampjaar and the crises of 1640 to 1660 show.

The connections between the two were deep. Trade rivalry shaped the Navigation Acts and the Anglo-Dutch Wars, the House of Orange linked Dutch and Stuart dynasties, William III led the Republic and then Britain against Louis XIV, and Dutch financial practices influenced English institutions. After 1688, the two states were allies against France for most of the following decades, and Dutch capital flowed into British funds. At the end of Anne's reign, her death on 1 August 1714 brought George, Elector of Hanover, to the throne as George I, under the Act of Settlement, with little disturbance, a transition that opened a new period in which Britain, rather than the Republic, would be the dominant maritime and financial power, a story continued in Chapter 80 and in the diplomatic system of the eighteenth century.

A comparison of the actual capacities of the two systems requires attention to evidence and to caution. The Dutch Republic, though small in population, could field large armies and navies in the later seventeenth century, and in the Grand Alliance of 1701 it undertook to provide a very large army, on the order of a hundred thousand men in all by treaty commitments, together with a substantial navy, which strained its finances. Its public debt grew steadily through the wars of William III and the War of the Spanish Succession, and by the 1710s the burden of interest was heavy, a factor in the Republic's later reluctance to continue the effort. Britain, with a larger population and a more flexible fiscal system, raised its revenues substantially after 1688, and its national debt grew from a few millions to a figure of more than thirty million pounds by the end of Anne's reign, by commonly cited estimates, with interest rates that fell over time as lenders gained confidence. The results of the Nine Years' War and the War of the Spanish Succession showed that Britain could sustain longer and larger commitments than any earlier English government, and scholars such as John Brewer and Marjolein 't Hart have attributed this to the combination of parliamentary control, administrative expertise, and public credit.

It is important not to turn the comparison into a story of the triumph of one model over another. The Republic's decentralized system was well suited to the defense of a small commercial territory with strong urban finance, but less so to the leadership of coalitions across Europe. Britain's system benefited from insularity, a larger domestic economy, and an expanding empire and trade, including the colonial and slave-based commerce that fed customs and excise revenues, as recent work on the composition of those revenues, discussed in the Quantitative History Study, has emphasized. And both depended on circumstances. A parliamentary system could break down, as it did in 1642, and a republican system could fall into crisis, as it did in 1672. What they shared was that rulers needed the consent of institutions representing the propertied, and that consent, because it was grounded in shared interests, could be a source of strength.

The reign of Anne ended on 1 August 1714, and the succession of George I, Elector of Hanover, passed without a challenge, in contrast to the fears of Jacobite insurrection that had dominated the previous years. The new king, who spoke little English, relied on Whig ministers who had supported the Hanoverian succession, and the Tory leaders of the last years of Anne's reign, tainted by suspicions of Jacobitism, were excluded from office, though a Jacobite rising in 1715 failed. The events of 1688 to 1714 thus ended with a settlement that would shape the politics of the eighteenth century, in which a limited monarchy, a Protestant establishment, an expanding public debt, and an increasingly powerful navy and army were the basis of Britain's rise. The Dutch Republic, by contrast, was entering a period of relative decline, as its commercial leadership eroded and its political system, deprived of a stadtholder, became increasingly dominated by regent oligarchies. How these two developments fit into the wider competition among European states is the subject of Chapter 80.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Authority in 1581, 1649, 1660, 1689, and 1707

What Changed

  • 1581: the Dutch provinces rejected their prince and governed through estates without a monarch.
  • 1649: England abolished monarchy and the House of Lords, though the regime rested heavily on the army.
  • 1660: monarchy returned, but without prerogative courts or arbitrary taxation.
  • 1689: the Crown was limited by statute and by fiscal dependence on Parliament, and the succession was redirected.
  • 1707: two kingdoms merged their parliaments into one state, Great Britain.

What Stayed the Same

  • Narrow political participation, based on property and religion, persisted in all regimes.
  • Established churches, whether Reformed, Anglican, or Presbyterian, remained tied to political authority.
  • Regional hierarchies and subordinate territories, such as Ireland and the Generality Lands, remained.
  • Administrative capacity grew steadily, in taxation and war finance, regardless of regime form.

Who experienced change most directly? Political elites, soldiers, dissenting clergy, and the Irish Catholic landowners experienced the sharpest changes. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural laborers, most women, and the urban poor, whose work and dependence changed less by regime. Why the pattern matters: It separates change in the form of regime from change in the actual capacity of the state, which grew across all of them.

Cause and Effect

Crisis, Constitutional Change, and State Capacity

Long-term causes

Composite monarchies with multiple institutions and churches, Reformation religious division, rising war costs, inflation, and traditions of consent through estates and parliaments.

Short-term causes

Philip II's repression and taxation in the 1560s and 1570s; Charles I's Forced Loan, ship money, and Prayer Book; James II's Catholic policy and the birth of an heir in 1688.

Event or transformation

The Dutch Revolt and the Republic; the British Civil Wars and Commonwealth; the Glorious Revolution and parliamentary-fiscal settlement.

Immediate effects

Dispersed sovereignty in the Netherlands; regicide and military rule in Britain; a limited monarchy with regular parliaments and conquest in Ireland and Scotland after 1688.

Long-term consequences

Strong public credit and fiscal-military capacity without absolutism; durable religious establishments and exclusions; Britain's rise and Dutch relative decline.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains these changes. Religion, taxation, warfare, dynasty, geography, and contingent decisions interacted differently in each case.

Note: Different capacities to borrow and tax shaped how each polity survived crises, but credit was an effect of political arrangements as well as a cause of them.

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

The Dutch Republic emerged from a revolt against Habsburg rule in which provincial and urban elites defended privileges and, finding no alternative prince, constructed a confederation governed by provinces, cities, and the States General. Its regent oligarchy, its commercial finance, and the recurring challenge of the House of Orange defined its politics, and its wealth rested on commerce and overseas systems that included coercion. The Stuart kingdoms produced successive constitutional experiments, from a fiscal and religious crisis under Charles I, through civil war, regicide, republic, and protectorate, to a Restoration that revived monarchy but not the pre-war order, and finally to the settlement of 1688 to 1689 and its fiscal consequences.

Students should compare, not simply recite. Ask where each polity located sovereignty, how it raised money, how it handled religion, who was excluded, and how it related to its subordinate territories and overseas commerce. Ask how the two systems interacted through war, trade, the House of Orange, and finance. And ask why neither development should be read as an inevitable step toward modern democracy. The review materials that follow ask students to use the evidence of this chapter to make such comparisons.

Chapter Summary

This chapter compared two responses to the crises of composite monarchy, religious division, and expensive warfare in northwestern Europe between the late sixteenth and early eighteenth centuries. The Dutch Republic emerged from a revolt against Philip II in which provincial and urban elites defended privileges, and, finding no acceptable replacement prince, built a confederation without a monarch. The Stuart kingdoms, joined in a personal union in 1603, passed through civil war, regicide, republic, protectorate, and restoration before arriving at a monarchy limited by statute and fiscal dependence on Parliament after 1688 to 1689. Neither outcome was designed in advance, and neither was a straightforward step toward modern democracy.

The Dutch Revolt, treated here for its constitutional consequences, moved from defense of provincial liberties in the 1560s to the Union of Utrecht in 1579 and the Act of Abjuration in 1581. The failure of foreign princes such as Anjou and Leicester left sovereignty with the provinces. By 1648 the Peace of Münster recognized the United Provinces, whose constitution was never codified but operated through provincial estates, the States General, municipal regents, and the stadtholder. Holland, supplying roughly three-fifths of the federal budget, and its city of Amsterdam held a weight that formal equality among provinces concealed.

Dutch government capacity rested on commerce, credit, and taxation. Baltic shipping, the Amsterdam staple, financial institutions such as the Exchange Bank of 1609, and the overseas systems of the VOC and WIC supported public finance that allowed the Republic to wage long wars. The same system rested on exclusions. Regent oligarchies governed cities, Catholics and other minorities were barred from many offices, the Generality Lands had no voice, and overseas wealth depended on violence, monopoly, and coerced labor. Toleration in practice existed alongside a privileged public church.

Political conflict in the Republic took the form of repeated contests between regent and Orangist interests: the crisis of 1618 to 1619, in which theology and constitutional authority merged and Oldenbarnevelt was executed; the crisis of 1650, the death of William II, and the first stadtholderless period under Johan de Witt; and the Rampjaar of 1672, in which foreign invasion, popular anger, and Orangist mobilization brought William III to power and ended in the murder of the De Witt brothers. William III's leadership against Louis XIV and his marriage to Mary Stuart tied the Republic's fate to the English succession.

In the Stuart kingdoms, James VI and I governed three kingdoms with separate institutions, and his successor Charles I faced fiscal weakness, parliamentary mistrust over taxation and imprisonment, and resistance to religious policy. The Petition of Right of 1628, the Personal Rule of 1629 to 1640, ship money, and Laudian reforms prepared the crisis. The Scottish National Covenant and the Bishops' Wars forced the king to call the Long Parliament, whose reforms were followed by division over the church, the Irish Rebellion, and control of the militia. The war that began in 1642 was therefore a war of three kingdoms, with fiscal, religious, constitutional, and inter-kingdom causes.

The Civil Wars produced new institutions and new political claims. The New Model Army made Parliament's victory possible and became an independent political force. The Putney Debates of 1647 revealed disagreements about franchise, property, and consent within the victorious coalition, and Pride's Purge, the trial, and the execution of Charles I in 1649 established a republic resting on a minority and an army. The Commonwealth and Protectorate attempted several constitutions, conquered Ireland and Scotland with great violence, raised heavy taxation, and expanded the navy, but could not secure broad legitimacy, and the Restoration of 1660 returned monarchy after the collapse of military rule.

The Restoration was not a return to 1640. Prerogative courts and ship money were gone, but religious division deepened under the Clarendon Code, finance remained inadequate, and French influence and the Catholic succession dominated politics through the Popish Plot and Exclusion Crisis, which produced Whig and Tory parties. James II's use of dispensing powers, a standing army, and Catholic appointments alienated the Anglican elite, and the birth of a son in 1688 precipitated the invitation to William. The Dutch intervention, supported by defections and elite disaffection, removed James and led to the joint monarchy of William and Mary.

The settlement of 1688 to 1689 limited the Crown through the Bill of Rights, the Mutiny Act, regular Parliaments, and, above all, fiscal dependence. It retained monarchy, an established church, and a narrow electorate. The Toleration Act gave qualified protection to Protestant dissenters but excluded Catholics and non-Trinitarians. Outside England, the revolution meant different things: a Presbyterian settlement and a Jacobite war in Scotland, and war, confiscation, and the penal laws in Ireland. The financial revolution, including the Bank of England and parliamentary-backed debt, gave the British state exceptional ability to borrow and fight.

The Act of Settlement of 1701, the reign of Anne, and the Union of 1707 completed the transformation. The Union, driven by succession and commercial concerns after the Darien disaster, created Great Britain while preserving Scottish law, church, and education; Ireland remained a separate subordinate kingdom. By 1714, when George I succeeded, Britain had a parliamentary monarchy with party politics and public credit, while the Dutch Republic, again without a stadtholder in most provinces, was entering a period of relative decline.

The central conclusion is comparative. Both polities developed substantial fiscal and military capacity without following the French model of royal absolutism, and both did so through representative institutions of property-holding elites. Yet both excluded most people from politics, based participation on property and religion, and depended on subordinate territories and overseas coercion. Limits on rulers must therefore be distinguished from political equality, and neither republican nor parliamentary government should be treated as an inevitable step toward modern democracy.

Key Terms

Stadtholder
A provincial officer of the Dutch Republic, originally a prince's lieutenant, usually held by a member of the House of Orange-Nassau and often combined with military command.
Regent
A member of the Dutch urban or provincial governing elite who held offices in councils, estates, courts, and charities.
States General
The confederal assembly of provincial delegations at The Hague, responsible for diplomacy, defense, and the Generality Lands.
Grand Pensionary
The chief salaried official of the States of Holland, who managed its agenda and diplomacy and often led Dutch politics.
Provincial sovereignty
The doctrine that each Dutch province held sovereign authority and acted with others through the Union.
Generality Lands
Territories governed directly by the States General without provincial representation, such as Staats-Brabant.
Union of Utrecht
The 1579 treaty creating a defensive and fiscal union among northern provinces and cities.
Act of Abjuration
The 1581 declaration in which the States General deposed Philip II for violating the provinces' rights.
Remonstrants
Followers of Arminius who presented a Remonstrance in 1610, opposed at Dort by Counter-Remonstrants.
Rampjaar
The "disaster year" of 1672, when France, England, and German bishoprics attacked the Republic.
VOC and WIC
The Dutch East India and West India Companies, chartered monopolies with quasi-sovereign powers.
Union of Crowns
The personal union of 1603 in which one monarch ruled England, Scotland, and Ireland as separate kingdoms.
Royal prerogative
The powers belonging to the Crown by law and custom, whose limits were disputed in the seventeenth century.
Parliamentary privilege
The rights claimed by Parliament and its members, including freedom of speech and petition.
Petition of Right
The 1628 parliamentary petition against taxation without consent, arbitrary imprisonment, billeting, and peacetime martial law.
Personal Rule
Charles I's governance without Parliament from 1629 to 1640.
Ship money
A levy for naval defense, extended inland in 1635 and contested in Hampden's Case.
National Covenant
The 1638 Scottish document defending the Kirk against religious innovation.
New Model Army
The professional parliamentary army created in 1645, which became a major political actor.
Levellers
A radical movement of the 1640s that argued for consent-based government, broader male franchise, and legal reform.
Pride's Purge
The army's exclusion of members from the Commons in December 1648, creating the Rump.
Regicide
The execution of a king, here Charles I on 30 January 1649.
Protectorate
The regime of 1653 to 1659 under the Instrument of Government, headed by Oliver and then Richard Cromwell.
Clarendon Code
Restoration laws enforcing Anglican conformity, including the Corporation, Uniformity, Conventicle, and Five Mile Acts.
Exclusion Crisis
The 1679 to 1681 struggle over excluding the Catholic James from the succession, which produced Whig and Tory parties.
Convention Parliament
An assembly meeting without a royal writ, as in 1660 and 1688 to 1689, to settle a constitutional vacancy.
Bill of Rights (1689)
The statute limiting royal suspending power, non-parliamentary taxation, and peacetime armies, and settling the succession.
Toleration Act (1689)
The statute exempting Protestant dissenters from penalties for separate worship while excluding Catholics and non-Trinitarians.
Financial revolution
The development after 1688 of parliament-backed public debt, the Bank of England, and a securities market.
Fiscal-military state
A state able to mobilize resources for war through taxation, borrowing, and administration.
Act of Settlement (1701)
The statute settling the succession on Sophia of Hanover and her Protestant heirs and placing conditions on future monarchs.
Acts of Union (1707)
The parallel statutes uniting England and Scotland into Great Britain with a single Parliament.
Old Style and New Style
The Julian and Gregorian calendars, ten days apart in this period and used respectively in England and in most of the Dutch Republic.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that early modern Europe produced more than one answer to the problem of strong government. The Dutch Republic and the Stuart kingdoms resisted the royal absolutism examined in Chapter 76, yet both mobilized money and men on a scale that rivaled monarchies. They did so through representative institutions of property-holding elites, public credit, and commercial wealth, and in doing so they changed what contemporaries and later historians understood as the sources of state power.

The chapter also reveals how contingent those outcomes were. Dutch republicanism followed from the failure of alternatives, and British parliamentary monarchy followed a century of civil war, regicide, restoration, and invasion. Neither the Republic nor the settlement of 1689 expressed a program for equal citizenship, and both coexisted with exclusion, conquest, and coerced labor. Seeing both clearly is a necessary condition for understanding the later uses of this history, in the Enlightenment political thought of Chapter 83 and in the revolutionary movements of Volume IV.

Within Part XII, the chapter links the fiscal-military state of Chapter 74, the Habsburg and French cases of Chapters 75 and 76, and the central and eastern European states of Chapters 78 and 79. It prepares Chapter 80 by showing why Britain and the Dutch Republic, allies under William III, would occupy different positions in the eighteenth-century balance of power. It also contributes to the argument of Volume III as a whole, that religion, commerce, empire, and state building were bound together, and that European political forms emerged through conflict and negotiation, not through the unfolding of a single model.

Finally, the chapter leaves open questions that later chapters take up: how overseas empire and slavery reshaped British and Dutch society, how toleration and secularization developed, and how the memory of 1581, 1649, and 1688 was used and distorted by later generations. Students should carry forward the habit of asking who held authority, who was excluded, and what evidence supports each claim.
