Chapter Opening

In 1917 the largest contiguous empire in Europe lost its monarchy in a matter of days, lost its provisional successor within eight months, and then entered years of civil and national war in which no single authority controlled the whole of its former territory. The Russian Revolution was not one event but a sequence of collisions over war, land, bread, military discipline, popular representation, and national self-rule.

Archival Image: Barricades in Petrograd, 1917

The Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division holds a photograph titled Days of Revolution: Barricades on the Liteinyi Prospect, Petrograd, 1917 (catalog number 2009631816). It can be viewed in the Library's online record at loc.gov/pictures/item/2009631816. The catalog identifies the year but not the day, so students should not assume that it depicts the first demonstrations of the February Revolution. Study it as evidence of how improvised barricades, a wide urban avenue, and armed and unarmed crowds could together turn a city street into a contested political space.

Petrograd, the Russian imperial capital, was a city of administrative ministries, aristocratic palaces, and some of the largest factories in Europe. It had been renamed from St. Petersburg in 1914 because the older name sounded too German once Russia was at war with Germany. In the winter of 1916 to 1917 it was also a city of long bread queues, overcrowded barracks, and unpaid or underpaid workers who lived in districts such as Vyborg, only a short distance across the Neva from the court. In the last week of February 1917 by the Russian calendar, those queues and factories became the setting for a demonstration that the police and army could not contain. Within days, the Romanov dynasty that had ruled Russia since 1613 was gone.

This chapter studies what happened next, and it insists that what happened next was far from inevitable. The February Revolution produced two competing sources of authority, a Provisional Government drawn largely from the old Duma elite and a Petrograd Soviet of workers' and soldiers' deputies. Neither could end a war that most Russians had come to detest, neither could rapidly satisfy peasants who wanted the landed estates, and neither could prevent the borderlands of the empire from asking what kind of political future they wanted. The October Revolution, in which the Bolshevik party under Vladimir Lenin and Leon Trotsky overthrew the Provisional Government in Petrograd, was a second transformation with its own causes. It did not instantly create Bolshevik rule over the former empire. It opened a struggle for that rule.

The chapter's purpose within Part XVII is to trace 1917, Bolshevik power, civil conflict, the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, and the destabilization of Russia and eastern Europe. It connects directly to the military and civilian experiences examined in Chapters 109 and 110, because it was the strain of total war that broke the Russian state. It also looks forward. The Central Powers that dictated terms at Brest-Litovsk would themselves collapse in the autumn of 1918, a story told in Chapter 112, and the peace settlements examined in Chapter 113 would have to address a postimperial eastern Europe whose frontiers were still being fought over. The Soviet order that emerged, and its relations with its neighbors, will matter again in Chapters 115, 119, 125, and 132.

Four themes organize the chapter. First, it distinguishes the collapse of an old authority from the creation of a new one. Second, it examines revolution as a social process that occurred in barracks, villages, factories, and provincial towns, not only in the corridors of Petrograd. Third, it treats the non-Russian peoples of the empire as political actors, whose declarations, councils, armies, and negotiations shaped the outcome. Fourth, it explains that the end of one civil conflict did not end every conflict: peasant rebellions, nationality wars, and frontier disputes continued after the major White armies were defeated.

Historians reconstruct these events from an unusually varied evidence base. It includes the minutes and decrees of the Provisional Government, the Duma, the Petrograd Soviet, and later the Soviet government; police and military censorship reports that summarize the mood of letters from the front; the memoirs of participants such as N. N. Sukhanov, Pavel Miliukov, Alexander Kerensky, and Trotsky, each of which advances a political case; diaries and letters of ordinary witnesses; newspapers and posters; photographs and film; foreign diplomatic reporting; and the text of treaties. Since the opening of Russian and former Soviet archives after 1991, provincial, party, and secret police records have allowed historians to see regional variation more clearly than earlier Cold War scholarship could.

A caution about dates is needed at the outset. Russia used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar used in western Europe, until the Soviet government decreed a change that moved the date from January 31 to February 14, 1918. This chapter uses the abbreviations OS (Old Style, Julian) and NS (New Style, Gregorian). Events inside Russia before February 14, 1918 are conventionally named by their Old Style dates, which is why the "February Revolution" occurred in March by the western calendar and the "October Revolution" in November. Where precision matters, both dates are given.

Finally, this volume's larger argument about European catastrophe needs a measured reading of the Russian case. The revolutions were a product of the war, but they were not only a consequence of it, and their eventual outcome was contingent on decisions, accidents, and mass movements that could have gone differently. Russia also has its own dedicated MGU History Series textbook. This chapter therefore concentrates on the points where Russian developments directly reshaped the political, military, and territorial order of Europe, and it does not attempt a complete history of Russia.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how military defeat, supply breakdown, inflation, and political distrust eroded the authority of the Romanov monarchy between 1914 and 1917.
  2. Trace the sequence of the February Revolution, from the demonstrations of February 23, 1917 (OS) to the abdication of Nicholas II and the formation of the Provisional Government.
  3. Analyze the system of dual power and explain why the Provisional Government's formal authority outran its practical control.
  4. Distinguish the political programs of the Kadets, Mensheviks, Socialist Revolutionaries, and Bolsheviks, and explain the disagreements over war, land, and the form of government.
  5. Evaluate why the Kerensky Offensive, the July Days, and the Kornilov affair changed the balance of political power in 1917.
  6. Interpret the October Revolution as both an organized seizure of central institutions and an event that depended on wider popular disillusionment.
  7. Assess the early Bolshevik decrees, the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly, and the concentration of power in 1917 and 1918.
  8. Analyze the negotiations and terms of the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk and its consequences for Ukraine, the Baltic region, Finland, Poland, and the Caucasus.
  9. Compare the main armed forces and movements of the Russian Civil War, and explain why it cannot be reduced to a struggle between two homogeneous armies.
  10. Evaluate competing historical explanations for Bolshevik survival and the contested reconstruction of the former imperial territories.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a monarchy that had survived the 1905 revolution collapse so quickly in 1917, and what does the speed of its fall reveal about the nature of its support?
  2. In what ways did the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet represent different claims to legitimacy, and why did their coexistence prove unstable?
  3. How did decisions made in villages, factories, and army units shape the course of revolution independently of the leaders in Petrograd?
  4. To what extent was the Bolshevik seizure of power in October a coup, a popular revolution, or something that does not fit either label well?
  5. Why did the Bolsheviks accept the harsh terms of Brest-Litovsk, and what did the treaty reveal about the relationship between revolutionary ideology and state survival?
  6. What evidence allows historians to understand the experiences of peasants, soldiers, and non-Russian populations, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  7. How did the national movements of the borderlands interact with the revolution, German occupation, and civil war?
  8. Why did the collapse of the Russian Empire not automatically produce stable independent states, and how did the later political order differ from region to region?

Historical Context

The Russian Empire of 1914 stretched from the Baltic and Poland to the Pacific, and from the Arctic to the Caucasus and Central Asia. Its population, roughly 170 million by the best estimates, included Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Poles, Finns, Estonians, Latvians, Lithuanians, Jews, Georgians, Armenians, Azeris, Tatars, and dozens of other peoples, practicing Orthodox Christianity, Catholicism, Lutheranism, Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, and other faiths. The imperial state ruled these populations through a combination of centralized bureaucracy, military force, Orthodox legitimacy, and varying degrees of regional autonomy, as in the Grand Duchy of Finland. The empire's political structure and its place among the great powers before 1914 are examined in Chapter 106, and the alliance system that drew it into war is traced in Chapter 107.

The monarchy had already been shaken once. The Revolution of 1905, set off by defeat in the Russo-Japanese War and the shooting of petitioners on Bloody Sunday in January of that year, forced Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto promising civil liberties and an elected legislature. The resulting State Duma had real but limited powers, and the emperor retained the authority to appoint ministers, dissolve the Duma, and rule by emergency decree. Between 1906 and 1914 the government pursued agrarian reform associated with the minister Petr Stolypin, encouraged industrial growth, and repressed the revolutionary parties. The result was a political settlement that satisfied few. Liberals wanted a government responsible to the Duma, socialists wanted a republic, and peasants still wanted the gentry's land.

Industrial Russia in 1914 was an uneven landscape. Rapid growth in Petrograd, Moscow, the Donbas, Baku, and Poland had created a concentrated urban working class, many of whose members retained ties to villages. Yet the empire remained overwhelmingly agrarian, with the great majority of its people living in the countryside. Rural society was organized around the village commune in much of European Russia, and the memory of serfdom, abolished in 1861, shaped peasant expectations about land and justice. Many peasants believed that the land belonged morally to those who worked it, a conviction that would prove politically decisive in 1917.

The war tested every part of this system. Russia mobilized roughly fifteen million men over the course of the conflict. Estimates of military dead range from about 1.7 to 2 million, with millions more wounded and several million taken prisoner, though the records are incomplete and the figures remain approximate. The experience of the Eastern Front is placed in a wider setting in Chapter 109, and the civilian strain of wartime mobilization across Europe is examined in Chapter 110. What distinguished the Russian case was the combination of enormous losses, a transport network that struggled to supply both the front and the cities, a wartime bureaucracy that many Russians distrusted, and a monarchy that had little political credit left to spend.

Students should also understand the geography of revolution. Petrograd was the capital, but it sat at the extreme northwest edge of the empire, close to the Finnish border and the Baltic front. Moscow was the older commercial center. The Donbas and Ukraine supplied coal, grain, and iron. The Volga basin and the Siberian railway connected European Russia to the east. Political control of the railway network, the telegraph, and the major cities would matter as much as control of the capital. Because the revolutions unfolded at different speeds in different places, historians increasingly study the provinces alongside Petrograd.

The Russian case also invites caution about historical memory. Soviet historiography later turned October 1917 into a founding myth, complete with canonical images of a mass storming of the Winter Palace that owed more to the film October (1928) than to the more modest events of the night. Western Cold War scholarship sometimes responded by portraying 1917 chiefly as a conspiratorial seizure of power. Recent historians have tried to hold both the popular mobilization and the party's organizational ruthlessness in view at once. This chapter adopts that balanced approach and distinguishes contemporary evidence from later commemoration.

Division I — The Collapse of Tsarism and the February Revolution

Division I · Section 111.1

Russia at War: Autocracy, Society, and Imperial Crisis, 1914–1917

Division I asks: why did the Romanov monarchy lose authority, and what replaced it?

The outbreak of war in August 1914 initially produced a wave of patriotic display, and the Duma largely suspended its criticism of the government. That unity did not last. The defeat at Tannenberg in East Prussia in late August 1914 and the heavy losses that followed revealed shortages of artillery shells, rifles, and trained officers. In the spring and summer of 1915 German and Austro-Hungarian offensives forced a Great Retreat from Poland and Galicia, uprooting hundreds of thousands of civilians. The army also expelled and deported many Jews and members of other minorities from frontier zones on suspicion of disloyalty, an episode that damaged relations between the state and its own subjects. Refugees from the western provinces filled Russian towns, adding to housing and food pressures.

The crisis of 1915 changed domestic politics. Voluntary organizations of the zemstvos and towns, and the War Industries Committees led by industrialists, took on supply tasks that the bureaucracy had failed to manage, and they drew their members into a political challenge to the government. In August 1915 a majority of Duma deputies formed the Progressive Bloc and demanded a ministry that enjoyed public confidence. Nicholas II rejected the demand. In the same month he dismissed his uncle Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich and assumed supreme command of the army himself. That decision tied the emperor personally to military outcomes. It also left Empress Alexandra and a rotating cast of ministers with the day to day direction of government in Petrograd, where the influence of the peasant holy man Rasputin was widely resented and exaggerated in public rumor. After Rasputin was murdered by aristocratic conspirators in December 1916, it was clear that even members of the elite no longer trusted the court.

By 1916 the economy showed the stress of three kinds of strain. Russia's railway system, built for peacetime traffic, was overloaded by military demands and lost locomotives and rolling stock faster than it replaced them. Grain was available in many regions, but peasants had little incentive to sell for a currency that was rapidly losing value, because manufactured goods were scarce and prices were rising. Urban prices rose far faster than wages. Many workers' households, including the families of soldiers, spent hours queuing for bread. The Brusilov Offensive of summer 1916 achieved substantial gains against Austria-Hungary but at enormous cost, and it did not break the strategic stalemate. Military censors reading soldiers' letters recorded declining confidence in the war's purpose, and desertion rose.

It would be too simple to say that the monarchy fell merely because the war went badly. Other belligerents endured heavy losses without revolution. The distinctive Russian problem was political. An autocratic system that insisted on its exclusive authority had by 1917 alienated the Duma opposition, the business and professional classes, much of the officer corps, urban workers, and many peasants simultaneously, and it possessed few intermediary institutions that could absorb discontent. When the emperor was at army headquarters in Mogilev, no one in Petrograd had the political standing to make credible concessions. Chapter 106 examined the reform debates before 1914. The war turned those unresolved debates into a crisis of survival.

Historical Vocabulary
Autocracy and Revolutionary Legitimacy

Autocracy (samoderzhavie) was the formal principle that the Russian emperor held undivided sovereign authority, which had been modified but not abandoned by the creation of the Duma in 1906. Its legitimacy rested on dynastic tradition, Orthodox sanction, and the claim that the sovereign stood above class and party. Revolutionary legitimacy refers to the different claims made in 1917 to rule in the name of the people: the Duma's claim to speak for the nation, the soviets' claim to represent workers, soldiers, and peasants, the Constituent Assembly's claim as an all-national elected body, and national councils' claims to speak for their own peoples. The term matters because after February 1917 the central question was no longer simply who held power but whose claim to rule would be accepted. Students should avoid assuming that "legitimate" meant "legal" or that only one claim was sincere.

The failure of wartime provisioning was not a straightforward absence of food. Russia's huge agricultural sector continued producing grain, but its marketing system weakened as rural households held back stocks, industrial goods disappeared from rural markets, transport capacity was commandeered for military movements, and government purchasing agencies competed with private intermediaries. In Petrograd, bread queues became an arena in which women could exchange information and collectively challenge officials. Factory wage increases could not consistently match prices, and long shifts strained the industrial population. The legitimacy problem was therefore cumulative: households witnessed a state capable of claiming their sons and taxing their earnings but increasingly unable to provide dependable bread. General national figures can disguise serious regional variation; some producing districts experienced shortages of manufactured goods rather than grain, while major consuming cities suffered interruptions even when food existed elsewhere.

The Progressive Bloc's demands also reveal why reform from above remained possible in theory but increasingly difficult in practice. Constitutional liberals wanted ministers who could cooperate with the elected Duma without necessarily abolishing the monarchy. Zemstvo organizers imagined more effective administration rather than an immediate social revolution. Some generals feared loss of army discipline more than the emperor's unpopularity. Their grievances overlapped with socialist attacks on autocracy but did not produce a unified alternative. Nicholas's refusals, recurring ministerial turnover and the court's suspicion of independent organizations prevented an orderly transfer of responsibility. By early 1917, when the imperial administration faced another urban emergency, each potential mediator had reasons to distrust the others. This helps explain why dramatic state collapse could follow a relatively localized initial uprising.

Division I · Section 111.2

Petrograd, February–March 1917: Women Workers, Strikes, and Soldiers' Mutiny

On February 23, 1917 (OS), International Women's Day, women textile workers in the Vyborg district of Petrograd walked out of their factories and appealed to the metalworkers to join them. Socialist organizations had planned commemorations, but historians generally agree that neither the revolutionary parties nor the authorities expected the scale of what followed. The marchers demanded bread, an end to the war, and the return of their men. Strikes spread rapidly the next day. By February 25 (OS) a general strike had paralyzed much of the capital, and crowds moved into the center along Nevsky Prospect and across the Neva bridges.

The military authorities, under the commander of the Petrograd garrison, General Sergei Khabalov, were ordered to disperse the demonstrators, and on Sunday, February 26 (OS), troops fired on a crowd at Znamenskaya Square and elsewhere, killing and wounding demonstrators. In earlier disturbances, such force had sometimes broken movements. This time the effect was different. The garrison consisted largely of reserve and training units packed into barracks, many of them recent conscripts and older reservists who had no wish to be sent to the front and who had family ties to the working class. Some units refused to fire. That evening a company of the Pavlovsky Regiment mutinied after its comrades had been shot at. The next morning, February 27 (OS), the Volynsky Regiment's training detachment killed its commander and joined the crowds, and other regiments followed.

The soldiers' decision was decisive. By the evening of February 27 (OS) the mutiny had spread across most of the garrison, soldiers and workers had seized arsenals and weapons, prisons including Kresty had been opened, and police stations were burned. Some police officials were killed. Casualties in the city over these days numbered in the hundreds, according to modern estimates, which is far lower than later conflicts but significant for a regime that had considered itself secure. The Tauride Palace, seat of the Duma, became a magnet for crowds, delegations, and armed units. At the same time the emperor, who had ordered the Duma prorogued on February 26 (OS), was far away and slow to grasp the situation.

Interpreting these days requires care with the sources. The memoir of N. N. Sukhanov offers a vivid but partisan view from within the emerging Soviet. Police and Okhrana reports of the winter had warned of unrest, but they also recorded that many popular grievances were economic and diffuse. Contemporary photographs, such as the Library of Congress image discussed in the Chapter Opening, record the visual environment but rarely identify dates or participants. Historians therefore reconstruct the February days from converging evidence: strike records, garrison reports, newspapers, diaries, and memoirs.

The February events were not planned by any political party. They resulted from a convergence of food shortages, wartime resentment, and an unresolved crisis of authority. The women who began the movement, many of whom were soldiers' wives (soldatki), had been central to bread queues and local protests since 1915. Treating the revolution as a political decision by leaders alone leaves out the crowds whose behavior made the regime's collapse possible. At the same time, crowds do not create new governments on their own. The question for the next days was who would claim the authority that the street movement had taken from the monarchy.

A careful account of the February days distinguishes simultaneous social movements from a single planned insurrection. Workers from different plants stopped production for different reasons; some emphasized bread, some wages and hours, others the war and monarchy. The growing crowds crossed bridges and converged on symbolic streets, while police commanders tried to maintain corridors of movement and protect government buildings. Women workers were not merely a picturesque prelude to an uprising subsequently directed by men. Their established factory networks and household responsibilities gave them organizational capacity and a powerful claim to public legitimacy. The movement accelerated when soldiers in reserve battalions interpreted orders to fire as orders to suppress people who resembled their own families. Soldiers' defections turned a policing crisis into a crisis of sovereignty.

The timing matters because surviving sources conflict over the scale of participation at particular moments. The city contained large numbers of strike participants, sympathetic residents and curious onlookers, who were not necessarily committed to the same political program. Memoirists writing after October sometimes assigned more purposeful leadership to their own parties than contemporary evidence can establish. Another source problem is the changing city itself: streets, bridges, barracks and the Tauride Palace provide stable geographical landmarks, but control of each shifted during the uprising. Students should ask not just when demonstrators entered an avenue, but when police withdrew, when a military unit changed sides, and when a committee claimed to command it. These are different sorts of historical events.

Division I · Section 111.3

Nicholas II Abdicates: The End of Romanov Government

On February 27 (OS) a group of Duma leaders, led by the Duma president Mikhail Rodzianko, formed a Provisional Committee to restore order and communicate with the army. This was not at first an act of revolution. Most of its members, including liberals of the Kadet party and moderate conservatives, hoped to save the monarchy by persuading the emperor to appoint a government responsible to the Duma. They feared that anarchy in the capital would damage the war effort and invite social upheaval. At the same time, they could not ignore the crowds at the Tauride Palace or the soldiers who were arriving there.

Nicholas II left army headquarters at Mogilev on February 28 (OS) to return to Tsarskoe Selo, but his train was diverted and ended at Pskov, the headquarters of the Northern Front under General Nikolai Ruzsky. There, over March 1 and 2 (OS), the emperor learned that the capital was in the hands of the insurgents and that army commanders did not believe that force could restore his authority. The chief of staff, General Mikhail Alekseev, canvassed the front commanders, and most of them recommended that the emperor abdicate in order to preserve the army and the war effort. The decision therefore depended on the military leadership as well as on the revolutionaries. Without army support, the monarchy lacked the means to repress the revolt.

On March 2 (OS), March 15 (NS), Nicholas signed an abdication in favor of his brother, Grand Duke Michael Alexandrovich, setting aside his own son Alexei, who suffered from hemophilia. The Duma emissaries who brought the news back to Petrograd discovered that crowds and soldiers would not accept another Romanov ruler. On March 3 (OS), Michael, after meeting with Duma and government figures, declined to assume the throne unless a future Constituent Assembly chose to offer it. This ended Romanov rule in practice. The dynasty, which had governed Russia since 1613, did not formally abolish the monarchy by decree, and Russia was not declared a republic until September 1917, but the question of monarchy had been removed from the practical political agenda.

The Provisional Government was announced on March 2 (OS), with Prince Georgy Lvov as minister president and minister of the interior, Pavel Miliukov, leader of the Kadets, as foreign minister, the Octobrist industrialist Alexander Guchkov as war minister, and Alexander Kerensky, a lawyer and Trudovik deputy who was also vice chairman of the Petrograd Soviet, as minister of justice. It declared an amnesty for political prisoners, freedom of speech, press, and assembly, the abolition of legal restrictions based on religion, nationality, and social estate, and a commitment to convene a Constituent Assembly. Within weeks it abolished the death penalty and enacted measures that extended rights to Jews and other groups, a legal emancipation of considerable importance for the empire's minorities. The government's difficulty was that it had received its authority from a self-appointed committee of the old Duma, not from an election, and it ruled alongside a rival institution.

The negotiations surrounding abdication demonstrate that even the emperor's immediate supporters prioritized the army's survival. Front commanders faced the prospect of continuing war while the capital, rail junctions and civilian ministries became ungovernable. Their advice to Nicholas arose from this strategic concern as much as from sympathy with street protesters. The form of his abdication was unusual: the emperor initially considered his son's succession but ultimately named his brother, raising questions about dynastic law that remained secondary once Michael declined immediate acceptance. Neither act settled who possessed ultimate constituent authority. The Provisional Government operated provisionally because it intended an elected assembly to decide Russia's durable constitution, yet it had to govern and conduct a war before an election could take place.

Initial reforms brought real consequences. The abolition of confessional and estate restrictions could change the legal position of Jewish residents and other populations previously subjected to imperial discrimination. Political prisoners returned, previously illegal organizations mobilized publicly, and the press carried new viewpoints. Yet reform decrees did not automatically solve the command problem at the front or the distribution problem in cities. In the provinces, local officials, professional bodies, soviets, soldiers' committees and national councils interpreted the revolution in their own ways. The appearance of a government in Petrograd thus announced rather than completed a state-building project.

Division I · Section 111.4

Dual Power: The Provisional Government, Petrograd Soviet, and Order No. 1

While the Duma leaders formed their committee, socialist activists revived the soviet, a form of workers' council that had first appeared in the 1905 revolution. On February 27 (OS) the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' Deputies held its first meeting in the Tauride Palace and was soon expanded to include delegates elected by soldiers' units, becoming the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies. It was dominated at first by Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, with Nikolai Chkheidze as chair and Kerensky and Matvei Skobelev among its leaders. Its executive committee had no formal governing role but it controlled the mood of the capital's workers and soldiers and the key means of communication, including newspapers, the printing presses, and supply networks.

Historians and participants describe the resulting arrangement as dual power (dvoevlastie). The Provisional Government held formal state authority, the ministries, diplomatic recognition from the Allies, and the continuity of administration. The Soviet held something different: the loyalty of workers and soldiers, and with it the practical capacity to approve or obstruct government orders. In the first weeks the Soviet's moderate leaders chose to support the government conditionally, in the belief that a bourgeois democratic stage of revolution required liberal participation. They also considered it dangerous to seize full power in a country at war. The result was a government that governed only so long as the Soviet allowed it to.

Order No. 1, issued by the Petrograd Soviet on March 1 (OS), March 14 (NS), brought this problem into the army. It was addressed to the Petrograd garrison and was intended to settle the relations between soldiers and officers after the mutiny. It authorized elected committees in military units, subordinated the garrison politically to the Soviet, placed weapons under the control of those committees, and provided that Duma orders should be obeyed only when they did not contradict the Soviet. It also relaxed rules concerning forms of address and salutes off duty. Although meant for Petrograd, its principles spread rapidly through the army and navy, and many soldiers understood it as proof that they now had rights as citizens in uniform. Commanders and conservatives blamed it for the loss of discipline. Historians usually argue that discipline was already collapsing and that the order codified and accelerated change rather than causing it from nothing.

Dual power looked different outside Petrograd. In provincial towns, local committees of public safety, soviets, trade unions, and municipal councils coexisted in varying combinations. Some provincial authorities simply continued the old administration under new titles. Elsewhere, workers' and soldiers' soviets exercised real authority over food supply and policing. Peasants in the countryside generally waited to see what the new government would do about land. The pattern of local power in 1917 was therefore far more varied than the Petrograd diagram of two competing centers suggests. The primary source below invites a close reading of the order that did so much to shape this arrangement.

Order No. 1 must be read clause by clause rather than compressed into the assertion that the Petrograd Soviet abolished military discipline. Its initial audience was the Petrograd District garrison. It directed election of committees, placed weapons under committee control, subordinated military units' political conduct to the Soviet, and required obedience to Duma military instructions only when these did not conflict with Soviet decisions. The order also retained discipline while soldiers were on duty and challenged humiliating conventions that separated officers from rank-and-file soldiers. Its broader circulation magnified its influence, but the interpretation and implementation of its clauses differed from unit to unit. The order was not equivalent to a nationwide legislative act establishing one uniform army committee system overnight.

The civil dimension of dual power was similarly intricate. The Provisional Government possessed ministers, formal decrees and international recognition, whereas soviets commanded active loyalty from workers and soldiers in particular institutions. Trade unions, factory committees and municipal bodies occupied intermediate positions. The Soviet leadership itself changed over 1917; early moderate socialist dominance later yielded ground to Bolshevik majorities in crucial cities. Neither side could reliably solve the army's dilemma: war continuation depended on soldiers, many of whom increasingly saw peace and local land reform as more urgent than diplomatic commitments. Dual power was therefore not a stable constitutional design but a shifting compromise produced by a revolution that had displaced one sovereign without establishing another.

Division II — War, Land, Labor, and the Crisis of the Provisional Government

Division II · Section 111.5

Lenin's Return, the April Theses, and the Crisis of Coalition Politics

Division II asks: why did the new political order fail to satisfy soldiers, workers, peasants, and national movements?

Vladimir Lenin, leader of the Bolshevik faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labor Party, had spent most of the war in Switzerland. Following the February Revolution he, along with other exiles, was permitted by the German government to travel through Germany in a sealed railway carriage to Sweden and Finland. German officials hoped that the return of antiwar revolutionaries would weaken Russia. Lenin arrived at the Finland Station in Petrograd on April 3 (OS), April 16 (NS), 1917. His intentions were not controlled by Berlin, but the journey later gave his enemies material for accusations of German sponsorship.

Lenin immediately set out a program, the April Theses, that startled many of his own party. He rejected support for the Provisional Government, argued that the revolution should pass from its "bourgeois" stage to a socialist one, condemned the war as an imperialist conflict, and called for power to be transferred to the soviets. The slogan that became associated with this stance was "All power to the Soviets," together with demands for peace, land for peasants, and bread. Within the Bolshevik party, leaders such as Lev Kamenev and, for a time, Joseph Stalin had favored conditional cooperation and the defense of the revolution. Lenin's reorientation, backed by his authority and by shifts in popular opinion, gradually won the party over, though debate persisted.

The government's own crisis came quickly. On April 18 (OS), Foreign Minister Miliukov sent a note to the Allies that appeared to reaffirm Russian war aims, including territorial ambitions, in contradiction to the Soviet's call for peace without annexations. Soldiers and workers demonstrated, and there were armed clashes in the capital. Miliukov and Guchkov resigned. The resulting coalition government of May 5 (OS), May 18 (NS) included socialist ministers for the first time: Kerensky as war minister, the Menshevik Irakli Tsereteli as minister of posts and telegraphs, Skobelev as minister of labor, and the Socialist Revolutionary leader Viktor Chernov as minister of agriculture. Moderate socialists thereby shared responsibility for continuing the war and for the government's failure to deliver quick change.

The central divisions of 1917 lay among the socialists as well as between them and the liberals. "Defensists" argued that Russia should continue a defensive war to protect the revolution from German domination while seeking a general peace. "Internationalists" argued that the war was an imperialist struggle and that revolutionary peace should be pursued immediately. The Mensheviks and the main body of the Socialist Revolutionaries inclined toward defensism and coalition, while the Bolsheviks and Left SRs inclined toward opposition. As coalition ministers became responsible for unpopular policies, the Bolsheviks gained the opportunity of an opposition that bore no responsibility.

Historical Vocabulary
Revolutionary Parties

Russian political parties in 1917 should not be mapped onto simple left and right. The Kadets (Constitutional Democrats) were liberals who sought a parliamentary government and continued the war. The Mensheviks were Marxist social democrats who believed that Russia needed a bourgeois democratic stage before socialism and supported cooperation with liberals. The Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs) were a populist socialist party with strong peasant support and a program of land socialization; by autumn 1917 they had split between a right wing that supported coalition and a Left SR wing that sided with the Bolsheviks. The Bolsheviks ("majority") were the faction of Russian social democracy led by Lenin that favored a disciplined vanguard party and, from April 1917, opposed support for the Provisional Government. The names are historical labels, not accurate descriptions: the Bolsheviks were not a majority of Russian opinion, and the Socialist Revolutionaries were not the most radical party. Understanding these groups helps explain the options available at each stage of 1917.

Lenin's political return was significant because he challenged the assumption that the democratic revolution necessarily required a prolonged partnership with liberal ministers. His April proposals called for opposition to the Provisional Government and a transfer of authority to soviets, while also addressing land and the war. These propositions did not immediately become settled Bolshevik policy. Other Bolsheviks initially preferred qualified support for the new order, and rival socialist leaders argued that Russia needed democratic consolidation before a socialist transformation. Public debate, party conferences and the changing composition of soviets helped determine whether Lenin's position would prevail. Treating the April Theses as a command automatically implemented by a disciplined machine would obscure these real disagreements.

The coalition crises also reveal a structural problem in democratic representation. Socialist ministers entered government partly to prevent a breakdown of authority and protect revolutionary freedoms, but participation made them responsible for food prices, military operations and postponed land reform. Moderate parties remained influential in provincial soviets and the countryside even as Bolsheviks gained strength among some urban workers and garrison units. Newspaper editorial positions, public demonstrations and soviet election returns therefore offer different measures of political support. The historian should not assume that a change in Petrograd's mood represented the whole empire, or that popular hostility to the war implied universal endorsement of Bolshevik government.

Division II · Section 111.6

Russia Remains at War: The Kerensky Offensive and Military Breakdown

The Provisional Government's decision to stay in the war reflected several pressures. Liberal ministers believed that Russia owed obligations to its Allies, including Britain and France, who were also its creditors. Many socialists feared that a separate peace would expose Russia to German domination and endanger the revolution. The Allies, and later the United States after its April 1917 entry into the war, expected Russia to continue fighting. Yet the army was changing. Soldiers' committees now claimed the right to discuss operations. Fraternization with enemy troops occurred along parts of the front. Desertions increased, especially as spring planting and the expectation of land redistribution drew peasant soldiers toward home.

Kerensky, as war minister, and the army leadership believed that a new offensive might revive morale and demonstrate Russian reliability to the Allies. The offensive began on June 18 (OS), July 1 (NS), 1917, in Galicia, with the main blow directed toward Lemberg (Lviv). Initial advances were achieved by shock units, but many regular units refused to attack or soon halted. On July 6 (OS) German and Austro-Hungarian forces counterattacked at Tarnopol (Ternopil) and the front broke. Russian units retreated in disorder, with desertions, looting, and the killing of officers reported. Chapter 109 places this campaign among the broader operations of the Eastern Front.

The offensive's failure had large political consequences. It discredited Kerensky's claim that a revolutionary army could fight effectively, and it intensified the debate between those who blamed the soldiers' committees and those who blamed the continued war. General Lavr Kornilov was appointed Supreme Commander in July. The death penalty was reintroduced for soldiers at the front, which many soldiers considered a betrayal of the revolution. The government also created special units, including women's battalions of volunteers, partly for propaganda value. Meanwhile, German forces captured Riga in early September (NS), opening the road toward Petrograd and increasing fears in the capital.

Army sources reveal the soldiers' point of view imperfectly. Many soldiers were peasants, and censors' summaries of letters repeatedly described a desire for peace and land more than for any specific party program. Officers, who were often from different social backgrounds, wrote differently and with less sympathy. The decay of discipline was not uniform. Some elite units, volunteers, and cavalry formations remained reliable, and the situation varied among fronts. What the evidence shows is a mass army that increasingly judged the war by whether it would bring peace and land, and a government that could not give either quickly.

The June offensive, commonly called the Kerensky Offensive and dated July 1 New Style, emerged from the Provisional Government's attempt to reconcile democratic revolution with continued alliance obligations. Ministers hoped military success would restore bargaining power and discourage opponents at home. Commanders planned attacks in Galicia against Austro-Hungarian formations, yet preparations occurred amid conflicts over officers' authority, soldiers' committees and war aims. An initial advance did not become a decisive strategic breakthrough. Counterattacks, deteriorating discipline and desertion exposed the limits of the army's ability to sustain major offensive operations. The military collapse was neither a single mutiny nor an immediate disappearance of all Russian armed forces. Some units continued to fight, hold territory and exercise effective discipline.

The offensive illustrates a political trap: to preserve credibility with the Entente, the government asked soldiers to accept further risk for a war whose aims were deeply contested. To retain the confidence of soldiers, it had to promise democratization and reform that altered the prewar command hierarchy. Senior officers, rank-and-file committees and coalition ministers differed about whether these objectives could coexist. Defeat made the contradiction more visible to factory workers and peasants with relatives at the front. Later accounts often present the offensive as certain to fail, but contemporary actors still debated prospects and alternative strategies. Its importance lies in how failure transformed arguments over policy into arguments over the legitimacy of those who had ordered it.

Division II · Section 111.7

Land, Factories, Bread, and Popular Revolution across Russia

The revolution outside Petrograd was shaped by village assemblies, local committees, and factory meetings that pursued their own priorities. Peasants after February organized volost committees and land committees, formed assemblies, and in many districts began to take control of estate land, forests, pastures, and livestock. At first they often sought to rent land at lower rates or to cut timber without payment. As the summer of 1917 wore on and as promised legal solutions failed to arrive, seizures became more common, especially in central Russia and the Volga region. Chernov, the SR minister of agriculture, tried to channel the process through land committees while awaiting the Constituent Assembly, but peasants often moved faster than the ministry.

The countryside's revolution was usually collective and communal. Villages divided seized land according to local custom, expelled or constrained landlords, and sometimes burned manor houses. The process included violence but also bargaining, and it varied by region according to landholding patterns, the presence of returning soldiers, and the strength of the commune. For peasants, this was not a socialist program imposed from above. It was a long awaited correction of injustice. Because peasants made up most of the population, their actions undermined the Provisional Government's authority as thoroughly as military defeat did.

In the cities, the revolution took the form of strikes, wage demands, trade union organization, and factory committees. Workers won the eight hour day in Petrograd in March through negotiation with employers and pressure on the government. Over the following months, as inflation eroded wage gains and some employers curtailed production or closed plants, factory committees began to claim a right to oversee hiring, supply, and sometimes management. The Petrograd conference of factory committees in June 1917 drew radical conclusions about workers' control. Bolshevik influence in the committees grew in the summer, as moderate socialist policies appeared to defend order over workers' demands. Armed workers' detachments, called Red Guards, were organized in many industrial districts.

Food supply linked town and country. The government introduced a grain monopoly and fixed prices in the spring, but peasants held back grain when prices did not match the cost of manufactured goods. Urban rations were cut, and shortages generated queues, accusations of hoarding, and violence. Railway breakdowns worsened distribution. These material conditions mattered because they made the promise of "peace, land, and bread" concrete. They also help explain why many workers and peasants judged parties less by their theory than by whether they could end the war and ease hardship.

Peasants and Factory Workers in 1917

For a peasant household in the central Russian countryside, 1917 was a year of anxious waiting and sudden action. The household depended on the labor of its members, many of whom were away in the army. Village assemblies, long used to settling disputes over land and taxes, became forums for deciding whether to wait for the Constituent Assembly or take the landlord's meadow. Women, who had borne much of the farm labor during the war, took part in food disputes and in protest. Returning soldiers brought news and a new sense of entitlement.

For a metalworker or textile worker in Petrograd, daily life meant long hours, crowded lodging in tenements and barracks near the factories, and a household budget stretched by inflation. Many workers still returned to their villages for harvests. Workers' clubs, newspapers, and trade unions offered education and community, while strikes and factory committees offered a voice. Women workers, who made up a large part of the wartime industrial workforce, were typically paid less than men and faced the double burden of factory labor and queueing for food. Their daily experience helps explain why economic and political demands were so closely entwined.

The countryside did not wait for legislation in Petrograd. Villages convened assemblies, drew up inventories of local landed estates, pressured landlords and sometimes seized forests, pasture and implements. Such initiatives did not constitute a centrally coordinated Bolshevik agrarian revolution. In many areas Socialist-Revolutionary ideas about land belonging to those who worked it were better known than Lenin's writings, while disputes among neighboring villages complicated simple accounts of peasant unity. Soldiers returning from the front carried political news and grievances, but they also brought competing views about property and order. The same local assembly might demand immediate redistribution while insisting that a future national Constituent Assembly should give the redistribution legal force.

Urban factory committees faced an equally difficult problem: regulating wages, dismissals and managerial authority while factories lacked fuel and raw materials. Workers could insist on inspection of accounts or participation in production decisions without initially demanding outright nationalization. Owners sometimes closed plants or reduced operations for commercial or political reasons, and shortages made motives difficult to assess. Bread supply, rent and childcare shaped workers' political decisions as much as party slogans. The illustrative cases in the Everyday Life box should be read as analytic composites unless supported by a named individual testimony. Comparing household evidence with committee records prevents the romanticization of revolutionary initiative as either complete popular control or simple disorder.

Division II · Section 111.8

Nationalities and the Unraveling Empire: Finland, Ukraine, Poland, the Baltics, and the Caucasus

The February Revolution released political energies in the borderlands that the imperial state had long constrained. The Provisional Government abolished national and religious restrictions, but it was reluctant to concede the empire's territorial integrity before the Constituent Assembly met. Many national movements began by demanding cultural rights and autonomy rather than independence, and only some moved to sovereignty as the year unfolded. This distinction is important, since later national histories sometimes read the whole process backward as a straight path to independence.

Finland, a Grand Duchy with its own Diet and administration, had enjoyed autonomy under the emperor but had experienced Russification pressures in the early twentieth century. In 1917 the Finnish Diet claimed authority over internal affairs, and in July it passed a law asserting its powers, which the Provisional Government rejected and answered by dissolving the Diet. After October the Finnish government moved toward full independence, which it declared on December 6, 1917. In Ukraine, activists formed the Central Rada in Kiev in March 1917 under the historian Mykhailo Hrushevsky. Its Universals, or proclamations, progressed from demanding autonomy in June, to a compromise with the Provisional Government in July, to declaring a Ukrainian People's Republic in November (still within a hoped for federation), and finally to full independence in January 1918.

In the Baltic provinces, Estonian and Latvian leaders pressed for autonomy and local self government. In April 1917 the Provisional Government granted Estonia an autonomous governorate with a representative Land Council, and Latvian political life, divided by the war and the German occupation of the west, was influenced by the Latvian Riflemen units that played a significant role in military and political events. Lithuania was largely under German occupation, and in September 1917 a conference in Vilnius selected a Council of Lithuania. Poland presented a different case. The Provisional Government recognized in March 1917 the right of Poles to independent statehood, but most Polish territory was held by the Central Powers, who had in November 1916 proclaimed a nominal Kingdom of Poland. In the Caucasus, Georgian, Armenian, and Azerbaijani political leaders, alongside Russian officials and soldiers, organized regional authorities, and after October a Transcaucasian Commissariat took shape. Muslim congresses in Kazan and elsewhere raised the demands of Tatars and other Muslim peoples of the empire, and Jewish parties such as the Bund and the Zionist movement also acted in this political space.

The borderlands' movements did not agree among themselves. Ukrainian leaders disputed borders with Poles and with Russian and Jewish urban populations, and landholding conflicts divided nationalists and peasants in different ways. National councils were often led by intellectuals and had uneven peasant support at first. Yet their very existence meant that the political question of 1917 was never simply who would rule Petrograd. As examined in Division V, these conflicts developed into wars of independence and annexation after Brest-Litovsk.

An imperial geography of 1917 requires more than one map. The legal borders of the Russian Empire, the territories occupied by German and Austro-Hungarian forces, and the areas where a national council exercised real authority did not coincide. Division III — July to October: The Struggle for Revolutionary Power

Division III · Section 111.9

The July Days, Failed Coalitions, and the Limits of Revolutionary Government

Division III asks: how did the Provisional Government lose authority, and how did the Bolsheviks move from opposition to control of the capital?

Between July 3 and 5 (OS), July 16 to 18 (NS), armed soldiers, sailors, and workers demonstrated in Petrograd against the government. The immediate trigger was news of the offensive's failure and a government crisis arising from the withdrawal of Kadet ministers over concessions to Ukraine. The First Machine Gun Regiment, sailors from Kronstadt, and many Putilov workers took to the streets with slogans demanding power for the soviets. The Bolshevik leadership was divided about whether to support the movement, which many party militants had helped organize at the base. Crowds surrounded the Tauride Palace, and Chernov was nearly seized by a mob before Trotsky intervened.

The uprising lacked a clear plan and failed. Loyal units arrived, the Soviet's leaders declined to take power, and the government used documents purporting to show that Lenin was a German agent to discredit the Bolsheviks. Historians have generally found no evidence that Lenin was a controlled German agent, though German financial support for some antiwar activities existed. Even so, the accusation was effective at the time. Lenin went into hiding and then to Finland, Kamenev and Trotsky were arrested, and Bolshevik newspapers were closed. Prince Lvov resigned, and Kerensky became prime minister on July 8 (OS), July 21 (NS), heading a second coalition government.

The July Days showed both the radicalism of the capital's workers and soldiers and the limits of the Bolshevik leadership's control. They also left the revolution more polarized. Conservatives and many officers came to believe that only a strong hand could restore order. Radical workers and soldiers concluded that the Provisional Government and its socialist allies were hostile to their interests. In August, Kerensky convened a State Conference in Moscow to display national unity, but it exposed the divisions between the left and the right more than it healed them.

The important point is not that the government defeated a Bolshevik coup attempt, since the Bolshevik leadership had not decided to seize power, but that the government's measures failed to restore authority. Soldiers and workers remained armed, the Soviet remained a rival power, and the war continued to erode the army. At the same time, the repression of July proved temporary. Within weeks the political environment shifted again, as the next section shows.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From February to July 1917

What Changed

  • The monarchy was replaced by a Provisional Government, and civil liberties and equal legal rights were declared
  • Socialists moved from outside government to participation in it, and public mood shifted from enthusiasm to frustration
  • The army's chain of command was reshaped by soldiers' committees, and the offensive's failure ended hopes of a revived war effort
  • The Bolsheviks moved from a small party to a mass organization with growing influence in soviets and factory committees

What Stayed the Same

  • The war continued, and so did the shortages of food, transport, and fuel
  • Land remained unredistributed by law, while peasants moved toward seizure
  • Dual power persisted in the form of overlapping authority and divided loyalty
  • Many provincial administrators, judges, and officials continued in their posts with modified titles

Who experienced change most directly? Soldiers, urban workers, and political activists felt the greatest change, as did Jews and other groups who gained legal rights. Who experienced greater continuity? Many peasants and provincial officials experienced continuity in daily burdens, at least until land seizures gathered pace. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the political revolution of February did not resolve the social grievances that had driven it, which created an opening for parties offering more radical answers.

The July Days were shaped by urgent demands from armed soldiers and industrial districts, but the demonstrators were not all acting on one agreed political plan. Some called for all power to the soviets; others opposed transfer to the front or complained about food and wages. The Bolshevik leadership debated whether it could lead the protest without launching a premature confrontation. Government supporters interpreted the armed crowds as a threat to constitutional order, while investigations into Bolshevik dealings with Germany helped justify arrests. The accusations require cautious treatment: the fact that Germany facilitated Lenin's journey or that political financing was investigated does not by itself prove that the July demonstrators were controlled by Berlin.

The suppression narrowed radical freedom temporarily, but it did not restore a durable government majority. Moderate socialists could reject the Bolshevik demand for immediate soviet government while also resisting a purely military crackdown on workers and soldiers. The July crisis also exposed how quickly political labels could reverse: activists who had been treated as defenders of revolution in March were called subversives in July. In asking whether there was continuity between February and July, students should trace changes in the authority to appoint ministers, control a factory, command a regiment and distribute food, rather than assume one linear move toward dictatorship.

Division III · Section 111.10

Kornilov's March on Petrograd and the Bolsheviks' Political Recovery

The Kornilov affair of late August 1917 (OS) was a confused episode in which each side suspected the other of conspiring. General Lavr Kornilov, appointed Supreme Commander in July, favored restoring military discipline and, with the support of conservative politicians, industrialists, and some officers, wanted greater authority over the capital. Kerensky first appeared to cooperate with these plans. Through an intermediary, Vladimir Lvov, messages were exchanged that each man interpreted differently, and Kerensky concluded that Kornilov intended to remove him. On August 27 (OS), September 9 (NS), Kerensky dismissed Kornilov, who refused to obey and ordered the Third Cavalry Corps under General Alexander Krymov toward Petrograd.

The response of the capital's population determined the outcome. The Petrograd Soviet organized a committee to struggle against counterrevolution. Railway workers delayed and rerouted trains, telegraph operators disrupted communications, and agitators from the Soviet and Bolsheviks spoke to Kornilov's troops, many of whom had been told that they were defending the government. Workers' Red Guards were armed, and Bolshevik prisoners were released to help organize defense. Krymov's advance dissolved without fighting, and he committed suicide after meeting Kerensky. Kornilov and several other generals were arrested.

The affair changed politics in three ways. First, it destroyed the credibility of the political right as a partner for the government and enhanced fears of counterrevolution among soldiers and workers. Second, it discredited Kerensky, who had alienated both the left and the officers and who proclaimed Russia a republic on September 1 (OS), September 14 (NS), through a Directory of five ministers. Third, it brought the Bolsheviks back into legitimate politics. Their members had been in the front line of mobilization against Kornilov, and the Bolshevik slogan of soviet power appealed to an electorate frightened by counterrevolution and disappointed in coalition.

By September, Bolshevik delegates held majorities in the soviets of Petrograd and Moscow. The Petrograd Soviet elected Trotsky as its chair on September 25 (OS), October 8 (NS), displacing the Menshevik and SR leadership. Bolshevik strength was concentrated in the large cities, in the garrisons nearest the front, and among industrial workers, while they were much weaker in the countryside. The Democratic Conference that Kerensky summoned in September and the Pre-Parliament that followed in October did not generate a stable coalition. By October the Provisional Government had lost the confidence of much of the left and the right, a position that the Bolsheviks did not create alone but exploited.

Kornilov's attempt to move troops toward Petrograd became a crisis partly because communications among the commander in chief, Kerensky and intermediaries broke down amid mutually incompatible expectations. Historians continue to debate whether Kornilov planned a straightforward personal dictatorship, imagined a restoration of discipline supported by the premier, or acted under conditions of misunderstanding that turned disagreement into rupture. What matters is that the episode convinced many workers and soldiers that a military counterrevolution was possible, while officers increasingly distrusted the government for abandoning an army commander. Local defense committees, railway workers and activists disrupted troop movement, demonstrating the power of infrastructure workers in revolutionary politics.

The Bolsheviks gained opportunities from this mobilization without having initiated every action against Kornilov. Their organizers participated in defense efforts, while trade unions and other socialist movements also acted. Weapons and organizational networks did not simply evaporate when the immediate emergency ended. Elections in key soviets soon reflected sharp shifts of confidence, particularly in Petrograd and Moscow, though the countryside remained politically diverse. This is a crucial causal link between the July setback and October: government action that suppressed one perceived threat could inadvertently restore the authority of an opposition that claimed to defend the revolution more consistently than its ministers.

Division III · Section 111.11

The October Insurrection: Petrograd, the Military Revolutionary Committee, and the Winter Palace

Lenin, still in hiding in Finland, wrote in September and October urging the Bolshevik Central Committee to prepare an armed uprising while the party held majorities in the capital's soviets. He believed that delay risked another Kornilov style attempt or a German advance on Petrograd, and that the Provisional Government's authority was fragile. Many party leaders hesitated. On October 10 (OS), October 23 (NS), Lenin attended a secret Central Committee meeting in Petrograd, where a resolution declaring an armed uprising on the agenda was passed by ten votes to two. Kamenev and Grigory Zinoviev opposed it, arguing that the party should rely on the coming Congress of Soviets and the Constituent Assembly, and Kamenev made his objections public in a non-Bolshevik newspaper.

The mechanism through which the insurrection took place was less a party conspiracy than an institution of the Petrograd Soviet. On October 12 (OS) the Soviet established a Military Revolutionary Committee, nominally to defend the capital against German attack and counterrevolution, under Trotsky's guidance. The committee sent commissars to army units and arranged to command the garrison's loyalty. When Kerensky tried on October 24 (OS) to shut down Bolshevik newspapers and move against the committee, forces linked to it seized the initiative, occupying bridges, railway stations, the telephone exchange, the telegraph agency, the post office, and the State Bank, with little resistance. Historians therefore differ over how to describe the event. It was organized by a party and a Soviet committee, yet it required the acquiescence of the garrison and the absence of defenders loyal to the government.

On the night of October 25 to 26 (OS), November 7 to 8 (NS), the Winter Palace, where ministers of the Provisional Government had gathered, was surrounded and entered by units of soldiers, sailors, and Red Guards. The cruiser Aurora fired a blank round as a signal. The defense, which included cadets, a few Cossacks, and a women's battalion, melted away or surrendered, and ministers were arrested. Kerensky had left the city earlier to seek loyal troops. His attempt, together with General Pyotr Krasnov's Cossacks, failed at Pulkovo Heights on October 30 (OS). Casualties in Petrograd were few. The more dramatic image of a mass storming derived from later Soviet commemorations and film, an example of how memory can overwrite contemporary evidence.

Elsewhere the transfer of power was more contested. In Moscow, fighting between Bolshevik forces and defenders of the Provisional Government lasted about a week and killed hundreds. In many provincial cities, soviets took power peacefully within weeks, in others there were disputes, and in some regions, including parts of the Don, Ukraine, and the Caucasus, other authorities rejected the Bolshevik government outright. The seizure of Petrograd was a precondition for Bolshevik rule but not its fulfillment.

The operational course of the October insurrection requires a careful separation between preparation and dramatic later mythology. The Military Revolutionary Committee, created within the Petrograd Soviet, claimed to safeguard the capital and the revolution against military threats. Its commissars and sympathetic units gradually displaced the authority of Provisional Government officials over key communications and military installations. By the time the Winter Palace was occupied, many essential institutions had already shifted to the insurgents. Fighting occurred and deaths were not nonexistent, but the familiar cinematic image of an immense crowd charging the palace is principally a product of later theatrical and film representations.

Other cities followed different trajectories. Moscow saw substantial fighting between pro-Bolshevik and anti-Bolshevik forces, and provincial soviets did not all recognize the Petrograd coup. Army fronts, Cossack regions and national councils assessed the news in light of their own interests. The Bolshevik victory in the capital thus established an institutional center from which a new government could issue decrees, but it did not create universal enforcement capacity. A decree on paper could announce land reform or peace only if agents, soldiers, local committees and transportation networks cooperated. This helps explain why the next months combined rapid legislative innovation with expanding coercion and territorial dispute.

Division III · Section 111.12

The Second Congress of Soviets, Representation, and Competing Claims of Legitimacy

The Second All-Russian Congress of Soviets of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies opened at the Smolny Institute on the evening of October 25 (OS). Of about 650 delegates, the Bolsheviks held roughly half or a little less, with the Left SRs adding a further significant bloc. Mensheviks and Right SRs protested the seizure of power and the assault on the Winter Palace, and many walked out. Trotsky and others dismissed the departing delegates, a move that effectively ended the Congress's role as a broad socialist coalition forum. The remaining delegates endorsed the transfer of power to the soviets, approved the Decrees on Peace and Land, and elected a new Central Executive Committee (VTsIK).

The Congress also approved the formation of a Council of People's Commissars, called Sovnarkom, headed by Lenin. Trotsky became commissar for foreign affairs and Stalin commissar for nationalities. At first the council consisted only of Bolsheviks, because other socialist groups declined to join. Intense debate followed. The railway workers' union, Vikzhel, threatened a strike and demanded a broad socialist coalition. Negotiations lasted several weeks, and Bolshevik leaders divided over concessions. In December, several Left SRs entered the government, forming a short lived coalition, but the Bolsheviks retained dominance.

The problem of legitimacy defined this period. The Bolsheviks argued that the soviets embodied a more direct and class based democracy than a parliament, that the Congress had approved the new government, and that revolutionary necessity justified their action. Their critics argued that the Congress had been unrepresentative, since many delegates had been elected before the political shifts of the autumn, that a party had taken power by force, and that the Constituent Assembly was the only institution that could speak for all Russia. National councils, peasants' soviets, and army committees each offered other claims. The coming elections to the Assembly would become the test of these competing sources of legitimacy.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Represented the Revolution?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing positions documented in speeches, newspapers, resolutions, and memoirs. They are not quotations from any single named person. These groups did not possess equal power, and the aim is historical understanding, not equivalence.

The Bolshevik leadership: The soviets, not a parliament elected under unsettled wartime conditions, were the genuine organs of the working people. Taking power quickly was necessary to end the war, give land to peasants, and prevent counterrevolution. A party with a disciplined organization was entitled to lead in the name of the soviets.
Mensheviks and Right Socialist Revolutionaries: A single party had seized power in a capital city by force, in the middle of a war, before the nation could vote. This risked civil war and a dictatorship that would isolate the revolution from the peasantry and invite German or counterrevolutionary victory. The Constituent Assembly should decide the government.
Petrograd soldiers, sailors, and workers: Many soldiers and workers supported soviet power because it promised peace, protection against counterrevolution, and an end to inequality. Their support was often practical and conditional, and different units and factories weighed the arguments differently.
Peasants in the villages: Their interest was in land, and many regarded the Land Decree as confirming their own actions. Their loyalty to any party depended on whether it left them the land and did not take their grain.
National councils such as the Ukrainian Central Rada: The Petrograd soviets spoke for Russian workers and soldiers, not for the whole population of Ukraine or the borderlands. Each people should decide its own future through its own representative institutions.

Documentary comparators include the Soviet government’s Decrees on Peace and Land, the Ukrainian Central Rada’s Universals, and the U.S. diplomatic record of the Constituent Assembly and early Soviet rule. These records document competing contemporaneous claims but do not constitute verbatim testimony from the reconstructed perspectives above.

The Second Congress of Soviets gathered delegates who had obtained mandates in different local and military institutions under rapidly changing conditions. Its composition and its right to speak for the whole population were contested. Bolsheviks and their supporters defended the congress as a more direct form of revolutionary representation than the old Duma-based government. Menshevik and Right Socialist-Revolutionary opponents argued that the seizure of the capital had preempted a broad democratic settlement and made genuine coalition impossible; many withdrew from the congress. Left Socialist-Revolutionaries did not initially share precisely the same position as the Right SR leadership and later entered a governing coalition. Students should therefore identify the date and institutional setting of each intervention rather than quote 'the socialists' as a single voice.

The congress's peace and land decrees gave immediate political weight to Bolshevik promises. Yet adoption by delegates did not settle whether future authority would rest in soviets, an elected Constituent Assembly, party-controlled executive institutions or a federation of national councils. The coming Assembly election supplied an unusually important test: the Socialist-Revolutionaries' extensive support among peasants meant that electoral popularity was not the same as Bolshevik dominance in Petrograd's soviet. The legitimacy dispute cannot be resolved by treating either city soviet or national ballot as transparent representation of every social interest. Each rested on a particular electoral process, calendar and theory of authority.

The comparison should also include actors outside the two largest parties. Ukrainian Central Rada proclamations claimed legitimacy from national representation, whereas delegates from soldiers' committees derived authority from armed units. Some workers supported Bolshevik demands for workers' control without endorsing an unrestricted party monopoly. The source problem is that later speeches and memoirs often frame these alternatives as doomed or treacherous. Contemporary resolutions, published congress minutes, election results and dated proclamations permit students to reconstruct options as participants saw them rather than as a settled outcome.

Division IV — Bolshevik Government, Political Alternatives, and Brest-Litovsk

Division IV · Section 111.13

Sovnarkom and the Early Soviet Decrees: Peace, Land, Workers, and Nationalities

Division IV asks: how did the Bolsheviks attempt to govern, and why did their early policies intensify conflicts over political authority and territory?

The new government issued decrees at remarkable speed. The Decree on Peace, adopted by the Congress on the night of October 25 to 26 (OS), appealed to all belligerent peoples and governments for an immediate democratic peace without annexations or indemnities and announced the end of secret diplomacy. The Decree on Land abolished private ownership of landed estates without compensation and transferred them to the disposal of local land committees and soviets pending the Constituent Assembly. It relied on a model peasant mandate compiled from hundreds of local peasant demands and drawn largely from the Socialist Revolutionary program. In effect, the decree legitimized what peasants were already doing, and it won the Bolsheviks tolerance in the countryside, though not necessarily loyalty.

Other early decrees addressed workers' control of industry (November 1917), the abolition of social estates and titles, the nationalization of banks in December, the separation of church and state (January 1918), and the restriction of opposition newspapers. A Supreme Council of National Economy was created in December to coordinate industry. On November 2 (OS), November 15 (NS), Sovnarkom issued the Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia, which proclaimed the equality and sovereignty of the empire's peoples and their right to self-determination, including secession. The declaration marked a sharp contrast with imperial policy and appealed to nationalities, but its practical application would be tested when national governments actually seceded.

The decrees reveal a gap between revolutionary promise and administrative capacity. Workers' control in factories frequently produced disputes with managers and engineers, falling output, and local initiatives that the center could not coordinate. The bureaucracy of the old state resisted: many civil servants went on strike or declined to cooperate with the new government, and Bolsheviks relied on improvisation, party members, and workers' committees to run institutions. Their theory expected the new state to wither over time, but the practical task was to build a state that could feed cities and mobilize forces.

These policies also affected non-Russian communities and religious minorities in different ways. The legal equality of Jews and other minorities, already granted in 1917, was maintained, and the Bolsheviks condemned antisemitism officially, but the separation of church and state and the property decrees threatened religious institutions. For national movements, the promise of self-determination was complicated by the Bolshevik assumption that class interests ultimately outweighed national ones, and by the government's claims over borderland institutions.

The Decree on Land drew substantially on demands already articulated in peasant movements and Socialist-Revolutionary agrarian programs. This helped the Bolsheviks appeal beyond their narrowest urban organizational base, but it also created a tension between village expectations of local control and the government's later demands for grain procurement. The Decree on Peace offered a public appeal for an end to the conflict without annexations or indemnities, while practical negotiations with the Central Powers soon produced terms that violated those principles. These gaps between program and implementation are not incidental: they illustrate the challenge of governing amid military collapse and economic shortage.

The early Soviet government also faced the question of who could appoint administrators, operate railways and handle banks. Different ministries and local committees responded with compliance, obstruction or negotiated improvisation. Calls for workers' control could refer to inspection, supervision or collective management; they did not immediately mean that all industry had been efficiently nationalized. A declaration of national self-determination was likewise meaningful but did not resolve disagreements over Ukraine or the Caucasus. Students should treat early decrees as interventions in ongoing conflicts, not as automatic descriptions of how the former empire was governed.

Division IV · Section 111.14

The Constituent Assembly, Coalition Breakdown, and the Concentration of Bolshevik Power

Elections to the Constituent Assembly, scheduled by the Provisional Government, began on November 12 (OS), November 25 (NS), 1917, and the Bolsheviks allowed them to proceed. They were the only nationwide elections held by universal suffrage in the revolutionary period, though voting continued over several weeks, was disrupted in some regions, and records are incomplete. Modern estimates indicate that the Socialist Revolutionaries received about 40 percent of the vote, with the Bolsheviks about 24 percent, the Kadets about 5 percent, the Mensheviks only a few percent, and various national and regional parties, including Ukrainian socialists, winning large shares in their regions. Because the SR lists were drawn up before the split between Right and Left SRs, the number of SR delegates overstated the actual strength of the Left SRs allied with the Bolsheviks.

The Bolsheviks drew their strength from Petrograd, Moscow, the northern industrial districts, and the army units of the northern and western fronts, while the SRs dominated the countryside. The results can be read in several ways: as evidence that the Bolsheviks lacked majority support, or as evidence that they controlled the key cities and military units and that voters were choosing among parties that did not clearly reflect the later divisions. What is clear is that the Bolsheviks did not win a parliamentary majority and that many peasants voted for the party of the Land Decree's original authors.

The Assembly met in the Tauride Palace on January 5 (OS), January 18 (NS), 1918. On that same date, January 18 New Style, armed forces loyal to the new Soviet authorities fired on a demonstration of Assembly supporters in Petrograd, killing and wounding participants before the Assembly completed its proceedings. In the Assembly, Chernov was elected chair, and the majority refused to endorse the Soviet decrees on power. The Bolsheviks and Left SRs withdrew, and in the early hours of January 6 (OS) the guards ordered the remaining delegates to leave. The Central Executive Committee issued a decree dissolving the Assembly. Protest was limited, partly because the soldiers and workers of the capital did not rally to it, and partly because the Bolsheviks had control of the armed forces in Petrograd.

The dissolution left the revolution with a single party dominant in the soviet system and a growing security apparatus. The Cheka, established on December 7 (OS), December 20 (NS), 1917, under Felix Dzerzhinsky, was authorized to combat counterrevolution and sabotage and soon acquired broad powers of arrest. Kadet leaders had been declared enemies of the people and arrested. The Left SRs remained in government until March 1918, when they resigned over Brest-Litovsk, which left the Bolsheviks to govern alone. The Third Congress of Soviets in January 1918 proclaimed a Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic. Historians dispute how far this concentration of power was driven by ideology, wartime emergency, or the improvisations of a beleaguered party, a question returned to in Section 111.20.

The Constituent Assembly election was conducted under difficult conditions across a fragmented empire, and voting took place over different days rather than as a single uniform national event. The Socialist-Revolutionary list drew very large support in rural districts, although divisions between Right and Left SRs complicate inferences from some party labels. Bolsheviks performed much more strongly in certain large cities and among some soldiers. The result showed a basic inconsistency between different claims to a popular mandate. Neither an argument about imperfect electoral rolls nor a claim that soviets were more immediate representatives of workers automatically erased this inconsistency.

The Assembly met on January 18 New Style, January 5 Old Style, 1918. Pro-Assembly demonstrations were fired upon that same day in Petrograd, before the chamber's overnight proceedings and its forcible dispersal; the text must not place the shootings on the previous day. After its dissolution, a range of non-Bolshevik socialists continued to organize opposition, some of it parliamentary, some underground and some later armed. The Cheka's establishment in December 1917 furnished a coercive instrument, but its reach and practices expanded over time rather than springing into a fully developed terror apparatus overnight. The Left SR partnership likewise survived until the dispute over Brest-Litovsk and subsequent July 1918 rupture.

Division IV · Section 111.15

From Armistice to Brest-Litovsk: Peace Negotiations and Territorial Costs

The Soviet government sought an immediate armistice on all fronts and, when the Allies refused to negotiate, opened separate talks with the Central Powers. An armistice was signed at Brest-Litovsk, the headquarters of the German Eastern Front, on December 15, 1917, and peace negotiations began on December 22. The Central Powers were represented by Germany's foreign secretary Richard von Kühlmann, Austria-Hungary's foreign minister Ottokar Czernin, and military figures including General Max Hoffmann. The Soviet delegation was initially headed by Adolph Joffe and from January by Trotsky, who used the talks as a platform for revolutionary propaganda in the hope that workers in Germany and Austria-Hungary would revolt.

The negotiations exposed a divide among the Bolsheviks. Lenin considered peace essential, because the army had dissolved and the new regime could not resist. A group of "Left Communists" led by Nikolai Bukharin urged a revolutionary war to spark European revolution. Trotsky proposed the position of "neither war nor peace": declare the war over and refuse to sign. Germany, meanwhile, drew strength from the separate treaty it concluded on February 9, 1918 with the Ukrainian People's Republic, which promised grain and provided a pretext to claim that Soviet Russia did not represent Ukraine. When Trotsky declared on February 10 that Russia would stop fighting but would not sign, the Germans resumed their advance on February 18, moving rapidly through Dvinsk, Minsk, and toward Pskov and Narva, and reaching Kiev in early March. Opposition to signing collapsed, and on February 23 the Bolshevik Central Committee narrowly voted to accept German terms, after Lenin threatened to resign.

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk was signed on March 3, 1918 and ratified by the Fourth Congress of Soviets later that month. Its territorial provisions were severe. Russia gave up claims to Poland, Lithuania, and Courland, whose future would be decided by Germany and Austria-Hungary in consultation with their populations. Russia was to evacuate Livonia and Estonia, where German police authority was to be established, to withdraw from Finland and the Åland Islands, and to recognize the Ukrainian People's Republic and conclude peace with it. Under Article IV, Russia was to facilitate evacuation of the eastern Anatolian provinces and their return to Turkey, while Ardahan, Kars, and Batum were to be evacuated by Russian troops; their political reorganization was to proceed with neighboring states, especially Turkey. This distinction in the treaty wording is important even though subsequent military and political outcomes favored Ottoman claims. Russia was also to demobilize its army and refrain from propaganda against the signatories. Supplementary agreements in August 1918 added financial obligations. Estimates of the losses are approximate and depend on what is counted, but they are commonly described as around a quarter to a third of the empire's European population, and a large share of its coal, iron, and industrial capacity.

The treaty must be read for what it did and did not do. It ended Russian participation in the war and allowed Germany to shift forces west, although Germany kept many troops in the east to occupy its gains. It did not create stable independent states: German and Austro-Hungarian occupation in Ukraine, the Baltic region, and Poland supported client or protectorate arrangements. It also did not last. After the armistice in the west on November 11, 1918, the Soviet government annulled the treaty on November 13, but control of the territories passed through civil and national wars rather than through the withdrawal of Germany alone. The relationship of the treaty to the collapse of the Central Powers is taken up in Chapter 112, and its place in the peace settlements in Chapter 113. The seizure of Russian resources in 1918 and the relocation of the Soviet capital from Petrograd to Moscow in March also signaled how deeply the treaty changed the strategic position of the revolution.

Historical Vocabulary
Armistice and Separate Peace

An armistice is an agreement to suspend fighting, which may precede but does not itself constitute a peace treaty. A separate peace is a treaty made by one member of an alliance with the enemy without the consent of its allies. Russia's agreement with the Central Powers in 1917 and 1918 was a separate peace, since it was made against the wishes of Britain and France, who had been bound by the 1914 understanding not to conclude peace separately. The Soviet government regarded the older secret treaties and alliance obligations as products of imperial diplomacy, which is why it published some of them. For students, the term matters because it explains Allied hostility to Soviet Russia and the German argument that the treaty was valid, while the Soviet government treated it as a forced concession to be annulled when circumstances changed.

The Brest-Litovsk documents distinguish a declaration of state policy from a map of effective control. Article I of the March 3 treaty ended the formal state of war between Soviet Russia and the Central Powers, and Article II constrained political agitation. Article III removed territories west of the agreed line from Russian sovereignty, subject to further delimitation. Article VI contained specific obligations toward the Ukrainian People's Republic, Estonia and Livonia, Finland and the Åland Islands; these provisions also reflected German military occupation and security priorities. Article IV drew a particularly important distinction: Russia was to help evacuate eastern Anatolian provinces and secure their return to Turkey, while Ardahan, Kars and Batum were to be cleared of Russian troops and their future relations reorganized in consultation with neighboring states, especially Turkey. It does not accurately reproduce Article IV to call all three districts a direct cession using the same wording as eastern Anatolia.

Russia's agreement followed the Central Powers' renewed February 1918 advance. Trotsky's policy of ending the war without accepting the proposed peace terms failed when German troops resumed operations, giving Lenin a stronger case that refusal endangered the survival of the Bolshevik state. The Ukrainian People's Republic had separately concluded an agreement on February 9, so it should never be treated merely as an appendix of the later Soviet Russian treaty. These legal instruments were not implemented in a political vacuum: national councils, workers' institutions and armed forces contested each other across the territories named. The German defeat later in 1918 undermined the entire arrangement. The chapter should present the March treaty as a decisive and highly coercive settlement rather than an enduring blueprint for the eventual national borders of eastern Europe.

Division IV · Section 111.16

Security, Economic Breakdown, and the Beginnings of Organized Civil Conflict

In the first half of 1918 the Bolshevik government created institutions of coercion and state control. The Workers' and Peasants' Red Army was established by decree in January and, under Trotsky as commissar for war from March, was reorganized on the principle of conscription and discipline rather than volunteer militias and elected officers. The government recruited former tsarist officers, called military specialists, supervised by political commissars, which provoked controversy within the party. The Cheka expanded its apparatus, and revolutionary tribunals began to try political offenses. In July 1918 a constitution for the RSFSR was adopted, formalizing the soviet system and denying political rights to groups defined as exploiters.

Economic disruption was severe. Industrial output fell, cities lost population, and exchange between town and country broke down. In May and June 1918 the government created a food dictatorship and committees of poor peasants to seize grain from those it considered hoarders, and on June 28 it nationalized large industry. These measures were responses to hunger and war, but they also expressed ideological assumptions about class conflict. Peasants who had welcomed the Land Decree resented requisitioning, and the conflict over grain would shape the rural experience of the civil war.

Opposition formed quickly. In the Don and Kuban regions, former officers and Cossack leaders, including Generals Mikhail Alekseev and Lavr Kornilov, created the Volunteer Army. After a difficult winter retreat from Rostov known as the Ice March, the army survived under Anton Denikin when Kornilov was killed in April 1918. Socialist opponents, including the SRs, organized conspiracies and uprisings. In July 1918 the Left SRs, angered by Brest-Litovsk and grain policy, assassinated the German ambassador Wilhelm von Mirbach and attempted an uprising in Moscow that the government suppressed. On August 30, Fanny Kaplan, an SR, wounded Lenin, and in Petrograd the Cheka chief Moisei Uritsky was killed. The government responded on September 5 with a decree on Red Terror authorizing mass arrests and executions.

The execution of Nicholas II, his wife, and their five children and attendants at Ekaterinburg on the night of July 16 to 17, 1918 occurred as anti-Bolshevik forces approached the Urals. The local Soviet carried out the killing, and the central authorities' role has been debated, but they accepted and publicized the death of the former emperor, while concealing the fate of the family at first. The event shows how rapidly confrontation was turning into a conflict that would be fought without restraint. The structure of the civil war, in which armies and governments competed across vast distances, is the subject of the next division.

Early Soviet economic problems inherited much of the wartime transport and provisioning crisis, then acquired new dimensions through political conflict. Private owners and managers feared expropriation, factory committees pressed for control, and railway workers defended professional autonomy. The new executive tried to stabilize supply while issuing decrees that weakened older property rights. The emergence of coercive security institutions was closely related to these struggles, but it is misleading to describe every restriction imposed in 1917–18 as part of a settled system of War Communism. That broader label applies principally to emergency policies and economic practices intensified during the civil war after mid-1918.

Armed resistance did not begin everywhere in the same way. In the south, anti-Bolshevik officers gathered volunteer forces; elsewhere local socialist, national or military committees rejected the new government's authority for different reasons. The emergence of the Red Army reflected the inadequacy of improvised guards and the government's need for sustained military organization, including contentious use of former imperial officers under political supervision. This institutional rebuilding under extreme pressure helps explain why the new state could become more centralized even while proclaiming popular self-rule. The process was neither a straightforward continuation of tsarist authority nor an uncomplicated realization of soviet democracy.

Division V — Civil War, Borderlands, and the Contested Postimperial Order

Division V · Section 111.17

Reds, Whites, Greens, and Foreign Intervention: Civil War, 1918–1921

Division V asks: why did the Russian Revolution develop into multiple civil and national wars, and what political order emerged from them?

The civil war began in earnest in the late spring of 1918. The Czechoslovak Legion, some 40,000 or more former Austro-Hungarian prisoners of war and deserters who had been organized to fight for the Allies and were being transported east along the Trans-Siberian railway to Vladivostok, clashed with local Soviet authorities in May. Their rising, sparked by disputes over disarmament and by an incident at Chelyabinsk, placed much of the Volga and Siberian railway under anti-Bolshevik control. In Samara, members of the dissolved Constituent Assembly formed a government called Komuch, and later efforts to unite the anti-Bolshevik forces produced the Ufa Directory. In November 1918 Admiral Alexander Kolchak seized power at Omsk, with the support of officers, and proclaimed himself Supreme Ruler.

The forces opposing the Bolsheviks were never unified. The White movement, which included monarchists, liberals, and military officers, was led by Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin in southern Russia, Yudenich in the northwest, and, in 1920, Baron Pyotr Wrangel in Crimea. Their slogan of "Russia, one and indivisible" alienated nationalist movements in the borderlands and made cooperation with Ukrainians, Finns, Poles, and others difficult. They also struggled with peasant hostility, because the Whites often returned landlords and requisitioned supplies. Socialist rivals of the Bolsheviks, such as the SRs, fought on several sides, and Greens, a term for peasant armed bands that resisted both Reds and Whites, were important in Ukraine and central Russia. Nestor Makhno's anarchist insurgent army in southern Ukraine at times allied with and at times fought the Bolsheviks.

Foreign intervention involved many states for varied purposes. British forces landed at Murmansk in 1918 and, with French, American, and other detachments, at Arkhangelsk in August. Japan sent a large force to the Russian Far East, and American troops also entered Siberia. French forces occupied Odessa in late 1918 and withdrew in April 1919. Allied motives included keeping war supplies from German hands, assisting the Czechoslovak Legion, defending Allied interests, and, increasingly after November 1918, opposing Bolshevism. The scale of intervention was limited compared with the main fighting by Russian forces, and divisions among the Allies and war weariness limited its effectiveness, though Soviet propaganda treated it as imperialist aggression and the White cause was tainted by its association with foreign support.

The Reds held the central territory with Moscow and Petrograd, the main railway hub, and much of the former army's stores and industrial capacity, and they used interior lines of movement. Trotsky's organization of the Red Army, with conscription and commissars, enabled it to field several million men on paper, though desertion remained a serious problem. In 1919 Denikin advanced as far as Orel, south of Moscow, and Yudenich neared Petrograd in October, but both were driven back. Kolchak's army collapsed in Siberia, and he was captured and shot in early 1920. Wrangel's forces evacuated Crimea in November 1920. Meanwhile the Polish-Soviet War of 1919 to 1921 and risings against Soviet requisition in Tambov and elsewhere showed that defeating the Whites did not end the fighting.

The civil war's geography helped determine its political outcome. Bolshevik-held central regions contained significant population, manufacturing and crucial railway junctions, enabling the movement of troops and supplies between threatened fronts. White armies operated from more distant bases in Siberia, the south and northwest and often depended on unstable local alliances or foreign supply. Their leaders differed over monarchy, military dictatorship and constitutional futures, but their resistance to Bolshevism did not translate into a convincing common land settlement. Peasants who opposed requisitions might still fear the restoration of former landowners associated with White commanders. This helps explain why the Reds could defeat important opponents while remaining unpopular among many rural communities.

The Czechoslovak Legion episode exposes the weakness of any simple Red-versus-White narrative. The Legion consisted largely of soldiers seeking a route out of Russia to continue the Allied war effort, and its confrontation with Soviet forces spread along a vast railway corridor in 1918. Local conflicts and contested orders escalated into armed control of towns, which assisted anti-Bolshevik governments such as the Committee of Members of the Constituent Assembly in Samara. Allied intervention likewise had changing aims, including protecting war stores, assisting the Legion and opposing Bolshevik consolidation; Japanese motives in the Far East were not identical to British or French ones. Foreign involvement should not be treated as a single centrally controlled crusade.

Division V · Section 111.18

Revolutionary Violence, Peasant Resistance, Famine, and War Communism

The civil war involved extensive violence against civilians as well as combatants. The Bolshevik Red Terror, formalized in September 1918, used the Cheka and revolutionary tribunals to arrest and execute hostages and suspected opponents, including clergy, former officials, and members of the propertied classes. White forces also carried out executions, reprisals, and mass killings in areas they controlled, and the White regimes' police and military courts operated with limited restraint. Atamans and local commanders on all sides acted with considerable autonomy. Historians continue to debate figures: totals of deaths from execution cannot be established precisely, and sources from the Cheka and from the Whites were each produced by partisan organizations.

Antisemitic violence was a notable feature of the war in Ukraine and neighboring regions. Pogroms against Jewish communities were carried out by Ukrainian nationalist units, White forces, Red Army units, and independent armed bands, in many cases involving murder, rape, and plunder. Estimates of deaths vary widely, commonly from tens of thousands to over one hundred thousand depending on method, and the number of Jews injured, displaced, or impoverished was far greater. The violence reflected a long history of antisemitism in the region (see Chapter 103), the breakdown of order, and the stereotype that associated Jews with Bolshevism. Soviet authorities officially condemned pogroms and punished some perpetrators, while Jewish self defense groups and relief organizations tried to protect communities.

The government's economic policy during the war, later called War Communism, combined nationalization of industry, state control of trade, labor mobilization, and forced requisition of grain, called prodrazverstka, which was systematized in January 1919. Peasants resisted by hiding grain, reducing sowing, and, in some regions, rising against requisition squads. The Tambov rebellion of 1920 to 1921 under Alexander Antonov involved tens of thousands of insurgents and was suppressed by large Red Army forces with severe measures against civilians. The Cossack regions of the Don faced harsh treatment after Red victories, including a January 1919 policy of "decossackization" that contributed to further revolt.

Epidemic disease and famine killed far more people than combat. Typhus epidemics spread with troop movements and urban crowding. A famine in 1921 to 1922, centered on the Volga region and the south, followed drought, requisitioning, and agricultural disruption, and is commonly estimated to have killed around five million people, though estimates vary. International relief efforts, including the American Relief Administration under Herbert Hoover and the work of Fridtjof Nansen, helped feed millions. Total civil war deaths, including military dead, civilian victims of violence, and deaths from disease and hunger, are generally estimated in the range of several million to over ten million, and the uncertainty reflects the lack of reliable records. More than a million people left Russia as emigrants. The Kronstadt rebellion of March 1921, in which sailors who had been among the revolution's earliest supporters demanded free soviets and an end to requisition, was crushed, but in the same month the Tenth Party Congress adopted the New Economic Policy, replacing requisition with a tax in kind.

War Communism combined centralized requisition, controls over trade, nationalization of important enterprises and military priorities under circumstances of acute scarcity. Historians debate whether these measures were mainly emergency expedients or also expressions of ideological goals, and they were not uniformly administered across the countryside. Peasants frequently answered requisition with concealed stocks, reduced marketed grain or armed resistance, while cities lost population as workers sought food elsewhere. The catastrophic famine that culminated in 1921–22 cannot be explained by one variable. Drought in critical producing regions, civil-war destruction, disease, disruption of transport and coercive procurement all contributed, with estimates of mortality varying according to geography and methodology.

Political and communal violence must be differentiated by agents and purpose. Cheka executions and the officially announced Red Terror involved state security organs, while White terror, local vengeance and violence by independent armies followed differing command structures. The mass pogroms against Jews in Ukraine and neighboring regions were perpetrated by multiple military and irregular formations, not by one homogeneous actor, and estimates of victims vary widely. Such events were not incidental background to Russian civil war: they shaped flight, survival strategies, and the legitimacy of competing governments. The 1921 Kronstadt and Tambov conflicts show that opposition to Bolshevik requisition could arise among groups previously sympathetic to revolutionary transformation.

Division V · Section 111.19

Finland, Ukraine, Poland, the Baltics, and the Caucasus: Competing Sovereignties

Each borderland followed a distinct path through war, occupation, and negotiation, which illustrates why imperial collapse did not produce a single pattern. In Finland, the independent government in Helsinki faced a civil war from January 1918 between the "Reds," supported by Russian garrisons and drawn from the industrial workers of the south, and the "Whites" under General Carl Gustaf Mannerheim, who were supported by German troops that landed in April. The Whites won in May. Around 36,000 people died, many of them Red prisoners who perished in camps or were executed. Finland's postwar frontier with Soviet Russia was settled in the Treaty of Tartu in 1920.

Ukraine experienced the most complicated sequence. The Central Rada declared independence in January 1918, signed a separate treaty with the Central Powers in February, and was displaced in April by a German-backed Hetmanate under Pavlo Skoropadsky. After the German collapse, a Directory led by Symon Petliura and Volodymyr Vynnychenko overthrew the hetman in December 1918. A Western Ukrainian People's Republic was proclaimed in eastern Galicia in November 1918 and fought a war with Poland. Soviet governments, Whites, French troops in Odessa, Polish forces, and peasant armies including Makhno's contested the territory, and Kiev changed hands repeatedly between 1918 and 1920. Soviet control was established by 1920 in most of the central and eastern lands, while western territories passed to Poland in the 1921 Treaty of Riga. The Soviet Ukrainian republic would later be a founding member of the USSR, a development beyond this chapter, and Ukraine returns to this textbook in Chapters 119, 132, and 139.

In the Baltic region, Estonia declared independence on February 24, 1918, on the eve of German occupation, and fought a War of Independence in 1918 to 1920 against Soviet forces, assisted by Finnish volunteers, British naval support, and local units, and concluded the Treaty of Tartu with Soviet Russia in February 1920. Latvia proclaimed its independence on November 18, 1918 and fought Soviet forces and, later, German volunteer units. Lithuania declared independence in February 1918 under German occupation and defended itself against Soviet, Polish, and German irregular forces. These states were small and fragile, but their governments obtained recognition and control of territory by 1920.

Poland re-emerged as a state in November 1918 as the Central Powers collapsed, under Józef Piłsudski as head of state, and its frontiers were not settled. The Polish-Soviet War of 1919 to 1921 involved Polish advances toward Kiev in 1920, a Red Army counteroffensive that reached the outskirts of Warsaw, and a Polish victory in August 1920. The Treaty of Riga of March 18, 1921 established a frontier east of the line suggested by the Allies, placing substantial Ukrainian and Belarusian populations in Poland. In the Caucasus, Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan became independent in May 1918 after a Transcaucasian Federation dissolved. The Ottoman Empire, Germany, and later Britain intervened, and Soviet forces took Azerbaijan in April 1920, Armenia in late 1920, and Georgia in early 1921, with the Treaty of Kars in October 1921 fixing the frontier with Turkey. Bessarabia, in the southwest, voted in 1918 to unite with Romania. The territorial outcomes in all regions rested on armies and diplomacy more than on declarations alone, and many minorities found themselves in states dominated by another nationality, a problem examined in Chapter 113.

The chronology of the borderlands differs from the chronology of Petrograd politics. Finnish independence in December 1917 was followed by civil war between opposing Finnish political and military forces; German intervention affected the outcome, but Finland's constitutional and national project was not simply invented in Berlin. The Baltic declarations of 1918 initially coexisted with German occupation and competing Soviet military claims. Poland's restoration of sovereignty in November 1918 was followed by a war with Soviet Russia over territories with overlapping Polish, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Lithuanian and Jewish populations. The Treaty of Riga in March 1921 fixed a contested frontier for that period, not a universally accepted final national settlement.

Ukraine illustrates the instability of international recognition without a reliable monopoly of force. The Central Rada, the German-backed Hetmanate, the Directory, rival Soviet governments, White armies and independent peasant formations held different territories at different times. No single clean 'Ukraine in 1918' map captures those shifts. In the Caucasus, Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan briefly established national republics in 1918 while confronting competing claims, Ottoman and later British involvement and Soviet military expansion. By 1921 their independence had been overturned in different military and political processes. The Belarusian People's Republic's claims also deserve acknowledgement, while distinguishing declarations from effective control or broad diplomatic recognition.

Division V · Section 111.20

From Romanov Collapse to a Contested Eastern European Order: Causes, Outcomes, and Historical Debate

By 1921 the Bolsheviks controlled most of the territory of the former empire's Russian core, much of Ukraine, Belarus, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, but not Finland, the Baltic states, Poland, or Bessarabia. The government had survived through a combination of coercion, organization, control of the center, tactical retreats, and, for many peasants and workers, a judgment that the alternatives were worse. It had also been transformed by the struggle, becoming a militarized, centralized one party state. The Whites had been defeated, but the social order of the empire was gone: the monarchy, the gentry's estates, the old officer corps, the imperial bureaucracy, and much of the middle class had disappeared or fled.

The new eastern European order was unstable. A belt of new or restored states stretched from Finland to Poland and Romania, bordering a Soviet state that claimed the legacy of revolution and a Germany humiliated by defeat. These states had to build armies, institutions, and national identities while containing minorities and border disputes. Their emergence cannot be explained as the fulfillment of preexisting national destinies, since many of them, such as Lithuania or Latvia, had been shaped by German occupation, civil war, and the collapse of the Central Powers as much as by long nationalist movements, and since several nationality movements, such as the Ukrainian and Belarusian, did not succeed in creating lasting independent states in this period. The study of these developments belongs alongside the peace settlements of Chapter 113 and the interwar politics of Chapter 114.

Historical Debate

Explaining Bolshevik Victory

The question: Why did the Bolsheviks seize power in 1917 and, against many opponents, retain it through the civil war? Historians disagree about how to balance popular support, organization, coercion, leadership, geography, and the weaknesses of their opponents.

Interpretation A: Organization, leadership, and coercion: One tradition, associated in different ways with historians such as Richard Pipes, emphasizes the role of Lenin's party organization, its willingness to use force, and its suppression of rivals. In this view, the October seizure was a minority party's seizure of power in a collapsing state, and the party's institutions, including the Cheka, the Red Army, and the one party state, explain survival.
Interpretation B: Social support and popular dynamics: Another tradition, associated with historians such as Alexander Rabinowitch and later social historians, emphasizes that the Bolsheviks in 1917 were a relatively flexible mass party that grew by responding to demands for peace, land, and soviet power, and that workers, soldiers, and peasants made the revolution their own. In the civil war, many peasants and workers preferred the Reds to the Whites, because the Whites seemed likely to restore landlords and the old order.

Evidence: The two positions use the same evidence differently. Election results of 1917, soviet resolutions, soldiers' letters, and factory committee records are cited for popular support. Cheka orders, Red Army discipline, requisition records, and suppression of rival parties are cited for coercion. Archival work since 1991 has shown regional variation, which supports neither a purely conspiratorial nor a purely popular account.

Additional factors: Most current scholarship combines several explanations: the Reds' central geographic position and control of railways and arsenals; the divisions and political limits of the Whites, who could not offer a convincing program on land or nationality; foreign intervention that was too limited to defeat the Reds but sufficient to help Bolshevik propaganda; and the Bolsheviks' skill in exploiting a collapsed state. Rather than two opposing camps, the field is better described as overlapping emphases.

Why it matters: How historians explain Bolshevik victory affects how they understand the origins of the Soviet state, the extent to which later Soviet authoritarianism grew from the 1917 revolution or from the civil war, and how far alternatives existed in 1917 and 1918.

Cause and Effect

Imperial Collapse and Reconstruction

Long-term causes

An autocratic system with limited institutions for representation, unresolved agrarian grievances, an industrializing economy with a concentrated working class, and multinational empire pressures that had been controlled rather than resolved.

Short-term causes

Military defeat and exhaustion, transport and supply breakdown, inflation, the loss of elite and army loyalty, and political crises in 1915 and 1916 that left the monarch isolated.

Event or transformation

The February Revolution ended the monarchy, the Provisional Government and Soviet shared unstable power, and the October Revolution put the Bolsheviks in control of the central government.

Immediate effects

Brest-Litovsk, German occupation of the western borderlands, civil war, intervention, national governments, and the breakdown of economic life.

Long-term consequences

A one party Soviet state, a new belt of postimperial states in eastern Europe, unresolved minority and frontier problems, and a lasting division of European politics.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Individual decisions, contingent events such as the Kornilov affair, mass movements, and the interaction of war and revolution all mattered. The Bolshevik victory was not inevitable and the new states' survival was equally uncertain.

Historical complexity: The same war that destroyed the Romanov state also destroyed the Habsburg and Ottoman states and weakened Germany, so Russian collapse was part of a wider eastern European and Middle Eastern imperial crisis explored in Chapter 112.

The chapter ends with a necessary caution about periodization. The year 1917 is conventionally treated as a turning point, but the conflicts it opened continued to 1921 and beyond. The defeat of the major White armies did not end peasant rebellions, nationality conflicts, or disputes over frontiers. The Soviet order that emerged by 1922 was neither the inevitable result of Marxist ideology nor a pure product of circumstance. Its relationship to Europe, as a revolutionary challenge, a neighbor, and eventually a great power, forms a thread that runs through the remainder of this volume and into Volume VI.

Historians disagree over which forces were decisive in the Bolshevik victory. Rex Wade emphasizes the institutional and political sequence of 1917; Sheila Fitzpatrick and S. A. Smith illuminate the social and revolutionary processes that went beyond party headquarters; Alexander Rabinowitch reconstructs party and soviet politics with attention to flexibility and local support; Orlando Figes highlights war, popular experience and social disintegration. These descriptions identify differences of analytical emphasis, not quotations or mutually exclusive camps. A persuasive judgment must relate the collapse of military and state authority to the choices of parties, the capacity of the Bolsheviks to organize armed forces, the opposition's failures, and the position of peasants and national movements.

One conclusion follows from the geography of the conflict: destroying an imperial monarchy was easier than deciding who would rule its former territory. Local soviets, military organizations, national councils and elected assemblies offered rival forms of legitimacy, while war and civil conflict repeatedly moved the frontier between lawful claim and effective power. Bolshevik reconstruction created a far more centralized government than many activists had anticipated, yet it did not restore the Romanov order. The eventual formation of the Soviet Union in 1922, beyond this chapter's principal endpoint, underlines the distinction between surviving civil war and creating an enduring federal constitutional structure. The legacy carried into the wider European postwar settlement was therefore both a revolutionary state and a landscape of disputed borders and remembered violence.

The chapter's final causal balance should reject teleology. A late 1916 observer could not have known that an uprising by women workers would precipitate abdication, a summer coalition crisis, an autumn Bolshevik insurrection, a separate peace and successive civil wars. Each link depended on decisions made under uncertainty, including whether officers would obey, whether ministers would continue fighting, whether peasants would wait for the Constituent Assembly and whether national councils would cooperate with Petrograd. Restoring this sense of alternatives makes the eventual outcome intelligible without presenting it as predetermined.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the collapse of Russian imperial authority in 1917, the struggle for revolutionary power, the creation of Bolshevik government, the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, and the civil and national wars that reshaped eastern Europe through 1921. Its central argument is that the Russian Revolution was a sequence of overlapping transformations rather than one event, and that the collapse of an old authority did not guarantee the establishment of a new one.

The Romanov monarchy fell because the strains of a long war, military defeat, transport and supply breakdown, inflation, and the alienation of almost every politically significant group combined with the monarchy's refusal to share power. The women workers' demonstrations of February 23, 1917 (OS) became a revolution when Petrograd soldiers refused to repress the crowds and joined them, and when army commanders concluded that the emperor could not be defended. Nicholas II abdicated on March 2, 1917 (OS), and his brother Michael declined the throne.

The February Revolution created a system of dual power in which the Provisional Government held formal authority and the Petrograd Soviet held practical influence over soldiers and workers. Order No. 1 and soldiers' committees changed the army, while the government's decision to remain at war and to defer land reform to a Constituent Assembly left central demands unmet. Peasants seized land, workers formed factory committees, and national movements in Finland, Ukraine, the Baltic region, the Caucasus, and elsewhere pressed for autonomy and, increasingly, independence.

Lenin's April Theses redirected the Bolsheviks toward opposition to the Provisional Government and support for soviet power. The coalition governments and the failed Kerensky Offensive undermined moderate socialists, while the July Days exposed the volatility of the capital and the Bolsheviks' vulnerability. The Kornilov affair then reversed the political situation, discrediting the right, weakening Kerensky, and returning the Bolsheviks to a leading position in the soviets of Petrograd and Moscow.

The October insurrection of November 7 to 8, 1917 (October 25 to 26 OS) was an organized seizure of key institutions carried out through the Petrograd Soviet's Military Revolutionary Committee, made possible by the loyalty or neutrality of the garrison and the weakness of the government's defenders. It was less dramatic than later commemorations suggested, and its success in Petrograd did not give the Bolsheviks control of the country. The Second Congress of Soviets approved the Decrees on Peace and Land, but the withdrawal of other socialists and the question of representation left legitimacy contested.

The early Soviet government issued far reaching decrees but possessed limited administrative capacity. The Constituent Assembly elections of November 1917 gave the Socialist Revolutionaries the largest vote and the Bolsheviks about a quarter, and the Assembly was dissolved after one session in January 1918. The Cheka, created in December 1917, and the one party dominance that followed marked a narrowing of political pluralism, though historians continue to debate how far that outcome was intended and how far it was a response to crisis.

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk of March 3, 1918, signed after German offensives forced a divided Bolshevik leadership to accept terms, removed Russia from the war at heavy cost. It detached Poland, Lithuania, and Courland, required evacuation of Livonia, Estonia, and Finland, recognized the Ukrainian People's Republic, and restored districts of the Caucasus to the Ottoman Empire. The treaty opened the way to German occupation and client arrangements across the western borderlands, but it also proved short lived, and the Soviet government annulled it in November 1918 after the armistice in the west.

The civil war that followed was a complex series of conflicts, not a clash of two homogeneous armies. Reds, Whites, Socialist Revolutionaries, peasant Greens, anarchists, national armies, and foreign interventionists all took part. Violence against civilians, including Red and White terror and antisemitic pogroms, requisition and peasant rebellion, famine, and epidemic disease caused millions of deaths by estimates that remain uncertain. The Bolsheviks prevailed through central position, organization, coercion, and the divisions and limitations of their opponents, as well as through a mixture of popular acceptance and exhaustion.

By 1921 the former empire's territory had divided into a Soviet state, a belt of independent or restored states from Finland to Poland and Romania, and contested frontier regions. These outcomes varied by region and were shaped by war, occupation, and diplomacy more than by any simple movement toward national self determination. The destabilization of Russia and eastern Europe set the conditions for the peace settlements of Chapter 113, the interwar politics of Chapter 114, and the confrontation between the Soviet state and its neighbors traced through Chapters 115, 119, and 125.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the strains of total war could destroy an empire and how the destruction of an old state could open a struggle without a predetermined outcome. In the volume's larger argument about European catastrophe, the Russian case demonstrates that the war's effects were not only battlefield losses but also the collapse of political authority, the militarization of politics, and the redrawing of frontiers by armies and occupation as much as by treaty.

The revolutions also connect regions that textbooks often separate. The failure of the Russian army shaped the German war effort, the Allied intervention linked Murmansk, Odessa, and Vladivostok to Paris and London, and German occupation, Ottoman advances, and Polish, Baltic, Ukrainian, and Caucasian politics intertwined in 1918. The Soviet state that emerged by 1921 was not yet the Soviet Union of later decades, but it was already a new kind of European political actor, whose ideology, security institutions, and neighbors would shape the continent from Chapter 115 to the end of the Cold War in Chapter 132.

Several continuities survived the chapter. Peasant attachment to land, the problem of governing a multinational territory, the role of the army in politics, and the tension between claims of popular representation and concentrated authority did not disappear in 1921. Later memory, whether Soviet, Western, or national, would turn the revolutions into founding myths or warnings, which makes the careful use of evidence essential. The borderland states, in turn, built their national histories partly in opposition to the revolution and the civil war, a pattern visible again in Chapters 136 and 139.

The next chapter turns to the other side of the same crisis. Chapter 112 examines how, in 1918, the Central Powers themselves collapsed through military defeat, mutiny, revolution, and the disintegration of the Habsburg, Hohenzollern, and Ottoman orders, setting the stage for the peace settlements of Chapter 113. Read together, the two chapters explain how the old European imperial order in the east and center ended within little more than a year.
