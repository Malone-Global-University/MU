Athens, Empire, and the Peloponnesian War

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 478/7 BCE, Greek states gathered on the small island of Delos to continue a war against Persia. Seventy-odd years later, one of them lay besieged and starving, its walls being pulled down, its fleet surrendered, and its empire gone. Between those two moments, the same city had made itself the most participatory democracy in the Greek world and the most coercive imperial power in the Aegean, and had helped pull much of the Greek world into a war that left no one secure.

Among the surviving evidence for the Athenian Empire are fragments of stone. On the Athenian Acropolis, the Athenians inscribed lists recording the small portion of allied tribute, one sixtieth, that was dedicated each year to the goddess Athena. Modern scholars have pieced these fragments together into the Athenian Tribute Lists, which record, year by year from 454/3 BCE, the names of paying communities scattered across the Aegean, the Hellespont, Thrace, Caria, and Ionia and the one-sixtieth portions of their tribute dedicated to Athena, from which historians can often infer the tribute actually paid. No other document makes so plainly visible the fact that dozens of Greek cities, each with its own gods, magistrates, and traditions, were paying money to Athens in a system administered from a single city. Beside those stones stands a very different kind of evidence, the narrative of the Athenian historian Thucydides, who lived through most of the war of 431 to 404 BCE and undertook to explain it while it was still being fought.

This chapter asks a question that has shaped ancient history writing since Thucydides himself: how did the alliance created to continue the war against Persia become an Athenian empire, and why did the resulting struggle for power between Athens, Sparta, and their allies culminate in a war that transformed the Greek world? The chapter takes that question in stages. It begins where Chapter 11 ended, with the transfer of anti-Persian leadership toward Athens in the years after the Persian Wars. It follows the gradual hardening of the Delian League into an empire. It examines how Athenian democracy developed inside that imperial setting. It then widens to the interstate system, showing how Athens, Sparta, Corinth, and other states drifted toward war in 431 BCE, how the war unfolded through the Archidamian phase, the Sicilian catastrophe, and the long struggle in the Aegean, and how it ended in Athenian surrender in 404 BCE and a violent political aftermath that concluded with the restoration of democracy in 403 BCE.

Two temptations must be avoided. The first is the celebratory picture of a "Golden Age of Athens," in which democracy, art, and philosophy flourish while the costs of empire are pushed offstage. The second is a battle-by-battle military narrative in which the war becomes a sequence of campaigns disconnected from politics, money, religion, and social life. Both distortions are tempting because Athens produced so much evidence. Athens was a democracy for its adult male citizens and a coercive power over many allied communities; both statements are true, and neither cancels the other. The chapter also resists the opposite simplification, in which democracy is treated as the cause of empire or empire as the price of democracy. The relationships between them were real, but they were historically contingent and must be argued from evidence.

The chapter also places Athens within a larger geography. The war was not merely a contest between Athens and Sparta across central Greece. It reached the Chalcidice and Thrace, the Hellespont and the Black Sea grain route, the coast of Asia Minor, the western Greek colonies of Sicily and southern Italy, and the courts of Persian governors. Persia, defeated in Greece in 480 to 479 BCE as Chapter 11 explained, did not vanish from Greek affairs. In the last decade of the war, Persian money proved strategically decisive. This is one of several points at which the chapter connects the Greek world to the wider Mediterranean and western Asian world examined throughout Volume I.

Finally, the chapter demonstrates how historical knowledge is constructed. Thucydides is the essential narrative source and also a brilliant, selective, and interpretive author who composed speeches he could not have transcribed verbatim and who framed the war around a theory of its causes. Inscriptions, comedy, oratory, later biography, and archaeology supply evidence he does not, and sometimes complicate his account. The chapter therefore teaches students to read Thucydides as evidence, not as an unmediated window onto events.

Within the Volume, this chapter concludes the political history of classical Greece that Chapters 10 and 11 began. It leaves the deep treatment of Greek religion, drama, philosophy, literature, architecture, and art to Chapter 13. Where the Parthenon, tragedy, or intellectual life bear directly on empire and civic identity, they appear here briefly, but their full treatment belongs there. The chapter closes with a paradox that later chapters of this Volume, and the fourth-century Greek world beyond it, must take up: Sparta won the war, but no durable settlement of the Greek world followed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the transition from the Hellenic anti-Persian alliance to the Delian League and explain why historians describe the shift from league to empire as gradual.
  2. Explain the mechanisms of Athenian imperial control, including tribute, naval dominance, cleruchies, garrisons, officials, and the suppression of revolts.
  3. Describe the constitutional development of Athenian democracy after the Persian Wars, including the reforms associated with Ephialtes and the citizenship law of 451/0 BCE, and identify who was included in and excluded from political participation.
  4. Analyze the relationship between Athenian democracy and Athenian empire without reducing either to a simple cause of the other.
  5. Distinguish long-term structural tensions from immediate disputes in explaining why war came in 431 BCE, and evaluate Thucydides's account of causation as an interpretation.
  6. Compare the strategies of Athens and Sparta in the Archidamian War and assess the consequences of the Athenian plague.
  7. Interpret Thucydides's presentations of Mytilene and Melos as literary reconstructions and distinguish the two episodes.
  8. Assess why Athenians approved the Sicilian Expedition and why it ended in catastrophe.
  9. Connect military crisis, Persian financial intervention, and political breakdown in the years 413 to 404 BCE without treating any as a mechanical cause of the others.
  10. Evaluate the significance of the Thirty, the restoration of democracy in 403 BCE, and the failure of Spartan victory to produce a stable Greek settlement.
  11. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of literary, epigraphic, and other evidence for reconstructing the Athenian Empire and the war.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. At what point, if any, does leadership of an alliance become empire, and what kinds of evidence would allow historians to identify that point?
  2. How could a state that expanded political participation among its own citizens restrict the autonomy of many allied communities, and what does that paradox reveal about ancient ideas of freedom?
  3. Did democracy encourage imperial expansion, did empire sustain democracy, or were the two connected in more contingent ways?
  4. Why did the rivalry between Athens and Sparta escalate into war in 431 BCE, and what roles did Corinth, Corcyra, Megara, and other states play?
  5. How reliable is Thucydides as a guide to the causes and conduct of the war, and how do inscriptions and other sources correct or complicate him?
  6. What made the Athenian plague and the Sicilian Expedition turning points, and what alternative outcomes were plausible at the time?
  7. How did Persian money and diplomacy alter the balance between Athens and Sparta?
  8. Why did Athenian democracy break down twice, in 411 and 404 BCE, and why was it restored?
  9. Why did Spartan victory fail to produce durable leadership over the Greek world?

Historical Context

Chapter 10 compared two very different political orders. Sparta was a state whose citizens, the Spartiates, lived as full-time soldiers supported by the labor of a subjugated population of helots, and whose institutions combined a dual kingship, a council of elders, elected magistrates called ephors, and an assembly. Athens, after the reforms associated with Cleisthenes in 508/7 BCE, was developing a form of citizen self-government in which the assembly of adult male citizens held real authority. Chapter 11 then examined how these two cities, with many other Greek states, resisted the Persian invasions of 480 to 479 BCE, and how the aftermath left them stronger in different ways. Sparta retained its prestige as the leader of the land-based Peloponnesian alliance. Athens emerged with a large fleet, a rebuilt city, and a reputation for having borne the brunt of the naval war at Salamis.

The Persian Wars had not ended Persian claims or influence in the Aegean. Many Greek cities of western Asia Minor entered the Athenian-led alliance after 479 BCE, while Persia continued to claim authority in the region and retained the resources to re-enter Greek affairs decisively when circumstances allowed. After the victories of 479 BCE, Greek forces under Spartan and then Athenian leadership campaigned in the Hellespont, in Cyprus, and along the Thracian coast. The question of who would organize and pay for continuing the war, and what would happen to Greek communities liberated from Persian control, created the opening for a new alliance.

The chapter's period, 478/7 to 403 BCE, is conventionally divided into two parts. The first, called by Thucydides the Pentekontaetia, is the roughly fifty years between the Persian invasions and the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War in 431 BCE. It is the period in which the anti-Persian alliance became an Athenian empire and in which relations between Athens and Sparta oscillated between cooperation, hostility, and limited war. The second part is the Peloponnesian War itself, which Thucydides reckoned as a single conflict of twenty-seven years, though modern historians commonly divide it into the Archidamian War (431 to 421 BCE), the uneasy Peace of Nicias, the Sicilian Expedition, and the final Aegean or Decelean war (413 to 404 BCE).

The chapter draws on a wider variety of evidence than the earlier chapters of Part II. Thucydides, writing in Greek prose and breaking off in the year 411 BCE in mid-narrative, is the central source for the war and a major source for the Pentekontaetia, though his account of the fifty years is deliberately compressed. Xenophon's Hellenica continues the story from 411 BCE to 362 BCE. Herodotus, though he ends earlier, matters for the background. Plutarch, writing several centuries later, preserves biographical traditions about Pericles, Nicias, and Alcibiades that use earlier lost sources but require caution. Athenian inscriptions, including the tribute lists, treaties, financial accounts, and decrees such as those concerning Erythrai, Chalcis, and Methone, allow historians to test literary claims against documents produced by the Athenian state. Comedy, especially the plays of Aristophanes, and later Athenian oratory offer contemporary and near-contemporary perspectives on war, politics, and everyday life, though always through particular literary and rhetorical conventions. Archaeology, including the study of fortifications, coinage, and the Athenian silver mines at Laurion, adds material evidence.

A final piece of context is chronological. Athenian years began in mid-summer, and Athenian officials were dated by the year of their tenure, which is why many dates in this chapter are given in double form such as 462/1 or 478/7 BCE. Many dates for the early Pentekontaetia are inferred from Thucydides, later writers, and inscriptions, and remain approximate or debated. The chapter states this where it matters.

Section 12.1

From the Hellenic League to the Delian League

The Delian League grew out of a crisis of leadership. After the Greek victories at Plataea and Mycale in 479 BCE, Spartan and Athenian forces continued the war, and in 478 BCE a Greek fleet under the Spartan regent Pausanias campaigned in Cyprus, brought much of the island temporarily under allied control, and then captured Byzantium. Pausanias's conduct, according to Thucydides, alienated many of the allies, in particular the Ionian and island Greeks, who found his manner arrogant and his style of command, in some later traditions, tinged with Persian display. Sparta recalled him, and when it sent a successor, the allies would not accept Spartan leadership. Thucydides reports that Sparta did not press the matter, being reluctant to continue a distant overseas war and anxious about the future conduct of its commanders abroad. Modern historians add that Sparta's leadership was heavily dependent on the security of its own land base and its helot system, which made long naval campaigns far from the Peloponnese unattractive.

The allies then turned to Athens. In 478/7 BCE, representatives of Athens and many Aegean communities, chiefly on the islands and the coasts of Thrace, the Hellespont, and Ionia, swore an alliance whose stated aim, in Thucydides's account, was to compensate themselves for their losses by ravaging the territory of the Persian king. A common treasury was established on the sacred island of Delos, and the Athenians appointed officials called hellenotamiai, "treasurers of the Greeks," to receive the contributions. The Athenian statesman Aristides is credited by ancient tradition with the first assessment of what each member should contribute, and Thucydides gives the initial total as 460 talents a year. The figure is the ancient historian's report, and modern scholars use it with caution, though the general picture of an initial assessment in money for some members and ships for others is widely accepted.

Two features of the early alliance need emphasis. First, it was an alliance of states, at least in form. Members swore oaths, including a pledge, in Athenian tradition, to have the same friends and enemies, and the alliance had a council, called a synod, meeting on Delos, in which each member, in theory, had a vote. Second, contributions were not uniform. Some communities, notably the larger islands of Chios, Lesbos, and Samos, supplied ships and crews, while others paid tribute in money. This distinction was later to matter greatly, because a member that commuted its contribution to money reduced its independent naval capacity and became increasingly dependent on Athenian sea power, while a member that supplied ships retained some capacity to resist Athens.

Historical Vocabulary
Phoros and Cleruchy

Phoros is the Greek word for the contributions paid by members of the Delian League, usually translated "tribute." The English word can mislead. It suggests a conquering power demanding payment from the beginning, whereas the earliest phoros was an assessed allied contribution to a common war fund. Only over time, as Athens controlled the assessment, collection, and use of the money, did the payments come to look like imperial tribute, and even then Athenian sources present them as contributions to a common defense that Athens continued to provide.

A cleruchy was an Athenian settlement established on land taken from or granted within an allied community. Cleruchs remained Athenian citizens, serving as hoplites and retaining their political rights, and were not the founders of a new independent colony. Cleruchies served several purposes, including relieving Athenian poverty, providing garrison strength in sensitive locations, and giving Athens direct control of territory. To the allied communities on whose land they were placed, they were a visible loss of land and autonomy.

Both terms matter because they show that Athenian imperial power was built out of specific institutions and practices, not from a single declaration. Historians reconstruct these practices mainly from inscriptions and from Thucydides's often compressed narrative.

The analytical point that must govern the rest of the chapter is that the Delian League did not begin as an openly declared Athenian empire. The alliance was formed through the willing participation of many allies, though on uneven terms and with binding oaths. Its transformation, as the next section shows, occurred through the gradual hardening of Athenian practices, the increasing use of coercion, and the political and financial choices of both Athens and its allies. Thucydides himself, though he has a clear view of the process, does not provide a date for the point at which league became empire.

Section 12.2

From Alliance to Athenian Empire

The first decades of the League were successful. Under Athenian leaders, above all Cimon, son of Miltiades, the alliance drove Persian garrisons from Eion at the mouth of the Strymon and from other positions in Thrace, cleared piracy from the Aegean islands, and, according to Plutarch and later tradition, won a great double victory at the Eurymedon River in southern Asia Minor around 466 BCE. These achievements were real and gave the alliance a genuine claim to serve the common interest of the Greeks in the Aegean. They also strengthened Athens's fleet and its prestige, and created an expectation of Athenian command.

The stages by which leadership became empire are visible, in outline, in Thucydides's account of the revolts and reductions of allies. According to Thucydides, when Naxos tried to leave the alliance, in a date usually placed in the 470s BCE, the Athenians besieged it and forced it back, and he describes this as the first case in which the alliance's rules were overridden against the will of a member. He then observes that the allies themselves contributed to the change. Many, he says, preferred to pay money rather than send ships and men, in order to avoid the burden of service, and this left them militarily weaker while Athenian naval power grew from the payments that they made. This is a significant claim, because it locates part of the explanation in the choices of allied communities and not solely in Athenian aggression, though it is also Thucydides's judgment and reflects his own perspective.

The revolt of Thasos, around 465 to 463 BCE, shows a harder edge. Thasos was a wealthy island with mines and mainland trading interests on the Thracian coast, and a dispute with Athens over those interests led it to revolt. Athens besieged the island for about three years, and after its defeat Thasos was compelled to tear down its walls, surrender its fleet, pay an indemnity and continuing tribute, and give up its mainland holdings and mines. The episode shows what secession would cost.

Further evidence comes from inscriptions. The tribute lists, which begin with the year 454/3 BCE, show that Athens now recorded the quota of one sixtieth of each member's payment as a dedication to Athena. The listing of hundreds of communities, sorted in later years into geographic districts (Ionian, Hellespontine, Thracian, Carian, and island), is evidence for the scale and administration of the system. The same year, according to the traditional reconstruction, the treasury was moved from Delos to Athens. Ancient sources give different explanations, including fear of Persian attack and Samian advice, and modern scholars have debated the exact date and whether the move was the significant break it is often taken to be. It is clear, however, that by the middle of the fifth century Athens physically controlled the funds.

Other Athenian measures reinforced control. Athens established cleruchies on allied land in places such as Euboea, Naxos, and Andros, and planted colonies at strategic points, of which the most important was Amphipolis on the Strymon in 437/6 BCE. It placed garrisons and resident officials in some communities. It required certain allies to bring legal cases to Athens, and inscriptions concerning Erythrai, Chalcis, and other communities show Athens dictating aspects of their constitutions, oaths, and judicial arrangements. A widely discussed but chronologically uncertain measure, the Coinage Decree, sought to require allies to use Athenian silver coinage and weights. Its date and enforcement are debated. Taken together, these practices show that Athens was increasingly treating allies as subjects. Athenian speakers in Thucydides's history, including Pericles, are shown describing the empire as something like a tyranny that it would be dangerous to give up, and the ancient writer known as the Old Oligarch, whose pamphlet on the Athenian constitution probably dates to the 420s, described a system in which Athens's control of the sea and allied law courts was fundamental to democratic strength.

The analytical question is therefore: at what point does leadership become empire? Historians do not offer a single universally accepted answer. Some have pointed to the suppression of Naxos, the defeat of Thasos, the transfer of the treasury, or the imposition of constitutional and judicial controls in the 440s. Others have argued that the League had become an empire by the time the Persian threat receded and that the real question is when Athenians and their allies began to recognize it as such. This debate is the subject of a Historical Debate feature later in the chapter. What can be said with confidence is that the evidence shows a steady increase in Athenian coercive power over decades. It also shows that the change was uneven, that some allies were treated more harshly than others, and that Persian pressure itself became less immediate, reducing the persuasiveness of the original justification.

One consequence deserves note here because it recurs in the chapter. A peace between Athens and Persia, traditionally associated with the Athenian Callias and dated around 449 BCE, is mentioned in later sources, though some historians doubt that a formal treaty was concluded, and ancient writers disagreed even about its existence. If some kind of settlement did occur, the League's original raison d'être was effectively over, and yet the tribute continued. Whether by formal treaty or informal understanding, the reduced Persian danger made Athenian claims to be defending Greek freedom increasingly difficult to reconcile with the reality of Athenian control.

Section 12.3

Democracy after the Persian Wars

While Athens's external power expanded, its internal government changed. Chapter 10 traced the development of Athenian political institutions to the Cleisthenic reforms and beyond. By the 460s BCE, the Areopagus, a council of former archons, still retained an important political role, including a vaguely defined guardianship over the laws and the conduct of magistrates. The politician Ephialtes, with the support of the young Pericles, attacked its power, and around 462/1 BCE the Areopagus was stripped of most of these political functions. Its jurisdiction over homicide and certain religious matters remained. Ephialtes was assassinated soon afterwards, and the details of the reform are debated because they depend on later sources, including the Constitution of the Athenians attributed to Aristotle's school and Plutarch.

The effect of the reform was to transfer authority toward three bodies. The ekklesia, or assembly, was open to all adult male citizens and met regularly on the Pnyx hill to make decisions on war, peace, alliances, finance, and other public matters. The boule, or council of 500, whose members were chosen by lot from the ten tribes established by Cleisthenes, prepared business for the assembly and handled daily administration. The dikasteria, or popular courts, staffed by large juries of citizens chosen by lot, handled a very wide range of legal disputes and political prosecutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Demokratia

The Greek word demokratia combines demos, the people or citizen body, and kratos, power or rule. The term is attested only after the Persian Wars, and some ancient writers used it critically. The demos could also mean the ordinary citizens as opposed to the wealthy, and critics, including the Old Oligarch, understood democracy as government in the interest of the poorer majority.

Athenian democracy differed profoundly from modern representative democracy. Citizens took part directly in the assembly and courts, and most magistracies were filled by lot, on the principle that any qualified citizen could serve. Elections were used for offices requiring particular skill, above all the ten strategoi (generals), and these officers, including Pericles, could be reelected repeatedly and were the most important political leaders. But the citizen body was narrow. Women, enslaved people, and resident foreigners (metics) had no political rights. Democracy in this sense is a description of how citizens governed themselves, not a claim to universal equality.

Other measures reinforced participation. Jurors and, later, members of the council received modest pay for service, a reform traditionally connected to Pericles, which allowed poorer citizens to spend days on public business without losing a day's income. The precise chronology of payment for different offices is uncertain and the evidence is fragmentary. The use of sortition for most offices weakened aristocratic claims to office as a matter of birth or wealth, though wealthy families continued to supply many of the elected generals and the most influential speakers in the assembly. Ostracism, a procedure by which citizens could vote to send a prominent politician into ten years of exile, was used in the fifth century to settle political disputes, and it removed Cimon in 461 BCE after his policy of cooperation with Sparta collapsed.

Pericles must be described carefully. He was elected a general in most years from the 440s until his death in 429 BCE, he was an outstanding speaker, and he supported public building, pay for service, and a policy of firm resistance to Sparta. But he did not "rule" Athens, and he was vulnerable to the assembly's judgment. In 430 BCE, after the plague and the first phase of the war, the Athenians deposed him from the generalship and fined him, and then reelected him. Nor did he invent Athenian democracy; its foundations lay in the Solonian and Cleisthenic reforms of earlier generations, and its further development involved many actors. Thucydides's often-quoted judgment that under Pericles Athens was in name a democracy but in fact rule by its first citizen is an ancient author's evaluation, and should be treated as an interpretation of Pericles's influence, not a description of the institutions.

In 451/0 BCE, a law proposed by Pericles restricted Athenian citizenship to those whose parents were both Athenian, in place of the earlier standard of an Athenian father alone. The reasons are debated. Suggested explanations include limiting the number of claimants to the benefits of citizenship, including jury pay and, later, grain distributions and other privileges, protecting Athenian property and marriage networks from aristocratic marriages with foreign families, and defining the citizen body as it enlarged its powers. Whatever the motive, the law shows that participation was a valued privilege, and the boundary of citizenship was consciously narrowed as democratic participation expanded. In social terms, women's roles in transmitting citizenship became legally more significant even as women remained excluded from political rights.

The Athenian democracy should therefore be described with precision: an unusually direct form of citizen government, in which adult male citizens participated extensively, joined by principles of lot and pay that opened office and service to many, but which excluded the great majority of people who lived in Attica. The resident population included many thousands of enslaved people, whose numbers are uncertain and much debated, and a substantial metic community. Estimates of the adult male citizen population at the beginning of the war vary widely, commonly falling between about 30,000 and 60,000, and no figure is secure.

Section 12.4

Democracy and Empire: Connected but Not Identical

The developments of the last two sections raise one of the most significant interpretive problems in the chapter. Athens expanded political participation among its own citizens while it restricted the autonomy of many allied communities. How were these two developments related?

Several connections are well supported by evidence. Empire provided money. The tribute, together with revenue from the silver mines at Laurion and other sources, helped to pay for the large navy, for the construction program on the Acropolis and elsewhere, and, plausibly, for some forms of public pay. Thucydides reports that just before the war Athens drew a yearly revenue of about 600 talents from the allies and held a reserve of some 6,000 talents of coined silver on the Acropolis, alongside a fleet of about 300 triremes ready for service. These are his figures, and they should be treated as an ancient report, not an audited account. It is important to be careful about the Parthenon. The building was financed from Athenian public funds, including the treasury of Athena, and the tribute contributed a share of that treasury through the dedication of the sixtieth. Ancient sources record debate about whether Pericles was using allied money improperly, but to describe the Parthenon simply as built with money stolen from the League overstates the direct link and ignores the disputed nature of the accounts.

Empire also mobilized the poor. Athenian naval power depended on rowers, and the oarsmen of the trireme fleet were largely drawn from the poorer citizens, the class known as the thetes, along with metics, hired foreigners, and, at times, enslaved men. Because naval service gave men from the poorest citizen class an essential military role and a paid one, it is plausible that the fleet increased their political weight. This is an old argument, and it appears in the Old Oligarch, but the extent to which naval service caused democratic development is uncertain, since the reforms of Cleisthenes and Ephialtes were not obviously designed for such a purpose and the poor already had a voice in the assembly.

Empire also created practical business for democratic institutions. Allied cases came to Athens, the assembly voted on the assessment of tribute, on treaties, and on the conduct of allied revolts, and the empire created many salaried positions. A large body of officials, garrison commanders, and colonists relied on the state. In this sense, imperial administration could increase the amount of public work that democratic bodies had to handle.

Yet it would be a mistake to claim that democracy caused empire or that empire created democracy. Democratic institutions were developed before the League was formed, and Athens's early imperial expansion was led largely by the aristocratic Cimon. Other Greek states with oligarchic governments, above all Sparta, also dominated their neighbors, and Sparta's rule over Messenia through helotage was a form of subjugation of its own. Nor do the facts support the view that democracy inevitably drove Athens toward aggression: Athenian assemblies sometimes rejected imperialistic proposals and sometimes accepted them, and Thucydides's speeches present strikingly different views of empire. The relationship was one of interaction, contingent on circumstances, leaders, and resources.

What the evidence does show is the paradox that Athenians themselves seem to have recognized. They claimed to be defending freedom and yet exercised power over others. Thucydides's Athenian speakers attempt to justify empire by the necessity imposed by fear, honor, and interest. Allied and enemy voices in the same history, and later writers such as the orator Isocrates, denounce the empire as tyranny over fellow Greeks. Democratic self-government and imperial domination coexisted, and the coexistence created the tensions that later shaped Athenian politics and the war.

Section 12.5

Athens, Sparta, and the Pentekontaetia

Relations between Athens and Sparta in the fifty years after the Persian Wars were unstable, but war was not predetermined. In the years immediately after 479 BCE, Sparta objected to the rebuilding of Athenian walls, and Themistocles, according to Thucydides, deceived Spartan envoys until the work was complete. Even so, cooperation continued for some time, and Cimon, who believed in partnership between the two leading Greek states, was known for his friendliness toward Sparta.

A crisis came around 464 BCE, when a major earthquake devastated Sparta and triggered a major revolt involving helots, especially in Messenia, joined by some perioikoi. The Spartans asked their allies for help, and Cimon persuaded the Athenians to send a hoplite force. When the force arrived, the Spartans dismissed it, according to Thucydides out of fear that the Athenians might be open to the helots' cause. The Athenians took the dismissal as an insult, broke their alliance with Sparta, and allied with Sparta's enemy Argos. Cimon was ostracized in 461 BCE. This episode shows an important feature of Spartan society: the Spartan state's dependence on a subject population shaped its foreign policy and its anxieties about outsiders. It also shows how domestic Athenian politics and foreign policy were tied together.

What modern historians call the First Peloponnesian War (roughly 460 to 446 BCE) was a series of separate conflicts rather than one continuous war. Athens made alliances with Argos and Thessaly and with Megara, whose defection from the Peloponnesian League gave Athens a foothold on the Isthmus, and built the Long Walls linking the city to its harbors at the Piraeus and Phalerum. Athens fought Corinth, Aegina, and Boeotian forces, and it dispatched a major force to Egypt in support of a revolt against Persia, a venture that ended in disaster around 454 BCE. Spartan forces defeated Athens at Tanagra in 457 BCE, but Athens won at Oenophyta soon afterward and gained control of much of Boeotia. Aegina was forced to join the League. Over the following decade, however, Athens lost its mainland holdings, and after the revolts of Euboea and Megara around 446 BCE, it accepted a settlement.

The Thirty Years' Peace of 446/5 BCE recognized two alliance systems, each free to manage its own members, with a provision for arbitration of disputes and a prohibition on either side accepting the other's members into its alliance. Athens gave up its Peloponnesian possessions, including Nisaea and Pegae, and Athens's control of its own alliance was accepted. It is a mistake to view the peace as merely a truce awaiting war. It was a genuine attempt to set rules for coexistence, and it lasted for about fifteen years. Its provisions, however, contained ambiguities that later events would test, particularly about neutral states and the treatment of allied disputes.

The rivalry between Athens and Sparta must not be treated as if only the two of them mattered. Corinth, a commercial power and the mother-city of many colonies, had major interests in the Adriatic and the Gulf of Corinth and viewed Athenian expansion in the west and in Megara with hostility. Megara, situated on the Isthmus, was a vulnerable prize between the two alliance systems. Aegina, a former maritime power, resented its subordination. Thebes dominated Boeotia and had its own quarrels with Athens and with its neighbor Plataea. These states pursued their own interests and sometimes pressed their alliance leaders toward positions the leaders might not have chosen.

In 440 to 439 BCE, the powerful island of Samos revolted against Athens in a dispute with Miletus, and Athens, led by Pericles, suppressed the revolt after a difficult nine-month siege. Thucydides records that some Peloponnesian states considered helping Samos, and that Corinth argued against intervention. The episode is important because it shows that the empire depended on the ability to suppress rebellion quickly and that the Peloponnesian League had not yet decided to fight to defend other states' independence from Athens.

Section 12.6

Why War Came in 431 BCE

Thucydides, in a famous passage, distinguishes between the causes publicly alleged by the two sides and what he considered the truest cause of the war, the growth of Athenian power and the fear that this inspired in Sparta. This formulation has become one of the most influential explanations of war in the Western tradition. But it is an interpretation, not a neutral report, and students should distinguish structural tensions from the sequence of particular events.

Historical Vocabulary
Prophasis and Aitia

Thucydides uses two Greek terms in discussing the origins of the war. Aitiai are the publicly stated grievances or complaints exchanged by the states before the war. Prophasis, a word with a complicated range of meaning, is the term he uses in the passage where he names the growth of Athenian power and the fear it aroused in the Spartans as the "truest" prophasis, though he also says it was the least openly discussed. Scholars have translated the word as "cause," "explanation," or "pretext," and interpret Thucydides's meaning in different ways.

The distinction matters because it shows Thucydides making an argument about the structure of causation, and because it invites a question the chapter poses throughout: can a structural explanation replace an account of the specific decisions of the states involved?

Long-term tensions. The growth of Athenian power after 479 BCE created a large, fleet-based empire in the Aegean that Sparta and its allies could not easily match at sea. The Peloponnesian League and the Athenian alliance operated in different spheres, and each had strong interests in defending its position. Sparta, an oligarchy that often supported oligarchies elsewhere, was uneasy at Athenian sponsorship of democracies in allied cities, though Athenian policy was not uniformly consistent on this point. Fear, the disputed distribution of influence, and the prestige of leadership all fed the rivalry. But these tensions had existed for many years without producing a general war.

Immediate disputes. The immediate chain of events began in the northwest. In 435 BCE, a quarrel over the city of Epidamnus, a Corcyrean colony on the Adriatic coast, brought Corinth and Corcyra, its own colony, into conflict. Corcyra, possessing a large fleet, appealed to Athens for an alliance. The Athenians, after a debate in which they heard both Corcyrean and Corinthian speakers, chose a defensive alliance with Corcyra, sending a small force. Thucydides says the Athenians recognized that a war with the Peloponnesians was coming and wished to keep Corcyra's fleet out of Corinthian hands. In 433 BCE, a Corinthian and Corcyrean fleet clashed at Sybota, in a battle in which Athenian ships took part, and Corinth regarded Athens as having broken the Thirty Years' Peace.

A second dispute concerned Potidaea, a Corinthian colony in the Chalcidice that was a tribute-paying member of the Athenian alliance. Athens, apprehensive that Potidaea would revolt with Corinthian help, ordered it to demolish the wall facing Pallene, provide hostages, and dismiss its Corinthian magistrates. Potidaea revolted in 432 BCE, with some backing from the Macedonian king Perdiccas and with Corinthian volunteers, and Athens besieged it. A third grievance concerned Megara, against which Athens passed measures, known as the Megarian Decree or decrees, excluding Megarians from the harbors of the Athenian empire and from the Athenian marketplace. The date, wording, and actual effect of the measures are uncertain, and Thucydides mentions them mainly as one of the Spartan allies' complaints. The idea that the decree alone caused the war, which is found in Aristophanes's comic treatment and in later writers, cannot be sustained; it appears to have been one grievance among several.

In 432 BCE, at a meeting of the Spartan assembly, Corinthian, Megarian, and other speakers complained of Athenian conduct, while Athenian envoys present in Sparta defended their empire. King Archidamus urged caution, warning that Sparta was unready for a war against a naval power with large reserves of money. The ephor Sthenelaidas argued for war, and the assembly voted that the treaty had been broken. Sparta consulted the oracle at Delphi, according to Thucydides, and was told that it would win if it fought with all its strength. A congress of allies then voted for war. Diplomatic efforts continued, including Spartan demands that Athens lift the siege of Potidaea, restore the independence of Aegina, and revoke the Megarian measures, and the Athenians, led by Pericles, refused to make concessions under threat.

The war opened in spring 431 BCE, with a surprise Theban attempt to seize Plataea, a small Boeotian city allied to Athens. The attack, which took place in a time of formal peace, made clear that the treaty system had collapsed. These events show why it is misleading to see the war as the mechanical result of one factor. Athenian growth mattered. So did Spartan anxiety, Corinthian interests, the vulnerability of neutral communities, the working of alliance obligations, and decisions made on both sides in 433 to 431 BCE. Thucydides provides the most influential ancient account, but his selection and emphasis reflect his own analytical judgment, and modern historians continue to debate how much weight to give the structural factor he named.

Historical Debate

When Did the Delian League Become an Empire?

The question: Historians and ancient writers describe the alliance of 478/7 BCE and the Athenian state of the 430s BCE with different vocabulary, "league," "alliance," "hegemony," and "empire." Was there a moment when one turned into the other, and if so, which?

Interpretation A — There were identifiable thresholds: Some scholars point to specific moments, such as Athens's forced retention of Naxos, the crushing of Thasos, the transfer of the treasury to Athens in 454, or the imposition of constitutional and judicial controls on allies in the 440s. On this view, a decisive step can be dated to the point at which an ally lost the right to leave, or at which Athens gained sole control of the money.
Interpretation B — The change was gradual and no single date is adequate: Other scholars emphasize incremental hardening. Athens acquired leadership through service and consent, accumulated power through the commutation of ships into money, and used coercion increasingly, with different allies experiencing different degrees of control. On this view, any single date imposes false precision on a process that was continuous and uneven, and the same events could appear as League business to Athenians and as subjection to allies.

Evidence: The debate rests on Thucydides's narrative, which shows a sequence of revolts and suppressions, and on inscriptions, including the tribute lists and decrees regulating individual allies. Both types of evidence have limits. Thucydides writes retrospectively and with an argument, while inscriptions preserve only part of the record and their dates are often uncertain. The later Athenian speakers in Thucydides, who freely call the empire a form of rule, complicate the question by showing that Athenians also spoke of it in different ways at different times.

Why it matters: The answer affects how one understands Athenian responsibility. If the change came at a specific time, one can ask whether it was deliberate policy. If it was gradual, it becomes harder to assign a single decision and easier to see the role of circumstance, allied choice, and the incentives of power.

Student task: Choose one proposed threshold and identify the evidence that would most strongly support treating it as the turning point. Then explain what evidence in this chapter would support the view that no single threshold exists.

Section 12.7

The Archidamian War, 431–421 BCE

The first ten years of the war are named after the Spartan king Archidamus, who led the initial invasions of Attica. The opposing strategies reflected the strengths of each side. Sparta and its allies possessed the stronger land army and planned repeated invasions of Attica, ravaging crops and farmland in the hope of provoking the Athenians to fight a decisive hoplite battle they would probably lose. Athens, by contrast, possessed the stronger navy and enormous financial reserves. Pericles's strategy, as Thucydides presents it, was to avoid a major land engagement, to bring the rural population inside the walls of Athens and the Long Walls, to rely on grain imported by sea, and to use the fleet to raid the Peloponnesian coast and maintain control of the empire. His strategy was not cowardice or a refusal to fight. It was a calculated attempt to exploit Athenian naval superiority and to exhaust Spartan willingness to continue, though it required Athenians to endure the sight of their farms burned and depended on the assumption that the empire would remain loyal.

The strategy had costs from the beginning. Attic farming families, who had lived in the countryside, crowded into the city, and the first Peloponnesian invasion in 431 BCE provoked anger among those who watched their land destroyed. In 430 BCE, plague struck. Thucydides, who says he suffered from it himself, described the symptoms in detail. The outbreak killed a large number of Athenians, including soldiers on campaign, and recurred in 427/6. He reports heavy losses among the hoplites and cavalry, and modern estimates of total mortality vary, with some scholars proposing that a quarter or more of the population may have died, though the evidence does not permit a secure figure. Modern attempts to identify the disease, including typhoid fever, typhus, and others, remain inconclusive, and the description in Thucydides does not permit a definitive diagnosis. Pericles was fined and removed from office in 430, restored soon afterwards, and died of the plague in 429 BCE.

The death of Pericles left leadership more contested. Among his successors was Cleon, a wealthy tanner's son and a forceful speaker whom Thucydides depicts unsympathetically and Aristophanes lampoons in comedy. Both authors were hostile witnesses, and modern scholars are wary of treating their portraits as complete. Cleon was one of the political figures who called for a more aggressive war, including in the Mytilene debate discussed in the next section. Nicias, a wealthy and cautious general, represented an alternative approach.

The war in the following years was widespread. Sparta besieged Plataea, which eventually surrendered in 427 BCE, with the men executed and the city destroyed, an episode driven in part by Theban interests. Athens raided the Peloponnese. The most dramatic turn came in 425 BCE, when the Athenian general Demosthenes fortified a headland at Pylos, on the Messenian coast, threatening to stir the helots to revolt. A Spartan force occupying the nearby island of Sphacteria was cut off. While it was still trapped, Sparta arranged a local truce and sent envoys to Athens to seek peace, but the Athenian assembly, with Cleon influential in the debate, demanded terms Sparta would not accept, and negotiations failed. Thucydides presents this as Athenian overconfidence, a judgment historians treat as his interpretation. The siege continued until Cleon and Demosthenes attacked the island, and about 292 hoplites, including some 120 Spartiates, surrendered to the Athenians. The surrender shocked the Greek world, since Spartans were not supposed to give up their arms.

Sparta then reacted with a new strategy under the commander Brasidas, who led a small force of helots and mercenaries to Thrace and the Chalcidice, where he persuaded a number of Athenian allies to revolt, promising them freedom. In 424/3 BCE, he captured Amphipolis, and the historian Thucydides, the Athenian general assigned to the region, arrived too late to prevent it and was exiled. His exile gave him unusual opportunities to observe both sides and shaped the History he had begun at the outbreak of the war. In 422 BCE, Cleon led an Athenian force to recover Amphipolis, and in the battle both Cleon and Brasidas were killed. Athens had also suffered a serious defeat at Delium in Boeotia in 424 BCE.

With both sides exhausted and the leading war advocates dead, Nicias negotiated the Peace of Nicias in 421 BCE, a treaty intended to last fifty years. It called for the restoration of most conquests, but Sparta's allies were divided. Boeotia, Corinth, Megara, and Elis refused to accept parts of it, and Athens retained Nisaea, and Sparta failed to secure the return of Amphipolis. Athens and Sparta concluded a separate defensive alliance. Argos, which had remained neutral during the war, formed a coalition of discontented Peloponnesian states with Athenian support, and in 418 BCE Sparta won a major victory over Argos and its allies at Mantinea, restoring its position in the Peloponnese. The peace thus did not resolve the underlying rivalry. The two sides remained formally at peace, but neither was secure.

Everyday Life and Social History

War behind the Walls

For ordinary Athenians, the first years of the war meant uprooting. Farming households from the demes of Attica moved into the city, sheltering in the spaces between the Long Walls, in small huts and temples, and in whatever accommodation they could find. Thucydides says many resented leaving their family shrines and local communities. The countryside itself was left to be ravaged each summer, and farmers looked out across the walls at smoke rising from their orchards and fields. Crowded conditions and summer heat provided conditions in which epidemic disease could spread rapidly.

The plague affected every part of life. Thucydides describes breakdown in funeral customs, as families were unable to perform proper rites, and a decline in respect for law and religion as people despaired of both divine and human justice. These are his observations, and although he wrote them with rhetorical purpose, they show that contemporaries experienced the epidemic as a moral and religious as well as biological disaster. Public and private religious life was affected, and the traditional gods failed to protect the city. The plague also removed many soldiers and rowers and weakened the Athenian ability to conduct campaigns.

The war also imposed heavy costs on those who served. Rowers included poorer citizens, metics, hired foreigners, and enslaved men, in proportions that varied over time and remain debated, and they manned the fleet, hoplites provided the land army, and property owners paid special war taxes. The war also affected enslaved people. In the later years, when the Spartans fortified Decelea in Attica in 413 BCE, Thucydides reports that more than twenty thousand slaves, many of them skilled workers, deserted, a striking measure of the war's effect on Athens's labor system. Women's experiences are poorly documented in the literary sources, but they bore the burdens of grief, of maintaining households in the absence of men, and of participating in mourning and religious ritual.

Section 12.8

War, Empire, and Moral Choice

Thucydides devoted some of his most powerful passages to episodes in which Athens used its power against other Greek communities. Two of these, Mytilene and Melos, are often paired, but they differ substantially and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Mytilene, on the island of Lesbos, was one of the few remaining allies that provided ships and retained relative independence. In 428 BCE, its oligarchic leadership revolted against Athens, with Spartan support promised but late in coming. Athens besieged the city, and when it surrendered in 427 BCE, the Athenian assembly, on the proposal of Cleon, voted to execute all the adult male Mytileneans and enslave the women and children. On the next day, some Athenians had second thoughts, and a second debate was held. Thucydides presents speeches by Cleon, who argued that the empire was a tyranny sustained by fear and that mercy would be mistaken for weakness, and by Diodotus, who argued that harshness would not deter revolt, since desperate rebels would have no incentive to surrender, and that the demos of allied cities was a useful ally. The assembly reversed its decision by a narrow margin. A second trireme was sent to overtake the first and arrived just in time. The Athenians executed only those judged most responsible, a little over a thousand men according to Thucydides, and pulled down the walls, took over the fleet, and settled cleruchs on Lesbos.

Melos was a different case. It was a small island in the southern Aegean, a Spartan colony that was never a member of the Athenian alliance. It had initially remained neutral, but earlier Athenian coercion, including an Athenian attack and ravaging of its territory in 426 BCE, had produced open hostility by 416 BCE, when Athens attacked again. Thucydides presents a dialogue between Athenian envoys and the Melian magistrates in which the Athenians argue that questions of justice arise only between equals, that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must, and that the Melians' hope of Spartan help is illusory, while the Melians appeal to justice, to the gods, and to Spartan aid. The Melians refused to submit, Athens besieged the town, and after it fell, the men were killed and the women and children enslaved, and Athens sent colonists. That much is documented by later sources as well as Thucydides.

Both episodes must be read with care. Thucydides himself explains that he could not report speeches word for word and that he made speakers say what he thought the situation required, keeping as close as possible to the general sense of what was actually said. The Mytilenean debate and the Melian Dialogue are therefore literary reconstructions. The Melian Dialogue in particular, which is presented in the form of a dramatic exchange different from the other speeches in the history, should not be treated as a diplomatic transcript or as Athens's official foreign-policy doctrine. It is Thucydides's presentation of a confrontation and should be read as part of his broader reflection on power, justice, and the self-deception that accompanies imperial ambition. It appears shortly before the Sicilian Expedition, and many readers have seen a deliberate literary connection between Athenian arrogance at Melos and the disaster that followed, though Thucydides does not draw that link explicitly.

Historically, the episodes illustrate different facets of the empire. Mytilene shows how Athens punished an ally that revolted and how the assembly debated the limits of punishment. Melos shows how Athens dealt with a small island outside its alliance that refused to submit. The two together show that the empire's coercive character was not exceptional but a recurring feature of Athenian policy, that Athenians debated it, and that the outcomes depended on particular political circumstances.

Primary Source Window

Thucydides on the Mytilenean Debate, and the Athenian Tribute Lists

Creators: Thucydides, Athenian historian; the Athenian state, through the boards that inscribed the tribute quota lists · Dates: the History was composed in the last decades of the fifth century BCE; the lists begin with the year 454/3 BCE · Types: Narrative history with reconstructed speeches; inscribed public records on stone (epigraphic evidence)

Context: Book 3 of Thucydides's History describes the debate at Athens in 427 BCE over the punishment of Mytilene. The tribute quota lists, by contrast, are lists of allied communities and the small portions of their tribute dedicated to Athena, inscribed on stone on the Acropolis. Both bear on the question of how Athens governed its empire, but they are different sorts of evidence.

Paraphrase of Thucydides, History, Book 3 Thucydides has Cleon argue that Athens's empire is a tyranny over unwilling subjects who obey out of fear and not goodwill, and that reversing a decision only shows weakness. He has Diodotus reply that the question is one of advantage, not justice, that harsh penalties do not deter people who believe they may succeed, and that killing the whole population of a rebel city would drive future rebels to fight to the end. This is a paraphrase of the argument, not a translation, and the speeches themselves are Thucydides's compositions.

Description of the tribute lists The inscribed quota lists record, year by year, the names of allied communities grouped by region, with the one sixtieth portion of their tribute that Athens dedicated to Athena. They show which communities were paying, in which years, and in what amounts. They do not explain why communities paid, what they thought of paying, or how Athenians justified the system.

Analysis: Read together, these two sources complement each other. Thucydides offers a sophisticated argument about Athenian imperial psychology and policy, set in a debate whose essential outline, including the two assembly votes and the narrow reversal, is likely to reflect events even if the speeches are not verbatim. The inscriptions, by contrast, offer administrative evidence that Athens actually collected tribute from many communities across the Aegean, and they allow historians to test the narrative against records produced by the imperial administration. Modern scholarship emphasizes the value of combining the two, since neither alone captures the whole system. Both sources come from Athens or the Athenian point of view. The voices of allied citizens, of the Mytileneans who were nearly massacred, and of the enslaved and unfree are largely absent.

Section 12.9

The Sicilian Expedition

The Sicilian Expedition of 415 to 413 BCE is the greatest strategic catastrophe of the war, but it should not be treated as obviously doomed from the outset. To understand it, students should first understand why Athenians thought it might succeed. Sicily was one of the wealthiest regions of the Greek world and was rich in grain. It was politically fragmented, containing many independent Greek, indigenous, and Punic communities, and Syracuse, a Dorian colony of Corinth, was its largest and most powerful Greek city, with ties to Sparta's allies that might grow. Athens had intervened in Sicily earlier, in the 420s, and had ties with some Sicilian cities. Athenians were confident after their achievements, and the peace with Sparta gave them resources and time. In 416/5 BCE, the city of Segesta, in western Sicily, appealed to Athens for help against Selinus, which was backed by Syracuse, and claimed to have great wealth to fund a campaign.

The debate in the assembly was divided. Nicias opposed the expedition, warning that Athens still had enemies at home and that conquering Sicily would be difficult. Alcibiades, a young, wealthy, and charismatic aristocrat, related to Pericles and a former pupil of Socrates, argued for it, promising that Athens could extend its power in the west and that the risk was small. Nicias tried to discourage the assembly by asking for a very large force, but the assembly, contrary to his hopes, voted for that force, and Nicias, Alcibiades, and Lamachus were appointed as generals. According to Thucydides, the fleet that sailed in summer 415 BCE, with 134 triremes and thousands of soldiers, was the most costly and most magnificent force that had ever left a single Greek city. Thucydides also emphasizes the emotional intensity of the departure.

Just before sailing, one night, the stone herms, protective statues of Hermes with a head and phallus, were mutilated across Athens. The act, whose perpetrators and motives are unknown, was treated as an omen and as a possible conspiracy against the democracy. Alcibiades, already accused of profaning the Eleusinian Mysteries, was implicated. He sailed with the fleet but was recalled to stand trial and, fearing conviction, escaped and defected to Sparta. Historians disagree about the extent to which these prosecutions were political persecution, religious panic, or a genuine response to a threatening conspiracy, and Thucydides himself acknowledges that the truth was never established. The point that matters here is that the expedition lost one of its most energetic commanders before it had achieved any of its principal strategic objectives.

The campaign went badly. With Alcibiades gone, Nicias and Lamachus remained in command. Lamachus pursued an energetic strategy but was killed in fighting near Syracuse in 414 BCE, and thereafter Nicias was the sole surviving original commander. Nicias hesitated. Athens won an early battle near Syracuse, but the Athenians failed to exploit it, and Syracuse prepared its defenses. In 414 BCE, Athens began to build a wall to enclose the city, but Alcibiades, in Sparta, urged the Spartans to send help and to fortify Decelea in Attica, and the Spartan commander Gylippus arrived at Syracuse and helped its defenders rebuild. Nicias, in poor health, asked to be recalled, but the Athenians instead sent reinforcements under Demosthenes in 413 BCE. After an unsuccessful night attack, Demosthenes advised withdrawal, but Nicias hesitated, and when an eclipse of the moon occurred in August, he delayed again on religious advice. The Syracusans blocked the harbor and defeated the Athenian fleet, and the Athenian land army, in retreat, was destroyed. Nicias and Demosthenes were executed, and the survivors, in the thousands, were imprisoned in the stone quarries of Syracuse, where many died.

Thucydides describes the result as the greatest disaster of the war, the loss of an army and a fleet. The blame is often assigned to individual failings of Alcibiades, Nicias, or the assembly, but the disaster had several causes: the difficulty of the objective, the division of command, Athens's initial underestimation of Syracuse's strength, the arrival of Spartan help, the availability of harbors, and the decision not to withdraw earlier. Alcibiades should not be turned into a single explanation, since much of the expedition's failure took place after he had left. Nor was the campaign the product of unmanageable folly. It was a gamble with real prospects, undertaken by a democracy that had shown it could be cautious and bold by turns. For the wider Mediterranean, the expedition drew many Greek communities of Sicily and southern Italy into the conflict and demonstrated how far Athenian strategy had extended beyond the Aegean.

Section 12.10

Political Crisis and the War in the Aegean

The defeat in Sicily was a shock, but Athens did not collapse. It had the resources of the empire, and it responded with emergency measures, including a board of older officials (probouloi) to advise on policy and a change in the imperial revenue system: in 413 BCE, according to Thucydides, Athens replaced tribute with a five percent tax on seaborne trade in allied ports, in the hope of raising more. The empire, however, was now under severe pressure. Sparta had fortified Decelea and could plunder Attica throughout the year, and the flight of enslaved workers, including many from the Laurion mines, damaged the silver economy. Casualties in Sicily had reduced Athenian manpower.

The war now shifted to the Aegean. Many allied communities in Ionia and elsewhere saw an opportunity to revolt, and the Persian governors, or satraps, Tissaphernes in Sardis and Pharnabazus in the Hellespontine region, sought Spartan help to recover tribute owed to the King. From 412 BCE, treaties between Sparta and Persia, negotiated in stages, promised Persian financial support for the Peloponnesian fleet in return for Spartan acknowledgment of Persian claims in Asia Minor. The terms were disputed in later years and contradictory, but the effect was to make Persia a decisive participant. Thucydides is critical of Spartan willingness to abandon Greek cities in Asia to the King. The arrangement exposed a striking contradiction in Spartan liberation rhetoric: the coalition challenging Athenian rule now depended increasingly on Persian resources and accepted Persian claims in Asia Minor. Persian officials, acting from changing imperial interests, exploited the divisions of the Greek world, and Persia had not disappeared from Greek affairs after 479 BCE.

In 411 BCE, a group of Athenian oligarchs, led by Peisander, Antiphon, and, more ambiguously, Theramenes, exploited war weariness and the promise of Persian support, which Alcibiades, now seeking to return from exile, said would follow a change of constitution. Through intimidation and persuasion, they persuaded the assembly to dissolve the democratic council and establish a Council of Four Hundred. The new regime claimed that political participation would extend to a body of Five Thousand citizens able to serve with person and property, but this body was not effectively constituted under the Four Hundred. In practice, the Four Hundred ruled violently, and they ended pay for office, killed opponents, and tried to make peace with Sparta. At Samos, the Athenian fleet, made up largely of rowers, refused to accept the coup, swore to defend the democracy, and appointed its own generals. Within months, the Four Hundred fell, Theramenes helped to overthrow them, and a broader regime, the Five Thousand, which Thucydides praised as a moderate mixture of oligarchy and democracy, governed briefly. By 410 BCE, after Athenian naval victories at Cynossema and Cyzicus, full democracy had returned.

The oligarchic coup should not be explained simply as democracy failing because Athens was losing. Athenian democracy had faced military defeats before without collapsing. The coup succeeded because of a specific combination: financial exhaustion, fear of Persian support for Sparta, the influence of aristocratic clubs (hetaireiai) which had long existed, lingering political distrust after the religious and political crisis of 415, a connection emphasized by some historians, and skillful manipulation by a small group of men. The fleet's resistance shows that democracy also retained committed defenders, especially among the naval crews whose social position it had helped elevate. The episode demonstrates that the war's pressures produced political fragmentation but that the outcome was not predetermined. After the restoration, the assembly adopted the decree of Demophantos in 410 BCE, which required citizens to swear to defend the democracy and kill anyone who overthrew it.

The war continued in the Hellespont, where the Athenians won a series of victories that helped secure the grain route from the Black Sea, on which Athens depended. Alcibiades, recalled by the fleet in 411 and formally restored in 407 BCE, returned to Athens to a triumphant reception. His role, however, remained controversial and contingent, and when an Athenian squadron was defeated by Lysander's fleet at Notium in 406 BCE, while Alcibiades was absent from the station, he lost the generalship again and went into exile.

Section 12.11

Defeat: Arginusae, Aegospotami, and 404 BCE

The final phase of the war turned on ships and money. In 407 BCE, Sparta appointed Lysander as admiral, and Cyrus the Younger, son of the Persian king Darius II, arrived in western Asia Minor as commander with authority over the satrapies and a personal interest in supporting Sparta. Lysander cultivated Cyrus, obtained funds to pay Spartan crews at rates that attracted rowers from Athenian ships, and organized supporters in allied cities into political clubs favorable to Sparta. This allowed Sparta to develop the naval and financial means to challenge Athens. It is misleading to say that Sparta won solely through the superiority of its hoplites. What defeated Athens was Sparta's acquisition of a navy, backed by Persian money.

Athens made a last major effort. In 406 BCE, after Athens mobilized a large fleet, drawing on an unusually broad pool of rowers, including citizens and noncitizens, and promising freedom to enslaved men who served in the emergency fleet, it defeated the Spartans at Arginusae, near Lesbos. A storm, however, prevented the rescue of many shipwrecked Athenian sailors and the collection of the dead, and when the generals returned, the assembly, in a heated atmosphere, tried them together, in violation of normal procedure, and executed six of the eight. The episode, later recognized by many Athenians as a grievous injustice, illustrates both the passions of wartime politics and the fragile relationship between the democracy and its commanders. It also cost Athens experienced leaders at a moment when it desperately needed them. The Spartans, who had offered peace after Arginusae, according to some ancient sources, were rebuffed by the assembly.

In 405 BCE, Lysander, reappointed to command by a device that made him nominal deputy of another admiral, moved to the Hellespont, captured Lampsacus, and threatened the Athenian grain route. The Athenian fleet, encamped at Aegospotami, a beach on the opposite side of the strait, was caught off guard. After several days of Athenian challenges, Lysander attacked when the crews had disembarked, and captured most of the ships almost without a fight. He executed thousands of Athenian prisoners. Xenophon is a key source, though he was writing later and from a particular perspective, and the details of the Athenian negligence and possible treachery among the generals remain debated.

Athens was now cut off from its grain supply and from its empire, which quickly fell to Lysander's forces as he installed friendly governments, often with Spartan garrisons and harmosts, or governors, in allied cities. Spartan forces besieged Athens by land and sea, and by spring 404 BCE the city, suffering from famine, agreed to terms. According to Xenophon, the terms included the demolition of the Long Walls and the fortifications of the Piraeus, the surrender of most of the fleet, the recall of exiles, and the acceptance of Spartan leadership. Corinth and Thebes are said to have proposed the complete destruction of Athens and the enslavement of its people, but Sparta refused, recalling Athens's service in the Persian Wars. The walls were demolished, Xenophon says, to the music of flute-girls, as Greeks celebrated what they considered the beginning of freedom. That description reflects a Spartan-friendly, Greek-liberation rhetoric more than a neutral account.

The war therefore ended when Sparta acquired the means to destroy Athens's maritime system, and not simply because it defeated Athens on land. The transformation was possible because of Persian resources, because the empire's allies switched sides, and because Athenian decision-making, under stress, sometimes made costly errors. It was not the result of one battle or one commander. It also created a new problem: Sparta and its allies had defeated a hegemon and now faced the question of what to put in its place.

Section 12.12

The Thirty, Democratic Restoration, and the Meaning of the War

The end of the war brought a second breakdown of democracy. Under Spartan pressure, the Athenian assembly in 404 BCE appointed a commission of thirty men to draft laws and govern the city. The Thirty, led by the intellectual and politician Critias, a relative of Plato, and initially including the moderate Theramenes, quickly established a regime backed by a Spartan garrison. They executed or drove into exile large numbers of their opponents, confiscated property, disarmed most citizens, and created a privileged list of three thousand. Theramenes, who objected to the extremes, was condemned and forced to drink hemlock. The Thirty were not simply a normal constitutional alternative to democracy. Ancient sources, including Xenophon, the orator Lysias, and the Constitution of the Athenians, differ in detail, but agree on the regime's violence. Historians today often treat it as a violent, Spartan-imposed government supported by a minority of Athenians. The number of victims is disputed, and the ancient claims, such as that of 1,500 killed, are figures from partisan sources.

Resistance began at the border. Thrasybulus, an Athenian exile, seized the fortress of Phyle in the winter of 404/3 BCE and, gathering strength, occupied the Piraeus. In a battle at Munychia, Critias was killed. The Thirty were deposed and replaced by a moderate board of Ten, and the oligarchs withdrew to Eleusis. Sparta, initially supporting the oligarchs, eventually sent the king Pausanias, who mediated a settlement. In 403 BCE, democracy was restored at Athens, and the city adopted an amnesty, covering most of those involved in the earlier regime, except for the leaders of the Thirty and a few others, and requiring that no one be prosecuted for past acts. The oligarchs at Eleusis were later reincorporated. Modern scholars highlight the restoration as remarkable, since it was carried out through negotiated reconciliation rather than mass retribution. The democracy also carried out a revision of laws and the introduction of new legislative procedures in the years that followed. Recent scholarship emphasizes repeated democratic breakdown and recovery during the war rather than a single uninterrupted democracy ending in 404 BCE.

The chapter's central problem can now be answered. The alliance created to continue the war against Persia became an Athenian empire through a gradual accumulation of practices that increased Athenian control while reducing allied autonomy. Democracy at Athens developed in parallel, helped by the resources of empire and the mobilization of the fleet, but not caused by it or causing it. The struggle between Athens, Sparta, and their allies emerged from long-term structural tension, immediate disputes involving Corcyra, Potidaea, and Megara, and the interests of states like Corinth. The war transformed the Greek world by destroying the Athenian empire, weakening Athens's fleet, walls, and dominance, devastating populations, and eroding norms of conduct among Greek states.

Athens lost its empire, its fleet supremacy, its fortifications, and its position as the dominant Aegean power. But Athenian democracy survived the crisis in reconstructed form. Sparta had won the war, but victory did not lead to stable leadership. It faced the same problem Athens had faced before: how to turn military success into sustainable leadership over fiercely independent poleis. Sparta imposed narrow governments and garrisons, and it quickly fell out with its former allies, Corinth and Thebes among them, and with Persia. Chapter 13 turns from politics to the cultural life of the Greek world, including the religion, drama, philosophy, literature, and art that flourished in these decades, before the Volume moves to Rome in Part III. The fourth-century Greek world, marked by shifting hegemonies and eventually by the rise of Macedon, grew out of the unsettled aftermath examined here.

Multiple Perspectives

Living under Athenian Power

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across sources. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and the groups did not possess equal power.

From the perspective of a poor Athenian citizen who rowed in the fleet: Naval service provided pay, a role in the defense of the city, and a voice in the assembly. Naval service made poorer citizens militarily important, a development historians have often linked to their political importance, though the strength and direction of that relationship remain debated. The risks of service, especially in Sicily, were severe.
From the perspective of a citizen of an allied city such as Mytilene or Thasos: Tribute, garrisons, cleruchs, and Athenian legal oversight limited the community's autonomy, and revolt risked siege and severe penalties. Within allied cities, however, opinion was divided, and some democratic groups looked to Athens for support against local oligarchs, while oligarchs looked to Sparta.
From the perspective of an enslaved worker or metic in Attica: Enslaved people worked in mines, workshops, and households and had no political rights, and metics paid special taxes and served in the fleet without citizenship. The war offered some, such as the slaves who deserted to Decelea or enslaved men promised or later rewarded with freedom for service in 406, unusual opportunities, but the system as a whole depended on their exclusion.
From the perspective of a Spartan or a Peloponnesian ally: Athenian power appeared threatening, and Sparta's allies, especially Corinth, claimed that Athens enslaved Greek cities. Sparta itself feared the vulnerability of its helot system and hesitated to fight at a distance, while its later dependence on Persian money exposed contradictions in its claim to liberate Greeks.
Cause and Effect

The Road to the Peloponnesian War

Long-term causes

The Persian Wars and the formation of the Delian League; the growth of Athenian naval power and its imperial practices; Sparta's leadership of the Peloponnesian League and its dependence on its own security at home; competing alliance systems and commercial interests, notably those of Corinth.

Short-term causes

The disputes over Epidamnus and Corcyra, Athens's alliance with Corcyra, the battle of Sybota, the revolt of Potidaea, the measures against Megara, Corinthian pressure on Sparta, and the Spartan vote of 432.

Event and immediate effects

The war began in 431 BCE. It brought invasions of Attica, the plague, the deaths of Pericles and other leaders, the revolt of allies, and brutal treatment of defeated communities.

Long-term consequences

The destruction of the Athenian empire, the temporary end of Athenian democracy and its reconstruction, Spartan hegemony without stability, Persian involvement in Greek affairs, and the exhaustion of Greek states.

Historical complexity: This chain is not inevitable. Athens and Sparta had lived at peace for fifteen years, and the war might have been avoided by different choices at Corcyra, Potidaea, or Sparta. Thucydides's structural explanation captures something important, but the outcome depended on contingent decisions, on Corinthian pressure, and on the interests of many actors.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Athenian Democracy from Cleisthenes to 403 BCE

What Changed

  • The Areopagus lost most of its broader political and supervisory powers after Ephialtes, while retaining important judicial and religious functions, and authority shifted toward the assembly, council, and courts
  • Pay for service widened the practical scope of participation
  • The citizenship law of 451/0 narrowed the citizen body
  • The war produced two oligarchic interruptions, in 411 and 404 to 403, and formal safeguards, such as the decree of Demophantos, after the first
  • Restoration in 403 was accompanied by amnesty and legal revision

What Stayed the Same

  • Adult male citizens remained the only participants in the political process
  • Sortition, a large council, and popular courts remained the core of the system
  • Wealthy families continued to supply many leading generals and speakers
  • Women, metics, and enslaved people remained excluded

Who experienced change most directly? Poorer citizens gained the most from the expansion of paid service and participation, while allied communities, Athenian oligarchs, and the victims of the Thirty experienced political change as loss or violence. Women and enslaved people experienced comparatively greater continuity in their exclusion. Why the pattern matters: The record shows that democracy was neither static nor linear, and that it was capable of both radicalization and recovery.

Chapter Summary

This chapter asked how an alliance created to continue the war against Persia became an Athenian empire, and why the resulting struggle for power between Athens, Sparta, and their allies culminated in a war that transformed the Greek world. It began in 478/7 BCE with the formation of the Delian League after Spartan withdrawal from leadership, and it emphasized that the League did not begin as an openly declared Athenian empire.

The transformation was gradual. Tribute replaced ships for many members, Athens suppressed Naxos and Thasos, moved the treasury to Athens in 454, and established cleruchies, garrisons, officials, and judicial controls. Historians disagree about whether any single moment marks the change, and the evidence, drawn from Thucydides and inscriptions such as the tribute lists, shows steadily increasing coercion.

Within Athens, democracy deepened. The reforms associated with Ephialtes shifted power from the Areopagus to the assembly, council, and popular courts, and sortition and pay widened participation. Pericles was a leading figure but not a ruler, and the citizenship law of 451/0 narrowed the citizen body. Women, metics, and enslaved people remained outside political life.

The relationship between democracy and empire was real but contingent. Empire supplied substantial resources, and historians have often linked naval service to the increasing political importance of poorer citizens, though the precise causal relationship remains debated, and democracy predated the empire, and neither caused the other. The chapter's paradox is that Athens expanded participation among its citizens while restricting the autonomy of many allies.

Relations with Sparta oscillated between cooperation and conflict. The helot revolt and dismissal of Cimon's force, the First Peloponnesian War, and the Thirty Years' Peace of 446/5 show an unstable but not predetermined rivalry, involving Corinth, Megara, Aegina, Thebes, and others with their own interests.

War came in 431 BCE through the interaction of structural tension and immediate disputes over Corcyra, Potidaea, and Megara. Thucydides's account of Athenian growth and Spartan fear is an influential interpretation, not the last word, and the Megarian measures alone did not cause the war.

The Archidamian War pitted Spartan land power against Athenian naval power. The plague, the death of Pericles, Pylos, Brasidas, and Amphipolis shaped the first phase, which ended in the Peace of Nicias without resolving the rivalry. Mytilene and Melos, read through Thucydides's reconstructed speeches, illustrate different facets of imperial coercion.

The Sicilian Expedition was a gamble that seemed plausible when approved and became a catastrophe through divided command, Spartan intervention, and delay. It was followed by allied revolts, Persian financial support for Sparta, and the oligarchic coup of 411, which the fleet at Samos and later events reversed.

Sparta won the war when it acquired a navy and Persian money under Lysander. Arginusae, the execution of the generals, and Aegospotami led to the surrender of Athens in 404, the loss of empire, fleet, and walls, and the violent regime of the Thirty.

Democracy returned in 403 through resistance and negotiated amnesty, and Athenian democracy survived in reconstructed form. Sparta's victory produced no durable settlement of the Greek world, which sets the stage for the fourth-century Greek world and for Chapter 13's turn to Greek religion, philosophy, literature, and art.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how power, participation, and rivalry interacted in one of the best-documented societies of antiquity. Athens demonstrates that broad citizen participation could coexist with coercive rule over others, and that both could be sources of strength and vulnerability. It also demonstrates how historians must weigh a brilliant but interpretive narrative against inscriptions and other evidence.

The chapter also shows the connections between the Greek city-states and the wider world. Persian money helped decide the war, Sicily linked the Aegean to the western Mediterranean, and the Black Sea grain route tied Athenian survival to distant regions. The Greek world was never a closed system, and the pattern of the earlier chapters of this Volume, in which Greek and non-Greek societies interacted throughout the Mediterranean, continues here.

Later developments were shaped by these events without being determined by them. The fall of the Athenian empire and the unstable Spartan settlement opened the way for the shifting hegemonies of the fourth century. The debates over democracy, empire, and justice continued to be discussed by later Greek and Roman writers, and Thucydides became one of the most read historians of power in later European thought, a reception that requires the same careful reading that the chapter recommends.

For the argument of Volume I, the chapter closes the political story of classical Greece. The Greek world had produced a democracy, an empire, and a devastating war between competing systems, and the questions of how to build durable political order among independent communities would reappear in Rome, whose own expansion is the subject of Part III. Chapter 13 now turns to the religion, drama, philosophy, literature, and art through which the Greek world understood itself in these same decades.
