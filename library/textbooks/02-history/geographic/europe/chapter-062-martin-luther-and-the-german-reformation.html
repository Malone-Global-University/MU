Martin Luther and the German Reformation

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

A university professor in a small Saxon town wrote a letter to an archbishop, enclosed a list of propositions for scholarly debate, and set in motion a controversy that neither he nor the archbishop could control. Within four years the professor stood before the Holy Roman Emperor and a full imperial assembly; within four decades his teaching had been built into the laws, churches, schools, and governments of dozens of German territories and cities; and in 1555 the Empire's own constitution acknowledged that western Christendom was no longer a single religious body.

The surviving evidence allows historians to begin with a precise date and a precise act. On or about 31 October 1517, Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar and professor of biblical theology at the young University of Wittenberg, wrote to Albrecht (Albert) of Brandenburg, Archbishop of Mainz, objecting to the way an indulgence was being preached in territories close to Saxony. With the letter he sent a Latin list of ninety-five theses on the power of indulgences. The letter survives, and the theses were soon printed. What does not survive, in any contemporary record, is a description of the famous scene in which Luther hammers the document to the door of the Castle Church. That story is attested only later, and historians remain divided over whether it happened. Beginning the chapter with the letter rather than the hammer is a small decision, but it points to the chapter's method: the Reformation must be reconstructed from evidence, not inherited as legend.

The dispute Luther opened was not, at first, a plan to found a new church. It was a scholarly and pastoral objection about repentance, forgiveness, and the proper limits of papal authority, voiced by a man trained in the language and procedures of late medieval theology. What turned it into a Reformation was the interaction of several forces that no single actor controlled. Luther's own thinking moved quickly, from criticism of an abuse to a reinterpretation of how sinners are made right with God. Printers in Wittenberg, Leipzig, Nuremberg, Augsburg, Basel, and elsewhere discovered that his writings sold. Preachers, town councillors, nobles, and ordinary readers took up his language and applied it to their own grievances. A protective prince, Frederick the Wise of Saxony, declined to hand him over. And an emperor, Charles V, who ruled an enormous inheritance and faced wars on several fronts, could not simply crush the movement.

This chapter treats the German Reformation as the product of that interaction. It first examines the Empire's political setting and Luther's formation, then the indulgence controversy and the widening conflict of 1518 and 1519. It analyzes the great writings of 1520, the trial at Worms, the German Bible, and the structure of Lutheran theology. It then follows the movement into towns, households, schools, and villages, including the violent crisis of the Peasants' War, and examines the creation of territorial churches, the politics of the imperial diets, the Augsburg Confession, the Schmalkaldic League, the war of 1546 and 1547, and finally the Peace of Augsburg of 1555. Throughout, it asks how a religious controversy became part of the constitutional order of the Holy Roman Empire.

Several caution flags apply. First, the movement should not be reduced to Luther's biography; Chapter 62 treats him as a central actor within printers' workshops, princely courts, city councils, village assemblies, and imperial politics, all of which shaped what "Lutheran" came to mean. Second, the Reformation was not a single event or a uniform process. It reached different places at different speeds and in different forms, and many communities remained Catholic. Third, Luther's theology must be described on its own terms. It was not a modern doctrine of unrestricted private judgment, and it was not simply a protest against corruption. Fourth, the chapter must confront Luther's later writings against Jews, which are part of the historical record and part of the movement's legacy, and which are examined critically in Section 62.19.

The chapter draws on a distinctive body of evidence: Luther's own letters, sermons, and treatises; printed pamphlets and woodcuts; city council minutes; princely correspondence; church visitation records; records of the imperial diets; the texts of peasant articles, confessions, and imperial recesses; and visual sources such as the portraits and illustrations produced in the workshop of Lucas Cranach the Elder. Each has strengths and distortions. Printed polemic reveals what writers wanted audiences to believe, not necessarily what audiences believed. Later recollections, including Luther's own and the table talk recorded by his students, are valuable but retrospective. The chapter therefore distinguishes contemporary evidence from later tradition wherever the difference matters.

Within Part X, Chapter 61 established the institutional, devotional, and intellectual conditions of the late medieval church that made Luther's challenge intelligible. Chapter 62 explains how that challenge became a durable reform movement. Chapter 63 follows the Reformed and radical movements that diverged from Lutheranism, Chapter 64 the separate English transformation, Chapter 65 Catholic renewal and the Council of Trent, and Chapters 66 and 67 the confessional wars that followed. Within Volume III, which examines Renaissance, Reformation, empires, and states, this chapter shows one pathway by which religious conflict and state formation became intertwined, a theme that carries through the dynastic and constitutional histories of Part XII.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political structure of the Holy Roman Empire around 1500 and why it shaped both the possibilities and the limits of religious reform.
  2. Trace Luther's intellectual development and avoid reducing it to a single dated conversion experience.
  3. Explain what indulgences were, why they became controversial in 1517, and how the debate over them developed into a dispute about authority.
  4. Distinguish documented evidence about the Ninety-Five Theses from the later tradition of the church door.
  5. Analyze the principal Lutheran doctrines, including justification by faith, scriptural authority, law and gospel, and the priesthood of all believers, and distinguish them from simplified modern descriptions.
  6. Assess how printing, preaching, translation, song, and image spread reform, and why oral and visual communication mattered alongside print.
  7. Compare the responses of princes, city councils, peasants, clergy, women, and imperial authorities to Lutheran reform.
  8. Interpret the Twelve Articles and the Peasants' War as social and religious events, distinguishing peasants' demands from Luther's own goals.
  9. Evaluate Luther's anti-Jewish writings in their historical context and as part of his legacy.
  10. Connect the Diets of Speyer, the Augsburg Confession, the Schmalkaldic League, and the Schmalkaldic War as stages in the legal and military consolidation of Lutheran churches.
  11. Evaluate the Peace of Augsburg as a political settlement, including what it permitted, whom it excluded, and why it did not end religious conflict.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a dispute about indulgences, a practice many contemporaries already criticized, produce a lasting division when earlier criticisms had not?
  2. How did Luther's understanding of salvation change the meaning of repentance, the sacraments, the priesthood, and the church?
  3. What roles did printing, preaching, song, and image play in spreading reform, and what limits did literacy and distance impose?
  4. Why did Luther survive when earlier reformers such as Jan Hus had not?
  5. Why did some princes and cities adopt reform while others remained Catholic, and how did motives of conviction, politics, and finance interact?
  6. How did the same religious language serve very different social aims, as in the Peasants' War?
  7. How did the constitutional structure of the Empire turn a religious dispute into a question of territorial law?
  8. What did the Peace of Augsburg settle, what did it leave unresolved, and why should historians avoid reading it as a charter of religious freedom?

Historical Context

As examined in Chapter 61, the western church around 1500 was both a spiritual institution and a vast network of property, courts, offices, and revenues. Calls for reform were not new. Councils, humanists, preachers, and lay confraternities had demanded clerical discipline, better pastoral care, and relief from financial exactions, as Chapter 53 showed in connection with the Great Schism and conciliarism and Chapter 59 in connection with Erasmus and Christian humanism. Jan Hus, executed at Constance in 1415, had left a church in Bohemia that Rome tolerated uneasily. Luther's challenge therefore entered an environment full of criticism. What distinguished it was not simply the charge of abuse but the claim that the church's understanding of salvation had been wrongly constructed.

The setting was the Holy Roman Empire, whose political forms are explained more fully in Chapter 46. The Empire was a loose union of territories under an elected emperor, and since 1438 the emperors had been Habsburgs. In 1519 the crown passed from Maximilian I to his grandson Charles, already king of Spain and ruler of the Netherlands and the Habsburg lands, who became Charles V. His empire was global in scope, and the Reformation was only one of several demands on his attention; the dynastic scale of his rule is the subject of Chapter 75. The Reformation's survival depended heavily on Charles's absences, his wars with France, and the Ottoman advance in Hungary, as discussed in Chapter 79.

Technology and communication formed a second element of context. Printing with movable type had spread across the German lands since the mid fifteenth century, as Chapter 55 explained, and cities such as Nuremberg, Augsburg, Basel, and Strasbourg had substantial printing industries. Wittenberg was not among them in 1517; it acquired a significant press only as demand for Luther's works grew. Literacy remained limited, concentrated among clergy, officials, merchants, and artisans in towns, so the Reformation's reach depended on reading aloud, sermons, images, and song as well as books.

A note on dating is necessary. All dates in this chapter follow the Julian calendar, which was in use throughout the German lands until the Gregorian reform of 1582. Catholic territories adopted the Gregorian calendar in stages after that date; many Protestant territories of the Empire did not do so until 1700. The difference does not alter the sixteenth-century narrative, but students should know that contemporaries used the Julian reckoning.

Finally, the term "Reformation" itself requires care. Contemporaries spoke of the "gospel," of "evangelical" teaching, and of reform; the label "Lutheran" was first used by opponents and only gradually accepted. "Protestant" derives from the formal protest of 1529 examined in Section 62.16. The expression "the Reformation" as the name of a historical epoch is a later convention, and historians continue to debate how far the movements it covers should be treated as a single process. This chapter uses "evangelical" for the movement's own early self-description and "Lutheran" for the confessional tradition that took shape around Luther's and Melanchthon's teaching.

Section 62.1

The German Lands and the Holy Roman Empire around 1500

The Holy Roman Empire was not a nation-state and should not be read as an incomplete Germany. It was a composite political order whose emperor, elected by seven electors (the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne, the king of Bohemia, the Count Palatine of the Rhine, the Duke of Saxony, and the Margrave of Brandenburg), claimed an inherited universal dignity but depended on the cooperation of powerful subordinate authorities. These included secular princes, ecclesiastical princes such as prince-archbishops and prince-bishops who were both spiritual leaders and territorial rulers, imperial counts, imperial knights, and the so-called free and imperial cities, a group numbering somewhere between sixty and eighty depending on how one counts. The Empire also included regions where German was not the principal language, notably Bohemia, parts of Italy, and the Netherlands.

Political decisions of common concern were made at the Imperial Diet (Reichstag), which met in three colleges: electors, other princes and prelates, and the imperial cities. Reforms associated with the Diet of Worms of 1495 had established an Eternal Public Peace to restrain private warfare and created an Imperial Chamber Court (Reichskammergericht), tying the emperor and the estates into a shared legal framework. This framework would matter greatly after 1517, because religious disputes quickly became disputes about which authority could enforce what, and where. The Empire offered no single machinery capable of suppressing a movement quickly, and no simple mechanism for protecting one either.

Ecclesiastical and political jurisdictions overlapped in complicated ways. Bishops governed dioceses and, in many cases, territories; the church held substantial lands and revenues; canon law courts claimed jurisdiction over marriage, clergy, and many disputes among laypeople. Complaints about the cost and reach of Roman authority had been collected in lists of grievances presented by the German estates, known as the Gravamina of the German Nation, and the concordats negotiated between popes and German authorities in the fifteenth century had left unresolved questions about who controlled appointments and payments. Many laypeople also resented clerical exemptions from taxation and municipal law, a source of anticlerical feeling that reformers could harness but did not create.

German intellectual life added further tensions. Humanist scholars, discussed in Chapters 57 and 59, had developed new standards of philological criticism and a reforming interest in the Bible and the church fathers. The controversy over Johannes Reuchlin, a humanist jurist who in the 1510s opposed a campaign to confiscate and destroy Jewish books, divided German scholars, theologians, and the imperial court, and showed that arguments over learning, orthodoxy, and the treatment of Jews could already mobilize broad audiences. Beyond the Empire, the Utraquist church in Bohemia, which gave the chalice to the laity, stood as a long-standing reminder that reforming movements could survive within the empire's orbit.

This was therefore a political landscape in which reform was both possible and contentious. It was possible because authority was dispersed: a prince who protected a dissenting preacher faced no central police. It was contentious because the same dispersal meant that rulers jealously guarded their jurisdictions, bishops defended their rights, and cities worried about both. The Reformation succeeded where individual rulers or councils chose to support it, and failed where they did not.

Section 62.2

Martin Luther: Monastic Life, Wittenberg, and Theological Development

Martin Luther was born in 1483 in Eisleben, in the county of Mansfeld in Saxony, the son of Hans Luder, who had risen from peasant stock to a position in the region's copper mining and smelting economy. His father wanted him to study law. He attended school in Mansfeld, Magdeburg, and Eisenach, and in 1501 entered the University of Erfurt, where he received a master of arts in 1505. In July 1505, according to later accounts, he was caught in a violent thunderstorm near Stotternheim and vowed to become a monk; a few weeks later he entered the Augustinian Hermits at Erfurt. The storm story depends largely on Luther's own later recollections, and historians treat it as a significant memory of a decision rather than as a detailed record of its causes. Whatever the immediate trigger, the move reflected a serious concern with salvation widely shared in his culture.

Monastic life exposed Luther to the full discipline of late medieval spirituality: the liturgy of the hours, regular confession, ascetic practice, and intense scrutiny of the self. Ordained a priest in 1507, he was sent to study theology, and his superior, Johann von Staupitz, became his mentor and eventually his successor as professor of biblical theology at Wittenberg. In 1512 Luther received his doctorate in theology and took up the chair that required him to lecture on scripture. Between about 1513 and 1518 he lectured on the Psalms, Romans, Galatians, and Hebrews. These lectures, preserved in his own and his students' notes, show a mind engaged with the language of scripture, with Augustine, and with contemporary scholasticism, which he increasingly criticized as overconfident about human ability to prepare for grace.

Luther's central difficulty, as he later described it, concerned the "righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17. If God's righteousness meant the justice by which God condemns sinners, the gospel seemed to him a burden, not a promise. He later wrote that he came to read it as the righteousness God gives to the believer, so that a sinner is accounted righteous through trust in Christ and not through accumulated works. When and how this understanding arose is a genuine historiographical problem. The principal account comes from a preface Luther wrote in 1545, near the end of his life, and describes an experience often associated with a room in the Wittenberg Augustinian cloister (the "tower experience"). Historians disagree about whether the breakthrough happened as early as about 1514 or as late as about 1518 or 1519, and many describe a process of development in which different insights converged. This chapter therefore treats his theology as formed across years, not as the product of one dated moment.

Wittenberg itself was a small town of roughly two thousand inhabitants on the Elbe, the seat of the Saxon electors, and the University of Wittenberg, founded in 1502, was still new and in need of distinction. Frederick the Wise invested in it, in a relics collection housed in the Castle Church, and in the career of Lucas Cranach, who arrived as court painter in 1505. The relics collection and the associated indulgences were substantial and illustrate that Luther was not opposing a foreign practice but one embedded in his own prince's religious policy. Understanding how a local university, a local prince, and a local preacher came to be bound together helps explain why the controversy proved difficult to extinguish.

Section 62.3

The Indulgence Controversy and the Ninety-Five Theses, 1517

To understand the controversy, one must understand the sacrament of penance. Late medieval teaching held that a sinner who confessed to a priest, felt contrition, and accepted a penance received absolution from guilt and from eternal punishment, but still owed a debt of temporal punishment, which could be worked off on earth or in purgatory after death. An indulgence was a remission of some or all of that temporal punishment, granted by church authority drawing on the "treasury of merits" of Christ and the saints. Indulgences were not, in official doctrine, a purchase of forgiveness for sins, and theologians insisted that they required contrition and confession. In practice, however, indulgences were frequently preached with promises that blurred these distinctions, were linked to cash contributions, and could be applied on behalf of the dead, which gave them particular emotional force.

Historical Vocabulary
Indulgence

In late medieval Catholic teaching, an indulgence was a remission of the temporal punishment due for sins whose guilt had already been forgiven in confession, granted by church authority. It could be gained by prayer, pilgrimage, or charitable gifts and, from the fifteenth century, could be applied to souls in purgatory. It was not a license to sin and not, in doctrine, forgiveness for sins; modern descriptions of it as "buying forgiveness" reproduce Luther's critique and the worst preaching, not official teaching. It matters here because Luther's attack on the practice exposed disagreement about repentance, the church's treasury, and papal authority over purgatory.

The indulgence of 1517 was the product of an entanglement of finance and ambition. Pope Leo X had authorized an indulgence to support the rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica in Rome, a project begun under Julius II. Albert of Brandenburg, a young member of the Hohenzollern dynasty already archbishop of Magdeburg and administrator of the diocese of Halberstadt, sought in 1514 the archbishopric of Mainz as well, which carried an electoral vote. Holding several major sees at once required papal dispensations, and the fees were enormous. Albert borrowed heavily from the Fugger banking house of Augsburg. An arrangement allowed him to share the proceeds of the indulgence preached in his territories, with one portion going to Rome and the other to repay his debt. Historians agree on the outline of this arrangement, though they differ on the exact amounts and on how directly the preachers' instructions linked cash to salvation.

The Dominican preacher Johann Tetzel, appointed to preach the indulgence in the archdiocese of Magdeburg and neighboring territories, became the figure most associated with its excesses. Frederick the Wise forbade the sale in Electoral Saxony, partly to protect his own indulgence revenues and partly from doubts about the campaign, but Wittenberg residents crossed the border to buy it. Luther, hearing from parishioners who presented indulgence letters as if they ended the need for repentance, responded as a pastor and professor. On or about 31 October 1517, the eve of All Saints, he wrote to Albert, enclosed the Latin theses, and invited disputation.

Primary Source Window

Martin Luther, Ninety-Five Theses (Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences), 1517

Creator: Martin Luther, Augustinian friar and doctor of theology, Wittenberg · Date: 1517, sent with a letter to Albert of Brandenburg on or about 31 October · Type: A Latin academic disputation, soon printed in several cities and translated into German

Context: The theses were framed as propositions for scholarly debate, in the format universities used for testing arguments. They were written in Latin for theologians. Printers in Nuremberg, Leipzig, and Basel issued editions within weeks, and German translations soon circulated, so the document reached audiences Luther did not primarily address.

Paraphrase of the argument (not a translation) The opening theses argue that Christ's call to repentance means the whole of a Christian's life should be repentance, not merely a sacramental act. Later theses argue that the pope can remit only penalties he himself has imposed, that souls in purgatory are not under his direct jurisdiction in the way preachers implied, and that true contrition brings forgiveness without an indulgence letter. Several theses criticize the preaching of indulgences as misleading to the faithful and as damaging to charity toward the poor. Others pose the questions laypeople were asking: why the pope, if he has such power over purgatory, does not empty it from charity, and why he does not rebuild St. Peter's with his own wealth. The closing theses urge Christians to follow Christ through suffering and to trust in God's mercy rather than in a false security.

Analysis: The theses are not a manifesto of a new theology. They stay largely within the framework of late medieval penitential thought while challenging its practical consequences, and they repeatedly insist that the pope himself would be offended by what preachers said in his name. They also reveal the pressure Luther felt from lay questions. At the same time, the emphasis on inward repentance and on the "true treasure of the church" as the gospel of grace anticipates themes he would soon develop more radically. As evidence the document is limited: it records Luther's position in October 1517, not the beliefs of his readers, and it cannot tell historians how many of his early supporters actually shared his ideas.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about repentance and indulgences?
  2. Who created it, and why would the form of an academic disputation shape its tone?
  3. What can historians learn from the theses about popular religion, church finance, and lay questioning in 1517?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, including how it was received and by whom?

The popular tradition that Luther nailed the theses to the door of the Castle Church on 31 October 1517 rests on a short chain of testimony. The earliest known account comes from Philip Melanchthon, written after Luther's death, and Melanchthon did not live in Wittenberg in 1517. A marginal note by Luther's secretary Georg Rörer, discovered in the twentieth century, supports the idea of some public posting. Against this, Luther's own early letters speak of sending the theses to bishops, not of a dramatic public act, and the University of Wittenberg regularly used the church door as a noticeboard. Historians therefore divide: some regard the posting as plausible and unremarkable, since doors served as bulletin boards; others regard it as unproven; and some stress that even if it occurred it was far less important than the printing and circulation that followed. What is secure is the letter to Albert and the rapid publication of the text.

Albert forwarded the theses to Rome, and the controversy moved quickly. The theses were reprinted and read well beyond academic circles, and by early 1518 Luther was known across the German lands. Humanists tended to see an ally against scholastic obscurantism; other theologians saw a challenge to papal authority. A pamphlet debate began, with the Dominican Sylvester Prierias and the Ingolstadt theologian Johann Eck among the first Catholic respondents. The question was no longer indulgences alone but who had authority to define them.

Section 62.4

From Academic Disputation to European Controversy, 1518–1519

In April 1518 Luther attended a chapter meeting of the German Augustinians at Heidelberg, where he presented a set of theses on human ability, grace, and works. The Heidelberg Disputation gave more systematic expression to themes that distinguished him from his teachers: that human works, however good they appear, cannot make a person righteous before God, and that God is known through suffering and the cross rather than through human speculation about glory. The disputation made an impression on younger scholars, including Martin Bucer, who would become a reformer in Strasbourg. It also demonstrates that by 1518 Luther's critique of indulgences had become part of a larger theology of grace.

Meanwhile, Rome had begun proceedings against him. Luther was summoned to appear in Rome, but Frederick the Wise intervened, and the case was shifted to the Imperial Diet meeting at Augsburg in autumn 1518. There, in October, Luther was examined by Cardinal Tommaso de Vio, known as Cajetan, a distinguished Dominican theologian and papal legate. Cajetan demanded a retraction; Luther asked to be shown from scripture where he had erred. When Cajetan refused to debate, Luther left Augsburg, and Frederick again declined to surrender him. A papal envoy, Karl von Miltitz, sought a negotiated quieting of the dispute in 1519 but failed.

Politics intervened. Emperor Maximilian I died in January 1519, and the election of his successor became the dominant concern of the German princes and the papacy. The main candidates were Charles of Habsburg, king of Spain, and Francis I of France; Frederick the Wise himself was considered. The Fugger bank and other financiers supplied funds that helped secure Charles's election in June 1519. The pope's reluctance to alienate Frederick during the election gave Luther breathing space, and the electoral agreement Charles accepted included a promise that no German would be placed under the imperial ban without a proper hearing, a promise Luther's supporters would later cite.

In June and July 1519 Luther met Johann Eck at a public disputation in Leipzig, in the territory of Duke George of Albertine Saxony, a staunch Catholic. Luther's Wittenberg colleague Andreas Karlstadt debated Eck first on free will and grace; then Luther and Eck turned to papal primacy. Eck pushed Luther into acknowledging that some positions of Jan Hus condemned at Constance had been Christian and that councils could err. That admission carried grave weight, because Constance had condemned Hus to death and because the conciliar tradition had been a recognized check on the papacy. Luther's position thereafter was that only scripture was an infallible authority. Eck's tactic of identifying Luther with Hus was effective politically: it connected him to a condemned heresy and made reconciliation harder.

By the end of 1519 the issues had broadened beyond indulgences. Luther's German sermons and tracts on penance, baptism, and the Lord's Supper were reprinted in large numbers, and printers in many cities found a market. Humanist supporters such as Ulrich von Hutten and Franz von Sickingen, imperial knights with their own political grievances, offered to protect him. The controversy was now simultaneously theological, political, and communicative, and each dimension reinforced the others.

Section 62.5

The Reformation Writings of 1520

In 1520 Luther published three treatises that together define the movement's program. They differed in language, audience, and purpose, which is one reason they should not be read as a single manifesto. To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, written in German in the summer and printed in August, addressed the emperor, princes, and knights, and urged them to reform the church because the clergy would not. Luther argued that the Roman church had protected itself behind three "walls": the claim that spiritual power was superior to secular power, that only the pope could interpret scripture, and that only the pope could call a council. He denied each, appealing to the shared baptism of all Christians, and he catalogued grievances familiar to German audiences, including papal taxation, the abuse of benefices, and the cost of Roman bureaucracy. The treatise sold out its first printing in days, according to contemporary reports, and it shows the political reach of his message.

The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, published in Latin in October, was written for theologians. Here Luther examined the sacramental system and argued that the church's practice had "captured" the sacraments in three ways: by withholding the cup from the laity, by teaching transubstantiation as a philosophical explanation, and by treating the Mass as a sacrifice offered by priests. He reduced the sacraments that he considered biblically instituted to baptism, the Lord's Supper, and, provisionally, penance. The treatise was more radical in its theology than the address to the nobility, and Erasmus and other sympathizers reacted with alarm.

The Freedom of a Christian, issued in Latin and German in November, was a pastoral treatise on Christian life. Its central paradox, in paraphrase, is that a Christian is free from every law as a matter of salvation, because faith alone unites the believer with Christ, yet is bound in service to every neighbor by love. Good works follow from faith; they do not earn it. The work is shorter and less polemical than the other two, and it reflects the positive theology that Luther considered the heart of his message.

These treatises appeared as Rome was preparing its response. The papal bull Exsurge Domine, issued in June 1520, condemned forty-one propositions drawn from Luther's writings and threatened excommunication if he did not recant within sixty days. The bull's publication in Germany was complicated by local resistance; the papal agents Johann Eck and Jerome Aleander had difficulty having it posted in some places, and in a few university towns students disrupted its reception. In Wittenberg on 10 December 1520 Luther and a group of students burned the bull and volumes of canon law. The act was symbolic, and it signaled a break with the legal system of the medieval church. Rome excommunicated Luther in the bull Decet Romanum Pontificem of 3 January 1521.

Taken together, the writings of 1520 show the progressive widening of Luther's challenge: from abuses to authority, from authority to the sacramental structure, and from the sacramental structure to the nature of Christian life. They also show his rhetorical range. He wrote for princes, scholars, and ordinary Christians, in Latin and German, and in each register he proved effective. That adaptability, and the willingness of printers to publish him, contributed as much to the movement's spread as the content of any single argument.

Section 62.6

Excommunication, Charles V, and the Diet of Worms, 1520–1521

The excommunication of Luther raised a legal problem. Under the traditions of the Empire, a person condemned by the church could be placed under the imperial ban, but Charles V had promised in his electoral agreement that no subject would be banned without a hearing, and the German estates, including many who disliked Luther's doctrine, insisted on that principle. Frederick the Wise pressed the point. The result was an unusual compromise: Luther would be summoned to the Diet of Worms, convened in January 1521, to answer questions, under a safe conduct issued by the emperor.

Luther arrived in Worms in April 1521 to great public interest. On 17 April he was shown a table of his books and asked whether he acknowledged them and whether he would recant. He asked for time to consider, a request that was granted, and on 18 April, before the emperor and the estates, he gave a long answer in German and Latin. He distinguished among his books and refused to retract any unless he was convinced by scripture or clear reason, because, he said, his conscience was bound by the word of God. The emperor, a young man who regarded himself as a defender of the church, replied the next day that he would proceed against Luther as a heretic, citing the tradition of his Catholic ancestors.

The famous words "Here I stand, I can do no other" require caution. They do not appear in the earliest records of Luther's reply, and they are first attested in printed versions of the speech that appeared soon afterward. Historians generally regard the substance of his refusal as secure, since multiple contemporary accounts agree on it, but doubt that the dramatic phrase can be treated as a verbatim record. The words nevertheless became a central part of the movement's memory, as the later cultural prominence of the scene illustrates; the distinction between what happened at Worms and how it was remembered is one students should keep in view.

The diet was divided. Many estates favored negotiation; some princes sympathized with Luther's criticisms of Rome without sharing his theology. Eventually the emperor issued the Edict of Worms. The document was dated 8 May, but Charles V signed it on 26 May 1521, one day after the formal closing of the Diet. It placed Luther under the ban of the Empire, forbade anyone to shelter or support him, and ordered his books burned. The edict's legal effect was real but limited. Charles was soon absent from Germany for almost a decade, dealing with revolts in Spain and war with France, and enforcement was left to the princes and city councils. Where authorities sympathized with reform, the edict was ignored or enforced selectively, so Luther's ban became a permanent legal tension in imperial politics.

On his return journey Luther was seized by a party of armed men and taken to the Wartburg, a castle near Eisenach. The abduction had been arranged by Frederick the Wise, who wished to protect Luther without publicly defying the emperor. For about ten months Luther lived there in disguise as "Junker Jörg," a knight, while rumor circulated across Europe that he had disappeared or been killed. The episode shows how political protection, organized discreetly by a prince, translated into the survival of the movement's leader.

Section 62.7

Wartburg, Bible Translation, and the German Vernacular

At the Wartburg Luther wrote sermons, treatises, and, most consequentially, a German translation of the New Testament. Working from Erasmus's Greek edition of 1516 and its revised edition of 1519, with the Latin Vulgate at hand, he completed a draft in a matter of weeks, and Melanchthon helped revise it in Wittenberg. The "September Testament" was printed in September 1522 with illustrations by Cranach's workshop, including woodcuts for the Book of Revelation that identified the beast with the papacy. The first printing sold quickly, and further editions followed. Luther then began translating the Old Testament with collaborators including Melanchthon, Justus Jonas, Johann Bugenhagen, and the Hebrew scholar Matthäus Aurogallus. The complete German Bible appeared in 1534.

It is a mistake to say that Luther invented the German Bible or the German language. German translations of the Bible, made from the Vulgate, had been printed since the 1460s, and the Latin church had not forbidden all vernacular scripture. Nor was there a single German language: spoken varieties differed greatly, and written forms varied by region. Luther's contribution lay elsewhere. His translation worked from the original languages, aimed at idiomatic and vigorous expression, and drew on the chancery language used by Saxon administrators, which was widely intelligible across central Germany. Its success, together with its constant reprinting, helped extend a shared written form of German over the following generations, though regional dialects continued to be spoken and written for centuries.

Translation had theological consequences. If the Bible could be read in German by anyone literate, the claim that the clergy were the necessary mediators of scripture was undermined. Luther's translation notes and prefaces, however, also show his concern that readers be guided. He wrote introductions to individual books to direct interpretation, and he later compiled catechisms to provide basic instruction. The movement's emphasis on scripture therefore went together with a strong interest in teaching, preaching, and supervised reading, a point developed in Section 62.8.

The social reach of vernacular scripture should not be exaggerated. Literacy was far from universal; estimates for German-speaking regions around 1500 range widely, with the great majority of the population unable to read, though rates were higher in towns and among men of certain trades. Most people experienced the Bible by hearing it read or preached, by seeing images, and by learning from household instruction. The German Bible mattered not only because individuals read it but because it became the foundation of preaching and catechesis, and because it gave reformers a shared text to which they could appeal against inherited custom.

Section 62.8

The Foundations of Lutheran Theology

Lutheran theology can be understood as an effort to reorganize Christian life around one question: how is a sinner made right with God? Luther's answer, justification by faith, held that salvation is a gift of divine grace received through trust in Christ, not a reward earned by human merit. Because humans are bound by sin, he argued, they cannot prepare themselves for grace by their own effort; faith itself is a gift. Luther also described the believer as at once righteous and a sinner (in Latin, simul iustus et peccator): righteous because Christ's righteousness is credited to the believer, sinful because the old self persists until death. This conviction made repentance a lifelong condition and weakened the logic of penitential systems built around measured satisfactions.

Historical Vocabulary
Justification

In Christian theology, justification refers to how a sinner is made or declared righteous before God. In Luther's teaching, justification is a gracious act of God received by faith, in which Christ's righteousness is imputed to the believer; good works are its fruit, not its cause. Late medieval theologians generally taught that grace and human cooperation both had roles, and Catholic teaching was later defined at the Council of Trent, examined in Chapter 65. The term matters because the disagreement over justification, more than any other single doctrine, separated Lutheran from Catholic understanding, and it is easily distorted in modern shorthand such as "faith versus works."

Scriptural authority was the second pillar. Luther held that the Bible is the supreme norm of Christian teaching, an idea summarized later in the Latin slogan sola scriptura. He believed that scripture is sufficiently clear in its central message that the church should be judged by it, not the reverse, and he rejected the claim that popes and councils are infallible. This did not mean that every individual could interpret the Bible however they wished. Luther relied on trained preachers, creeds inherited from the early church, and catechisms, and he rejected what he regarded as erroneous interpretations by Karlstadt, the radicals, and later the Zwinglians. Historians regard the modern idea that Luther inaugurated an unrestricted right of private judgment as a later projection, one that would more accurately describe some radical Protestants discussed in Chapter 63.

Luther's treatment of law and gospel structured both preaching and pastoral care. The law, in his usage, shows people their sin and need, and the gospel proclaims the promise of forgiveness in Christ; each must be distinguished, and confusing them leads either to despair or to false confidence. His concept of the priesthood of all believers held that baptism makes every Christian a priest in the sense of access to God and responsibility to neighbors, and it undercut claims that clergy occupy a spiritually superior state. It did not abolish the ministry. Luther maintained that churches need ordained pastors called to preach and administer sacraments in good order, and the doctrine's practical effect was to redefine clerical authority around preaching rather than sacerdotal power.

Sacramental teaching was reduced but not abandoned. Luther affirmed baptism and the Lord's Supper as instituted by Christ, and in practice retained a form of private confession and absolution as valuable. He rejected transubstantiation as a philosophical gloss and rejected the Mass as a sacrifice, but he insisted on the real presence of Christ's body and blood "in, with, and under" the bread and wine, a position that would separate him from Zwingli and others at the Marburg Colloquy of 1529 (Section 62.17). Lutheran worship retained much from the medieval liturgy, including vestments, music, and the structure of the service, in contrast to more thoroughgoing Reformed reforms. The church was understood as the community of believers gathered around word and sacrament, visible where the gospel is rightly preached and the sacraments rightly administered, a definition that later appeared in the Augsburg Confession.

Luther's thought also had a political dimension, often summarized in the language of "two kingdoms" or "two regiments," by which God governs through spiritual and temporal authorities. Secular rulers are ordained by God to maintain order, and Christians owe obedience except where a ruler commands what God forbids. In practice this doctrine gave princes considerable religious authority and encouraged Lutherans to look to rulers for the protection of the church, a pattern examined in Section 62.15. Luther's debate with Erasmus over free will, in which Erasmus defended a limited role for human choice in 1524 and Luther replied in 1525 that the human will is bound, also showed the gap between his theology and that of the humanists who had initially welcomed his criticisms.

Section 62.9

Wittenberg Reform and Conflicting Visions of Religious Change, 1521–1522

While Luther was at the Wartburg, reform began in Wittenberg without him, and the results showed how quickly evangelical teaching could generate competing programs. Augustinian friars in the town questioned the validity of monastic vows and of the private Mass; many left their cloister. Priests began to marry, the first being Bernhard Feldkirch in 1521, and Melanchthon defended the practice on scriptural grounds. In December 1521 and at Christmas, Andreas Karlstadt, Luther's colleague, celebrated a Lord's Supper in German in which communicants received both bread and wine, and he abandoned traditional vestments. The town council issued an ordinance in January 1522 addressing worship, poor relief, and the removal of images from churches, and some images were destroyed by crowds.

Matters intensified with the arrival in December 1521 of lay preachers from Zwickau who claimed direct divine inspiration and questioned infant baptism. Melanchthon, faced with these claims, was uncertain how to respond and appealed to Luther. The elector, worried about disorder, hesitated to intervene. These events illustrate a central problem for the movement: if scripture was the highest authority and the clergy were not the sole guardians of interpretation, who was to decide which interpretations were true, and who was to determine the pace of change?

Luther returned to Wittenberg in March 1522 against the elector's wishes, arguing that he was needed. In eight sermons delivered from 9 to 16 March, the Invocavit sermons, he urged moderation: reforms should be preached before they were imposed, the weak in faith should not be coerced, and external changes should not be treated as necessary for salvation. He restored some elements of traditional worship, including the elevation of the host, as matters of Christian freedom. Karlstadt, displaced from his leading role, eventually left Wittenberg, was expelled from Electoral Saxony in 1524, and developed a different understanding of the Lord's Supper, as examined in Chapter 63. Thomas Müntzer, a preacher in Thuringia who also left Luther's circle, advocated a more spiritual and apocalyptic reform.

The Wittenberg crisis established a pattern. Luther would defend the gospel against Rome, but he would also resist what he regarded as unlicensed innovation, and he increasingly looked to the elector and to structured church order to channel reform. Historians of the movement have emphasized that Luther's decision to condemn radical changes was not merely a conservative reflex but flowed from his theology: since works do not save, he argued, there was no reason to force external changes on those who were not yet convinced. Critics, both contemporary and modern, have asked whether this stance also made reform dependent on political authority.

Section 62.10

Printing, Preaching, Pamphlets, and the Spread of Reform

The Reformation is often described as the first major religious movement conducted through print, and the description contains truth. In the early 1520s the number of printed works in German multiplied sharply, and Luther became the most frequently printed author in the German lands, with his sermons, treatises, and translations reissued in editions from Wittenberg and many other cities. Pamphlets, called in German Flugschriften, were short, cheap, and often illustrated with woodcuts; they circulated in editions of perhaps a thousand copies or more, and works judged successful were reprinted repeatedly by competing printers, which meant Luther received no royalties. Estimates of the total number of pamphlets and copies vary and should be treated cautiously, but historians agree that the scale was unprecedented for the German lands.

Printing did not operate alone. Preaching remained the principal means by which most people encountered reform, and evangelical preachers in towns often drew crowds who could not read. Songs mattered as well. Luther wrote hymns, and the first Wittenberg hymnals appeared in 1524, with melodies familiar from popular and liturgical tradition; the famous hymn "A Mighty Fortress" is generally dated to the later 1520s, although its exact date is uncertain. Singing in German gave congregations a participatory role in worship and carried doctrine in memorable form. The Nuremberg shoemaker and poet Hans Sachs published a poem praising Luther as the "Wittenberg nightingale" in 1523, evidence of artisan engagement with the movement.

Images carried religious messages to those who could not read. Lucas Cranach the Elder's workshop produced woodcuts, painted portraits of Luther that established his public image, and illustrations that visually contrasted Christ and the pope in works such as the Passional of Christ and Antichrist of 1521. Satirical prints portrayed the pope as the Antichrist and clergy as greedy or lazy. Catholic polemicists also used images and print, and Luther's opponents, including Johann Cochlaeus and Thomas Murner, produced extensive literature, a reminder that the print war was two-sided even though the evangelical side was initially more productive.

Cause and Effect

Why Lutheran Reform Spread, 1517–1525

Long-term causes

Widespread anticlericalism, resentment of papal taxation, a lively devotional culture seeking assurance of salvation, humanist biblical scholarship, an established printing industry, and a politically fragmented Empire in which no single authority could suppress dissent.

Short-term causes

The indulgence campaign of 1517, Luther's skill as a preacher and writer in both Latin and German, Frederick the Wise's protection, the imperial election of 1519, and Charles V's absence from Germany after 1521.

Event or transformation

Evangelical preaching and printed texts reached towns, courts, and villages across the German lands, and councils, preachers, and rulers began introducing reforms to worship and church order.

Immediate effects

Disputes over how far and how fast reform should proceed, the appearance of competing reformers, the movement's entanglement with urban and rural political demands, and a growing imperial and papal effort to contain it.

Long-term consequences: The formation of distinct confessional churches tied to territories and cities, and a durable alliance between religious reform and political authority.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the spread. Printing without political protection would not have saved Luther, protection without a receptive audience would not have produced a movement, and theology alone would not have motivated councils and princes. Historians continue to debate the relative weight of each element.

Historians have also questioned the assumption that the audience was passive. Readers adapted and sometimes transformed the message. Pamphlets by lay authors, including artisans, women, and clergy of lower rank, applied evangelical language to local grievances, and some preachers developed their own versions of reform. The movement was thus built from below as well as from Wittenberg, and Luther's later efforts to define and discipline the movement reflected, in part, a response to this energy.

Section 62.11

The Reformation in German Towns and Cities

The early Reformation was, to a remarkable degree, an urban movement. Cities provided concentrations of literacy, printers, educated clergy, and civic institutions that could receive and implement new ideas. Many also had existing disputes between councils, guilds, and church authorities over jurisdiction, taxation, and the privileges of clergy, so that evangelical preaching could be linked to municipal self-government. The historian Bernd Moeller influentially argued in the 1960s that the communal ideal of the late medieval city, in which citizens saw themselves as a sacred community, provided a receptive setting for reform. Later scholars have qualified that argument, noting the importance of rulers, social conflict within cities, and the variety of local circumstances.

Nuremberg offers a clear example. The council, led by figures such as the city secretary Lazarus Spengler, sympathized with evangelical teaching, and under pressure from preachers and citizens it organized a religious colloquy in 1525 after which it formally adopted reform, abolished the Mass, and reorganized the church under the council's supervision. The council also drew on local clergy such as Andreas Osiander and appointed preachers to the main churches. In Strasbourg, Bucer and others promoted a reform that diverged in some respects from Wittenberg, examined in Chapter 63. In Magdeburg, Bremen, Hamburg, and other northern cities, reform spread through preaching and the work of Johann Bugenhagen, a Wittenberg pastor who drafted church orders for several cities. Augsburg, a major commercial and banking center, became religiously mixed, with Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed communities and complex political divisions.

Not all cities embraced reform. Cities whose economies and political leadership were closely tied to the emperor or to Catholic networks, or whose councils feared social unrest, often remained Catholic or delayed. Some councils adopted reform for political reasons, wishing to assert jurisdiction over clergy or church property, and others adopted it from conviction. In many cases, guilds and popular preachers pressed councils, and councils sought to keep reform orderly. The result was a patchwork. By the mid 1530s a large share of the imperial cities had adopted some form of evangelical reform, although estimates depend on definitions and on which cities are counted, and in some cities confessional division remained a permanent feature.

Reform in cities involved more than doctrine. Councils took over functions formerly handled by clergy and religious institutions, including poor relief, education, and the regulation of morals. Monastic and convent properties were transferred to municipal control or converted to schools and hospitals. Clergy who remained became in effect municipal employees, subject to council oversight. These changes strengthened the authority of town governments and gave the Reformation an institutional foundation that outlasted the enthusiasm of its first years.

Section 62.12

Social Conflict and the German Peasants' War, 1524–1525

The Peasants' War was the largest popular uprising in the German lands before the modern period. It began in the summer of 1524 in the Black Forest region around Stühlingen and spread in 1525 through Upper Swabia, Franconia, Thuringia, Alsace, Tyrol, and Salzburg, with distinct local characters. Its causes lay in a long accumulation of rural grievances: rising dues and labor service, restrictions on the use of forests, meadows, and water, the loss of customary rights as lords extended their authority, harsher forms of serfdom in some regions, and the tightening of territorial law that favored lords. The rebels were not only poor peasants; village officials, craftsmen, miners, and townspeople also joined, and some nobles supported the cause.

The movement's most influential statement was the Twelve Articles of the Upper Swabian peasants, drafted in early 1525 in Memmingen and printed in large numbers. The articles are generally attributed to the furrier and lay preacher Sebastian Lotzer, with theological input from the preacher Christoph Schappeler. They combined practical demands with scriptural argument.

Luther's response illustrates the movement's tensions. In April 1525 he published an Admonition to Peace, which criticized the lords for their oppression and also warned the peasants against rebellion and against appealing to the gospel to justify violence. As reports arrived of violence by some bands, and as Thomas Müntzer's apocalyptic leadership in Thuringia seemed to confirm his fears, Luther issued a far harsher tract, Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of the Peasants, urging the authorities to suppress the revolt without mercy. The tract appeared as the princes were already crushing the rebels, and it damaged his reputation among many commoners. His position followed from his theology of two kingdoms and his dread of disorder, but it also reflected his dependence on princely protection.

Military force decided the outcome. The Swabian League under Georg Truchsess von Waldburg defeated bands in the southwest, and the princes of Saxony, Hesse, and Brunswick defeated the Thuringian army at Frankenhausen on 15 May 1525. Müntzer was captured and executed. Estimates of the dead commonly run as high as 100,000, though the figure is uncertain and some scholars suggest lower totals. Repression brought heavy fines, confiscations, and executions, and in many regions lords strengthened their authority. In Tyrol, Michael Gaismair, a former official, proposed a radical constitutional program after the revolt, though it never took effect.

Section 62.13

Marriage, Household Religion, Education, and Everyday Life

Lutheran reform transformed expectations of family life, with consequences for clergy, women, and children. Luther rejected the idea that celibacy was a higher spiritual state and argued that marriage is a divinely ordained estate open to all, including clergy. On 13 June 1525, he married Katharina von Bora, a former nun who had left the convent at Nimbschen in 1523 with eleven other women, helped by a merchant who regularly delivered herring. The couple lived in the former Augustinian cloister in Wittenberg, known as the Black Cloister, and raised their own children and several relatives and students. Katharina managed a large household, farm, brewery, and boarders, and her role shows the practical authority wives might exercise even as Lutheran preaching affirmed male headship in the household.

The parsonage became a new social institution. Married pastors lived in a household visible to the congregation, and their families were expected to model Christian domestic life. This changed the clergy's relationship to the community and created expectations, and difficulties, for pastors' wives and children. Clerical marriage also provoked ridicule and hostility from Catholic opponents, and in some places married clergy faced economic hardship and social suspicion.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Household as a Religious Space

Lutheran reformers placed unusual weight on household instruction. Luther's Small Catechism of 1529, written after church visitations in Saxony revealed widespread ignorance of basic doctrine, presented the Ten Commandments, the Creed, and the Lord's Prayer in brief forms that heads of households were to teach to children and servants, and its companion, the Large Catechism, served preachers. Hymn singing, prayer, and Bible reading became household practices where literacy allowed. Education also changed: reformers argued that cities and princes should support schools for boys and, in many cases, girls, in order to train pastors and officials and to ensure that all could read scripture. Melanchthon's curricular reforms earned him the title "teacher of Germany" in later tradition. Poor relief was reorganized through the common chest, a municipal fund combining former church endowments and donations, replacing begging and fraternity-based charity with supervised assistance.

These reforms must be assessed with care. Women gained access to education in some places and were expected to be able to teach their children, but they lost the option of convent life, which had offered some women education, authority, and independence. Evidence for what ordinary households actually did is uneven; visitation records often show that pastors complained of ignorance and neglect, suggesting that ideals outran practice. Continuities with medieval practice, including saints' days, charitable obligations, and local custom, persisted in many places.

Women participated in the movement in varied ways. Some wrote pamphlets defending evangelical teaching, such as Argula von Grumbach, a Bavarian noblewoman who in 1523 publicly challenged the University of Ingolstadt for its treatment of a young evangelical scholar; others, such as the former nuns who left convents, acted on conviction at great personal risk. Katharina Zell, a reformer's wife in Strasbourg, wrote and ministered actively. At the same time, Lutheran church orders generally excluded women from ordained ministry and reinforced domestic roles. The Reformation therefore expanded some opportunities and constrained others, and gender historians continue to debate its net effect.

Section 62.14

Religious Discipline, Dissent, and the Limits of Reform

A movement that appealed to scripture against authority inevitably faced the question of who would decide among conflicting interpretations. Luther and his colleagues answered that the church must teach according to scripture as rightly understood, and that preachers, theological faculties, rulers, and church orders should maintain doctrine and order. The Wittenberg theologians condemned Karlstadt, Müntzer, and the radical Anabaptist movements that emerged from 1525, and they urged rulers to suppress what they regarded as heresy or sedition, a stance that placed Lutheran governments among those enforcing religious conformity. Chapter 63 treats the Anabaptists on their own terms, including the severe persecution they faced from Catholic and Protestant authorities alike.

The reformers also created new disciplinary mechanisms. Church visitations, examined further in Section 62.15, examined pastors' doctrine and conduct and parishioners' knowledge and behavior. Church ordinances prescribed worship and regulated marriage, education, and poor relief. Preachers used the pulpit and the confessional to supervise morals. In some respects this continued medieval efforts at discipline, and in others it intensified them, since the elimination of certain medieval devotional options and the integration of church and secular authority made enforcement more systematic.

Relations with other religious communities were uneven and often hostile. Early in his career Luther had been more sympathetic to Jews than many contemporaries. In his 1523 treatise That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew, he criticized the church's treatment of Jews and urged friendly engagement, though his aim was their conversion to the gospel as he understood it. This was a missionary hope, and it involved assumptions that Jews themselves rejected. Jewish communities in the German lands lived under precarious conditions, subject to local expulsions, restrictive legal regimes, and recurring accusations. Jewish advocates such as Josel of Rosheim negotiated with emperors and princes for protection. As Luther grew disappointed that Jews did not accept evangelical teaching, his attitude hardened, a development examined in Section 62.19. Muslims were also subjects of discussion; the Ottoman advance made the Turkish threat a constant presence in Luther's writings, and he addressed it in works such as On War against the Turk of 1529.

The limits of reform also concerned Luther's relationship with humanists. Erasmus, who had criticized clerical abuses and promoted biblical study, declined to join the movement and defended free will against Luther, as noted in Section 62.8. Other humanists became evangelical leaders, while still others remained Catholic reformers, a divergence relevant to Chapter 65. The early Reformation thus drew on shared concerns, but it also divided sympathizers over fundamental questions of authority, grace, and the church's unity.

Section 62.15

Princes, Territorial Churches, and Ecclesiastical Reorganization

After 1525 the center of gravity of the movement shifted from preachers and city assemblies to territorial rulers. Frederick the Wise died in May 1525 and was succeeded by his brother John, called the Steadfast, who openly supported reform. In Electoral Saxony, John asked Luther and Melanchthon to organize a systematic inspection of parishes. The first visitation, begun in 1527, sent commissions of theologians and officials to examine pastors, parish finances, schools, and the religious knowledge of laypeople. Melanchthon's Instruction for the Visitors of 1528 provided guidelines. The visitors found widespread ignorance and neglect, which prompted the catechisms and new church ordinances. A permanent institution, the consistory, was established in Wittenberg in 1539 to oversee marriage law, clerical discipline, and church administration.

Hesse followed a somewhat different path under Landgrave Philip, who adopted reform in 1526 and founded at Marburg in 1527 what is often described as the first Protestant university. In northern Germany, Bugenhagen's church orders for Brunswick (1528), Hamburg (1529), and Lübeck (1531) established patterns for cities. In 1525 Albert of Brandenburg-Ansbach, Grand Master of the Teutonic Order, secularized the Order's Prussian lands and became duke of Prussia under Polish overlordship, creating a Lutheran duchy on the Baltic. Beyond the Empire, Lutheran reform took root in Denmark and Norway in the 1530s and in Sweden after the Riksdag of Västerås in 1527, as monarchs used it to reorganize church property and authority, a pattern relevant to Chapter 79's treatment of northern states.

The introduction of territorial churches had major consequences. Rulers assumed oversight of church property, including the lands of dissolved monasteries, which they used for schools, hospitals, pastors' salaries, and, in some cases, state finances. Clergy became in effect a salaried service answerable to princely or municipal authorities. Reformers theorized this arrangement, in part, through the idea that in an emergency secular rulers, as leading members of the Christian community, could act as "emergency bishops" in the absence of church authority willing to reform. In practice that emergency arrangement became permanent. The tradition of princely supervision of church life, sometimes later called the territorial church system, shaped German Protestantism for centuries and helps explain why Lutheran churches generally supported existing political order.

Motives for princely adoption varied and should not be reduced to cynicism or piety. Some rulers, like Philip of Hesse and John of Saxony, were evidently convinced; others saw advantages in controlling church property, resisting the emperor, or consolidating authority. Many acted for a mixture of reasons, and contemporaries were as aware of this as modern historians. Subjects usually had little say, and conversion of a territory often meant that parishes were reorganized by decree, although effective change in belief and practice usually took a generation or more.

Section 62.16

Imperial Politics and the Diets of Speyer, 1526 and 1529

Historical Vocabulary
Imperial Diet (Reichstag)

The Imperial Diet was the assembly of the estates of the Holy Roman Empire, meeting irregularly in various cities, to deliberate with the emperor on taxation, defense, legal reform, and, after 1520, religion. Its members belonged to three colleges: electors, other princes and prelates, and the imperial cities. A decision reached by the Diet and confirmed by the emperor was published as a recess (Abschied) and had legal force. It was not a modern parliament, since members represented themselves or their territories, not a population, and decisions depended on negotiation. It matters because religious reform became an issue of imperial law within this assembly, and because Lutheran estates claimed rights as members.

With Charles V away from Germany, the Diets of the 1520s were presided over by his brother Ferdinand, archduke of Austria, and dominated by Charles's conflicts with France and the Ottoman Empire. At Nuremberg in 1522 and 1524 the estates avoided enforcing the Edict of Worms and called for a general council, while expressing hope that evangelical preachers would teach only the gospel. In 1526 the political situation changed. The Habsburgs faced war with France in the League of Cognac, and in August the Ottoman sultan Suleiman I destroyed the Hungarian army at Mohács and killed King Louis II, leaving Ferdinand a claimant to the crowns of Hungary and Bohemia. The Ottoman threat to Habsburg territory made the emperor and his brother dependent on the estates for money and troops.

The Diet that met at Speyer in 1526 agreed on a recess that, in paraphrase, stated that until a general council could meet, each estate should live, govern, and conduct itself in matters concerning the Edict of Worms as it could answer to God and the emperor. Evangelical rulers interpreted this as authorization to continue reforming their territories, while Catholics considered it a temporary measure. Its ambiguity enabled the establishment of territorial churches in the following years, including the visitation in Saxony.

By 1529 the political calculus had shifted. Charles's armies had prevailed in Italy, he was close to peace with the pope and with France (the Treaties of Barcelona and Cambrai followed in mid 1529), and he wished to reassert religious unity. The second Diet of Speyer, convened under Ferdinand in March and April 1529, with a Catholic majority, revoked the interpretation of 1526: no further religious innovations were to be introduced in territories that had not yet adopted them, and the Mass was to be allowed everywhere. On 19 April 1529 a minority of estates issued a formal protest, the Protestation. Six princes joined in it: Elector John of Saxony, Landgrave Philip of Hesse, Margrave George of Brandenburg-Ansbach, the brothers Ernest and Francis of Brunswick-Lüneburg, and Prince Wolfgang of Anhalt. They were supported by representatives of fourteen imperial cities. The protest was renewed on 20 April, and on 25 April the protesting estates issued a formal Instrument of Appeal to the emperor, a council, and an impartial judge. They argued that a majority could not override matters of conscience, and that the 1526 recess could not be repealed without unanimous consent. The term "Protestants" came to designate those who had protested, and it later expanded to cover adherents of the Reformation more broadly.

The Protestation demonstrates the constitutional edge of the conflict. Evangelical estates framed their position as defense of rights within the imperial system, not as rebellion against it. They also accepted the authority of the emperor in other matters. At the same time, the Ottoman advance on Vienna in the autumn of 1529, which failed after a short siege, showed how external pressure shaped the dynamics: Charles and Ferdinand needed support, and negotiated compromise remained a recurrent necessity for imperial politics.

Section 62.17

The Augsburg Confession and the Definition of Lutheran Belief, 1530

The Protestation of 1529 exposed a practical problem: evangelical estates needed a clear statement of what they believed, both to demonstrate their orthodoxy to the emperor and to distinguish themselves from other movements. Divisions among reformers made this urgent. In October 1529 Philip of Hesse brought Luther, Melanchthon, Zwingli, Johannes Oecolampadius, and Bucer together at Marburg in the hope of forging a political and theological alliance. The Marburg Colloquy produced agreement on fourteen of fifteen articles. The fifteenth, on the Lord's Supper, was irreconcilable: Luther insisted on the bodily presence of Christ in the sacrament, while Zwingli taught a more symbolic understanding. The disagreement, treated in Chapter 63, divided the evangelical movement along lines that would persist.

Charles V, having made peace with the pope and France, returned to Germany in 1530 and summoned a Diet to Augsburg, announcing his hope of resolving religious disputes and organizing resistance to the Ottomans. Because Luther remained under the imperial ban, he could not travel into Habsburg-influenced Augsburg; he waited at Coburg, in Saxon territory, and communicated by messenger. The task of preparing the Saxon statement fell to Melanchthon, who drew on earlier drafts and on the Torgau Articles that had been prepared for the elector. On 25 June 1530 the Saxon chancellor Christian Beyer read the confession aloud in German before the emperor and the assembled estates, a deliberate choice to make the statement public.

The Augsburg Confession, 1530

Creator: Philip Melanchthon, with Luther's approval, for the Lutheran estates · Date: presented 25 June 1530 at the Diet of Augsburg · Type: A confessional statement, issued in German and Latin versions, addressed to the emperor

Context: The confession was presented as a statement of faith intended to show that the evangelical estates held the true catholic and apostolic teaching. It comprised twenty-eight articles, twenty-one summarizing doctrine and seven describing practices the reformers had changed.

Paraphrase (not a translation) The first articles affirm the Trinity, original sin, and the person of Christ in terms drawn from the early creeds. It is evidence of how Lutheran leaders presented themselves in 1530, not a complete account of their theology or of ordinary believers' practice. It also omits topics, such as purgatory and papal authority in detail, on which the evangelicals' differences with Rome were sharp. Catholic respondents read its moderation as evasion.

Charles V commissioned Catholic theologians, including Eck and Cochlaeus, to prepare a response. Their Confutation, read on 3 August, rejected significant parts of the confession and demanded that the evangelical estates return to the church. Melanchthon drafted an Apology of the Augsburg Confession, published in 1531, defending the document in detail. A group of south German cities, including Strasbourg, Constance, Memmingen, and Lindau, presented their own Tetrapolitan Confession because of differences on the Lord's Supper, and Zwingli sent his own statement. The final recess of the Diet, issued in November 1530, gave the evangelical estates until April 1531 to return to the old church, threatened enforcement through the Imperial Chamber Court, and was rejected by the Lutheran estates.

The Diet therefore failed to restore unity, but it achieved something unintended: it gave Lutherans a founding text. The Augsburg Confession would eventually become the formal basis for legal recognition in 1555 and one of the principal documents of the Lutheran tradition, along with Luther's catechisms and later statements gathered in the Book of Concord of 1580. This textual anchoring contributed to a process of confessional definition, in which churches were increasingly identified by written statements rather than by loyalty to a person or by shared practice alone.

Section 62.18

The Schmalkaldic League and Lutheran Consolidation

The threat of imperial enforcement raised an old question: could subjects resist the emperor? Luther, who had stressed obedience to authority, was initially reluctant to authorize armed resistance. Legal advisers, however, argued that the emperor's authority was limited by the constitutional rights of the electors and estates, and that resistance to unlawful imperial action in defense of the estates' rights was permissible. In December 1530 Lutheran estates met at Schmalkalden, and in February 1531 they formed a defensive alliance, the Schmalkaldic League, led by Elector John of Saxony and Landgrave Philip of Hesse. Members included several princes and cities, among them Magdeburg, Bremen, and Brunswick, and its membership grew over the following years.

The League was a political and military organization. It established a common fund, a council, and a structure for raising troops, and it negotiated with France and England, though no lasting foreign alliance emerged. The League's survival depended on the emperor's preoccupation elsewhere. In 1532 Charles, needing support against the Ottomans, agreed to the Nuremberg Standstill, suspending proceedings against Protestants until a council met. In 1534 Philip of Hesse, with French support, restored the Protestant Duke Ulrich to Württemberg, which had been in Habsburg hands, and this expansion strengthened Lutheranism in the southwest.

Consolidation also had a theological side. In 1536 the Wittenberg Concord, negotiated with Bucer, achieved a formula on the Lord's Supper acceptable to the south German theologians and Luther, reducing the divide with the cities. In 1537, in preparation for a council summoned by Pope Paul III, Luther wrote the Schmalkald Articles, a sharper statement than Melanchthon's confession, which defined points on which Lutherans would not yield, notably justification and the rejection of papal supremacy. The council was delayed, however, and then convened at Trent in 1545 without Protestant participation, as discussed in Chapter 65.

The movement continued to expand. When Duke George of Albertine Saxony, a stalwart Catholic, died in 1539, his brother Henry introduced reform in Dresden and Leipzig. In the same year the Elector of Brandenburg, Joachim II, began to move toward reform. Efforts at theological negotiation continued, notably the Colloquy of Regensburg of 1541, where moderate Catholics and Lutherans reached provisional agreement on justification but could not agree on the sacrament and authority. Meanwhile, the bigamy of Philip of Hesse in 1540, which Luther and Melanchthon had counseled should be kept secret, became a scandal that weakened the Landgrave's political position and damaged the League's moral authority.

Historical Debate

Why Did Lutheran Reform Take Root Unevenly?

The question: By the 1540s Lutheran reform had taken hold in much of northern and central Germany and in many imperial cities, while the Habsburg lands, Bavaria, and most ecclesiastical principalities remained Catholic. Why did reform succeed in some places and fail in others?

Interpretation A: Political and institutional conditions were decisive. Some historians stress that rulers and councils chose confession according to their interests and constitutional position. Princes who could gain control of church property and resist the emperor had incentives to adopt reform; cities with strong councils and communal traditions could do so without a ruler's interference; territories dependent on the Habsburgs or governed by bishops had less room to maneuver. Bernd Moeller's work on imperial cities and later studies of princely policy exemplify this approach.
Interpretation B: Religious conviction, preaching, and communication mattered independently. Other historians emphasize that evangelical preaching, printed works, and local religious movements created demand for reform that rulers could not simply manufacture. In this view, politics shaped the timing and form of change, but reform spread where preachers and communities were persuaded, and where they were not, it failed or was suppressed.
Interpretation C: Confessionalization. A further approach, associated with Heinz Schilling and Wolfgang Reinhard, argues that from the mid sixteenth century Lutheran, Catholic, and Reformed churches each developed similar mechanisms of doctrine, discipline, and education that supported state building. This interpretation explains why confessional boundaries hardened but is less concerned with the origins of the movement.

Evidence: Historians draw on council minutes, visitation records, church ordinances, printed pamphlets, correspondence of rulers, and records of preaching. Each type of evidence favors certain explanations: official records tend to emphasize political decisions, while sermons and pamphlets reveal religious motives.

Why it matters: If politics was decisive, the Reformation's geography resembles a map of princely and civic decisions; if conviction mattered more, it resembles a map of preaching and communication. Most current scholarship treats these as overlapping, not exclusive, explanations.

Section 62.19

Luther's Later Years, Controversies, and Death, 1530–1546

After 1530 Luther remained the movement's most famous figure but not its sole director. He continued to lecture at Wittenberg, preaching regularly in the town church, and produced commentaries, including a lengthy series of lectures on Genesis. His collected conversations, the Table Talk, were recorded by students and guests over many years, and they give vivid but unreliable evidence, since they are retrospective notes by others and vary in their transmission. His health deteriorated, and he was often ill and frequently irritable. He also faced internal disputes, including the antinomian controversy with Johann Agricola in the late 1530s over the role of the law in Christian life, and disagreements with Melanchthon over sacramental teaching and conciliation with other reformers. His later polemics against the papacy grew increasingly violent in language, notably Against the Roman Papacy, an Institution of the Devil, published in 1545.

Luther's most troubling writings concern Jews. His early hope of converting them by gentle persuasion gave way to disappointment, and by the late 1530s he wrote that Jews were obstinate in rejecting the gospel. In 1543 he published On the Jews and Their Lies, followed by other tracts. In these writings he recommended that synagogues and Jewish schools be burned, that Jewish homes be destroyed, that prayer books and the Talmud be confiscated, that rabbis be forbidden to teach, that Jews be denied safe conduct and compelled to perform manual labor, and, if these measures did not suffice, that they be expelled. The writings combine theological accusations inherited from medieval Christian anti-Judaism with violent rhetoric and concrete policy proposals. These writings were rooted in Christian anti-Judaism rather than the racial antisemitism that emerged more fully in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, but that distinction does not lessen the severity of Luther's recommendations or their later historical reception, and the language and proposals were extreme even by sixteenth-century standards.

These works did not appear in a vacuum. Jews had been expelled from many German cities and territories in the later Middle Ages and early modern period, and anti-Jewish preaching, accusations of ritual murder and host desecration, and economic restrictions were common. In 1536 Elector John Frederick of Saxony prohibited Jews from traveling through his territories, and Josel of Rosheim, who had defended Jewish communities before imperial and princely authorities, appealed to Luther to intercede; Luther refused. Josel and other Jewish leaders continued to negotiate with rulers and to write in defense of their communities, demonstrating that Jews were active participants in these events and not merely victims. Some Protestant and Catholic contemporaries criticized Luther's tracts, and some rulers did not follow his recommendations.

Historians disagree about how the writings should be interpreted. Some emphasize continuity with medieval Christian anti-Judaism and treat Luther as a participant in a long tradition, differing from modern racial antisemitism, which defined Jews by descent and not by religion. Others stress the contribution of his rhetoric and his authority to later hostility, noting that the tracts were reprinted and invoked in later centuries, including by Nazi propagandists in the 1930s. Both observations can be true: the distinction between religious anti-Judaism and racial antisemitism is analytically important, and it does not diminish the harm the writings caused or their later exploitation. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries many Lutheran churches, including the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America in 1994 and several German Lutheran bodies, have formally repudiated these writings, a form of institutional reckoning that itself belongs to the movement's history.

Luther died on 18 February 1546 in Eisleben, his birthplace, where he had traveled to mediate a dispute among the counts of Mansfeld. He was buried in the Castle Church in Wittenberg. By then he had lived to see Lutheran churches established across large parts of Germany and Scandinavia, but he had not seen their legal recognition, and his death left the movement in the hands of princes, theologians such as Melanchthon, and pastors who would contest his legacy for generations. It is important to distinguish Luther's lifetime from the movement's subsequent development: the Lutheran church that obtained legal status in 1555 was shaped by many people and by political circumstance, and not simply by one man's intentions.

Section 62.20

The Schmalkaldic War, Augsburg Interim, and Renewed Conflict, 1546–1552

By the mid 1540s Charles V was freer to act. The Peace of Crépy with France in 1544 and a truce with the Ottomans in 1545 relieved pressure, and the opening of the Council of Trent in December 1545 gave the emperor a framework for religious reform that excluded Protestant participation. Charles prepared a military solution. He secured the support of Duke Maurice of Albertine Saxony, a Lutheran who was a cousin and rival of the Ernestine elector John Frederick and who was promised territory and the electoral dignity in return for neutrality or active alliance. The Schmalkaldic League was divided, its leaders were not in full agreement, and the scandal of Philip of Hesse had weakened its cohesion.

The war began in the summer of 1546. Charles used the legal tool of the imperial ban against John Frederick and Philip of Hesse, framing the conflict as enforcement against disobedient princes rather than a religious war, a distinction that allowed some Protestants, including Maurice, to side with him. After indecisive campaigning in the south, the decisive engagement took place at Mühlberg on the Elbe on 24 April 1547, where imperial forces defeated the Saxon army and captured John Frederick. Philip of Hesse surrendered and was imprisoned. In the Wittenberg capitulation, John Frederick lost the electoral dignity and a large portion of his territories, which passed to Maurice. The League collapsed as a military force.

Charles then tried to impose a religious settlement. At the so-called armed Diet of Augsburg of 1547 and 1548, he issued the Augsburg Interim, published on 15 May 1548, a provisional ordinance intended to govern religion until the council concluded. It restored many Catholic practices and doctrines, including the seven sacraments and the authority of bishops, while granting two concessions: communion in both kinds for the laity and permission for already married clergy to remain married. Many Protestants regarded the Interim as a betrayal. In south German cities such as Constance and Augsburg, where imperial power was strongest, Protestant worship was suppressed or restricted, and some preachers fled. Elsewhere, resistance and adaptation prevailed.

In Saxony, Maurice and Melanchthon sought a compromise, the Leipzig Interim, which conceded certain practices as adiaphora, matters indifferent to salvation, a category already present in Luther's thought. This provoked severe criticism from more rigid Lutherans, who argued that yielding in matters of ceremony under compulsion was in effect denying the faith, and the resulting "adiaphoristic controversy" divided the movement. Magdeburg, which refused the Interim and was placed under the ban, became a center of resistance and of printed polemic, a pattern of urban defiance that illustrates the continuing role of cities.

The imperial triumph proved short-lived. Maurice, who had received his rewards, grew concerned about the emperor's growing authority, the imprisonment of fellow princes, and the prospect of Habsburg dynastic hegemony. In 1552, after secretly negotiating with other princes and with Henry II of France through the Treaty of Chambord in January, Maurice turned his army against the emperor. Charles, caught off guard and ill, fled from Innsbruck. The Treaty of Passau, signed in August 1552, secured the release of Philip of Hesse and provided that the Protestants would not be molested until a future imperial diet discussed religion. The Interim lapsed. Maurice was killed in battle in 1553, but his intervention had demonstrated that imperial power could not impose confessional unity by force, and it set the stage for a negotiated settlement.

Section 62.21

The Peace of Augsburg, 1555, and the Limits of Religious Settlement

The Imperial Diet that opened at Augsburg in early 1555 was presided over by Ferdinand, acting for the emperor, who was physically present in the Netherlands and increasingly inclined to withdraw from German affairs. Negotiations were long and difficult. Catholic estates were willing to recognize Lutheran practice but wished to protect ecclesiastical lands and bishoprics; Lutheran estates wished for secure legal status and, ideally, protection for their coreligionists in Catholic territories. The recess agreed on 25 September 1555 was therefore a compromise among estates, not a statement of principle about religious freedom.

Primary Source Window

The Religious Peace of Augsburg (Imperial Recess), 1555

Creator: The Imperial Diet of Augsburg, under King Ferdinand acting for Emperor Charles V · Date: 25 September 1555 · Type: An imperial legal settlement, published as part of the recess of the Diet

Context: The recess followed the failure of the Augsburg Interim, the princely revolt of 1552, and the Treaty of Passau. Its religious provisions established a legal framework among the estates of the Empire.

Paraphrase of principal provisions (not a translation) Neither the emperor nor the estates were to attack or coerce estates adhering to the Augsburg Confession because of their religion, and the same protection applied to Catholic estates. Estates of both religions were to enjoy peace and the exercise of their religion within their territories. Subjects who did not wish to adhere to their ruler's religion were permitted to emigrate, selling their property and leaving without penalty to their honor, subject to the usual dues. A "spiritual reservation" provided that a Catholic prelate who converted would lose his office and revenues. In imperial cities where both religions were already practiced, both were to be allowed to continue.

Analysis: The text shows how religious pluralism was handled as a question of rights among the Empire's constituent powers. It recognized Catholic and Lutheran estates, but it did not recognize Reformed Protestants, Anabaptists, or other groups, and it did not guarantee individual freedom of conscience. The right to emigrate shows an effort to reduce conflict by allowing dissenters to leave. As a source, the recess reflects the compromises of the participants and the assumptions of its legal language, but it does not describe how subjects experienced it or how consistently it was applied.

The settlement's key terms were as follows. First, it established peace between Catholic estates and estates adhering to the Augsburg Confession, a recognition that gave Lutheran rulers legal security. Second, it granted rulers and city councils the authority to determine the religion of their territories, the ius reformandi. Third, subjects who disagreed with the ruler's choice had the ius emigrandi, the right to emigrate. Fourth, the Ecclesiastical Reservation barred Catholic prelates who converted from retaining their territories and revenues, thereby protecting Catholic control of bishoprics and abbeys. Fifth, the recess recognized the property holdings of secularized church lands as of the Treaty of Passau in 1552. Sixth, imperial cities with mixed populations were to allow both religions where both were established, which produced a number of parity cities, among them Augsburg. A further point was contested: Ferdinand issued a separate declaration protecting Lutheran subjects of ecclesiastical princes from forced conversion, but it was not part of the recess, and Catholic estates denied its force.

The later formula cuius regio, eius religio ("whose region, his religion") is often cited as the principle of the settlement. It was coined by the jurist Joachim Stephani around the turn of the seventeenth century, summarizing the pattern in Latin, and it is a convenient shorthand but not a text of the recess. Moreover, it can mislead. The Peace did not establish religious toleration in the modern sense; it granted a collective, territorial right to rulers, not individual rights to subjects, and it applied only to two confessions. Reformed churches, which had developed in the Palatinate and elsewhere, as examined in Chapter 63, were not covered, and their later claim to protection became a source of conflict.

The settlement did not resolve the Empire's religious tensions. Disputes persisted over ecclesiastical property, over the Ecclesiastical Reservation, over the status of Reformed Protestants, and over the enforcement of rights within mixed territories. The Peace nevertheless gave the Empire a framework within which confessional coexistence was possible for several decades. Charles V, disillusioned, abdicated his titles in the Netherlands in October 1555 and in Spain in January 1556, and his brother Ferdinand succeeded as emperor in 1558. Subsequent conflicts, treated in Chapters 66 and 67, tested the settlement and ultimately led to the Peace of Westphalia of 1648.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Life Before and After Lutheran Reform

What changed?

  • Preaching and catechism replaced the Mass as the central instruction of laypeople in Lutheran territories, and worship was conducted in German.
  • Monasteries, convents, private Masses, saints' cults, and many pilgrimages were abolished or sharply reduced, and church property was reorganized under rulers or councils.
  • Clergy married and became salaried officials, and territorial rulers assumed supervisory authority over church life.
  • Religious allegiance became a matter of imperial law, tied to territories and legally recognized confessions.

What remained continuous?

  • Lutheran churches retained much medieval liturgy, vestments, music, and church buildings, and the major festivals of the Christian year.
  • Rulers and councils continued to claim authority over religion for the sake of order, and the regulation of moral life remained a public concern.
  • Anti-Jewish teaching and restrictions persisted and in some respects intensified.
  • Local custom, folk belief, and agricultural religious rhythms persisted despite reformers' efforts.

Who experienced change most directly? Clergy, monks and nuns, urban residents, and subjects of reforming rulers. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural villagers, whose daily practice changed more slowly, and populations in Catholic territories. Why does the pattern matter? It cautions against treating the Reformation as an instantaneous break and shows that its effects were uneven in time, place, and social group.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the German Reformation from the indulgence controversy of 1517 to the Peace of Augsburg of 1555. It argued that Luther's challenge became a Reformation not through theology alone but through the interaction of religious conviction, printing and preaching, territorial government, and the constitutional politics of the Holy Roman Empire. The Empire's dispersed authority allowed reform to survive where no single power could suppress it, but also made the movement dependent on the choices of rulers and councils.

Luther's theology developed over years of monastic life, biblical lecturing, and controversy. The indulgence dispute drew attention to a practice many found troubling, but the larger issue was how sinners are reconciled to God. Luther's emphasis on justification by faith, scriptural authority, and the priesthood of all believers reshaped the understanding of repentance, the sacraments, and the ministry. His theology was neither an unrestricted doctrine of private judgment nor a simple critique of abuses; it was a distinct vision of Christian life with its own demands for teaching and discipline.

The conflict escalated in 1518 and 1519 through the hearings at Augsburg and the Leipzig Disputation, and in 1520 through the great treatises and the papal condemnation. At Worms in 1521, Luther refused to recant and was placed under the imperial ban, but the edict was unevenly enforced because of Charles V's absences, Frederick the Wise's protection, and the political circumstances of the Empire. The Wartburg refuge and the German Bible extended the movement's reach, though not through print alone: sermons, songs, images, and discussion carried evangelical ideas to many who could not read.

The movement spread into towns, where councils and guilds adopted reform for mixed motives, and into households, where marriage, catechism, and schooling were reshaped. The Peasants' War of 1524 and 1525 showed that evangelical language could support social protest, and Luther's harsh response, together with the princes' military repression, aligned Lutheranism more closely with established authority. The Reformation therefore expanded some forms of participation and constrained others, and it affected women, clergy, townspeople, and peasants in different ways.

After 1525 territorial rulers became the movement's principal institutional agents. Visitations, church ordinances, consistories, and the redirection of church property built territorial churches. At the Diets of Speyer the estates debated their authority over religion, and the Protestation of 1529 gave Protestants their name. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 defined Lutheran belief for imperial purposes, and the Schmalkaldic League provided collective defense. These developments show how doctrine, law, and military alliance became interlinked.

Luther's later years included productive pastoral and theological work and also harsh polemic, including the anti-Jewish writings of 1543 that this chapter has treated critically. Those writings drew on medieval anti-Judaism, called for severe measures against Jewish communities, and later became part of a troubling legacy. Jewish leaders such as Josel of Rosheim acted to defend their communities, and modern Lutheran churches have repudiated the writings. Luther's death in 1546 occurred before the movement achieved legal security, and the movement's later history was shaped by many others.

The Schmalkaldic War, the Augsburg Interim, and the revolt of Maurice of Saxony demonstrated both the emperor's military power and its limits. Imperial victory at Mühlberg did not produce religious unity, and compulsion provoked resistance, internal Lutheran disputes, and a princely revolt. The Treaty of Passau and the Peace of Augsburg followed from this failure, not from a commitment to toleration.

The Peace of Augsburg recognized Catholic and Augsburg Confession estates, authorized rulers to determine the religion of their territories, permitted dissenting subjects to emigrate, and protected Catholic control of bishoprics through the Ecclesiastical Reservation. It excluded Reformed and other Protestants, did not establish individual religious freedom, and left several questions unresolved. Its importance lies in its role in the constitutional order of the Empire and in setting the terms of later confessional conflict, examined in Chapters 66 and 67. The German Reformation thus emerges not as an inevitable step toward modern religious liberty but as a contingent transformation that created enduring confessional institutions and enduring tensions.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Indulgence
A remission of temporal punishment for sins already forgiven in confession, granted by church authority and extended in the late Middle Ages to souls in purgatory.
Penance
The sacrament and practice by which a sinner confesses, shows contrition, receives absolution, and performs satisfaction, central to the controversy of 1517.
Purgatory
In Catholic teaching, the state in which souls undergo purification after death; Luther came to reject the claim that the church could shorten it through indulgences.
Ninety-Five Theses
Propositions for academic debate on indulgences, sent by Luther to Archbishop Albert in 1517 and soon printed widely.
Justification by faith
Luther's teaching that sinners are made right with God by grace, received through faith in Christ and not earned by works.
Sola scriptura
The principle that scripture is the supreme norm for Christian teaching; in Lutheran practice it operated alongside creeds, catechisms, and ordained preaching.
Law and gospel
Luther's distinction between God's demand, which exposes sin, and God's promise, which gives forgiveness in Christ.
Priesthood of all believers
The teaching that baptism gives all Christians access to God and responsibility toward neighbors; it did not abolish ordained ministry.
Real Presence
The teaching that Christ's body and blood are truly present in the Lord's Supper, which Luther defended against symbolic interpretations such as Zwingli's.
Exsurge Domine
The papal bull of June 1520 condemning forty-one propositions drawn from Luther's writings.
Edict of Worms
The imperial decree of 1521 placing Luther under the ban and forbidding his writings, unevenly enforced.
Imperial ban
A legal penalty placing a person outside the protection of imperial law, usually requiring proper proceedings.
Flugschrift
A short, inexpensive printed pamphlet, often illustrated, that carried religious and political arguments to a wide audience in the early Reformation.
Evangelical
The early self-description of reform supporters, derived from the word for gospel, preceding the label "Lutheran."
Twelve Articles
A statement of grievances and demands issued by Upper Swabian peasants in 1525, combining scriptural argument with practical economic and legal claims.
Visitation
An official inspection of parishes, clergy, and schools, used in Saxony from 1527 to assess and reorganize church life.
Church ordinance
A legal text prescribing worship, clerical duties, schooling, poor relief, and discipline in a territory or city.
Consistory
A body of theologians and officials supervising church administration, marriage law, and clerical discipline under the ruler's authority.
Common chest
A municipal or parish fund pooling former church endowments and donations to support the poor, clergy, and schools.
Imperial Diet
The assembly of the estates of the Holy Roman Empire, meeting in three colleges and issuing recesses with legal force.
Protestation
The formal protest of April 1529 by a minority of estates against the revocation of the religious recess of 1526, origin of the term "Protestant."
Augsburg Confession
The statement of Lutheran belief presented in 1530 and later the legal basis for recognition in the Peace of Augsburg.
Schmalkaldic League
The defensive alliance of Lutheran princes and cities formed in 1531, led by Saxony and Hesse.
Adiaphora
Matters regarded as indifferent to salvation, such as certain ceremonies; the concept caused bitter dispute during the Interim.
Augsburg Interim
The provisional religious settlement imposed by Charles V in 1548, largely Catholic in content with concessions on the chalice and clerical marriage.
Ius reformandi
The right of a territorial ruler or city council to determine the religion of the territory, recognized in 1555.
Ius emigrandi
The right of subjects who disagreed with the ruler's religion to leave, selling their property, recognized in the Peace of Augsburg.
Ecclesiastical Reservation
The provision that a Catholic prelate who converted would lose his office and revenues, protecting Catholic control of bishoprics.
Cuius regio, eius religio
A later Latin formula summarizing the principle that rulers determined territorial religion; not a phrase of the 1555 recess.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a religious dispute changed the institutional structure of a major European political system. The Reformation in the German lands was not only a change in belief but a change in who governed religious life, how churches were funded and staffed, what children learned, and what rulers and subjects owed one another. These changes were built from many choices by preachers, printers, councils, princes, and ordinary people, and no single actor controlled them.

The chapter also reveals continuities. Lutheran churches retained much of the medieval inheritance, and the habit of territorial supervision of church life drew on long traditions of rulers' involvement in religion. At the same time, the settlement of 1555 gave the Empire a new legal category, the confessionally recognized estate, with consequences for law, diplomacy, and identity that lasted until the settlement of 1648. The chapter's treatment of Luther's anti-Jewish writings is a reminder that the movement's history includes harm as well as creativity, and that Jewish communities were historical actors in the same period.

For Volume III, the German Reformation illustrates one path by which religious change and state formation became intertwined. Chapter 63 will show other paths in the Swiss cities and in radical movements, Chapter 64 a different one in England, and Chapter 65 the Catholic response. In Part XII the dynastic and constitutional histories will show how confessional identity shaped the early modern state system. Students should carry forward the habit of asking how religion, politics, communication, and social structure interacted, and should avoid reading the Peace of Augsburg as the predetermined birth of modern toleration. It was a contingent, fragile, and limited settlement, and understanding it as such is the best preparation for the conflicts examined next.
