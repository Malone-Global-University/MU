Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1968, a conflict over dormitory visiting rules at a new suburban university in Paris became, within weeks, a national crisis that halted factories, trains, and broadcasts across France. Yet the same decade also produced quieter confrontations: a sewing-machine workshop at a Ford plant in Essex, a meeting of women in an Oxford college hall, a signed list of names in a Paris weekly, and a referendum campaign in Italy. Together they raise one question: why did the most prosperous generation of Europeans yet seen challenge so many forms of authority?

On the night of May 10 to 11, 1968, students in the Latin Quarter of Paris tore up paving stones, overturned cars, and built barricades in the streets around the Sorbonne, while police advanced against them with tear gas and batons. The images, broadcast by radio reporters who described events as they unfolded, shocked a country that had enjoyed more than two decades of sustained economic growth. Within days, workers occupied factories in Nantes and Normandy, and by the end of May millions of employees across France had stopped work. The government of President Charles de Gaulle, which had presented itself as the guarantor of order and modernization, seemed for a short time to be losing control of the capital.

This chapter treats those weeks as one dramatic episode within a much longer and wider transformation. Students occupied universities in Rome, Turin, and Trento, marched in West Berlin, and demonstrated in London's Grosvenor Square. Factory workers in northern Italy launched in 1969 the largest wave of strikes in the postwar republic. Women who had worked in student organizations, trade unions, and political parties began to ask why movements dedicated to liberation so often left them typing leaflets and making coffee, and why the law still limited their pay, their property, their bodies, and their choices. Gay and lesbian activists, young musicians, filmmakers, publishers, priests, and parents all became participants in debates over what authority should be allowed to demand of individuals. These developments were connected, but they were not identical, and the chapter takes care to distinguish them.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I explains the social foundations of the protest generation: population change, educational expansion, youth consumer culture, and the international political conflicts, above all the Vietnam War, that gave radicals shared causes. Division II reconstructs the French crisis of May and June 1968 and asks why a government that appeared on the verge of collapse won a decisive electoral victory within weeks. Division III widens the lens to West Germany, Italy, Britain, and other European societies, where different universities, labor movements, and political memories produced different outcomes. Division IV turns to second-wave feminism and to changes in sexuality, reproduction, and family law, the area in which the longer consequences of the period were arguably most profound. Division V examines popular culture, everyday life, religion, backlash, and inequality, and weighs what the protest era did and did not change.

The chapter follows a thesis stated early and tested throughout: the movements associated with 1968 challenged political and cultural authority, but their most enduring consequences often emerged through a longer transformation of universities, workplaces, families, laws, social movements, and everyday expectations. The French historian Michelle Zancarini-Fournel has argued that reducing this history to "May 1968" obscures its longer chronology and broader geographical and social scope, and this chapter adopts that caution. It uses the phrase "1968" as shorthand for a protest cycle that ran, with variations, from the mid-1960s to the late 1970s and reached well beyond students.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. It includes government statistics on enrollment, strikes, births, and marriages; legislation and parliamentary debates; tracts, posters, and newspapers produced by movements themselves; police and union records; memoirs and oral histories collected decades afterward; photographs, films, and recordings; and opinion surveys. Each type carries limitations. Movement documents show what activists wanted to say, not what most contemporaries thought. Oral histories are shaped by later memory, and the memory of 1968 has itself become a political battleground. Statistical series use national definitions that cannot always be compared. Students should therefore read this chapter as an exercise in distinguishing what happened from what has been remembered, claimed, or condemned about it.

Within Part XIX, Chapter 130 moves from the resistance of Soviet-dominated Europe examined in Chapter 129 to the social upheavals of western Europe, while recognizing that protest in the two halves of the continent shared some of the same languages of dignity, participation, and truth. Within Volume VI, the chapter shows that the Cold War division of Europe was experienced not only as diplomacy and military confrontation but as a struggle over how ordinary people were allowed to live. The next chapter, Chapter 131, will show that changing social expectations unfolded alongside economic instability, international détente, and the political transformation of southern Europe.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how postwar prosperity, demographic change, educational expansion, and mass media created the social conditions for the protest movements of the 1960s.
  2. Trace the chronology of the French crisis of May and June 1968, from Nanterre to the legislative elections, and distinguish its student, labor, and governmental dimensions.
  3. Compare the protest movements of France, West Germany, Italy, and Britain, identifying how national institutions and political memories shaped different outcomes.
  4. Distinguish student radicalism, industrial labor militancy, antiwar activism, counterculture, and political violence rather than treating them as a single movement.
  5. Analyze the origins and aims of second-wave feminism, including its relation to the New Left and its distinct intellectual and organizational traditions.
  6. Assess reforms concerning contraception, abortion, divorce, equal pay, and homosexuality, distinguishing legal change from practical access and recognizing national differences.
  7. Interpret primary sources, including movement documents, speeches, and statutes, with attention to their authors, purposes, and limitations.
  8. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of 1968 as revolution, reform, generational revolt, workers' struggle, and cultural transformation.
  9. Contextualize changes in family life, religion, music, cinema, and media within uneven national and social conditions.
  10. Evaluate who benefited from social liberalization, who remained excluded, and why the period produced both lasting change and persistent inequality.
  11. Connect the protest era to the later developments of the 1970s examined in Chapters 131 and 132.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did prosperous, increasingly educated societies experience an extraordinary wave of challenges to authority, and why did material improvement not guarantee political contentment?
  2. How did a dispute about university regulations in one suburb of Paris become a national crisis, and why did the French government survive it?
  3. In what respects were the protests of 1968 an international movement, and in what respects were they a set of separate national events?
  4. What were the relationships, and the tensions, between students, industrial workers, and established labor organizations?
  5. Why did women who participated in New Left movements develop independent feminist organizations, and how did those movements differ among countries?
  6. How should historians distinguish the effects of the protest movements from changes already underway or driven by governments, unions, courts, and reformist parties?
  7. How did commercial youth culture and political dissent interact, and why should they not be conflated?
  8. What does it mean to say that the consequences of 1968 were substantial but uneven, and what evidence would support or challenge that claim?

Historical Context

The societies in which the protests occurred had been reconstructed with remarkable speed. As Chapter 126 explained, western Europe experienced, from the early 1950s until the early 1970s, a period of rapid economic growth driven by reconstruction, American assistance, expanding trade, rising productivity, and the extension of social insurance. Real incomes rose, housing improved, and household goods such as refrigerators, washing machines, radios, and televisions entered ordinary homes. Chapter 127 showed that Britain, France, Italy, and West Germany emerged from the war with new constitutional arrangements, party systems, and welfare provisions. Chapter 128 showed that the loss of overseas empires, from Indochina and Algeria to the wider British and French imperial worlds, had altered national self-images and brought new migrants to European cities.

These changes had unequal results. Growth narrowed some disparities and left others untouched. Many western European states retained political arrangements that concentrated authority in a few hands. France under de Gaulle's Fifth Republic, established in 1958, gave exceptional powers to a president who commanded loyalty among many citizens and deep suspicion among others. West Germany was governed from December 1966 to October 1969 by a "grand coalition" of Christian Democrats and Social Democrats whose size left little room for parliamentary opposition, and its public institutions still included people who had served the Nazi state. Italy was dominated by Christian Democracy within a system in which the large Communist Party was excluded from national government. Britain's institutions, including its universities, remained strongly hierarchical. Beyond these four countries, Spain and Portugal remained dictatorships, Greece fell under a military junta in April 1967, and the Soviet-dominated states of eastern Europe, examined in Chapters 125 and 129, imposed far harsher constraints on dissent.

Postwar Europe also experienced a major demographic bulge. Births rose in the late 1940s and, in many countries, remained high into the early 1960s, so that by the second half of the 1960s unusually large cohorts were entering secondary schools, universities, and the labor market. Contraceptive technologies spread, particularly oral contraceptives from the early 1960s, and fertility rates began a long decline from the middle of the decade, although the pace and legal circumstances varied sharply by country. Urbanization continued, with migration from countryside to city and, in Italy, from the poor south to the industrial north. The war itself, its occupations and genocides, remained a living memory. Many young Germans, Italians, and French citizens asked what their parents had done between 1933 and 1945, while others asked why the societies built after 1945 seemed so reluctant to examine that question.

Finally, the Cold War framed the political imagination of the period. The Vietnam War, escalated by the United States from 1965, offered to young Europeans a vivid example of what they regarded as imperial violence, even as the Soviet suppression of the Hungarian Revolution in 1956 and the invasion of Czechoslovakia in August 1968, examined in Chapter 129, discredited authoritarian communism for many on the left. The movements of 1968 therefore did not fit neatly into either bloc. Many activists rejected both American military power and Soviet communism, and looked instead to anticolonial struggles in Algeria, Cuba, Vietnam, and Africa, to Chinese Maoism, to Latin American revolutionary writing, or to older libertarian and anarchist traditions. The result was a New Left that criticized the established left as much as the right.

The chapter's source base is correspondingly broad. Historians reconstruct the period from legislation and official statistics; police, union, and party archives; newspapers, magazines, tracts, and posters; recordings and films; letters, diaries, and memoirs; and oral-history interviews gathered from participants. The last category is especially important for women's history, because many feminist groups kept few formal records, but it is also the category most affected by hindsight. A recurring task for students will be to ask when a source was created, by whom, and for what audience.

The Postwar Generation and the Origins of the Social Revolt

Why did prosperity, educational expansion, youth consumer culture, and international political conflict create conditions favorable to movements challenging established institutions?

Section 130.1

Postwar Prosperity, Population Change, and the Emergence of Youth Society

The generation that came of age in the 1960s grew up in conditions unlike those of its parents. The parents had known the Depression, mass unemployment, dictatorship, and war, and many measured success by security and the avoidance of catastrophe. Their children knew mostly rising wages, expanding welfare provisions, and the expectation, mistaken as it later proved, that growth would continue indefinitely. Detailed reconstruction and economic growth belong to Chapter 126, but several features matter here. Real incomes rose substantially across western Europe; working hours in many industries fell; paid holidays lengthened; and households acquired durable goods that had been luxuries in the 1930s. Housing construction expanded, though shortages persisted in many cities, and new suburban estates and high-rise developments reshaped the urban landscape.

Demography reinforced these changes. Births had risen sharply after the war and stayed high in many countries into the early 1960s, so that the number of people aged fifteen to twenty-four grew markedly between the late 1950s and the later 1960s. In West Germany births peaked around 1964; in France the postwar rise, assisted by family allowances and pronatalist policy, was especially pronounced; in Italy the fall from older high levels was slower, and births remained comparatively numerous in the south. This was not a uniform "baby boom" experienced identically in every country, and historians use the term cautiously. But in every case the later 1960s saw a conspicuous youth presence in schools, universities, streets, and public debate, and young people were increasingly regarded as a distinct social group with distinct needs.

Rising prosperity did not mean political contentment. Several features of the period encouraged dissatisfaction precisely because expectations were rising. Young people compared their experience not with the poverty of the past but with the promises of the present: more schooling should open more opportunities, and a society that could produce so many goods should be able to treat its citizens more democratically. When institutions seemed unresponsive, whether an overcrowded university, an authoritarian workplace, a church that regulated private life, or a government that spoke to the public in the tones of an earlier age, the gap between promise and experience became a source of grievance. Social scientists later described such situations as a rise in "relative deprivation," meaning a sense of injustice that grows when conditions improve but do not improve as fast as expectations.

The term "youth generation" must be used carefully. Young Europeans entered very different environments. A minority attended universities, which, as the next section explains, were expanding rapidly but remained socially selective, with students drawn disproportionately from middle-class and professional families. A larger number left school at fourteen to sixteen for apprenticeships, factories, shops, farms, and domestic or service employment. Many young working people, especially those who earned wages early, participated in the new consumer economy through records, clothing, motorcycles, and dance halls, but they had far less access to the political and intellectual networks in which student radicalism formed. Young women and young men faced different expectations about education, marriage, and paid work. Rural youth, migrant youth, and young people in dictatorial southern Europe had still other experiences. Throughout the chapter, therefore, "the young" refers to several groups with overlapping but distinct interests, and one of the most important historical questions is when and how those groups came into alliance or conflict.

Section 130.2

The Expansion of Universities and the Crisis of Educational Authority

European universities of the early twentieth century had educated small elites. After 1945, governments increasingly regarded higher education as an economic and social investment, needed to supply engineers, teachers, administrators, and scientists for modern economies and welfare states. They also faced public pressure from families who regarded university education as a route to advancement. Enrollments rose rapidly, but institutional reform lagged. Many universities remained organized around powerful professors, formal lectures delivered to hundreds of students, high-stakes examinations, and administrative rules that treated students as dependents rather than participants. The distance between the new scale of the system and its inherited authority structures became a principal source of conflict.

Historical Vocabulary
Mass University

A higher-education system that enrolls a large and rapidly growing share of an age cohort, as distinct from the older elite university that educated a small, socially selected minority. Sociologists used the phrase for the transformation of the 1950s and 1960s. It does not mean that universities had become socially egalitarian, because class and gender barriers persisted. It matters in this chapter because many 1968 grievances arose from the mismatch between mass enrollment and elite-era governance, including crowded lecture halls, remote professors, and limited student voice.

The institutional contexts differed. In France, universities were part of a centralized national system under the Ministry of National Education. The state guaranteed admission to students holding the baccalauréat, which meant that the university could not control numbers, and the Parisian system, centered on the old Sorbonne, was overburdened. The government created new campuses, including the University of Nanterre, opened in 1964 on the western edge of Paris, built amid shantytowns and housing for migrant workers in the same suburb. Reformers in the 1960s, under the so-called Fouchet reforms of 1966, introduced selective measures that students feared would restrict access and tie education more closely to labor-market needs. A student at Nanterre therefore experienced a campus cut off from the cultural life of Paris, with inadequate facilities, and administered by authorities who regulated residence-hall visits, in particular visits between men's and women's residences.

In West Germany, the universities enjoyed academic autonomy but were dominated by the "ordinarius" professors who held chairs and wielded wide personal authority, while assistants and students had little voice. Critics identified a continuing presence of older officials and academics who had built careers before 1945 and had rarely been challenged. In Italy, a system of open admission to university for graduates of some secondary tracks, later extended to all in 1969, produced swelling enrollments, but teaching staffing, facilities, and examination practices remained inadequate; chairs were similarly powerful, and the university was closely tied to patronage networks. In Britain, the Robbins Report of 1963 provided a principle that university places should be available to all qualified by ability and attainment who wished to pursue them, and new universities and expanded older ones followed. British universities tended to be smaller and more residential, with closer staff-student contact, and their protests generally took less dramatic forms. Even so, sit-ins at the London School of Economics in 1967 and 1968 and disputes at provincial universities and art colleges showed the same questions about governance, disciplinary authority, and the purposes of education.

What students demanded changed over time. Early campaigns concerned practical matters: housing, grants, class sizes, library access, examination rules, and the right to hold political meetings on campus. Within a short period, in many places, such local demands connected to a broader critique. Students argued that universities trained them to serve existing economic and political arrangements, and that the "neutrality" of knowledge disguised its links to industry, the military, and the state. Sociology and political science students in particular, studying capitalism, bureaucracy, colonialism, and social inequality, often drew on the work of Marx and of the Frankfurt School, and of authors who criticized modern societies as repressive despite their prosperity. The German-American philosopher Herbert Marcuse, in One-Dimensional Man (1964), argued that advanced industrial societies absorbed and neutralized dissent, a thesis that resonated with many students even when they had not read the book closely. In France, Guy Debord and the Situationists attacked the consumer "spectacle," and writers associated with the journal Socialisme ou Barbarie criticized both capitalist and Soviet bureaucracy.

Why did opportunity generate protest rather than gratitude? Several explanations are plausible and mutually compatible. Universities concentrated large numbers of young people in one place, with time, shared experience, and informal networks that made organizing easy. Students were trained to criticize and analyze, and many were encountering sharp critiques of their own societies for the first time. They were also in an ambiguous social position: not yet adult workers, not simply children, and uncertain about their future status in a labor market that might not reward their qualifications. Some historians emphasize anxiety about the declining value of degrees as numbers rose; others stress the moral critique of affluence as materialistic and complicit with war and inequality. Students should be cautious about reducing the movement to either explanation, because activists themselves held different motives and because most students did not become protesters at all.

Section 130.3

Youth Culture, Popular Music, Mass Media, and Generational Conflict

While students argued about universities, a larger and more commercial transformation reached young people across social classes. The word "teenager," imported from American usage in the 1950s, described a new market and a new social category: young people with disposable income, leisure time, and a shared interest in music, clothing, film, and magazines. Their consumption was facilitated by technologies that had become inexpensive: transistor radios, record players, 45-rpm singles, and, increasingly, television sets. Rock and roll from the United States reached Europe in the mid-1950s, and in several countries it was accompanied by moral panic. West German press coverage of "Halbstarke" (roughly, "half-strong," or rowdy youths) in the late 1950s, British alarm over Teddy Boys, and French debates over the "blousons noirs" (black jackets) revealed adult anxiety about working-class youth, delinquency, and sexual behavior.

By the early 1960s, Europe was producing its own youth culture. In Britain, a generation of musicians, many from working-class and lower-middle-class backgrounds and trained in provincial clubs and art schools, transformed American rhythm and blues and rock and roll into new forms. The Beatles, who recorded their first single in 1962, and the Rolling Stones, among many other groups, achieved international success, and "Swinging London" became a media image of fashion, photography, and sexual freedom. In France, the radio program and magazine Salut les copains, launched in 1962, helped create the "yé-yé" generation of young singers and fans. Offshore "pirate" radio stations, such as Radio Caroline, broadcast pop music to British listeners beginning in 1964 because the BBC and commercial licensed radio gave it limited airtime, until the Marine Offences Act of 1967 curbed them. In West Germany and elsewhere, American, British, and local music circulated through radio, jukeboxes, and concerts.

Historians have argued over how this culture related to politics. A purely commercial reading holds that youth culture was a market created by producers and advertisers who sold rebellion as a product. A more political reading emphasizes that young people used music, clothing, hair, and style to assert independence, challenge parental authority, and imagine alternative ways of living. Both readings contain truth, and they sometimes reinforced each other. Buying a record or wearing a fashionable jacket was not by itself a political act, and many fans of popular music disliked student radicals or ignored politics altogether. But popular culture created shared references, new spaces for sociability, and new means of communication that political activists could later use. A song, a poster, a style of dress, or a youth magazine could carry a sense of generational identity across class and national boundaries more easily than a political pamphlet.

Parental and institutional reactions were often hostile. Parents worried about sexual morality, particularly the behavior of daughters, about the effects of television and music on discipline, and about young people's apparent rejection of thrift and deference. Churches, newspapers, and politicians sometimes portrayed youth culture as a symptom of moral decay. Courts policed obscenity, as in the 1960 trial in Britain over the publication of Lady Chatterley's Lover, which ended in acquittal and became a symbol of changing standards. These conflicts show that generational tension was real, but they should not be exaggerated: most young people maintained close relationships with their families, and many shared their parents' values on work, religion, and marriage.

Everyday Life and Social History

Young People's Leisure in the Early 1960s

For a teenage apprentice or shop assistant in a European industrial city, weekday life was organized around work and family, and leisure was concentrated in evenings and weekends. Dance halls, cafés, cinemas, football grounds, youth clubs, and, in rural areas, parish and village festivals, were the principal places where young people met. A young worker might spend a wage packet in part on a record, a transistor radio, or clothing, but often handed part of the earnings to parents. Young women commonly faced stricter supervision than young men and were expected to leave paid work on marriage, though this expectation weakened over time.

Students lived differently. Those attending universities often lived away from home for the first time, in residence halls, rented rooms, or, in Italy and France, sometimes with relatives. Cafés, bookshops, film societies, and political meetings became centers of sociability. In France and elsewhere, residence-hall rules that restricted visits between young men and women became symbolic of an older moral order. The contrast between these environments is important: the protest movements recruited mainly in the student milieu, while the commercial youth culture reached a much wider and more socially mixed audience.

Television also changed domestic life. In many households, the arrival of a television set in the late 1950s or 1960s redirected family evenings and gave state broadcasters, such as France's ORTF, considerable power to shape public information. Government control of broadcasting became a political grievance in 1968, when journalists and technicians themselves challenged it.

Section 130.4

Antiwar Protest, International Solidarity, and the New Left

Student discontent acquired a political language through a series of international causes. The most important was the Vietnam War. After the United States began sustained bombing of North Vietnam and sent large ground forces in 1965, protests spread across Europe, even though no European state sent combat troops. Demonstrations in London, West Berlin, Paris, Rome, Stockholm, and many university towns condemned American policy, and activists criticized their own governments for diplomatic or economic cooperation with Washington. For many young Europeans, Vietnam seemed to expose the hypocrisy of Western claims to defend freedom and appeared to repeat the colonial wars that France had fought in Indochina and Algeria, discussed in Chapter 128. Opposition drew on pacifist, Christian, socialist, and humanitarian motives, not only revolutionary ones.

Other causes linked protest across borders. Solidarity with anticolonial movements in Algeria, Africa, and Cuba, criticism of apartheid in South Africa, opposition to nuclear weapons, and sympathy for civil-rights and Black Power movements in the United States circulated through publications, speeches, and personal contact. The campaign for nuclear disarmament, which in Britain had mobilized large marches to Aldermaston in the late 1950s, supplied organizational experience and a repertoire of demonstrations. Revolutionary figures such as Che Guevara, Ho Chi Minh, and Mao Zedong became symbols for some students, while others drew from Trotskyist, anarchist, council-communist, and libertarian traditions. The result was not a single ideology but a shared set of rejections.

Historical Vocabulary
New Left

A loose international current of left-wing activists and intellectuals, emerging from the late 1950s, who criticized both the Soviet model of communism, especially after 1956, and the compromises of established social-democratic and communist parties with parliamentary politics and welfare capitalism. The New Left emphasized participatory democracy, cultural criticism, anticolonialism, and the politics of everyday life, and often sought alliances among students, intellectuals, and marginal groups as well as industrial workers. The term was used by participants themselves, but it covered diverse groups, from reformist socialists to Maoists and anarchists. It is distinguished from the "Old Left" of parties and unions, though the two overlapped in practice.

The New Left had several characteristic forms of action. Teach-ins, public lectures and debates held in universities, spread from the United States to Europe. Activists produced pamphlets, newspapers, and posters, often cheaply by mimeograph or screen printing, and organized international conferences. In February 1968 the Socialist German Student Union and allied groups held an International Vietnam Congress in West Berlin, examined further in Section 130.9. Students followed events abroad closely, and a movement in one country could stimulate action in another: images and reports of American campus protests and the Vietnamese Tet Offensive of January and February 1968 circulated quickly through the press and television. At the same time, activists differed sharply about method. Peaceful demonstration, civil disobedience, sit-ins, and occupations were common; revolutionary rhetoric was widespread; and violence, whether against police, property, or persons, was supported by only a minority and debated bitterly. Students should avoid treating these categories as synonymous, and they should note that a given activist could move between them over time.

The New Left's criticism of the Soviet Union was also important. The suppression of the Hungarian Revolution in 1956 had led many communists in the West to leave their parties, and the invasion of Czechoslovakia in August 1968 intensified this rejection, as Chapter 129 explains. Western New Left activists thus often found themselves opposing both the capitalist West and the communist East, and some sympathized with reform currents inside eastern Europe, such as Prague Spring's "socialism with a human face." In practice, many western European communist parties, such as the French PCF, regarded the student radicals with suspicion as undisciplined, middle-class, and potentially provocative, a tension that shaped the events of May 1968.

Division I's central point is that the movement that emerged in 1968 was neither a spontaneous eruption nor a mere reaction to the Vietnam War. It arose from a social base created by postwar growth, was nourished by new cultural institutions and means of communication, and was articulated through an international political vocabulary shaped by decolonization, Cold War stalemate, and disillusionment with existing parties. It was shaped, too, by memory. In West Germany and Italy, the silence of an older generation about fascism gave the student movement a distinct moral urgency. These conditions created a potential for protest, but they did not determine its timing or its form, which depended on specific local events, as the story of Nanterre shows.

Section 130.5

Nanterre, the Sorbonne, and the Beginning of the French Student Revolt

The French crisis began at the margins of Paris. The University of Nanterre, opened in 1964 on the site of a former military airfield, brought together students of the humanities, sociology, and law in a setting that many found bleak. Its rules were enforced by a dean and administrators who regarded student life as a matter for discipline, and the campus was adjacent to shantytowns that housed migrant workers, a physical reminder of social inequality that some students noticed and discussed. Student activists, including groups of anarchists, Trotskyists, Maoists, and members of other tendencies, were active in sociology and elsewhere. They protested the Vietnam War and criticized university regulations, including the rules restricting visits to women's residences, which became a flashpoint of student resentment in the winter of 1967–68. In January 1968, when the minister responsible for youth and sports, François Missoffe, opened a new swimming pool at Nanterre, a student, Daniel Cohn-Bendit, publicly challenged him over the absence of any discussion of sexuality in a government report on youth. The exchange was minor but widely reported, and it showed how questions of sexual freedom and generational authority could enter political debate.

The immediate trigger of the organized movement was the arrest in Paris, on March 20, of several activists, including a Nanterre student, after attacks on American business premises in protest against the Vietnam War. On March 22 students at Nanterre occupied the administrative building and formed what became known as the March 22 Movement. It was a loose coalition, not a party, and brought together activists from different political tendencies who agreed on tactical action and on the criticism of university and society more than on a unified doctrine. Cohn-Bendit, a sociology student of French and German background, became one of its best-known spokesmen. Other participants included militants from Trotskyist and Maoist organizations and many students with no previous affiliation. Students should not treat Cohn-Bendit, or any other individual, as the organizer of the revolt: what historians see is a decentralized network of activists, many of them inexperienced, responding quickly to events.

Through April the movement continued, and on May 2 the Nanterre dean closed the campus after further disturbances. The next day, May 3, student activists gathered in the courtyard of the Sorbonne, in the heart of the Latin Quarter, to protest the closure and the threat of disciplinary action against Nanterre students. The rector of the Paris Academy, Jean Roche, asked the police to clear the courtyard, an extraordinary step because the university's autonomy had traditionally restricted police entry. Police arrested several hundred students, and the sight of students being loaded into police vans provoked angry crowds in the surrounding streets. Clashes lasted into the evening. Over the following days, as arrests mounted and some students received prison sentences, the demands crystallized: release of the arrested, withdrawal of police from the Latin Quarter, and reopening of the Sorbonne and Nanterre. On May 6, large demonstrations ended in violent clashes. The student union UNEF and the teachers' union of higher education, SNESup, called for a general strike in education.

The confrontation reached a climax on the night of May 10 to 11, remembered as the "night of the barricades." After a day of marches, students occupied streets in the Latin Quarter and built barricades of paving stones, cars, and street furniture. Police, ordered to clear the area, attacked in the early morning with tear gas and clubs; many demonstrators, bystanders, and police were injured, and cars were burned. Radio journalists reported live from the scene, and what listeners heard, together with images and accounts of police violence, aroused widespread public sympathy for the students and criticism of the government. Prime Minister Georges Pompidou, returning from a trip abroad, announced the reopening of the Sorbonne and the release of detained students, but by then the movement had passed beyond the original demands. The chronology of these weeks matters: the March 22 mobilization, the events of May 3, and the barricade confrontations of May 10 to 11 were distinct episodes, each with its own causes and effects.

Section 130.6

From Student Protest to General Strike: Workers, Unions, and Factories

The student revolt might have remained a Parisian university crisis had it not been joined by workers. On May 13, the unions of the main French federations, the Confédération générale du travail (CGT), the Confédération française démocratique du travail (CFDT), and others, called a one-day general strike and demonstrations in protest against police repression. Hundreds of thousands of people marched through Paris from the Place de la République to Denfert-Rochereau, with students and workers marching together, though with different banners and slogans. The government withdrew the police from the Latin Quarter, and students reoccupied the Sorbonne, which became an assembly place of speeches, debates, and improvised committees for the next month.

The one-day strike then changed character. On May 14 workers at the Sud-Aviation aircraft plant near Nantes occupied the factory and held the management captive in its offices. The next day workers at Renault's Cléon plant did the same, and within days the movement spread through factories across France: automobile plants, shipyards, mines, steel works, railways, airports, public services, department stores, and the state broadcaster. By the third week of May, strikes and occupations had halted much of the French economy. Fuel became scarce, transport was disrupted, and daily life in cities was impaired. Many of the first occupations were begun by younger workers, sometimes without the initial authorization of union leaders, a fact that suggests the influence of student action, although the strikes also grew from long-standing workplace grievances.

Those grievances were substantial. French wages had lagged behind growth; the minimum wage (the SMIG) was low; unemployment, particularly among young workers, was rising; many workers faced long hours and authoritarian management; and the legal position of trade unions inside firms was weak. Unions sought wage increases, a shorter working week, security of employment, and recognition of union rights at the workplace. These were demands within a framework of collective bargaining and not demands for the abolition of the existing order, although some workers did discuss workplace self-management, which the CFDT in particular supported.

The relationship between students and established labor organizations was uneasy. The CGT, the largest federation, was closely linked to the French Communist Party, which regarded the students as "adventurist" middle-class radicals and feared that student slogans would provoke repression or damage the party's claim to be the disciplined voice of the working class. CGT leaders insisted on keeping control of strikes, sometimes preventing students from approaching factory gates. The CFDT, a federation that had moved away from its Catholic origins and toward democratic socialism and worker self-management, was more open to dialogue. Smaller groups of students and young workers tried to build direct links, and some independent activists helped organize action committees in factories and neighborhoods. Workers were, however, neither uniformly revolutionary nor uniformly hostile to students. Many sympathized with the students' anger at the police and some with their critique of authority, while others resented what they saw as the privileges of university youth, and most focused on concrete workplace demands.

Quantitative Study

Counting the French Strike of May–June 1968

The size of the French strike wave of May–June 1968 is not established by a single audited count. Gérard Adam’s 1970 statistical study begins by questioning whether five or ten million people can meaningfully be counted when voluntary strikers, workers prevented from working, and those who performed emergency tasks are classified differently. Approximately seven million participants is a common historical order-of-magnitude estimate; accounts range from roughly six million to ten million or more depending on definition. It is not sound to identify every larger estimate as a simultaneous peak. A 1973 CRÉDOC study reports that INSEE relied on a very rough approximation of about 150 million working days lost in calendar year 1968, explicitly saying that precise working-day figures were unavailable. These are different quantities: individuals involved versus cumulative working time lost.

Why the figures vary: Gérard Adam, “Étude statistique des grèves de mai-juin 1968” (Revue française de science politique, 1970), emphasizes definitional boundaries between strikers, involuntary shutdowns and continuing essential tasks. The 150-million-day approximation reported by V. Scardigli for CRÉDOC in 1973 is an estimated annual loss rather than a complete register of every participant. Public-sector coverage and strike length create additional uncertainty.

What the figures show: Even cautious estimates establish that the mobilization was of exceptional national scale and cannot be reduced to a student movement. The numbers alone do not settle whether any individual striker advocated revolutionary political change.

Cause and Effect

Why Did French Student Protests Develop into a General Strike?

Long-term causes

Rapid economic growth with lagging wages and authoritarian workplaces; a centralized Fifth Republic that many regarded as paternalistic; weak union rights inside firms; a large young workforce; and a tradition of mass strikes and street politics in French history.

Short-term causes

Rising youth unemployment in 1967–68; the police entry into the Sorbonne and the violence of May 10 to 11; the union decision to call the May 13 protest; and workers' occupations at Nantes and Cléon.

Immediate effects

Paralysis of transport and industry, collapse of government authority in many spheres, and negotiations between unions, employers, and the government.

Long-term consequences

Wage and workplace reforms, recognition of union sections in firms, new debates over worker self-management, and lasting distrust between sections of the radical left and the Communist Party.

Historical complexity: The students did not "cause" the strike in a simple sense. Their actions, and the police response, provided an occasion and an example; the strike's depth depended on workers' own grievances and on the decisions of union leaders to support or contain the movement. Historians therefore describe a convergence of distinct movements rather than a single chain of events.

Section 130.7

Grenelle, De Gaulle, Elections, and the Political Outcome of 1968

By late May, France faced a political crisis. Opposition figures, including François Mitterrand and Pierre Mendès France, discussed the possibility of a transitional government, and on May 27 a large rally of left-wing groups gathered at the Charléty stadium in Paris. De Gaulle himself seemed uncertain; he had left on a state visit to Romania on May 14, in the midst of the escalating crisis, and returned on May 18 to a situation that tested his authority. His televised address of May 24, which proposed a referendum on participation in universities and enterprises, failed to calm the situation.

The government's first effective response was negotiation. Prime Minister Georges Pompidou, who had urged conciliation toward students, now led talks with unions and employers at the Ministry of Social Affairs on the Rue de Grenelle on May 25 to 27. The resulting "Grenelle agreements" offered substantial concessions: a rise in the minimum wage of about 35 percent, an average wage increase of around 10 percent, and commitments to reduce working hours and to recognize union organization within firms. These were significant gains, among the largest in postwar French labor history. Yet the agreements were a package of proposed terms, not an immediate settlement. When CGT leader Georges Séguy presented them to workers at the Renault plant at Boulogne-Billancourt, one of the symbolic strongholds of the French working class, the workers rejected them, and strikes continued in many sectors for days and, in some cases, weeks. The episode showed both the limits of union leaders' control over their members and the fact that many workers sought more than wages.

The decisive turn came on May 29 and 30. De Gaulle abruptly left Paris on May 29, without informing most of his government, and flew to the French military base at Baden-Baden, in West Germany, where he met General Jacques Massu, commander of French forces there. The precise purposes of the trip have been debated: historians discuss whether he was seeking assurances of army loyalty, considering resignation, or deliberately creating uncertainty. When he returned, on May 30, he gave a short, forceful radio address. He announced that he would not resign or replace Pompidou, dissolved the National Assembly, and called legislative elections. He warned of a threat of "totalitarian communism" and called on citizens to organize in defense of the republic. That afternoon several hundred thousand supporters of the government marched along the Champs-Élysées, in a rally that Gaullists estimated far higher than most independent estimates.

The elections, held on June 23 and 30, produced a sweeping Gaullist victory. The Gaullist party, renamed the Union for the Defense of the Republic (UDR) during the campaign, won an absolute majority of seats in the National Assembly on its own, a result without precedent in the Fifth Republic. The left, including the Communist Party and the federation around Mitterrand, lost seats. Several factors explain this outcome. The strikes were beginning to wear on public patience; many voters feared disorder, violence, and a Communist takeover; the government, by agreeing to Grenelle and by reopening the universities, had addressed some grievances; and the Communist Party, by its own caution, had distanced itself from revolutionary action and left the radical students isolated. The electoral system, based on two rounds in single-member districts, also magnified the Gaullist advantage. The vote was a verdict against disruption, not necessarily against reform, and the government that won it acted on reform in several areas.

The most important was higher education. The Education Minister, Edgar Faure, introduced the law of November 12, 1968, known as the Faure Law, which reorganized French universities by granting them autonomy, creating elected councils with student and junior staff representation, and encouraging multidisciplinary teaching units in place of the old faculties. The law was a response to student demands, but it also reflected government hopes of regaining control through a reform on its own terms. It led to the creation of new institutions, among them the experimental University of Paris VIII at Vincennes, and to the breakup of the old University of Paris into several universities. Within the workplace, a law of December 1968 recognized union sections inside firms. De Gaulle's own political fate, however, was less favorable: after losing a referendum on regional and senatorial reform in April 1969, he resigned, an outcome in which the legacy of the 1968 crisis, in the form of a general challenge to presidential authority, played an indirect role.

Section 130.8

What Did May 1968 Change? Revolution, Reform, and Historical Interpretation

Few events in modern European history have generated as much interpretation. At the time, the French sociologist Raymond Aron, a conservative critic, described the events as a kind of collective psychodrama, an eruption of expressive protest without a realistic revolutionary program. Others, such as the sociologist Alain Touraine, saw the emergence of a new type of social conflict centered on knowledge, culture, and authority in advanced industrial society. Marxists debated whether the strike had revealed the potential for a workers' revolution that party leaders refused to seize. By the twentieth anniversary of the events, in 1988, and the fortieth, in 2008, the discussion had become a debate about the legacy of an entire generation.

A first distinction is between immediate political outcomes and longer-term institutional and cultural consequences. In the short term, the revolution that some activists hoped for did not occur: the Gaullists were re-elected, the institutions of the Fifth Republic survived, and the Communist Party and unions, rather than the student groups, retained the dominant position on the left. Many radical groups fragmented in the early 1970s into rival Maoist, Trotskyist, and anarchist organizations. A reading that treats the events as a failed revolution is therefore defensible on its own terms. But the fact that the political order did not change does not mean that nothing changed. The Faure Law transformed the governance of universities. The Grenelle agreements and the law on union sections altered workplace relations. New political currents, including the autogestion (self-management) movement, ecological activism, and later feminist and regional movements, drew on 1968's repertoire. Cultural authority, including the authority of teachers, priests, parents, and employers, was challenged in ways that persisted.

A second distinction concerns what kind of event 1968 was. Interpretations differ on whether it is best seen as a student revolt, a workers' strike, a generational conflict, a cultural revolution, or a convergence of several distinct movements. The tension is real. Students and workers marched together on May 13, but their goals, organizations, and tactics differed. Michelle Zancarini-Fournel's work, mentioned in the chapter opening, argues for a "moment 68" that extended in time and space beyond the weeks in Paris, including regional, rural, and working-class experiences and the 1970s struggles over factories, prisons, immigration, and women's rights. Kristin Ross, in a study of the French memory of May, argued that dominant accounts, focused on students and later careers of famous ex-activists, obscured the participation of workers and immigrants. Arthur Marwick, working on the broader 1960s across several countries, argued for a "cultural revolution" of long duration, from around 1958 to 1974, in which changes in behavior and attitudes mattered more than political movements.

A third problem is attribution. Avoid treating every subsequent social liberalization as a direct result of the protests. The Neuwirth Law on contraception was adopted in December 1967, before the May events. Many reforms of the 1970s were enacted by governments and parliaments in response to long-term social change, pressure from reform organizations, and the work of experts and reformist politicians. At the same time, the climate of questioning that 1968 helped foster increased pressure for reform and changed what was thinkable. A careful historian will specify, for each reform, how much weight to place on movement pressure, institutional initiative, and prior trends.

Historical Debate

What Did May 1968 Change?

The question: Should May 1968 be understood chiefly as a failed political revolution, or as a successful social and cultural transformation?

Interpretation A: A limited political outcome with real but modest reforms. Some historians emphasize that the government survived, the Gaullists won the elections, and the radical groups fragmented. On this reading the main legacies were educational reform and workplace concessions, and claims of a cultural revolution overstate what was a limited and in some respects conservative outcome. Others add that capitalism proved adaptable: sociologists Luc Boltanski and Ève Chiapello argued that some themes of the 1968 critique, such as autonomy and creativity, were later absorbed into new management practice.
Interpretation B: A deep and lasting transformation of authority and everyday life. Other historians stress that the movement transformed relations between teachers and students, employers and workers, and men and women, and opened spaces for feminist, ecological, and other movements. On this view, a purely political yardstick misses the long-term effects on norms of authority, personal autonomy, and participation.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on legislation and election results, union and employer records, opinion surveys, movement documents, and oral histories. They differ in how they weigh short-term political results against long-term cultural change, and in how they treat later memory, including political attacks on the "legacy of 68" in French politics, notably by Nicolas Sarkozy in his 2007 presidential campaign.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians evaluate protest movements generally: whether they are judged by seizure of power, by institutional reform, or by changes in culture and expectation.

Section 130.9

West Germany: The Student Movement, Rudi Dutschke, and the Extraparliamentary Opposition

The West German movement was shaped by the legacy of National Socialism more directly than any other. Younger Germans born during or after the war confronted a society that had prospered under the "economic miracle" while avoiding sustained public reckoning with the crimes of the Third Reich. Trials of former perpetrators in the early 1960s, notably the Auschwitz trial held in Frankfurt from 1963 to 1965, had made the genocide a subject of public discussion, as Chapter 123 explained, but many students believed that former officials, judges, professors, and journalists with Nazi pasts remained in office. In 1966 the Christian Democrat Kurt Georg Kiesinger, who had been a member of the Nazi Party, became chancellor at the head of a grand coalition with the Social Democrats, an arrangement that seemed to many young critics to confirm the exhaustion of parliamentary opposition.

The Socialist German Student Union (SDS), originally the student organization of the Social Democratic Party, had been expelled from the party in 1961 for its radicalism and became a center of New Left thought. Its members, including Rudi Dutschke, a young sociology student from East Germany who had moved to West Berlin in 1961, combined study of Marx and the Frankfurt School with criticism of American policy in Vietnam and of the West German political establishment. Together with other groups they formed what became known as the extraparliamentary opposition, the Außerparlamentarische Opposition (APO), a loose network that rejected the grand coalition's authority and, particularly, its proposed emergency legislation. These emergency laws, which would permit the government to limit civil liberties in a state of emergency, aroused fears among students, intellectuals, and some trade unionists of a return to authoritarian practice; they were nevertheless passed by the Bundestag on May 30, 1968.

Two events radicalized the movement. On June 2, 1967, during a demonstration in West Berlin against the visit of the Shah of Iran, a student, Benno Ohnesorg, was shot and killed by a police officer, Karl-Heinz Kurras. Kurras was acquitted at trial; in 2009 it was revealed that he had also been an informant of the East German Stasi, a fact that complicated, but did not alter, the established account of the shooting itself. The killing convinced many students that the state used violence to suppress dissent and led to rapid growth in the movement. The second event was the attempted assassination of Dutschke on April 11, 1968, by a young man, Josef Bachmann, who shot him in the head on a Berlin street. Dutschke survived but suffered lasting injury and died in 1979 from complications related to it. Many activists blamed the hostile campaign against students conducted by the newspapers of the publisher Axel Springer, notably the mass-circulation Bild, and during the Easter weekend of 1968 protesters attempted to blockade Springer printing plants and distribution, leading to violent clashes with police, in which two people died. The debates that followed concerned police conduct, the role of the media, and the boundary between legitimate protest and violence.

Section 130.10

Italy's Long 1968: Universities, Student Radicalism, and the New Left

In Italy the protest began earlier than in France and lasted much longer, which is why historians often speak of the "long 1968" extending, in some interpretations, from the mid-1960s to the late 1970s. Higher education had expanded rapidly, but the system was crowded, underfunded, and governed by powerful chairs. In 1966 students at the new Faculty of Sociology in Trento, the first in Italy, began agitations over curriculum and teaching, and in 1967 occupations spread: at the Catholic University of Milan in November, at the University of Turin's Palazzo Campana, and then at Pisa, Rome, and many other institutions. Demands included more democratic participation in university decisions, reform of examinations, the elimination of authoritarian practices, and the right to hold assemblies. Italian student activists also condemned the Vietnam War and criticized the Christian Democrat-dominated state and the compromises of the established parties of the left.

The confrontation at Valle Giulia in Rome on March 1, 1968, became a symbol. Students attempted to occupy the Faculty of Architecture, which police had cleared earlier, and, when they approached, clashes broke out between students and police; the students fought back, and many police and students were injured. The poet and filmmaker Pier Paolo Pasolini responded in Il PCI ai giovani!!, published in L’Espresso in June 1968, with a deliberately provocative class analysis. He expressed sympathy for young police recruits drawn from poorer families and challenged the relatively privileged background of many university protesters, while explicitly opposing the police as an institution and acknowledging that the students had the just political position and police the unjust one. His argument provoked controversy across the left because it separated sympathy for particular officers’ social origins from approval of state repression. The "battle of Valle Giulia" marked a shift from sit-ins and assemblies to a more militant student politics, and it was treated by many activists as a founding episode, in which students no longer accepted being passive victims of police action.

Documentary context: Bibliographic notice for Pasolini’s June 16, 1968 publication in L’Espresso

The Italian movement's relation to established left organizations was complicated. The Italian Communist Party (PCI), the largest communist party in western Europe, was a mass organization with deep roots in working-class communities, but its leaders were wary of student radicalism, partly because they sought to demonstrate their reliability as participants in national politics. Student activists, for their part, often criticized the PCI as bureaucratic and reformist. Several organizations emerged to the left of the party, among them Lotta Continua, Potere Operaio, and Avanguardia Operaia, as well as the Il Manifesto group, expelled from the PCI in 1969, which became a newspaper. The groups differed in doctrine and strategy, but they shared an interest in linking students with industrial workers, an aim that the events of 1969 seemed to make achievable.

It is therefore misleading to speak of an Italian "1968" confined to students and a single year. The protests continued through 1969 and the 1970s in universities, schools, factories, neighborhoods, and the Catholic world, where groups of priests and lay Catholics also questioned authority. The Italian government liberalized access to university in 1969, allowing graduates of all secondary school tracks to enroll, which accelerated growth and reinforced the sense of crisis in universities. The long chronology also reflects Italy's particular combination of rapid industrial change, regional inequality, strong Catholic and Communist subcultures, and weak governing coalitions, which gave protest a persistent presence in public life.

Section 130.11

Workers, Factories, and Italy's Hot Autumn of 1969

The linking of student radicalism and labor militancy was more complete in Italy than in France, though it was never simple. Italian industry had grown rapidly during the "economic miracle," drawing hundreds of thousands of migrants from the poor south to factories in Turin, Milan, and other northern cities, where they confronted housing shortages, prejudice, and disciplined factory regimes. At large factories such as Fiat's Mirafiori complex in Turin, many assembly-line workers had little formal skill and few effective channels for grievances, while unions represented mainly skilled workers and had limited presence on the shop floor. From 1968 onward, young workers, many of them migrants, began to organize through assemblies, internal committees, and strike action, sometimes in cooperation with students who distributed leaflets outside the gates and sometimes independently of them.

The wave culminated in the autunno caldo, or Hot Autumn, of 1969, when the contracts of several million workers, notably in engineering and metalworking, were up for renewal. In the preceding months, workers had organized strikes against workplace conditions, wage differentials, and the pace of work; on July 3 a demonstration at Corso Traiano in Turin, outside Fiat, turned into a day-long confrontation with police. In the autumn, strikes of varying forms, including rolling stoppages and "hiccup" strikes designed to disrupt production with minimal loss of wages, spread across industry, and workers established factory delegates and held assemblies in which wage, hours, and workplace democracy were debated. The settlements of late 1969 and early 1970 gave large wage increases, often with flat-rate rises that benefited the lowest paid, shorter hours, and the right to hold assemblies in the workplace. Estimates of working hours lost in 1969 run to several hundred million, the highest level in the postwar period up to that point.

The unrest also occurred in a context of political violence. On December 12, 1969, a bomb exploded in the National Agricultural Bank in Piazza Fontana, Milan, killing seventeen people and wounding many more. Police initially blamed anarchists, and an anarchist railway worker, Giuseppe Pinelli, died in police custody in Milan days later, a case that produced intense controversy. Later judicial investigations pointed to neo-fascist responsibility and to protection of the perpetrators by elements of the state, though the legal outcomes were protracted and inconclusive. The episode is often described as the beginning of the "strategy of tension," a term used for attempts, by right-wing extremists and, according to investigators, some state officials, to provoke fear and push the country toward authoritarian solutions. It illustrates that the protest wave was also met by violent reaction.

Section 130.12

Britain and the Wider European Protest Generation

Britain's protests were smaller in scale and less revolutionary in aim than those of France or Italy, yet the country was a central site of the cultural transformations discussed in Section 130.3. British society in the late 1960s was marked by a Labour government under Harold Wilson that combined social reform with economic difficulty, including a devaluation of the pound in 1967. Antiwar demonstrations, organized by the Vietnam Solidarity Campaign, brought thousands to Grosvenor Square outside the American embassy in London on March 17 and October 27, 1968, with the March demonstration featuring violent clashes with police. Student activism included sit-ins at the London School of Economics and at several universities and colleges of art, and the campaign for nuclear disarmament maintained a tradition of mass peaceful protest. Political violence of the kind seen in Germany and Italy was rare, though a small group, the Angry Brigade, carried out bombings in 1970–71.

British legislation in these years was also significant, though not simply a product of protest. The Abortion Act 1967 and the Sexual Offences Act 1967 were passed as private members' bills with government support, following campaigns by reform organizations and by liberal members of both parties, and the Divorce Reform Act of 1969 changed the grounds of divorce. These reforms were part of what some contemporaries called the "permissive society," a phrase used both approvingly and as a term of abuse. They belonged to a reformist tradition of Parliament and pressure groups, which differed from the confrontational mobilization of continental Europe.

Elsewhere in Europe the movements took distinctive shapes. In the Netherlands, the Provo movement of 1965–67 in Amsterdam combined satirical happenings with proposals for urban reform, and in 1969 students occupied the Maagdenhuis, the University of Amsterdam's administrative building, in a dispute over university governance. In Denmark, Sweden, and Norway, students and activists campaigned over Vietnam and university democracy; in Sweden, Prime Minister Olof Palme took part in an antiwar march in February 1968, and Scandinavian states later developed influential social policies on gender and family. Student protests also occurred in Spain, where they challenged the Franco dictatorship; in Portugal; and in Yugoslavia, where Belgrade students demonstrated in June 1968 for social equality and democratic reforms. In Poland, student demonstrations in March 1968 were suppressed and accompanied by an official campaign with antisemitic overtones, and in Czechoslovakia the reform movement of the Prague Spring was ended by invasion in August; those events are examined in Chapter 129, and the point to note here is that students in eastern and western Europe sometimes used similar words of freedom while confronting very different states.

Multiple Perspectives

The Protest Generation as Seen by Different Groups

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified by historians. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not have equal power or responsibility.

Student activists: Many saw themselves as challenging outdated hierarchies in universities and in society, as acting in solidarity with people suffering from war and colonialism, and as creating new forms of democratic participation. Their views varied widely, from reformers who wanted better governance to revolutionaries who rejected the existing order.
Industrial workers: Workers often valued the students' challenge to authority but concentrated on wages, hours, job security, and dignity at work. Some welcomed cooperation, others distrusted privileged students, and many looked to unions and parties rather than to movements formed outside them.
Parents and older citizens: Many parents who had lived through war, occupation, and scarcity regarded protest as ingratitude and feared disorder. Others, especially those with experience of fascism, sympathized with young people's moral questioning or with the demands of working-class movements. Attitudes often depended on class, religion, and political loyalty.
Governments and police: Governments combined repression, as in the police actions in Paris and West Berlin, with negotiation and reform. Officials generally regarded themselves as defending order and legitimate institutions, and some used the language of anticommunism to describe protest, while others recognized grievances and enacted reforms.
Women participants: Women took part in student and labor movements but often found themselves assigned secondary roles and ignored when raising gender issues, an experience that led many to form independent organizations, as Division IV explains.

What shared concerns defined this transnational generation, and what separated them? Across countries, activists criticized hierarchy in universities, protested the Vietnam War, sought wider participation in decisions affecting their lives, and distrusted both established parties and Soviet communism. They exchanged ideas through travel, publications, and news broadcasts, and they used similar forms of protest. Their differences were as important. In France, protest briefly merged with a general strike and the state was shaken, but institutions survived. In West Germany, the confrontation with the Nazi past and with police gave protest a moral dimension and led to a long contest over participation and security. In Italy, student mobilization joined a powerful labor movement and a persistent political crisis. In Britain, protest was smaller and tended to flow into reform. In the dictatorships of the south and the communist states of the east, protest faced repression that made open organization difficult. The shared label "1968" therefore conceals a mosaic, and the interpretive task is to explain both the pattern and the variations.

Second-Wave Feminism, Sexuality, and the Transformation of Gender Relations

Section 130.13

The Origins of Second-Wave Feminism and the Challenge to Gender Hierarchies

Women's position in postwar European societies was marked by a combination of formal progress and persistent inequality. Women had gained the vote in most European countries by the late 1940s, as earlier chapters explain, and girls' access to secondary and higher education expanded in the 1950s and 1960s. Yet many legal and social arrangements assumed that a woman's primary role was that of wife and mother. In many countries, married women's rights over property, earnings, and employment were limited by law or custom. In France, married women could not take up paid work or open a bank account without their husband's permission until a reform of 1965. In West Germany, the civil code until 1977 stated that a wife was responsible for managing the household and could take paid work only insofar as it was compatible with her marital and family duties. Italian family law long gave the husband authority as head of the family. Wage scales commonly set lower pay for women's jobs, and many employers excluded married women or dismissed them on marriage or pregnancy. Domestic work and childcare, largely unpaid, fell mainly on women, even when they also worked outside the home.

Women's employment was nevertheless growing, particularly in service, clerical, and public-sector jobs and in light industry, and more married women and mothers remained in or returned to paid work. This created a contradiction between the ideal of the full-time housewife, promoted in advertising and some welfare policies, and the reality of many women's lives. Educational expansion also meant that more women entered universities, where they discovered both opportunities and discrimination. Books such as Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949), which analyzed women's subordination as a social construction rather than a natural fact, and later works such as Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) in the United States, circulated among educated women and prepared ground for a new politics.

The immediate impetus for many younger feminists came from the New Left. Women who participated in student movements, unions, and left-wing parties often found that, while the movements preached liberation, men dominated leadership, and women were frequently expected to do secretarial or domestic tasks and were sometimes dismissed when they raised issues of gender. Their frustration led many to meet separately, and from the late 1960s autonomous women's groups formed in many countries. This is a key point: women's liberation was not simply a consequence of male-led student activism. Feminist organizers had their own intellectual traditions, drawn from earlier feminism, existentialism, psychoanalysis, Marxism, and Christian and liberal reform, and their own grievances and campaigns concerning pay, work, domestic labor, contraception, abortion, violence, and representation. Their relationship with the New Left was one of both debt and conflict.

Historical Vocabulary
Second-Wave Feminism

The label, widely used by historians, for the feminist movements that emerged from the later 1960s and 1970s in Europe and North America, distinguished from the "first wave" of campaigns for suffrage and legal rights in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Second-wave feminists broadened the agenda to include equal pay, workplace discrimination, domestic labor, sexuality, reproduction, violence, and the claim that "the personal is political," meaning that private experiences of inequality had public causes and could be subjects of political action. The "wave" metaphor is imperfect: it suggests a single movement and neglects continuities with earlier decades, as well as the diversity of liberal, socialist, radical, trade-union, and Catholic feminisms. In this chapter the term refers to a family of movements, not one organization or ideology.

These groups developed distinctive methods. Consciousness-raising groups, small meetings in which women discussed their personal experiences of work, marriage, sexuality, and childcare, were intended to show that individual difficulties were shared and had social roots. Feminists published newsletters, pamphlets, and magazines, organized demonstrations and street theater, and set up women's centers, health collectives, and refuges. Many valued non-hierarchical organization, rejecting the leadership structures of parties and unions, although this created its own difficulties. The movement also contained serious disagreements: between socialist feminists, who tied women's liberation to class struggle; radical feminists, who emphasized patriarchy as a distinct system of domination; liberal feminists, who sought equal opportunity through law and existing institutions; trade-union women, who focused on workplace rights; and later lesbian feminists and women from migrant and minority communities, who criticized the movement's tendency to speak mainly for white, heterosexual, middle-class women.

Section 130.14

Women's Liberation in Britain: Equal Pay, Employment, and Collective Action

In Britain the women's movement emerged from a combination of trade-union activity, left-wing politics, and new feminist organizing. The first dramatic event was the strike of women sewing machinists at Ford's Dagenham plant in Essex in June 1968. About 187 women, who stitched car seat covers, were classified as unskilled, a grading that paid them considerably less than male workers, and they stopped work in protest. The strike lasted for several weeks, brought much of Ford's British production to a halt for lack of seat covers, and drew the intervention of Barbara Castle, the Secretary of State for Employment, who negotiated a settlement that raised their pay, though not to the level of the men. The Dagenham strike was not a feminist movement in the strict sense, since the women were trade unionists seeking fair pay, but it brought equal pay to national attention and helped generate support for legislation.

The women's liberation movement as such coalesced at the first national conference, held at Ruskin College, Oxford, in late February and early March 1970. Organized largely by women associated with the history workshop movement and left-wing groups, the conference attracted several hundred participants, far more than expected, and adopted four demands: equal pay, equal education and job opportunities, free contraception and abortion on demand, and free twenty-four-hour nurseries. Further demands were added in the following years, including legal and financial independence for women, an end to discrimination against lesbians, and freedom from violence and sexual coercion. Local groups, newsletters, and campaigns multiplied, and in November 1970 activists protested at the Miss World contest in London, an event often seen as a landmark of public feminist action.

Legislative change followed. The Equal Pay Act, passed by Parliament in May 1970, required that women's contracts include an equality clause, giving them the right to the same pay as men for the same or broadly similar work, or for work rated as equivalent under a job evaluation scheme. A vital factual distinction is that the Act, although approved in 1970, gave employers a five-year transition period and did not come into force until December 29, 1975. In the same year, the Sex Discrimination Act 1975 prohibited discrimination on grounds of sex in employment, education, and the provision of goods and services, and established the Equal Opportunities Commission. Subsequent amendment and litigation, including the later addition of the concept of work of equal value, were required to make the equal-pay principle effective, because many women worked in sectors where there were no male comparators.

Section 130.15

Feminism in France, Italy, and West Germany

In France the women's movement acquired national visibility after 1968. Small groups formed in the aftermath of May, and in August 1970 a handful of women laid a wreath at the Arc de Triomphe to honor the wife of the unknown soldier, an action usually taken as the public birth of the Mouvement de libération des femmes (MLF). The name, modeled on movements of national liberation, was applied by the press to a diverse network of groups, including radical feminists, socialist feminists, and a group around the psychoanalyst Antoinette Fouque that stressed sexual difference. Feminists published journals, held public meetings, and organized demonstrations, and they campaigned for access to contraception and abortion. Abortion was illegal in France and punishable by imprisonment, but was widely practiced, often clandestinely and dangerously, and women with means could travel to Britain or the Netherlands, where it was legal under conditions.

A turning point came in April 1971, when the weekly Le Nouvel Observateur published a manifesto signed by 343 women, including well-known writers and actresses, who declared that they had had abortions and demanded free access to contraception and abortion. The text was drafted with the participation of Simone de Beauvoir. It exposed the signatories to possible prosecution, though none was prosecuted, and it was widely discussed. In 1972 the trial at Bobigny of a young woman, Marie-Claire Chevalier, who had been prosecuted after an abortion following rape, and of the adult women who helped her, was defended by the lawyer Gisèle Halimi, who turned it into a public indictment of the law; the defendants were acquitted or received light sentences, and the law's enforcement was undermined. In 1973 the organization Choisir and a movement for freedom of abortion and contraception widened the campaign.

The legislative outcome came under a new centrist-right president, Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, who in 1974 appointed Simone Veil, a magistrate and Holocaust survivor, as health minister. Veil presented a bill to decriminalize abortion under defined conditions. The debate in the National Assembly in November 1974 was bitter, with sharp attacks from some conservatives, but the bill passed with the votes of the left and part of the governing majority. The Veil Law of January 17, 1975, permitted abortion in the first ten weeks of pregnancy under conditions of medical supervision and counseling, initially for a five-year trial period; it was made permanent in December 1979. Reimbursement by social insurance followed only in 1982, a reminder that legal availability and practical access were not the same. France also reformed divorce in 1975 and the legal status of parental authority in 1970, replacing the father's exclusive authority with joint parental authority.

Italy's feminist movement developed in a country where the Catholic Church and the Christian Democratic Party exercised great influence and where divorce and abortion were illegal. Feminist groups such as Rivolta Femminile, the Movimento di Liberazione della Donna, and many local collectives organized from 1970 onward, along with large organizations of women connected to the Communist Party and Catholic associations. Divorce was legalized by law in December 1970, after a long parliamentary campaign led by Socialist and Liberal deputies, supported by a lay movement. Catholic and Christian Democratic forces then promoted a referendum to repeal the law. On May 12 and 13, 1974, Italian voters rejected repeal, with roughly 59 percent voting to retain divorce, a result that surprised many commentators and indicated that Italian society had moved further from Church teaching than leaders had assumed. Family law was reformed in 1975, ending the legal primacy of the husband. After years of feminist campaigns and widespread illegal abortion, Parliament approved Law No. 194 in May 1978, allowing abortion under conditions during the first ninety days and later in cases of danger to health. The law remained contested, in part because it permitted doctors to refuse to perform abortions on grounds of conscience, a provision that over time limited access in some regions. A referendum in 1981 rejected both an attempt to repeal the law and a proposal for more radical liberalization.

West German feminism was shaped by the experience of the student movement and by a legal and political context in which the Federal Constitutional Court played a central role. Women's groups formed in West Berlin and Frankfurt from 1968, and a well-known early action was the "tomato throwing" at a meeting of the SDS in September 1968, when a woman delegate threw tomatoes at male leaders who had refused to discuss women's issues. Feminist networks challenged laws on marriage and work, and campaigned against Paragraph 218 of the penal code, which criminalized abortion. In June 1971, the magazine Stern published a declaration signed by 374 women stating that they had had abortions, a campaign associated with the journalist Alice Schwarzer, who later founded the feminist magazine Emma in 1977. In 1974 the Bundestag passed a law permitting abortion in the first twelve weeks, but in February 1975 the Constitutional Court struck it down as incompatible with the constitutional protection of life. A revised law in 1976 allowed abortion only on specific grounds, such as medical or social "indications," following counseling. Reform of the civil code in 1977 abolished the clause defining the wife's household duty and her employment as conditional on family obligations. German feminism was thus characterized by lively organization, strong cultural influence, and legal setbacks.

Comparisons show a pattern. In all three countries feminist movements pressed for contraception, abortion, equal pay, and legal equality in marriage; in all three they achieved significant reforms within about a decade; and in all three they met strong opposition from churches, conservative politicians, and some doctors. Differences reflected national institutions: in France, a mobilization culminating in a government bill; in Italy, the confrontation with a powerful Church and the use of referendum; in West Germany, the arbitration of constitutional law. Feminists also disagreed among themselves about strategy, including whether to work within parties and legislatures or to remain autonomous, and about whether to emphasize equality with men or women's difference.

Section 130.16

Sexuality, Reproductive Rights, Family Life, and Changing Social Norms

Feminism was one part of a wider change in attitudes and laws concerning sexuality, marriage, and the family. The availability of effective contraception was central. Oral contraceptives had been introduced in Europe in the early 1960s, but legal and practical access varied. In France, a law of 1920 had prohibited contraceptive propaganda and sales, and it was only with the Neuwirth Law of December 28, 1967, introduced by a Gaullist deputy, Lucien Neuwirth, that contraception was legalized under regulated conditions. Implementing decrees came slowly, and the law was fully operational only in the early 1970s, demonstrating the difference between a legal change and its practical availability. In Britain, the National Health Service (Family Planning) Act 1967 allowed local authorities to provide contraceptive advice regardless of marital status, and access for unmarried women expanded gradually. In Italy the ban on the sale of contraceptives was removed by a Constitutional Court ruling in 1971, and in West Germany contraceptive pills were widely used from the 1960s, with a marked fall in birth rates from the middle of the decade. In Catholic Spain and Portugal, under dictatorships, contraception remained restricted for longer.

Reform of laws on homosexuality followed distinct paths. In England and Wales, the Sexual Offences Act 1967 decriminalized certain private homosexual acts between consenting men over twenty-one, a limited change that did not apply in Scotland until 1980 or Northern Ireland until 1982 and excluded the armed forces and merchant navy. In West Germany, the 1969 reform of Paragraph 175, a provision inherited from the nineteenth century and sharpened under the Nazis, decriminalized sexual acts between adult men over twenty-one, though legal discrimination persisted and the paragraph was not completely repealed until 1994. In the early 1970s gay and lesbian liberation movements emerged in several countries, including the Gay Liberation Front in London in 1970, the Front homosexuel d'action révolutionnaire in France in 1971, and the Italian group FUORI! in 1971, while in West Germany the 1971 film It Is Not the Homosexual Who Is Perverse, but the Society in Which He Lives by Rosa von Praunheim helped spark activism. These movements overlapped with feminism, particularly in lesbian organizing, but they were also separate and sometimes conflicted with it.

Sexual freedom did not remove inequality. Section 130.17

Music, Cinema, Television, Publishing, and the Transformation of Popular Culture

The cultural dimension of the period was as important as its politics. Popular music remained at the center. British groups, along with continental performers, reached huge audiences through records, radio, and concerts, and by the end of the 1960s rock music had acquired an ambition to be art as well as entertainment. Festivals, such as the Isle of Wight Festival of 1970, drew large crowds, and in West Germany a distinctive experimental scene, later called Krautrock, developed. Music did not carry a single message: some songs expressed protest, others celebrated romance or escapism, and many listeners ignored politics entirely. The relationship between popular music and political radicalism was therefore one of affinity and overlap, not identity.

Cinema offered another arena. In France, the New Wave directors of the late 1950s and 1960s, among them Jean-Luc Godard and François Truffaut, had changed film style, and Godard's work became increasingly political in the later 1960s. In May 1968 filmmakers, including Godard and Truffaut, forced the abandonment of the Cannes Film Festival in solidarity with the strikers. Italian filmmakers, among them Pier Paolo Pasolini, Bernardo Bertolucci, and, in the early 1970s, Elio Petri, produced films addressing class, sexuality, and political power. In West Germany the Oberhausen Manifesto of 1962 had called for a new German cinema, and directors such as Rainer Werner Fassbinder, Werner Herzog, and Wim Wenders gave it international recognition in the 1970s, often dealing with the Nazi past and postwar society. Experimental theatre, including the Living Theatre and the Italian playwright Dario Fo, took political drama to workplaces and neighborhoods.

Publishing and media also changed. Cheap paperbacks, led by firms such as Penguin, brought critical theory and radical political writing to wider readerships. Movements produced their own newspapers and magazines: the Italian daily il manifesto, the French Libération (founded in 1973), the British underground press with titles such as International Times and Oz, whose editors were prosecuted for obscenity in 1971, and numerous feminist journals. Television, especially public broadcasters, was both an instrument of government and a site of conflict, as the 1968 strike at France's ORTF showed. Technology made reproduction cheap: offset printing, photocopying, and cassette recordings allowed small groups to circulate material and helped carry styles across borders.

A central analytical distinction is between commercial success and political influence. Industries were quick to market the appearance of rebellion, through fashion, advertising, and record sales, and critics argued that the market converted opposition into consumption. Others replied that commercial channels were also how alternative ideas reached mass audiences. The fact that some cultural products challenged convention did not mean that every audience read them as political statements, and many fans enjoyed them for pleasure. The more fruitful question is how audiences used cultural material: as entertainment, as identity, as information, or, for some, as a stimulus to action.

Section 130.18

Family, Religion, Education, and Changing Everyday Expectations

Change reached far beyond universities and organized movements, though more slowly and unevenly. Over the 1960s and 1970s, marriage rates in much of northern and western Europe began to fall, ages at first marriage in some countries rose after earlier declines, divorce and cohabitation increased, and families became smaller as fertility fell. More married women and mothers worked outside the home, and schooling extended; comprehensive secondary schooling, in Britain and in Sweden for example, and expanded vocational and academic education elsewhere, opened options to larger numbers of children. Authority within the family shifted in law and practice: in France, parental authority became joint in 1970; in West Germany and Italy, legal reforms in the 1970s moved toward the equality of spouses. Child-rearing manuals and schools increasingly stressed discussion over punishment, although physical discipline remained common in many households and schools.

Everyday Life and Social History

Family and Domestic Life, 1960s and 1970s

For many households, the most visible changes were in the home. A growing number of families owned washing machines, refrigerators, and televisions, which altered housework and leisure, though they did not abolish the unequal division of domestic labor. Surveys of time use from the period generally show women performing most housework and childcare even when employed outside the home, a "double burden" that feminists made a subject of political debate. Rural families in southern Italy, Spain, Portugal, and parts of eastern France and Germany often lived with older patterns of extended households, religious observance, and patriarchal authority, while urban professional families were more exposed to new ideas.

Housing mattered. Young couples in cities frequently began married life with parents or in cramped rented rooms, and the new housing estates on city edges offered modern amenities but weak community life, a source of what contemporaries called isolation among housewives. Migrant workers, from southern Italy to northern Italy, from Iberia and North Africa to France, and from Turkey and Yugoslavia to West Germany, often lived in separate dormitories or poor accommodations, and their families faced different pressures from those of the native-born, a subject developed in Chapter 128 and again in Chapter 137.

Experiences of the same changes differed sharply by class, region, and generation. A teenage daughter might welcome access to contraception and the prospect of a job, while her mother, who had raised a large family under strict rules, might see these changes as a threat to security. Historians therefore treat family change as a set of overlapping and contested transformations, not a single liberation.

Religion was among the institutions most affected, though not in a simple way. The Second Vatican Council (1962 to 1965) reformed Catholic liturgy, encouraged the use of vernacular languages in worship, expanded the role of the laity, and sought a new relationship with the modern world and with other Christians and religions. It raised expectations of change, and in some countries, including the Netherlands, and among Catholic activists in Italy and France, led to lively debate. But reform of worship and institutions was not identical with liberalization of sexual teaching. In July 1968 Pope Paul VI issued the encyclical Humanae Vitae, reaffirming the prohibition of artificial contraception, which disappointed many who had hoped for change and was widely ignored by Catholic laity in practice, while also provoking dissent among clergy and theologians. Church attendance declined markedly in several countries from the 1960s, particularly in the Netherlands, France, and Britain, and the process of secularization is sometimes dated to this period. This did not mean that religious commitment disappeared: many Europeans continued to practice or believe, the Church remained powerful in Italy, Ireland, Spain, and Poland, and new religious movements and forms of spirituality also emerged. Secularization differed greatly by region and confession, and Protestant and Orthodox traditions had their own trajectories.

The pace of change also varied by geography. Cities, universities, and the professional middle classes were more exposed to new ideas and lifestyles than small towns and rural areas. Northern Europe, notably Scandinavia and the Netherlands, tended to experience earlier change in attitudes to sexuality and gender than southern Europe, where dictatorships, conservative Catholicism, and rural social structures slowed it, though southern Italy and Spain were also transformed by migration, tourism, and television. The persistence of older institutions, including churches, families, local elites, and parties, should be emphasized as much as the changes: most Europeans in 1975 still lived in households headed by married couples, belonged to a church by baptism or custom, and voted for established parties.

Section 130.19

Backlash, Inequality, Radicalization, and the Limits of the Social Revolution

The changes of these years provoked strong opposition. Conservative politicians and commentators argued that protests and permissiveness threatened social order, family stability, and religious values; the Gaullist victory in 1968 and the "law and order" language used by governments in several countries reflected such fears. Disputes over educational authority led to curricular conflicts and to measures against radical teachers, such as West Germany's decree on civil servants. Churches and organizations of laypeople mobilized against divorce and abortion reform, as in the Italian referendum campaign of 1974 and the debates over the Veil Law in France, and some doctors invoked conscience. Employers resisted workplace reforms and equal-pay enforcement, and many ordinary citizens regarded feminist demands with suspicion or hostility, including some women who valued the role of mother and homemaker and felt that feminist rhetoric devalued it. Opposition was not simply reaction: it reflected values and interests that were also legitimate parts of the society under change.

A central question is who benefited. Students and university-educated people, many of them from middle-class families, gained from reforms in universities and from the opening of professions. Skilled and organized workers gained wages and rights, particularly in France and Italy. Women gained legal equality in marriage, formal rights to equal pay, and access to contraception and in many places abortion, but they remained concentrated in lower-paid occupations and in unpaid domestic work, and pay gaps persisted. Working-class women, rural populations, migrant families, and people with limited money or education often had less access to new freedoms or to the information, services, and legal means needed to use them. Immigrants and their children faced racism and legal restrictions that the protest movements, focused elsewhere, often neglected, though some activists, as Chapter 128 notes, did take up antiracist causes. Sexual minorities gained only partial legal recognition. Even in feminist movements, critics from working-class and minority communities pointed out that the agenda did not always represent them. Socially, the new openness in manners and sexuality sometimes meant freedom, but it could also reproduce exploitation, which feminists and others criticized.

Radicalization within smaller groups occurred in some countries and must be distinguished from the much larger nonviolent movements. In West Germany the Red Army Faction and the Movement 2 June, in Italy the Red Brigades and other groups, and in Britain the Angry Brigade used bombings, kidnappings, and assassinations, mainly in the 1970s. Italy's "years of lead" and West Germany's "German Autumn" of 1977, with the kidnapping and murder of the employers' leader Hanns Martin Schleyer and the deaths of imprisoned RAF leaders, were the most severe episodes. These groups arose from particular circumstances, including the experience of police violence, the belief that the postwar states were continuations of fascism, the influence of Third World guerrilla models, and the dynamics of small clandestine organizations. They were condemned by most former student leaders, by the labor movements, and by feminists, and they provoked state responses that restricted civil liberties. Treating them as the "true" outcome of 1968 would be as misleading as ignoring them.

Historical Debate

Genuine Emancipation, Selective Change, or Both?

The question: Did the protests and social changes of the 1960s and 1970s represent a broad emancipation of Europeans from restrictive authority, or a selective transformation chiefly of middle-class norms that left deeper inequalities in place?

Interpretation A: Broad emancipation. Some historians argue that the era expanded personal freedom, reduced the legal power of husbands, employers, teachers, and churches over individuals, gave women control over their fertility, decriminalized homosexual conduct, and created new rights and forms of participation that benefited large parts of the population, not only an elite.
Interpretation B: Selective transformation and persistent inequality. Others argue that the benefits were unevenly distributed, favoring educated, urban, and often white and heterosexual groups, that class, gender, ethnic, and regional inequalities persisted, and that commercial markets absorbed and monetized some of the new freedoms. On this view, the era's achievements were real but partial, and some of the movement's claims to universality were mistaken.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on legislation, wage and employment statistics, demographic series, opinion surveys, movement and union documents, oral histories, and studies of particular classes and regions. They differ in which groups they treat as typical and in the standards by which they measure change.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians assess social movements generally: by their formal achievements, by who gained them, or by the inequalities they left unaddressed. A synthesis often combines both interpretations, recognizing achievements and unresolved inequalities together.

Section 130.20

From Protest to Social Transformation: Review and Historical Synthesis

The chapter's arguments can now be brought together. Economic growth, educational expansion, and mass media helped create the conditions for protest: they produced a large, educated, and communicating young population with rising expectations, institutions that had not adapted, and shared references across borders. But these conditions did not determine the outcomes. They coexisted with long periods of quiescence, and the timing of the revolt depended on contingent events, such as the Berlin killing of Benno Ohnesorg, the police entry into the Sorbonne, and the spread of factory occupations after May 13. The outcomes also depended on political institutions, such as the French presidential system and its electoral rules, the West German grand coalition, and the Italian party system.

The relationships among the movements were complex. Student activism supplied example, energy, and a critique of authority; organized labor supplied mass participation and the capacity to gain concrete reforms, as in France's Grenelle agreements and Italy's Workers' Statute; feminist movements, drawing on the experience of the New Left and also on older traditions, shifted attention to unpaid labor, the body, and the family; sexual reform movements challenged legal and moral regulation; and cultural change spread new ideas of self-expression. These movements overlapped but also conflicted: students and union leaders distrusted one another, feminists criticized male radicals, and many workers and parents resisted cultural change. The important conclusion is that the history of 1968 is a history of interaction among distinct movements, not of a single wave.

It is also necessary to distinguish immediate political failure or compromise from reforms that developed over the following years. In the short term, no major western European government was overthrown by the movements; the Gaullists won in 1968, German and Italian governments weathered the crisis, and Britain's Labour government lost the 1970 election. Yet over the following decade many of the movements' concerns reached legislation: the Faure Law and the Workers' Statute, divorce reform, equal-pay and anti-discrimination legislation, and the legalization of abortion in France, Italy, and, with restrictions, West Germany. The longer perspective shows that movements often succeed indirectly, by changing the agenda and the expectations of institutions that then reform in their own way.

Continuity and Change Over Time

European Society Before and After 1968

What changed?

  • Universities acquired new structures of governance and student representation, and became mass institutions.
  • Workers in France and Italy gained wage increases and legal protection of union activity inside firms.
  • Women obtained greater legal equality in marriage, access to contraception, and in many countries legal abortion and formal equal-pay rights.
  • Homosexual conduct between adult men was partially decriminalized in England and Wales and West Germany, and gay and lesbian movements emerged.
  • Expectations about authority, participation, and personal choice shifted, and secularization advanced in many countries.

What remained continuous?

  • Existing party systems and constitutional structures survived in France, West Germany, Italy, and Britain.
  • Gender segregation of occupations and the gap between men's and women's pay persisted, as did the burden of unpaid domestic work.
  • Churches, family structures, and regional differences continued to shape life, particularly in southern Europe and rural areas.
  • Class and ethnic inequalities in education and employment remained.

Who experienced change most directly? University students and staff, organized industrial workers, and women of the educated and employed classes saw the most direct changes. Who experienced more continuity? Rural populations, older people, many working-class women, migrant families, and residents of the southern dictatorships. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that social transformation proceeds unevenly, and prevents both overstating and dismissing the significance of the protest era.

The consequences for conceptions of authority, individual freedom, gender equality, political participation, and social belonging were substantial but uneven. Authority came to require more explanation and consent, in schools, workplaces, families, and churches. Individual freedom, especially in matters of sexuality and family, acquired greater legal and cultural recognition. Gender equality advanced formally and partly in practice. Political participation expanded beyond voting to include new social movements, citizens' initiatives, and, later, environmental and other campaigns. Notions of belonging remained contested, as migration and ethnic diversity, discussed in Chapter 137, tested the limits of the new openness. The protest era cannot be reduced to either complete revolutionary success or failure.

The transition to Chapter 131 is direct. The changing social expectations described here unfolded alongside economic instability following the oil shocks of the early 1970s, international détente between the superpowers, and the transformation of southern European political systems, with the end of dictatorships in Portugal, Greece, and Spain. The protest generation's hopes were thus tested in a harsher economic climate, and the question of how Europe would combine social change with political and economic stability would shape the late Cold War.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the social and cultural upheavals of western Europe in the 1960s and 1970s, asking why prosperous and increasingly educated societies experienced a wave of challenges to authority and how those challenges contributed to lasting change. Its governing thesis is that the movements associated with 1968 challenged political and cultural authority, but their most enduring consequences often emerged through a longer transformation of universities, workplaces, families, laws, social movements, and everyday expectations.

Division I traced the social foundations. Postwar growth, a large cohort of young people, and expanding education created a generation with rising expectations. Universities grew faster than their governance and facilities, producing crowded lecture halls and conflict over authority. Youth culture, driven by music, radio, film, and fashion, created new identities and means of communication, although commercial culture and political dissent were not the same thing. International causes, above all the Vietnam War, together with anticolonial solidarity and disillusion with both Western capitalism and Soviet communism, gave rise to a New Left that criticized established parties of the left as well as the right.

Division II reconstructed the French crisis. Student protests at Nanterre, the March 22 Movement, the police entry into the Sorbonne on May 3, and the barricades of May 10 and 11 led to the one-day strike of May 13 and then to the largest general strike in French history, which involved millions of workers with their own grievances. The relationship between students and unions was marked by cooperation and distrust, and the CGT and the Communist Party worked to keep control of the strike. The Grenelle agreements offered substantial concessions, which many workers at first rejected. De Gaulle's address of May 30 shifted the crisis to the ballot box, and the Gaullist victory in June showed the limits of the movement politically, while the Faure Law and workplace reforms showed its institutional effects.

Division III widened the view to West Germany, Italy, Britain, and elsewhere. In West Germany, the movement was shaped by the legacy of National Socialism, the grand coalition, the killing of Benno Ohnesorg, and the attack on Rudi Dutschke, and its legacy included expanded participation, a more critical public memory, and also terrorism and the state's restrictive response. In Italy, a long 1968 linked student occupations to the Hot Autumn of 1969, which produced the Workers' Statute of 1970, while political violence, including the Piazza Fontana bombing, darkened the period. In Britain, protest was smaller and flowed more readily into parliamentary reform. The Netherlands, Scandinavia, and southern and eastern Europe showed further variations, and the comparison demonstrated that 1968 was an international wave composed of different national movements.

Division IV examined second-wave feminism and sexual reform. Women's liberation was not merely a product of the male-led student left: it drew on earlier feminist traditions and on women's own experiences in work, marriage, and political movements, and it developed distinct organizations, methods such as consciousness-raising, and a variety of tendencies. In Britain, the Dagenham strike and the Ruskin conference of 1970 led to the Equal Pay Act, passed in 1970 but in force only in December 1975, and the Sex Discrimination Act 1975. In France, the Manifesto of the 343 and the Bobigny trial led to the Veil Law of 1975; in Italy, feminist action, a divorce referendum in 1974, and Law 194 of 1978 changed family law and abortion; in West Germany, campaigns against Paragraph 218 produced a compromise shaped by the Constitutional Court.

Reforms concerning contraception, divorce, and homosexuality followed varied national paths and chronologies. The Neuwirth Law of 1967 in France and the Sexual Offences Act of 1967 in England and Wales preceded the main protest wave, and legal availability often differed from practical access. Gay and lesbian liberation movements emerged in the early 1970s, and the relationship between sexual freedom and continuing inequality was complex. The historical lesson is that attributing every liberalization to the protests oversimplifies a process in which governments, courts, churches, unions, and long-standing reform campaigns also played roles.

Division V turned to culture, everyday life, and legacy. Music, cinema, theatre, publishing, and alternative media circulated new ideas and styles across borders, and the market both spread and commercialized them. Family life, religion, and education changed, with falling fertility, rising divorce and cohabitation, expanding women's employment, and advancing secularization, but older institutions and regional inequalities persisted. The Second Vatican Council opened debate within Catholicism, while Humanae Vitae in 1968 reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception and was widely ignored.

The chapter also assessed backlash and limits. Conservative politicians, churches, employers, and many citizens opposed change, and some of their concerns were sincere and widely shared. The benefits of liberalization were unevenly distributed by class, gender, ethnicity, and region, and working-class women, rural populations, migrants, and sexual minorities often had less access to them. A small number of groups turned to terrorism, but they should be distinguished from the vast majority of activists, who pursued their aims through strikes, assemblies, elections, and legislation.

Historical interpretation of the period remains contested. The debate over whether 1968 was a failed revolution, a successful cultural transformation, a workers' struggle, a generational revolt, or a convergence of movements depends on what is measured and over what timescale, and the memory of 1968 has itself been used in political argument. The central analytical distinction of the chapter is between immediate political outcomes, which were modest, and longer-term institutional and cultural consequences, which were substantial but uneven.

In sum, postwar European prosperity did not eliminate demands for profound social change. The protests did not overthrow the major western European political systems, but they helped transform debates over university governance, labor relations, women's rights, sexuality, popular culture, and individual freedom. Chapter 131 will show how these changing expectations met economic instability, détente, and the democratization of southern Europe.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Baby boom
The rise in births after 1945, varying in timing and scale by country, that produced the large cohorts entering education and employment in the 1960s.
Mass university
A higher-education system enrolling a large share of an age group, in contrast to the earlier elite university; its growth outpaced governance reform.
Teenager
A term, imported from the United States, for young people as a distinct consumer and social category in the 1950s and 1960s.
Counterculture
Informal cultural movements rejecting dominant social norms through lifestyle, music, dress, and communal living; not identical to organized political protest.
New Left
An international current criticizing both Soviet communism and established left parties, emphasizing participation, culture, and anticolonial solidarity.
APO (Außerparlamentarische Opposition)
The West German extraparliamentary opposition of the late 1960s, a loose network opposing the grand coalition and emergency laws.
SDS
The Socialist German Student Union, expelled from the Social Democratic Party in 1961, a center of the West German New Left.
Teach-in
A protest form combining lectures and debate, often on the Vietnam War, spread from the United States to Europe.
Civil disobedience
Deliberate, usually nonviolent, breaking of a law or rule to protest injustice; distinct from riot or armed violence.
March 22 Movement
The loose coalition of Nanterre activists formed in 1968 that began the organized French student revolt.
General strike
A strike across many industries and services; in France in May 1968 it involved millions of workers in an unplanned and uneven wave.
Grenelle agreements
The package of wage and labor concessions negotiated on May 25 to 27, 1968, initially rejected by many strikers.
Faure Law
The French law of November 12, 1968, reorganizing universities with autonomy, participation, and multidisciplinary units.
Long 1968
The historians' term for protest cycles, especially in Italy, extending well beyond a single year.
Autunno caldo (Hot Autumn)
The wave of industrial strikes and assemblies in Italy in 1969, centered on contract renewals.
Workers' Statute
Italian Law No. 300 of May 20, 1970, protecting workers' freedoms, union activity, and against unjust dismissal.
Strategy of tension
A term for violence, beginning with the Piazza Fontana bombing in 1969, intended to provoke fear and authoritarian solutions in Italy.
Second-wave feminism
Feminist movements of the later 1960s and 1970s that expanded the agenda beyond suffrage to work, family, sexuality, and reproduction.
Consciousness-raising
Small-group discussion of personal experience intended to reveal shared social causes of inequality.
Women's liberation
The name used by many groups in the early 1970s for autonomous feminist organizing, notably in Britain and France.
Equal Pay Act 1970
British statute passed in May 1970, in force from December 29, 1975, requiring equality of contract terms for equal work.
Manifesto of the 343
The 1971 French declaration by women who stated they had had abortions and demanded legal reform.
Veil Law
The French law of January 17, 1975, decriminalizing abortion under conditions, initially for a trial period.
Law 194
The Italian abortion law of May 1978, allowing abortion under conditions and permitting conscientious objection by doctors.
Paragraph 218
The West German penal code provision on abortion, subject of 1970s reform and constitutional challenge.
Paragraph 175
The German penal code provision criminalizing sex between men; partly liberalized in 1969, repealed in 1994.
Neuwirth Law
The French law of December 28, 1967, legalizing regulated contraception.
Decriminalization
Removal of criminal penalties for conduct, which does not necessarily imply legal equality or social acceptance.
Humanae Vitae
The 1968 papal encyclical reaffirming the Catholic prohibition of artificial contraception.
Secularization
The long decline of religious institutions' social authority and, in many places, of religious practice; uneven and not equivalent to the disappearance of belief.
Radikalenerlass
The 1972 West German decree on vetting "radicals" for civil service, controversial for its effects on civil liberties.
Years of lead
The label for Italy's period of political terrorism, chiefly the 1970s and early 1980s.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the Cold War division of Europe was lived not only through diplomacy and alliances but through struggles over how people could study, work, love, worship, and speak. The protests of the 1960s revealed that stable and prosperous societies could generate deep dissatisfaction, and that economic success could raise rather than quiet demands for dignity and participation.

The chapter also reveals how social change occurs. The dramatic weeks of May 1968 produced no revolution, yet the decade that followed brought new laws, new institutions, and new expectations. Change came through the interaction of movements with governments, unions, courts, churches, and markets, and through the quieter shifts of households and workplaces. The most lasting results often took shape in the years after the streets emptied, and they were unequally shared.

Its legacies extended into later chapters. The language of participation, rights, and individual autonomy shaped later social movements and political debates, including those over migration and identity examined in Chapter 137, and the memory of 1968 became a contested political resource. The dissident movements of eastern Europe, discussed in Chapter 132, drew on different circumstances but shared a concern with dignity and truth. For Volume VI, the chapter adds an essential dimension: the history of postwar Europe includes the history of how ordinary people challenged and remade the authorities that governed their lives, and that history helps explain why contemporary Europe cannot be understood as the predetermined result of diplomacy or institutions alone.
