Chapter Opening

The European Union was not created in a single moment, and the euro was not introduced in a single act. Between the signing of a treaty in 1992, the entry into force of that treaty in 1993, the start of monetary union in 1999, and the arrival of banknotes and coins in 2002, European governments and institutions moved authority in stages, each of which required negotiation, ratification, and public acceptance.

In the early hours of January 1, 2002, cash machines in twelve European countries began to dispense euro banknotes, and over the following weeks shopkeepers, bank clerks, pensioners, and travelers exchanged familiar national money for a currency that none of them had ever held in their hands. Yet the euro already existed. Since January 1, 1999, it had served as the money of account of eleven participating countries, used in financial markets, bank settlements, and electronic payments, while national banknotes and coins continued to circulate as denominations of the new currency. The legal foundation for all of this had been signed in the Dutch city of Maastricht on February 7, 1992, but the treaty did not enter into force until November 1, 1993. Each of these dates marks a different kind of event, and a student who treats them as interchangeable will misunderstand how European integration actually worked.

This chapter asks how the European Communities developed into the European Union, why a group of governments chose to share a currency, and what political, economic, and constitutional tensions followed when European institutions acquired wider authority. Chapter 133 traced the foundations: the European Coal and Steel Community, the Treaties of Rome, the customs union, agricultural policy, and enlargement from six to twelve member states. Chapter 134 follows the transformation of a primarily economic Community into a Union with a wider range of policy fields, a common citizenship status, a central banking system, and a currency shared by a growing group of states.

The governing argument of the chapter is that the European Union emerged through negotiated transfers and exercises of authority, not through the replacement of European nation-states. Economic integration encouraged deeper cooperation, because removing barriers to trade and capital created pressures to coordinate monetary conditions and regulation. But common institutions and a common currency generated new questions about democratic representation, policy coordination, economic differences among members, and national sovereignty. Maastricht was a major turning point, but it was not the endpoint of integration, and the treaties of Amsterdam, Nice, and Lisbon, together with the failed constitutional project, show that the arrangement remained contested and unfinished.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I moves from the Single European Act of 1986 to the Maastricht negotiations of 1991 and 1992. Division II examines the institutional settlement, European citizenship, the ratification crisis, and the debate about sovereignty and democratic accountability. Division III, the longest, explains the design of Economic and Monetary Union, the Exchange Rate Mechanism crisis, the European Central Bank, and the introduction of the euro. Division IV follows institutional reform through the Amsterdam and Nice treaties, Schengen, the Common Foreign and Security Policy, the Charter of Fundamental Rights, and the rejected Constitution. Division V treats the Lisbon Treaty, the problem of democratic legitimacy, the political economy of the euro, and a concluding historical synthesis.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and also unusually limited. Treaties, protocols, the conclusions of European Council meetings, the Official Journal of the European Communities, judgments of the Court of Justice and national constitutional courts, central bank statutes and reports, referendum results, and public opinion surveys are all available. Memoirs, interviews, and diaries of participants add personal perspective but also reflect later self-justification. Many government archives for the 1990s remain closed or only partly opened under national rules that restrict access for decades, so historians writing about the period must rely heavily on published documents, early releases, and journalism, and must be cautious about claims of intention that the available record cannot confirm.

The chapter also keeps a methodological caution in view. The institutions described here still exist and have continued to change, so it is tempting to read their history as a straight line toward the present. The textbook rejects that approach. Governments in 1991 did not know that the euro would be adopted by so many states, that a constitutional treaty would fail, or that a financial crisis would later test the monetary system. Where the chapter points forward, it does so to explain significance, and it reserves the detailed history of the Yugoslav wars, postcommunist enlargement, migration and populism, the financial and sovereign-debt crises, Brexit, and the war in Ukraine for Chapters 135 through 139.

Within Volume VI, this chapter marks the point at which the Cold War settlement in Europe, examined in Chapter 132, was translated into a new institutional order in the western and then wider European space. Within Part XX, it connects the origins of integration in Chapter 133 to the post-Cold War tests that follow. Understanding Maastricht and the euro is therefore essential for understanding both the achievements and the tensions of contemporary Europe.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the effort to complete the internal market through the Single European Act encouraged debate about monetary and political integration.
  2. Trace the path from the Werner Report and the European Monetary System to the Delors Report and the three stages of Economic and Monetary Union.
  3. Analyze how the end of the Cold War and German reunification shaped, without simply determining, the Maastricht negotiations.
  4. Distinguish signature, ratification, entry into force, and operational implementation of treaties and monetary arrangements.
  5. Describe the three-pillar structure of the European Union established at Maastricht and explain the roles of the main institutions.
  6. Explain European citizenship as a status supplementing national citizenship, and identify the rights attached to it.
  7. Compare the ratification experiences of Denmark, France, and Britain and explain what they reveal about competing views of sovereignty.
  8. Evaluate the Maastricht convergence criteria, the Exchange Rate Mechanism crisis, and the institutional design of the European Central Bank.
  9. Distinguish the European Union, the euro area, and the Schengen Area, and the Eurosystem from the European System of Central Banks.
  10. Assess the purposes and results of the Amsterdam, Nice, and Lisbon treaties and the failure of the proposed Constitution.
  11. Interpret competing historical characterizations of the Union as federalization, interstate cooperation, or a system of shared governance.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did completing a market lead governments to consider a common currency, and why was that outcome not the only possible response to the problems of currency instability?
  2. To what extent did the end of the Cold War and German reunification cause, and to what extent merely accelerate, projects already under discussion before 1989?
  3. What did it mean to establish a European Union without replacing the member states, and how did the three-pillar design reflect that compromise?
  4. How did European citizenship change the legal and practical position of individuals, and what did it leave unchanged?
  5. Why did ratification of the Maastricht Treaty become a crisis in some countries, and why should national debates not be reduced to a contest between pro-European and anti-European camps?
  6. What advantages and difficulties arise when states share a currency but retain responsibility for taxation, public spending, and most economic policy?
  7. Why did successive treaty reforms prove necessary, and what do their different fates show about the limits of elite negotiation?
  8. What kinds of evidence allow historians to distinguish the intentions of negotiators from the consequences of the institutions they created?

Historical Context

Chapter 133 explained that the European Coal and Steel Community of 1951 and the European Economic Community and Euratom established by the Treaties of Rome in 1957 created common institutions among six states: a Commission with the right to propose legislation, a Council representing national governments, an Assembly that became the European Parliament, and a Court of Justice. By 1968 the Community had abolished internal customs duties and adopted a common external tariff, and the Common Agricultural Policy had become its largest area of spending. Enlargement brought Britain, Denmark, and Ireland in 1973, Greece in 1981, and Spain and Portugal in 1986. By the time this chapter begins, the Community had twelve members, very different levels of national income, and different histories of democracy, since Greece, Spain, and Portugal had emerged from authoritarian rule only in the 1970s.

The 1970s and early 1980s were a difficult period. The collapse of the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates around 1971 to 1973, the oil price shocks of 1973 and 1979, and the combination of inflation and unemployment known as stagflation unsettled national economies and made currency movements among European trading partners both more volatile and more politically damaging. The word sometimes used for the Community's condition in these years, Eurosclerosis, expressed a widespread sense, whatever its accuracy, that Europe was falling behind the United States and Japan in growth and technological competitiveness. Budget quarrels, especially over the British contribution, absorbed political energy until the Fontainebleau European Council of June 1984 settled the issue of a British rebate.

Three other background conditions matter. First, the Court of Justice had in 1979 established, in the case commonly known as Cassis de Dijon, that a product lawfully sold in one member state could in principle be sold in another, which opened a legal path toward mutual recognition of national standards. Second, the European Parliament, directly elected for the first time in 1979, adopted in 1984 a draft treaty on European Union associated with the Italian federalist Altiero Spinelli; governments did not accept it, but it kept constitutional ambitions in public view. Third, national governments held sharply different views. Britain under Margaret Thatcher favored market liberalization but resisted what it regarded as growing central authority, while France under François Mitterrand and Germany under Helmut Kohl, despite their differences, were prepared to deepen cooperation.

Finally, the reader should remember the sources of the evidence for this period. The primary documents are mostly official and public, and they record decisions rather than the private calculations behind them. Later memoirs by Jacques Delors, Helmut Kohl, and others, and journalistic accounts, tend to reflect the positions of the authors. Because many national archives for the late 1980s and 1990s remain restricted, interpretations of motive, especially concerning the relationship between German reunification and monetary union, should be treated as provisional and clearly distinguished from the documented sequence of decisions.

Section 134.1

The Single European Act and the Movement toward Political Union

By the mid 1980s the Community had achieved much of what the Treaties of Rome had promised in trade, but it had not created a genuinely unified market. The customs union was complete: internal tariffs had been abolished in 1968 and a common external tariff applied. Yet goods, services, workers, and capital still met a dense set of barriers that were not tariffs. National technical standards differed, professional qualifications were not recognized across borders, public contracts were commonly reserved for domestic suppliers, border checks delayed trucks, and many states maintained controls on the movement of capital. Economists and business groups argued that these barriers kept costs high and fragmented markets, and a study associated with Paolo Cecchini in 1988 estimated that removing them would produce gains of several percent of Community output, although such estimates were, and remain, contested because they rest on assumptions about competition and investment.

The political setting changed in 1984 and 1985. The settlement of the British budget question at Fontainebleau cleared a long quarrel. Jacques Delors became President of the Commission in January 1985 and chose the completion of the internal market as the program around which governments might unite. A White Paper prepared by the Commission under the responsibility of Commissioner Lord Cockfield and presented to the Milan European Council in June 1985 listed roughly three hundred legislative measures needed to remove physical, technical, and fiscal barriers. At Milan, heads of government took the unusual step of deciding by majority vote to convene an intergovernmental conference to revise the treaties, over the objections of Britain, Denmark, and Greece. The practice of unanimity in constitutional matters was thus formally preserved, since the conference could only produce a treaty all states agreed to sign, but the vote showed how contested the question had become.

The Single European Act was signed at Luxembourg on February 17, 1986, and at The Hague on February 28, 1986, by the twelve states, and it entered into force on July 1, 1987. The delay reflected the need for national ratification, which in Ireland required a decision of the Supreme Court in the Crotty case that a constitutional amendment, approved by referendum in 1987, was necessary before the state could ratify. The episode illustrates a theme of the chapter: European treaties acquired force through national constitutional procedures, and national courts and electorates therefore shaped the pace of integration.

The Act did several things. It set the objective of establishing the internal market, defined as an area without internal frontiers in which goods, persons, services, and capital would move freely, by December 31, 1992. It extended qualified majority voting in the Council to most measures needed to build the internal market, though not to taxation, to the free movement of persons, or to the rights and interests of employed persons, where unanimity continued to apply. It introduced a cooperation procedure that gave the European Parliament a second reading and so a greater legislative voice, and it gave Parliament a power of assent over accession and association agreements. It provided a treaty basis for the existing practice of meetings of the European Council, and it gave formal recognition to European Political Cooperation, the voluntary coordination of foreign policy, with a small secretariat. It also added or strengthened Community competence in research and technology, the environment, and economic and social cohesion, the last reflecting the concern of poorer members that the market would favor richer regions.

Three concepts should be distinguished. A customs union removes internal tariffs and adopts a common external tariff. A single market goes further, seeking to remove non-tariff barriers and to allow the free movement of the factors of production. A political union implies shared institutions of government, a shared foreign policy, and possibly shared sovereignty in more fields. The Single European Act belonged to the second category, but its logic pointed beyond it. The liberalization of capital movements, completed in most member states by a directive of 1988 and in force from July 1990, created conditions in which fixed exchange rates would be harder to defend without closer coordination of national monetary policies, which led directly to the monetary debates of the next section. A single market also required rules, regulators, and courts, and raised the question of who would exercise authority over them.

The response of the member states was not uniform. Margaret Thatcher supported the market program, which she saw as consistent with deregulation, but in a speech at Bruges in September 1988 she rejected what she described as a superstate directed from Brussels. Her speech and the reactions to it show that the internal market was both a point of agreement and the beginning of a larger dispute about the purpose of the Community. The central question of the next sections is why an effort to remove economic barriers led European leaders to consider additional political and monetary institutions.

Section 134.2

The Delors Report and the Origins of Economic and Monetary Union

Monetary cooperation in Europe predated the single market. At The Hague summit of December 1969, leaders asked for a plan for economic and monetary union, and a committee chaired by Pierre Werner, Prime Minister of Luxembourg, reported in October 1970 with a staged proposal to be completed within a decade. The plan was overtaken by the collapse of the Bretton Woods system, the oil shock of 1973, and divergent national responses to inflation. A more modest arrangement, the monetary snake of 1972, limited fluctuations among participating European currencies, but members entered and left it repeatedly and it lost credibility. The Werner Report therefore remained a precedent, not an achievement.

The European Monetary System, launched in March 1979 on the initiative of Chancellor Helmut Schmidt and President Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, was more durable. It created the European Currency Unit, an accounting basket of member currencies, and the Exchange Rate Mechanism, in which each participating currency had a central rate against the others and was expected to stay within agreed margins, normally 2.25 percent on either side, with a wider band for some currencies. Realignments of central rates were frequent in the early 1980s. After the French government's decision of 1983 to remain in the system and adopt a policy of strict control of inflation, the mechanism became more stable, and the Deutsche mark, backed by the Bundesbank's reputation for price stability, emerged as its anchor. Other governments came to see that this meant, in effect, importing German monetary conditions while having no voice in setting them, an asymmetry that some, especially in France and Italy, wished to remedy by creating a shared institution.

The difficulty was a basic tension. If a country permits free movement of capital, maintains a fixed exchange rate with its partners, and also wishes to conduct an independent national monetary policy, it cannot sustain all three, because investors will move funds when they expect the exchange rate to change. This tension, sometimes described as an impossible trinity, became acute once capital controls were being dismantled for the single market. Governments therefore faced a choice among abandoning fixed rates, restoring capital controls, or sharing monetary policy. The third option was monetary union.

At the Hanover European Council of June 1988, the heads of government established a committee to study and propose concrete stages toward economic and monetary union. Chaired by Jacques Delors, it consisted of the governors of the national central banks, along with Alexandre Lamfalussy of the Bank for International Settlements, Niels Thygesen, and Miguel Boyer. The presence of central bankers acting in a personal capacity gave the report credibility with financial markets and with the Bundesbank, although it also meant that its recommendations reflected the preferences of independent central banks. The committee reported in April 1989.

The Delors Report defined economic and monetary union as including the complete freedom of capital movement, the irrevocable fixing of exchange rates, and ultimately a single currency. It proposed three stages: a first stage of closer coordination within existing arrangements, a second stage in which a new institution would be created and begin to take on responsibilities, and a third in which exchange rates would be irrevocably fixed and policy would pass to the common institution. It argued that monetary policy would have to be conducted by a European System of Central Banks, structured on federal lines, independent of instructions from governments, and with price stability as its primary objective. It also argued that monetary union required binding rules on national budget deficits, because the committee anticipated that undisciplined national fiscal policies might damage a shared currency. It did not, however, set dates for the later stages.

The report set out expected benefits: lower transaction costs and the elimination of exchange-rate uncertainty within the internal market, greater price transparency, a stronger European position in international monetary relations, and protection against disruptive currency movements. At the Madrid European Council of June 1989, heads of government agreed that the first stage should begin on July 1, 1990. Britain agreed to this on conditions set out in a statement by the British government and did not join the Exchange Rate Mechanism until October 1990. The decision about later stages required an intergovernmental conference and a treaty. The historical point is precise: the Delors Report proposed a pathway to monetary union, and the decision to build the euro was made later, by governments, in the Maastricht Treaty.

Primary Source Window

Delors Report, April 1989: Report on Economic and Monetary Union in the European Community

Creator: Committee for the Study of Economic and Monetary Union, chaired by Jacques Delors · Date: April 1989 · Type: Expert committee report to the European Council; not a legal instrument

Documentary access (historical editions): European Central Bank, historical archives of the Delors Committee (1988–1989) · Delors Report, 1989, archival report record

Context: The committee was established by the Hanover European Council in June 1988 and reported before the Madrid European Council. Its members were mostly central bank governors, which shaped its emphasis on monetary stability and central bank independence. The document is a recommendation. Governments were free to accept, amend, or reject it.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation)The report describes economic and monetary union as a combination of an integrated market, a single currency or irrevocably fixed exchange rates, and a common monetary policy, supported by coordination of national economic and budget policies. It proposes that the union be reached by stages, in order that governments and markets could adapt gradually and that institutional change could be tested. It recommends a European System of Central Banks independent of governments and committed to price stability, and it recommends binding limits on national budget deficits. It leaves the timing of the second and third stages to be decided later.

Analysis: The report shows why contemporaries thought monetary union a logical extension of the internal market and why central bankers, particularly the Bundesbank's representatives, saw independence and price stability as conditions of their cooperation. Its limitations are equally important. It is a technical recommendation by officials, not an expression of public opinion, and it does not explain why governments, rather than markets or committees, decided to adopt a currency. Its emphasis on budget rules is a clue to later disputes about fiscal discipline, but its authors did not agree in advance on how such rules would be enforced. The report is silent on democratic accountability beyond the principle of independence.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the report propose, and what does it leave undecided?
  2. Who wrote it, and how might their institutional positions have shaped its recommendations?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the economic reasoning behind monetary union?
  4. What can they not establish from this source alone about why governments chose to adopt the euro?
Section 134.3

German Reunification, French-German Cooperation, and the Acceleration of Integration

The year 1989 transformed the setting of integration. The opening of the Berlin Wall in November, the fall of communist governments across eastern Europe, and the reunification of Germany on October 3, 1990, are examined in Chapter 132, and this section considers only their effect on the European Community. For the western European governments, the prospect of a larger and more powerful Germany raised questions about the balance of power and about the stability of existing institutions. For the Federal Republic, anchored in the Community since the 1950s, the Chancellor's government insisted that German unity and European integration were two sides of the same policy. For eastern European governments, the Community became a center of attraction, a theme developed in Chapter 136.

The institutional response began before reunification was complete. In December 1989, shortly after the Wall opened, the Strasbourg European Council decided to convene an intergovernmental conference on economic and monetary union. In April 1990 Chancellor Kohl and President Mitterrand sent a joint letter proposing a second conference, on political union, in order to strengthen the democratic and institutional character of the Community and to prepare a common foreign and security policy. The two conferences opened in Rome in December 1990 and ran in parallel through 1991. Germany's emphasis on political union was linked to its view that monetary union without stronger democratic institutions would be unstable, a view not shared equally by all partners.

The positions of the major actors differed. France had a long interest in sharing control of monetary policy. Since the mid 1980s French governments had accepted the discipline of the Exchange Rate Mechanism, and many officials regarded the Bundesbank's dominance as a loss of national influence that only a European institution could remedy. Germany's government, for its part, was prepared to accept monetary union only on terms that preserved the Bundesbank's commitment to price stability, which public opinion and the Bundesbank itself strongly favored, and the Bundesbank under its President Karl Otto Pöhl, and after 1991 Helmut Schlesinger, was not enthusiastic about a monetary union before economic convergence had been achieved. Britain remained skeptical. The Commission under Delors pressed for ambitious institutions. Smaller states, including the Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg, often favored strong Community institutions that would limit the influence of larger states.

Historians disagree about the relationship between reunification and monetary union. One widely discussed interpretation holds that France accepted German reunification in return for German agreement to monetary union. Another, stronger in recent scholarship, stresses that the monetary project was under way before the Wall fell, that Mitterrand had earlier pursued it for reasons of French monetary policy, that Kohl was independently committed to deeper integration, and that the two processes shaped each other without constituting a single bargain. The available documents show that contemporaries linked the two, but they do not show a formal exchange. The most defensible account distinguishes formal treaty negotiation from the broader diplomatic calculations, personal relationships, and political expectations surrounding it.

Germany's own monetary union with the German Democratic Republic, which took effect on July 1, 1990, also affected the European story. The conversion of East German marks to Deutsche marks at favorable rates, together with large transfers to eastern Germany, increased German public borrowing and contributed to higher German interest rates in the early 1990s, with consequences for the Exchange Rate Mechanism that are explored in section 134.10. Reunification thus influenced the monetary system both politically, by encouraging commitments to integration, and economically, by shifting German policy.

Cause and Effect

Why Integration Accelerated after 1989

Long-term causes

Decades of trade integration, the single market program, currency instability since the early 1970s, and earlier Franco-German cooperation created institutions and expectations on which leaders could build.

Short-term causes

The liberalization of capital movements, the preparation of the Delors Report, the fall of communist governments in 1989, and the approach of German reunification.

Event or transformation

The decisions of December 1989 and 1990 to hold intergovernmental conferences on monetary and political union.

Immediate effects

Parallel negotiations that produced the Maastricht Treaty, and a shift in the agenda of the Community from market completion to constitutional and monetary questions.

Long-term consequences

A Union with a central bank, a common currency among some members, and enlargement debates that included former communist states.

Historical complexity

Reunification did not by itself create monetary union, which had earlier roots; and it also produced economic effects, such as higher German interest rates, that made the monetary system harder to manage.

Evidence about private negotiations remains limited by restricted archives, so historians should be cautious about attributing a single motive to any government.

Section 134.4

The Maastricht Negotiations and the Treaty on European Union, 1991 to 1992

The two intergovernmental conferences, on economic and monetary union and on political union, worked through 1991 under successive presidencies. The Luxembourg presidency presented a draft in the summer of 1991 that organized the new Union as a structure of three pillars, with the existing Communities as the first and intergovernmental cooperation in foreign policy and in justice and home affairs as the others. The Dutch presidency's draft of September 1991 attempted to fold these fields into a more Community-based structure, and it was rejected by a majority of governments that wished to keep foreign policy and home affairs under intergovernmental control. The pillar structure was restored. The negotiation illustrates a recurring pattern: governments agreed that they should cooperate more, but they disagreed over who would control the cooperation.

The negotiating positions varied by state and by issue. Britain, under John Major, accepted monetary union as a possibility but insisted on the right to decide later whether to join, rejected the use of the word federal in the treaty, and refused to accept the Community's social policy provisions. France supported a strong monetary union with fixed dates and pressed for a common foreign policy. Germany insisted on strengthening the European Parliament and on conditions for monetary union that satisfied the Bundesbank. Spain, with Portugal, Greece, and Ireland, sought a cohesion fund that would assist poorer regions, which was agreed. Denmark and Ireland sought protections for national positions on defense, property, and ethical questions. Smaller states generally supported the Community method and wished to limit the intergovernmental character of the new pillars.

The European Council meeting at Maastricht on December 9 and 10, 1991, reached agreement. The central compromises were that monetary union would proceed to its third stage by 1999 at the latest, that states would have to meet convergence conditions to participate, that Britain would have an opt-out from the third stage and from the social protocol, in which the other eleven states proceeded on social policy without Britain, and that the treaty would not use the word federal while restating the aim of an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe. The treaty was signed on February 7, 1992, in Maastricht by the foreign and finance ministers of the twelve states.

Signature, however, was only the beginning. A treaty revision under the founding treaties required ratification by every member state according to its own constitutional rules. These ranged from parliamentary votes to referendums and, in Germany, a challenge before the Federal Constitutional Court. The treaty entered into force on November 1, 1993, after the last ratification was completed, and on that date the European Union was established and the European Economic Community was renamed the European Community. The distinction between signature in February 1992 and the establishment of the Union in November 1993 is central, and the intervening months are the subject of section 134.7.

What Maastricht created was a new framework, not a new state. It established the Union, citizenship of the Union, a timetable and conditions for monetary union, new fields of cooperation, a broader role for Parliament, and new institutions such as the Committee of the Regions. It did not create a European government with its own army, tax system, or power to decide its own competences. Its significance lay in combining market integration with monetary, political, and security ambitions, and in doing so in ways that were intentionally uneven, since opt-outs and different decision-making methods acknowledged divergent national positions.

Section 134.5

The Three Pillars of the European Union and the Institutional Settlement

The Treaty on European Union created an institutional structure that is often described as a temple with three pillars beneath a common roof. The roof was the single institutional framework of the Union, headed by the European Council, which the treaty charged with providing the impetus for development and defining general political guidelines. The pillars rested on different legal foundations and followed different decision-making methods, and understanding them is essential to understanding what Maastricht did and did not achieve.

The first pillar consisted of the European Communities, now comprising the European Community, the European Atomic Energy Community, and the European Coal and Steel Community. It was the supranational core, in which the Commission proposed legislation, the Council and, increasingly, the Parliament adopted it, and the Court of Justice interpreted and enforced it. Community law had direct effect and supremacy over conflicting national law, principles that the Court had established in its case law since the 1960s. The Treaty renamed the European Economic Community the European Community, a change that reflected its growing responsibilities in fields such as environment, consumer protection, education, and culture, and that made the term EEC obsolete after November 1993.

The second pillar was the Common Foreign and Security Policy, and the third was cooperation in justice and home affairs, covering asylum, immigration, border crossings, drugs, police, and judicial cooperation. Both were primarily intergovernmental. Decisions typically required unanimity, the Commission had a limited role, Parliament was consulted or informed, and the Court of Justice had little authority. The Western European Union was described in the treaty as an integral part of the development of the Union, a formula that left questions of defense open.

The treaty modified the institutions themselves. It introduced the co-decision procedure, under which the Parliament and Council adopt legislation jointly in specified fields, and it gave Parliament a power to approve the Commission as a body. It aligned the Commission's term with that of the Parliament, created a Committee of the Regions with advisory functions, and created a Cohesion Fund for the poorer states. It also provided for the European Court of Auditors to be an institution of the Communities. The Council continued to represent national governments, using qualified majority voting in many first pillar fields and unanimity in sensitive ones.

The treaty also introduced a principle intended to reassure national governments and publics, subsidiarity. In areas that are not within the Community's exclusive competence, the Community is to act only if the objectives of a proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the member states and can, because of the scale or effects of the action, be better achieved by the Community. The principle was a legal limit and a political message, and it was invoked by supporters of integration as well as critics. Its application remained difficult because the question of what is better achieved at Union level depends on political judgment. The treaty also provided that action should not go beyond what is necessary, a principle of proportionality.

Students should keep in mind that the Union of 1993 was not the Union of today. The pillars were removed as an organizing structure by the Lisbon Treaty, which took effect in 2009 and is examined in section 134.17. The distinctions among European Council, Council of the European Union, and Council of Europe also remain essential: the first is a body of heads of state or government, the second a body of ministers that legislates, and the third a separate organization founded in 1949 that is not an institution of the Union and which administers the European Convention on Human Rights.

Historical Vocabulary
Subsidiarity, Intergovernmentalism, and SupranationalismSubsidiarity is the principle that, outside areas of exclusive Community competence, decisions are to be taken at the lowest level at which objectives can be adequately achieved. Supranationalism describes decision-making in which common institutions can adopt rules that bind member states, even when some states disagree, as in the Community method of the first pillar. Intergovernmentalism describes cooperation in which governments retain control and decisions require agreement among them, as in the second and third pillars.

The terms are analytic labels, not rigid categories. Even in the first pillar, governments remained powerful through the Council and the European Council, and even in the other pillars, institutions developed routines of cooperation. The terms matter because the arguments of the 1990s over the Union's character were often arguments over which method should apply to which field.

Section 134.6

European Citizenship, Free Movement, and the Development of Political Rights

The Maastricht Treaty introduced citizenship of the Union. The treaty provides that every person holding the nationality of a member state is a citizen of the Union and that Union citizenship complements national citizenship and does not replace it. The distinction is fundamental. Each state determines who is its national under its own law, and Union citizenship follows from that status. There is no independent route to Union citizenship, and a state could not confer it without conferring nationality. The Edinburgh decision for Denmark in December 1992, discussed in section 134.7, restated the point in response to Danish concerns.

The treaty attached specific rights to the new status. Citizens of the Union had the right to move and reside freely in the territory of the member states, subject to the limitations and conditions in the treaty and implementing measures. They had the right to vote and to stand as candidates in municipal elections and in elections to the European Parliament in the member state of residence, under the same conditions as the nationals of that state, a right put into effect by directives of 1993 and 1994. They had the right to protection by the diplomatic or consular authorities of any member state in a third country where their own state was not represented, and the right to petition the European Parliament and to apply to a European Ombudsman, a new office whose holder is elected by Parliament and which receives complaints about maladministration by Community institutions.

These rights must be distinguished from the history of worker mobility. The Treaties of Rome had provided for free movement of workers, and Community law gave workers and their families rights of residence and equal treatment in employment. In 1990, directives extended residence rights to students, retired persons, and others with sufficient resources and health insurance, in response to the principle that the single market should be for people as well as capital. Maastricht gave these rights a constitutional place and a political name. The Court of Justice in the late 1990s and early 2000s, in cases such as those concerning social benefits for citizens residing in another state, described Union citizenship as a fundamental status of nationals of the member states, and gradually built a body of case law on equal treatment. The legislation consolidating residence rights is a directive of 2004, discussed in section 134.18.

The rights were limited. Union citizens did not gain the right to vote in national elections of another state, and states retained the power to impose conditions on residence, such as requirements of sufficient resources for those who were not working. The rights concerned individuals who were nationals of member states, so that third-country nationals who lived in the Union, often in large numbers, did not obtain citizenship of the Union. Their position, together with questions of migration and identity, belongs to Chapter 137. The practical exercise of citizenship depended on implementing legislation and administrative practice, and many citizens knew little about their rights. Surveys across the Union suggested that public knowledge of Union citizenship was uneven.

Citizenship also raised conceptual questions. Citizenship in the modern state combines legal status, political participation, and a sense of belonging. Union citizenship supplied the first of these in limited form, provided limited political participation, and left identity to national communities. Some observers saw it as the beginning of a post-national form of membership, others as a modest supplement that made existing mobility rights more visible, and critics suspected it of being a symbolic instrument for creating a European identity from above. The best historical judgment is that it was a real if modest addition to individual rights and an experiment whose significance would depend on later case law and politics.

Citizenship and Cross-Border Residence

Consider categories of people whose lives were affected by Union citizenship. Section 134.7

Maastricht's Ratification Crisis: Denmark, France, Britain, and National Debate

The signature of the treaty was followed by the most serious public controversy yet seen in the Community. Ratification was uncertain because several governments had promised referendums or faced divided legislatures, and because the treaty, long and complex, was difficult to explain. The ratification debates reflected different national political concerns, and it is a mistake to reduce them to a simple division between supporters and opponents of Europe.

Ireland approved the treaty by referendum on June 18, 1992, by a large majority. Denmark, however, held its referendum on June 2, 1992, and the electorate rejected the treaty by a narrow margin, roughly 50.7 percent against. The result mattered because the treaty could not enter into force without ratification by every state. Danish opponents expressed concerns about national sovereignty, the loss of control over monetary policy, the possibility of a common defense, and the distance of European decision-making from voters, while Danish supporters stressed the benefits of membership. The government and most of the parliamentary parties had supported ratification, and the vote showed a gap between political leadership and part of the electorate.

In France, President Mitterrand called a referendum, held on September 20, 1992. The result was a narrow approval, with approximately 51 percent in favor. The campaign had cut across party lines. Supporters stressed the prospects of monetary stability, a European framework for German power, and the French role in Europe, while opponents, from both left and right, argued that the treaty would reduce national sovereignty, increase unemployment through monetary discipline, or give too much weight to the Bundesbank's policies. The result also occurred in the middle of the currency crisis described in section 134.10.

In Britain, the government of John Major had negotiated the opt-outs but faced a divided Conservative Party, with a group of members of Parliament opposing ratification, and a parliamentary schedule delayed by the aftermath of sterling's departure from the Exchange Rate Mechanism. The government secured passage of the legislation in 1993, after a confidence vote in July, and the opt-out from the third stage of monetary union, and the non-participation in the social protocol, remained in place. The British position showed that the treaty could be ratified while a state retained significant exemptions.

The Danish problem was addressed by the Edinburgh European Council of December 1992. The Council adopted a decision, annexed to its conclusions, on certain problems raised by Denmark. It recorded that Denmark would not participate in the third stage of monetary union unless it later decided otherwise, that Denmark would not take part in the elaboration and implementation of decisions with defense implications, that Union citizenship does not replace national citizenship, and that certain justice and home affairs matters would be treated in a particular way. The decision was legally framed as an interpretation and political commitment for the Danish situation, and it did not amend the treaty. On May 18, 1993, Danish voters approved the treaty on this basis, with roughly 57 percent in favor. This was not a simple reversal under identical conditions, since the terms put to voters had changed.

Germany was the last to complete ratification. The Federal Constitutional Court, in a judgment of October 1993, held that the treaty was compatible with the Basic Law, while emphasizing that the Union remained an association of sovereign states whose democratic legitimacy rested in important respects on the national parliaments, and that the German legislature must keep sufficient substantive responsibilities. The judgment became a reference point in later debates on sovereignty. The treaty then entered into force on November 1, 1993.

Section 134.8

National Sovereignty, Democratic Accountability, and the Limits of Union Authority

The ratification crisis brought to the surface competing conceptions of political authority. One conception holds that sovereignty resides in the nation-state and is lent, not transferred, to common institutions, so that the legitimacy of European decisions depends on the consent of national parliaments and governments. Another holds that sovereignty can be pooled, so that states exercise power jointly in fields where acting alone no longer secures their objectives, and that the resulting system derives legitimacy from several sources at once. A third, federalist conception, held by a minority of political actors, envisaged a European political community with its own constitution and a government accountable to a European legislature.

The relationship among institutions was complex. National executives sat in the Council and the European Council and thus took part in European decisions, which were then often defended domestically as the result of European necessity. National parliaments ratified treaties and passed implementing laws, but their oversight of European legislation was uneven. The European Parliament, directly elected, gained powers in successive treaties but had no right to propose legislation, and turnout in its elections was often low. The Commission was neither elected nor wholly an agent of governments. The Court of Justice had expansive interpretive authority. The result was a system in which authority was distributed among bodies with different sources of legitimacy.

Critics described this as a democratic deficit. They argued that important decisions, especially in economic regulation and, after Maastricht, monetary policy, were becoming less directly accountable to national electorates, while the European Parliament was too weak and too remote to compensate. Defenders offered counterarguments. They emphasized that governments in the Council were accountable to national parliaments and voters, that treaties required national ratification, that the European Parliament was gaining powers, that courts offered judicial oversight, and that collective decisions could achieve results that individual states could not. Some also argued that the standard of comparison was misplaced, since the Union was not a state and should not be judged as though it were.

A further question was whether political integration required a common European identity. Some argued that democratic politics depends on a shared political community with a common language, media, and sense of solidarity, which Europe lacked, and that institutions could not outrun identity. Others argued that democratic communities can be built through shared institutions and rights, and that many national identities were themselves constructed over time, so that a union of different national communities could hold legitimate institutions. The debate has no simple answer, but it shaped the political language of subsequent decades.

Governments also did not accept identical obligations. The British and Danish opt-outs, the later Schengen exceptions, and the staggered adoption of the euro created a Union of different speeds and degrees of participation. Supporters regarded flexibility as the only way to keep all states in the project, and critics worried that it would erode common rules. The same tensions would shape the treaties of Amsterdam, Nice, and Lisbon, which are treated in Division IV and V.

Historical Debate

Sovereignty and Democracy in the Union

The question: Did the Maastricht Treaty and its successors weaken democratic self-government by moving decisions away from national electorates, or did they preserve and strengthen it by creating shared institutions that sustain the capacity of states to act?

Interpretation A, a democratic deficit: Decision-making by governments in the Council, expert institutions such as the Commission and the independent central bank, and a Court with wide powers placed key decisions beyond direct electoral control. Referendum results in Denmark, France, and later the Netherlands and Ireland suggest that parts of the electorate felt unrepresented. A parliament with limited powers and uneven public engagement could not supply the legitimacy that national parliaments provided.
Interpretation B, sovereignty pooled and legitimacy plural: Governments chose to share authority by treaty, with national ratification, and remain accountable to national voters. The Union functions as a system in which legitimacy derives from several sources, including national governments, the European Parliament, courts, and expert bodies charged with specific tasks. In fields such as trade, competition, and currency, collective decision-making allowed states to regain influence that they could not exercise alone.

Evidence: Both sides use treaty texts, ratification results, turnout in European elections, public opinion surveys, and constitutional court judgments, including the German judgment of 1993. They differ on the standard by which legitimacy should be judged.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether later referendums and political movements are seen as reactions to specific policies or as symptoms of a structural problem.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence, such as comparative survey data or voting patterns, would help decide between them.

Division outcome: Students should understand what Maastricht changed institutionally, how Union citizenship altered political and legal rights, and why ratification exposed competing views of sovereignty and democratic legitimacy.

Economic and Monetary Union and the Introduction of the Euro

Sections 134.9 to 134.12 · Principal period: 1989 to 2002

Section 134.9

The Three Stages of Economic and Monetary Union and the Maastricht Convergence Criteria

The Maastricht Treaty turned the Delors Report's proposal into a legal program. Economic and Monetary Union was to proceed in three stages, each with its own institutional and policy content. The first stage had in effect begun on July 1, 1990, when capital movements were liberalized and cooperation in economic and monetary policy was strengthened. The second stage began on January 1, 1994, and involved the establishment of the European Monetary Institute in Frankfurt, which prepared the technical arrangements for a single currency and the future central banking system, while national central banks moved toward independence and governments were required to avoid excessive deficits and to end central bank financing of public spending. The third stage, beginning on January 1, 1999, involved the irrevocable fixing of the exchange rates among participating currencies, the introduction of the euro, and the conduct of a single monetary policy by the European Central Bank.

The treaty did not allow every member state to join the third stage automatically. It established convergence criteria, conditions that a state had to meet in order to adopt the single currency, and it required the Council, meeting at the level of heads of state or government, to decide which states fulfilled them. The criteria concerned price stability, the government budget, exchange rates, and long-term interest rates. The logic was that a group of states entering a shared monetary system should have sufficiently compatible inflation and public finances for a common monetary policy to be workable, and that the conditions would protect the stability of the currency in which Germany, with its tradition of low inflation, was particularly interested.

The treaty also made the reference values subject to qualifying conditions. A government deficit above 3 percent of gross domestic product could be considered consistent with the criterion if the ratio had declined substantially and continuously and reached a level close to the reference value, or if the excess was exceptional and temporary. A public debt above 60 percent could be accepted if the ratio was sufficiently diminishing and approaching the reference value at a satisfactory pace. The treaty thus required a judgment by the Council and did not apply a mechanical rule. When the decision was taken in May 1998, several states with debt ratios well above 60 percent, including Belgium and Italy, whose debt exceeded 100 percent of national output, were admitted on the basis that the ratios were falling. Critics, particularly in Germany, regarded this as too lenient, and defenders considered it consistent with the treaty.

The distinction between the conditions for entry and the governance of those already inside is essential. The convergence criteria applied before a state joined. After entry, discipline over public finances was the task of the excessive deficit procedure and the Stability and Growth Pact, adopted in 1997, which are discussed in section 134.19. The criteria were also not a full theory of economic convergence. They did not measure productivity, wages, unemployment, or the structure of economies, which meant that states could meet them while still having very different economic conditions.

Historical Vocabulary
Economic and Monetary UnionEconomic and Monetary Union, abbreviated EMU, is the Treaty term for the process by which member states coordinate economic policies and, for those that qualify, adopt a single currency and a common monetary policy. It is not identical with membership of the European Union. A state may be a member of the Union without taking part in the third stage, as Britain and Denmark did through opt-outs and as Sweden did by not meeting the conditions for entry, and EMU's third stage is therefore associated with the euro area.

The term matters because it combines two elements in an asymmetric way. Monetary policy is centralized, while economic policy, notably taxation and public spending, remains primarily national, subject to coordination. Understanding this asymmetry is central to later debates about the euro.

Section 134.10

The Exchange Rate Mechanism Crisis of 1992 and 1993

The ratification of Maastricht coincided with the most serious crisis in the European monetary system since its creation. The Exchange Rate Mechanism had been stable since the late 1980s, partly because governments came to treat the mechanism as a commitment that did not require realignments, and partly because the liberalization of capital movements, completed in 1990, made it easier for investors to move funds across borders. These conditions meant that when economic policies and conditions diverged, markets could test the commitment quickly.

The divergence arose partly from German reunification. The German government financed the costs of reunification largely through borrowing, and the Bundesbank, concerned about inflation, kept interest rates high. Other countries in the mechanism, whose currencies were pegged to the mark, had to maintain high interest rates to defend their exchange rates, even as they faced weak growth and rising unemployment. The Bundesbank's priority was German price stability, and it did not feel obliged to adjust policy to the needs of the rest of the system. The difficulty was compounded by the Danish referendum of June 1992 and the uncertain outlook for the French referendum, which suggested that the path to monetary union might not be secure, and so reduced the credibility of the commitments.

Pressure mounted in the summer of 1992. Italy devalued the lira within the mechanism on September 13. On September 16, 1992, the date that became known in Britain as Black Wednesday, the British government raised interest rates sharply in a failed attempt to defend the pound, and later that day suspended sterling's membership in the mechanism, which Britain had joined only in October 1990. The Italian lira was also suspended on that day, and left the mechanism, and the Spanish peseta was devalued. Other countries, including Finland and Sweden, which were not formally in the mechanism but had linked currencies to it, allowed their currencies to float. In the following months, further devaluations took place, and in July and August 1993, renewed speculation against the French franc and others led to the decision of August 2, 1993, to widen the normal fluctuation margins of the mechanism to 15 percent on either side, effectively replacing a system of narrow bands with a loose one.

The crisis illustrated several lessons. Speculative capital flows, in an environment of liberalized capital, could overwhelm national central bank reserves, so that national intervention had limits. Political credibility mattered, because market participants judged governments' willingness to maintain policies, not only economic fundamentals. And fixed exchange rates, without a common monetary authority, depended on the cooperation of the central bank of the dominant country. Different observers drew different conclusions. Some saw the episode as evidence that a half-way system of fixed but adjustable rates was unstable and that monetary union, which would remove the possibility of speculation against currencies within the union, was the logical answer. Others saw it as evidence that countries with different economic conditions could not safely tie their currencies together, and that flexible rates were preferable.

The historian should avoid presenting the crisis as proof that the euro was either inevitably necessary or inherently unworkable. The crisis changed the political context. It weakened the credibility of the intermediate arrangements, increased the importance of the convergence criteria, which governments now had a strong incentive to meet, and contributed to a determination among several governments, including those of France and Germany, to continue the project. The British departure also strengthened the position of those within Britain who wished to remain outside the monetary union, and the country's later decision not to adopt the euro cannot be explained by this event alone.

Section 134.11

The European Central Bank, Monetary Independence, and the Governance of the Euro

The European Monetary Institute, established on January 1, 1994, in Frankfurt under the presidency of Alexandre Lamfalussy, did not conduct monetary policy. Its task was to strengthen cooperation among national central banks, to coordinate monetary policies, and to prepare the technical, legal, and organizational foundations of the third stage, including the choice of banknote designs, the planning of payment systems, and the framework for monetary policy operations. In December 1995, the Madrid European Council decided that the name of the new currency would be the euro and not the European Currency Unit, partly in response to German concerns about the earlier name.

The European Central Bank was established on June 1, 1998, and took up the monetary policy responsibility of the participating states on January 1, 1999. Its first President was Wim Duisenberg, formerly head of the Dutch central bank and, from 1997, President of the European Monetary Institute. The legal framework was the treaty and the annexed Statute of the European System of Central Banks and of the European Central Bank. The Statute created two related structures. The European System of Central Banks comprises the European Central Bank and the national central banks of all member states of the Union, including those that have not adopted the euro. The Eurosystem comprises the European Central Bank and the national central banks of the member states that have adopted the euro. It is the Eurosystem that carries out monetary policy for the euro area. The distinction is easily confused, and the ESCB's wider membership means it includes states outside the euro area.

The primary objective of the system is to maintain price stability, and, without prejudice to that objective, to support the general economic policies in the Union. The ECB announced in 1998 that it would interpret price stability as a year-on-year increase in a harmonized consumer price index of below 2 percent, and in 2003 it clarified that it aimed at inflation below, but close to, 2 percent over the medium term. The treaty also prohibited central bank overdrafts and direct credit to governments, and the so-called no bailout clause provided that neither the Union nor a member state would be liable for the commitments of another state. The ECB's role in banking supervision was at that time limited, and it contributed to the smooth conduct of national supervisory policies, a point of significance for later developments treated in Chapter 138.

The decision-making bodies are the Governing Council, which consists of the six members of the Executive Board and the governors of the national central banks of the euro area, and the Executive Board, which implements policy. A General Council includes the President, the Vice-President, and the governors of all national central banks. The treaty provides that neither the ECB nor national central banks nor their decision-making bodies may seek or take instructions from governments or Union institutions, and that governments must respect this independence. Accountability is provided through reporting obligations, including an annual report to the European Parliament, the Council, the Commission, and the European Council, and the President's appearances before the Parliament, as well as judicial review by the Court of Justice.

Central bank independence had roots in the German experience. Germany's constitutional arrangements gave the Bundesbank autonomy, and its reputation for low inflation was regarded as an asset by German voters and as a model by many economists, although other states, such as Britain and France, had traditions of government control that were changed in the 1990s. The design therefore reflected a particular judgment that price stability was best secured by an independent institution with a clear mandate. The judgment was not universal, and critics noted that independence limited the influence of elected governments on a key area of economic policy and that a single mandate could be too narrow.

The central analytical question is what advantages and political difficulties emerge when several countries share a currency but retain substantial responsibility for taxation, spending, and economic policy. The advantages include credibility, stability of prices, and elimination of currency risk within the area. The difficulties include the need for fiscal coordination, since a single interest rate cannot fit all economies at all times, and the absence of a large central budget, which in federal states helps to absorb shocks affecting particular regions. Economists drawing on the theory of optimum currency areas, a field in which Robert Mundell had been influential, debated whether the euro area met the conditions of labor mobility, wage flexibility, and fiscal transfers that would make a common currency sustainable. The debate began before the euro and has not been resolved.

Section 134.12

The Euro, 1999 to 2002: From Financial Currency to Everyday Money

The decision on which states would take part in the third stage was taken at a special European Council in Brussels on May 2 and 3, 1998. On the basis of the Commission's and the European Monetary Institute's reports, the Council decided that eleven states fulfilled the conditions: Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain. Greece did not meet the conditions at that time. Britain and Denmark had opt-outs, and Sweden, which had not joined the exchange rate mechanism and had not taken the legal steps required, did not participate. The conversion rates were fixed on December 31, 1998, and the euro began as a currency on January 1, 1999. Its relationship to the earlier European Currency Unit was one to one.

In the first three years, the euro functioned mainly as a financial currency. Bank accounts, financial markets, accounting, government debt, and electronic payments could be denominated in euros, and national banknotes and coins remained in circulation as denominations of the euro, so that a French customer who paid in francs was in legal terms paying in a subunit of the euro. The conversion rates were irrevocable. As examples, one euro was set at 1.95583 German marks, 6.55957 French francs, 1,936.27 Italian lire, and 166.386 Spanish pesetas. The two-stage introduction allowed banks and businesses time to prepare and the public time to become familiar with the idea.

Greece joined the euro area on January 1, 2001, after the Council decided in June 2000 that it met the conditions, at a rate of 340.75 drachmas to the euro. The accuracy of Greek statistical data used in the assessment was later questioned, and revisions to Greek figures are treated in Chapter 138. Denmark, in a referendum on September 28, 2000, decided against adopting the euro, and in Sweden, voters rejected adoption in a referendum on September 14, 2003. In Britain, the government in 1997 set out economic tests for joining, which were never judged to be met. Slovenia adopted the euro in 2007, Cyprus and Malta in 2008, and Slovakia in 2009, so that the euro area had sixteen members by January 1, 2009. The accessions of those states belong to the history of enlargement in Chapter 136.

The introduction of banknotes and coins on January 1, 2002, in twelve countries was a major logistical operation. Banknotes were produced in seven denominations, with common designs depicting architectural styles of European history, and coins in eight denominations, each with a common European side and a national side designed by the issuing country. Banks and retailers received euro cash in advance, a practice known as frontloading, so that cash was available on the first day. Banks converted accounts, wages, and prices, and retailers adapted cash registers, vending machines, and price displays. National cash remained legal tender for a transition period that ended no later than the end of February 2002 in most countries. Some small states that were not Union members, such as Monaco, San Marino, and Vatican City, used the euro by agreement.

The changeover was at once an economic and a symbolic event. Supporters emphasized that a common currency would save the costs of exchanging money, make prices comparable, and simplify travel and business. Public reaction was mixed. Opinion surveys recorded concerns that retailers had rounded prices upward during conversion, and in Germany the word Teuro, which plays on the German word for expensive, was chosen as the word of the year for 2002. Statistical studies suggested that the measured effect on overall inflation was smaller than many people perceived, partly because the prices of frequently purchased items rose more visibly than others. The episode illustrates that the public perception of an economic change can differ from aggregate statistics. For many people, replacing a national currency, with its images and associations, also meant loss of a familiar national symbol, while for others the euro was a sign of belonging to a larger community.

Everyday Life and Social History

Converting to the Euro

The changeover affected different people in different ways. A shopkeeper had to recalculate prices, adapt a cash register, count the new coins, and give change in euros while accepting old money for a period. A bank employee managed the transfer of accounts and the distribution of cash. A pensioner who had long thought in terms of francs, marks, or lire had to learn new values and was sometimes uncertain about whether prices were fair. A traveler crossing between countries now needed no exchange when moving between euro area states, which was an obvious convenience, while a tourist from outside the area saw prices more easily compared.

Because the conversion rates were awkward numbers, many people continued for years to calculate in the old currency. The experience combined the practical advantages of simplicity with worries over familiarity and national identity. The sources for this social history include opinion surveys, press reports, and the records of the central banks, and they should be read with attention to the difference between perception and measured prices.

Section 134.13

The Amsterdam Treaty, Schengen, and the Expansion of European Cooperation

Governments agreed at Maastricht that the treaty should be reviewed, and in 1996 an intergovernmental conference began preparations for institutional reform and a broader agenda. Two reasons made further reform urgent only a few years after the Union began. The first was enlargement, since the accession of Austria, Finland, and Sweden in 1995, and the expected application of postcommunist states, raised the question of whether institutions designed for twelve could function for twenty or more. The second was the sense that the Union needed to respond to public concerns about employment, crime, immigration, and the Union's distance from citizens, expressed in the ratification crisis.

The Treaty of Amsterdam was signed on October 2, 1997, and entered into force on May 1, 1999. It added a title on employment to the treaty, which gave the Union a coordinating role in national employment policies without transferring responsibility from the states. It incorporated the social policy arrangements that had been confined to eleven states at Maastricht, since the Labour government elected in Britain in May 1997 ended the British exclusion. It added a provision enabling action against discrimination on grounds such as sex, race, religion, disability, age, and sexual orientation. It extended and simplified the co-decision procedure and strengthened Parliament's role in approving the President of the Commission. It created a High Representative for the Common Foreign and Security Policy, a post to be held by the Secretary-General of the Council, and it provided for closer cooperation among subsets of states under certain conditions. Amsterdam did not, however, resolve the sensitive questions of the size of the Commission and the weighting of votes in the Council, which were left for later negotiation.

One of its most important features concerned the area of freedom, security, and justice. Matters of asylum, immigration, border controls, and civil law cooperation were transferred from the third pillar to the Community framework, subject to transitional arrangements, while police and judicial cooperation in criminal matters remained within the third pillar. Britain and Ireland negotiated the right not to take part in these measures, and Denmark had a special position.

The Schengen arrangements were integrated by the same treaty. The Schengen Agreement had been signed on June 14, 1985, at the Luxembourg village of Schengen by Belgium, France, West Germany, Luxembourg, and the Netherlands, as an agreement outside the treaties of the European Communities, on the gradual abolition of checks at their common borders. An implementing convention was signed in 1990. Its practical application began on March 26, 1995, for seven states, the original five together with Spain and Portugal. The arrangements involved the removal of checks on persons at internal borders and compensatory measures, including a common visa policy, a shared information system for border and police data, and reinforced controls at external borders. Italy, Austria, Greece, and the Nordic states joined between 1997 and 2001, and Norway and Iceland, which were not members of the Union, participated through association. Nine of the states that joined the Union in 2004 entered the area on December 21, 2007, for land and sea borders. The Amsterdam Treaty incorporated the Schengen acquis into the Union's legal framework through a protocol.

Schengen participation must be distinguished from Union membership and from participation in the euro area. Britain and Ireland, both Union members, did not join, while Norway, Iceland, and later Switzerland and Liechtenstein, which are not Union members, joined. States may be in the euro area and not in Schengen, or the reverse. The combination of easier cross-border movement and national responsibilities for policing, immigration administration, and border control raised problems of trust among states, since a state with weaker controls at an external border affected the others. The history of integration therefore increasingly involved matters once regarded as central responsibilities of national governments.

Section 134.14

Common Foreign and Security Policy and the Limits of European Collective Action

The Maastricht Treaty created the Common Foreign and Security Policy, replacing the more informal European Political Cooperation. The decision followed the collapse of the Soviet bloc and the end of the stable bipolar order that had structured European security. Governments sought to coordinate diplomacy and to give the Union a voice, in part because a reunited Germany needed to be embedded in a framework that reassured its neighbors, and in part because the Union's weight in trade and aid was not matched by a political presence. The policy was nonetheless intergovernmental. Foreign policy remained primarily the responsibility of national governments, decisions in most cases required unanimity, and the treaty spoke of eventual framing of a common defense policy, which might in time lead to a common defense.

The difference between cooperation on foreign policy and a unified foreign policy directed by a single government is fundamental. States could agree on joint positions, joint actions, sanctions, and statements, yet retain their own armed forces, diplomatic services, alliances, and national interests. Those with traditions of neutrality, such as Ireland, Austria, Finland, and Sweden, did not wish to be bound by a defense commitment, while others, notably Britain, regarded NATO as the cornerstone of European defense. Shared diplomatic objectives did not necessarily produce agreement on the use of force.

The conflicts in the former Yugoslavia, which began in 1991, exposed the limits of coordination. In June 1991, as Slovenia and Croatia declared independence, a remark associated with the Luxembourg foreign minister Jacques Poos that the hour of Europe had arrived expressed an expectation that European governments could manage the crisis. The Union's diplomatic efforts, joint missions, and recognition decisions did not prevent the escalation of violence, and the major turning points in Bosnia in 1995 and in Kosovo in 1999 involved American leadership and NATO military action. These events are examined in Chapter 135, and here it is sufficient to note that they influenced European debates about military capabilities and about the relationship among the Union, NATO, and the United States.

The Amsterdam Treaty created the High Representative and provisions for common strategies and for constructive abstention, in which a state could abstain without preventing others from acting. In December 1998, the British prime minister Tony Blair and French president Jacques Chirac issued a declaration at Saint-Malo stating that the Union needed the capacity for autonomous action backed by credible military forces. It marked a change in the British position, which had earlier opposed such a capacity as a threat to NATO. In 1999, the Cologne European Council launched the European Security and Defence Policy, and in December 1999 the Helsinki European Council adopted a headline goal of being able to deploy up to sixty thousand troops within sixty days, sustainable for a year, by 2003. Javier Solana took up the post of High Representative in October 1999. The Union conducted small civilian and military operations from 2003, working in some cases with NATO resources under agreed arrangements.

In 2003, the divisions among European governments over the invasion of Iraq demonstrated that agreement on a framework did not guarantee agreement on policy. Britain, Spain, Italy, and several central European states supported the United States, while France and Germany opposed it. The security policy advanced more slowly than economic and monetary integration, because it touched on the core attributes of statehood, because the states differed in their strategic cultures and capabilities, and because NATO, with American participation, remained the principal collective defense organization for most members. The episode also demonstrates a general lesson of the period: the Union could build institutions faster in fields where states were prepared to share authority than in fields where national responsibility was held to be inseparable from sovereignty.

Section 134.15

The Treaty of Nice and the Problem of Governing an Enlarging Union

The institutions of the Community had been designed for six states and adapted for twelve. By the late 1990s, with fifteen members after the accession of Austria, Finland, and Sweden on January 1, 1995, and a prospect of up to a dozen more, the Union faced questions that Amsterdam had left unresolved. The Commission had two members from each large state and one from each of the others, and a larger membership threatened to make it unwieldy. The weighting of votes in the Council had been established when the number of states was small and did not reflect the large differences in population among the new candidates. The size of the Parliament and the distribution of seats also required adjustment. In addition, the Union needed to decide how far decisions that required unanimity could be taken by qualified majority, since the risk of paralysis grew with the number of states.

The Treaty of Nice was negotiated at a long and difficult European Council in December 2000, signed on February 26, 2001, and entered into force on February 1, 2003. It provided that from 2005 each member state would have one member of the Commission, with a cap to be set when the Union reached twenty-seven members, which was a compromise between a large and a small Commission. It reweighted votes in the Council, giving larger states more votes, and introduced additional conditions for a qualified majority, including a majority of states and, on request, a minimum share of the Union's population. It extended qualified majority voting to a number of further areas, limited the size of the European Parliament and allocated seats among existing and future members, and simplified the provisions for closer cooperation among groups of states.

The negotiations demonstrated the tension between larger and smaller countries. Large states wished their population to be reflected in voting, while smaller ones were concerned that they would lose influence, and each regarded the outcome as a matter of national status as well as procedure. Germany, with a significantly larger population than any other state, had accepted equal numbers of votes with France in the past and now sought greater weight, which France resisted. The compromises were widely regarded as complex and unsatisfactory, and critics said that the treaty did not resolve the problem of effective decision-making in a larger Union. Irish voters rejected the treaty in a referendum in June 2001 and approved it in October 2002, after the government secured declarations concerning Irish neutrality.

The treaty maintained the roles of the main institutions: the European Council, which gave direction; the Council of Ministers, which legislated with Parliament; the Commission, which proposed and executed; and the Court of Justice, whose composition and structure were also adapted and which received a reformed system of lower courts. The Union's institutions were thus adjusted at the margins and not reconstructed. Nice also included a declaration on the future of the Union, which called for a broader debate on the delimitation of competences, the status of the Charter, simplification of the treaties, and the role of national parliaments, and so led to the process described in the next section.

The enlargement that Nice prepared took place on May 1, 2004, when Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia joined, and Bulgaria and Romania followed on January 1, 2007, bringing the Union to twenty-seven members. The detailed history of postcommunist transitions and accession negotiations belongs to Chapter 136. For present purposes, the point is that the institutional arrangements of Nice had to bear a considerably larger and more diverse Union. Institutional reform remained contentious despite broad agreement that enlargement required adaptation, because every change in voting and representation altered the balance of national influence.

Section 134.16

The Charter of Fundamental Rights and the Failed European Constitution, 2000 to 2005

The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union was drafted by a body chaired by Roman Herzog, former President of Germany and of its Federal Constitutional Court, and it was proclaimed in December 2000 by the Parliament, the Council, and the Commission at the Nice European Council. It collected in one text rights that were found in national constitutions, the European Convention on Human Rights, the case law of the Court of Justice, and other instruments, and it organized them under the headings of dignity, freedoms, equality, solidarity, citizens' rights, and justice. It included the right to life, the prohibition of torture, respect for private life and protection of personal data, freedom of expression and assembly, the freedom to conduct a business, equality before the law and non-discrimination, workers' rights, and rights of access to documents and good administration. At proclamation, it was a political declaration without binding legal effect as a treaty. It acquired the status of binding primary law only through the Lisbon Treaty in 2009, subject to the treaty framework and to special arrangements for certain states.

The Charter was one input into a wider constitutional debate. The Nice declaration was followed by the Laeken European Council of December 2001, which asked how the Union could become more democratic, transparent, and efficient, how competences should be divided, how the treaties could be simplified, and whether a constitution might be adopted. It established the Convention on the Future of Europe, chaired by the former French president Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, which met from February 2002 to July 2003 and included representatives of governments, national parliaments, the European Parliament, and the Commission, with representatives of candidate states participating. A draft constitutional treaty was presented in 2003, modified in an intergovernmental conference that concluded in June 2004, and signed in Rome on October 29, 2004, as the Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe.

The proposal aimed to replace the existing treaties with a single text, to give the Union a single legal personality, to include the Charter, to create a President of the European Council elected for a fixed term and a Union Minister for Foreign Affairs, to extend qualified majority voting with a new double majority rule, to clarify competences, and to give national parliaments a role in monitoring subsidiarity. It also contained provisions about symbols such as the flag and anthem and stated the primacy of Union law over national law. The aims were institutional clarification, greater transparency, treaty consolidation, and a more coherent constitutional framework. Critics, however, noted that the text was long and technical, and that the word constitution carried symbolic weight which the legal content did not necessarily match.

Ratification required the approval of all member states. A majority of states ratified, by parliamentary vote or referendum, including Spain in a referendum in February 2005. But in a referendum on May 29, 2005, French voters rejected the treaty with roughly 55 percent voting against, and on June 1, 2005, Dutch voters rejected it with about 61 percent against. The results led governments to declare a period of reflection and effectively ended the project in its original form.

Competing explanations for the votes have been advanced, and none is accepted by all historians. Some stress concerns about economic policy, including liberalization of services, unemployment, and the perceived threat of competition from the new member states. Others point to a lack of democratic legitimacy and a sense that elites were deciding for the people. Others stress the effect of domestic politics, such as dissatisfaction with the incumbent governments, and the fact that many voters used the referendum to express opinions on matters unrelated to the text. Still others emphasize sovereignty, national identity, anxiety about enlargement and the pace of integration, and the technical complexity of the proposal. Survey data show that voters gave different reasons depending on their political position. The mandatory distinction is that the proposed European Constitution was signed but never entered into force, and it must not be treated as an operative constitution of the Union.

Section 134.17

The Treaty of Lisbon and the Reorganization of European Governance

The rejection of the constitutional treaty by French and Dutch electorates in 2005 left European governments with a problem that went beyond the fate of a document. The European Union had expanded to twenty-five members in 2004 and would grow to twenty-seven with the accession of Bulgaria and Romania in 2007. Its institutions still operated through a complicated mixture of Community powers, intergovernmental pillars, qualified-majority voting, and unanimity. The proposed Constitution would have consolidated much of that framework, but it could not enter into force without every member state's ratification. Governments had to determine whether institutional reform could resume without demanding public approval for the same constitutional presentation. That question was not merely technical. It concerned how far political leaders could proceed after citizens had rejected a treaty and whether changes in form could resolve disagreements over authority.

After a period of reflection, governments agreed on a reform treaty rather than a constitution that would formally replace the founding texts. The German presidency of the European Council helped secure a negotiating mandate in June 2007. An intergovernmental conference completed the text that was signed in Lisbon on December 13, 2007. The Treaty of Lisbon amended the Treaty on European Union and the Treaty establishing the European Community, the latter renamed the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. The constitutional terminology of a Union foreign minister and an overtly constitutional document was dropped, although many institutional arrangements from the failed proposal reappeared. The signature was a political achievement, not the completion of the legal process. All member states still needed to ratify the new treaty under their own constitutional procedures.

Ireland exposed that uncertainty. Its constitution required a referendum on this treaty change, and on June 12, 2008, a majority rejected Lisbon. Voters expressed different objections, including concerns about neutrality, taxation, social policy, and whether their country's influence would decline. The campaign also showed the difficulty of explaining a long treaty whose effects could be described in competing and sometimes misleading terms. European leaders did not simply disregard the result. The Irish government negotiated legally significant assurances concerning particular national competences and policies, including neutrality, taxation and sensitive social questions; these assurances did not rewrite the underlying institutional reforms. A second Irish referendum on October 2, 2009, approved the treaty. Delays in other national ratifications, notably in the Czech Republic, also had to be resolved. Lisbon entered into force on December 1, 2009, nearly two years after signature.

The reforms reorganized the Union's legal structure. The European Community was succeeded by the European Union, which acquired a single legal personality for the purposes established by the treaties. The Maastricht three-pillar framework ceased to be the organizing structure, although foreign and security policy retained special intergovernmental procedures and restrictions on judicial and parliamentary authority. The European Council became a formal Union institution with a president elected for a longer term than the earlier rotating chairmanship. The High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy combined roles connected to the Council and Commission, with the goal of more coherent external action; that arrangement did not replace national foreign ministers or unify member states' strategic priorities.

In a wider range of policy fields the European Parliament became a co-legislator with the Council under what Lisbon called the ordinary legislative procedure. Changes to qualified-majority voting, including a population-based double-majority principle subject to transitional arrangements, were intended to improve decision-making in a larger membership. National parliaments gained formal means of reviewing whether proposed Union legislation respected subsidiarity. The treaties also established the framework for a citizens' initiative inviting the Commission to consider legislation when the specified support threshold was met. These arrangements enlarged possibilities for participation while retaining substantial roles for national governments and independent national legislatures. A procedure for voluntary withdrawal from the Union was expressly provided by Article 50, demonstrating that membership was conceived as a legal relationship among states rather than an irreversible constitutional merger.

Lisbon also altered the standing of rights. The Charter of Fundamental Rights, proclaimed in 2000 and revised in 2007, acquired the same legal value as the treaties through Article 6 of the Treaty on European Union. Its application to member states remained limited to situations in which they were implementing Union law; it did not grant the Union an unlimited authority to legislate about every right named in the Charter. The charter's position must therefore be distinguished from the never-effective 2004 constitutional treaty. Lisbon gave many substantive reforms legal effect without enacting the rejected Constitution. In historical perspective, this was a negotiated recovery from constitutional stalemate, but it did not settle whether the Union's division of powers was sufficiently understandable or democratically accountable to its citizens.

Section 134.18

Representation, Citizenship, and Democratic Legitimacy in the European Union

The successive treaties created a political system that was difficult to describe using categories drawn from a single national constitution. The European Parliament was elected by voters, but elections took place through national electoral systems and were often conducted as contests about domestic politics. The European Council consisted of national heads of state or government, whose domestic electorates could hold them accountable through national political processes. The Council of the European Union brought national ministers into lawmaking, while the Commission was expected to promote the general Union interest and propose legislation in fields assigned to it. The Court of Justice interpreted treaty obligations and guarded the uniform application of Union law. None of these institutions alone resembled a national parliament or executive, yet they collectively exercised public authority that affected everyday economic and political life.

Supporters of this arrangement argued that legitimacy came from more than one channel. Citizens directly elected the European Parliament, and the Council's ministers represented governments accountable to national parliaments and electorates. The treaties themselves were negotiated and ratified according to national constitutional rules. Judgments of the Court of Justice could make legal rights available to citizens regardless of the attitude of a particular national administration. Critics nevertheless asked whether the interlocking channels made responsibility less visible rather than more democratic. When a regulation or budgetary requirement was unpopular, a national minister might blame Brussels while European officials pointed back to decisions made by national governments in Council. Such disputes gave practical content to the phrase democratic deficit.

Historical Vocabulary
Democratic Legitimacy

The justified claim that institutions exercising public power are authorized, accountable and subject to political and legal control. Within the European Union legitimacy may be asserted through citizens' direct representation in Parliament, member governments' democratic mandates, transparent procedures, judicially enforceable limits and opportunities for participation. Debates over a democratic deficit concern whether these channels provide adequate public understanding, influence and accountability when authority is shared between levels of government.

The Parliament's position changed substantially between the first direct elections of 1979 and Lisbon's entry into force in 2009. Its members gained more influence over ordinary legislation, the Commission's appointment, and the budget. Yet institutional power did not guarantee high public engagement. Election turnout varied among countries, and aggregate turnout declined across several elections in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Voters may have cared strongly about Europe while regarding parliamentary elections as less consequential than national contests. They also faced electoral campaigns dominated by domestic issues or uncertain information about which institution controlled a particular policy. A sound quantitative account would therefore distinguish turnout, survey attitudes, institutional competence and actual policy outcomes instead of treating any one measure as a direct index of political attachment.

European citizenship, introduced at Maastricht, offered another mode of attachment. A French national residing in Belgium or an Italian national living in Germany could invoke specified Union rights to move and reside, and could vote in local and European Parliament elections in the member state of residence under applicable conditions. Individuals could petition the European Parliament or approach the European Ombudsman about relevant administrative matters. Citizenship remained derivative of nationality of a member state: the Union did not establish a separate mechanism for naturalization divorced from national membership. The differences mattered for migrants from outside the Union, residents without a member-state nationality, and families with mixed citizenship status. A right written in a treaty could expand possibilities without erasing administrative requirements, social discrimination, language barriers, or differences in national welfare systems.

Questions of identity made the political debate more than an argument about institutional diagrams. For some voters and politicians, Europe was a practical means of protecting peace, travel, trade and rights across national lines. For others, political decisions ought to remain as close as possible to communities defined by national history, language and political solidarity. These views did not necessarily divide citizens into two permanent camps. A person might support free movement and the euro but oppose a proposed treaty; another might criticize Union policies while accepting common legal institutions. Debates over citizenship could be especially sharp when governments disagreed about labor migration, residence or the meaning of equal treatment. Chapter 137 will examine migration and identity in greater depth; here their importance is to show that the Union's legal authority operated within societies whose political loyalties could not be assumed.

Lisbon attempted to bring those strands together by strengthening parliamentary lawmaking, assigning national parliaments a subsidiarity-monitoring role and creating the legal framework for the European citizens' initiative. Participation remained structured by the treaties: citizens could request consideration of a proposal through the initiative but could not compel the Commission to adopt legislation. Subsidiarity review did not give every national parliament an unrestricted veto. This balance reflected the fundamental character of the Union, neither an ordinary international organization acting only through unanimous diplomatic agreements nor a unitary state possessing general legislative power. The institutional problem was to make shared authority capable of action while retaining a publicly defensible connection to national and European democratic processes.

Section 134.19

The Political Economy of the Euro: Benefits, Constraints, and Structural Tensions

The euro was designed to resolve problems that had become visible during decades of European monetary cooperation. If several economies trade intensively but use different currencies, firms must pay to exchange money, manage the risk of exchange-rate changes and interpret prices expressed in different units. Tourists, cross-border workers and small merchants face similar complications. By adopting one currency, participating states eliminated exchange-rate fluctuations among themselves and made cross-border price comparisons simpler. For a multinational manufacturer or exporter, being able to make contracts in one currency could reduce uncertainty, although the overall benefits depended on patterns of trade and competition. For governments, the euro symbolized a commitment to a long-term monetary framework that national exchange-rate policy could no longer overturn unilaterally.

The benefits were accompanied by a transfer of authority. A government whose country used the euro could no longer reduce the foreign-exchange value of its currency to make exports temporarily cheaper or use its national central bank to set a policy interest rate adapted only to domestic conditions. The European Central Bank's monetary policy reflected conditions across the euro area. This common policy could be appropriate for the area as a whole while being restrictive for a member experiencing weak growth or unusually high unemployment. Conversely, a country experiencing a credit boom might find monetary conditions too accommodating for its own circumstances. These are not necessarily signs of institutional failure; they are consequences of operating one currency across economies that do not experience identical changes at identical times.

Other possible adjustments remained, but they could be socially and politically difficult. Countries could change wages and prices relative to trading partners, increase productivity, shift workers between industries or regions, or modify fiscal policy. Labor mobility might reduce unemployment differences, but moving for work required language skills, family adaptation and access to housing and public services. Wage adjustment could be especially painful when prices and debts were slow to change. The original euro-area members differed in employment structures, industrial specialization, public finances and degrees of economic competitiveness. The existence of common banknotes did not automatically align their productivity, labor institutions or public spending decisions. Consequently, monetary union made those structural differences more important for macroeconomic policy.

The institutional compromise was distinctive. Monetary policy for the euro area was centralized in the ECB and Eurosystem, but taxation, most public expenditure and much economic policy remained national responsibilities. Governments agreed to coordinate their policies and submit to common budgetary rules rather than to create a fully centralized treasury. The Stability and Growth Pact, established by a European Council resolution and regulations in 1997, supplied preventive surveillance and a framework for addressing excessive deficits. The familiar reference values of three percent of GDP for deficits and sixty percent for debt came from the Maastricht legal framework. Their application depended on procedures, judgments and treaty qualifications rather than on an automatic threshold machine. Fiscal disputes therefore involved not only economics but also the power to judge national budgets, the credibility of commitments, and the legitimacy of sanctions or flexibility.

Contemporaries debated whether monetary union could survive without larger common fiscal resources, more convergent economic structures or additional political integration. One interpretation held that common money would encourage greater coordination by making governments and firms adapt to the same monetary rules. Another warned that the system lacked a sufficiently powerful common fiscal capacity to stabilize an area-wide downturn and that national authorities might pursue incompatible fiscal or financial policies. Both arguments can be reconstructed from the design choices made in the 1990s. Neither should be presented as a certain prediction of the subsequent euro-area sovereign-debt crisis. The latter occurred in a different international and financial setting and belongs to Chapter 138, which can evaluate later outcomes with the benefit of evidence unavailable to Maastricht's negotiators.

These questions also concerned political choices about solidarity. Who should bear the costs of economic adjustment when one member experienced recession while another prospered? To what extent should the Union encourage common protections while allowing different national welfare and taxation systems? What counted as fair treatment between governments that followed different budget policies? Monetary integration did not eliminate such distributive questions. Instead, it transferred some decisions from national monetary authorities to common institutions while leaving others within national political systems. The euro thus altered both the economic instruments available to European states and the forums in which arguments about responsibility were conducted.

By 2008 the global financial crisis was testing financial systems internationally. The European monetary structure had delivered a major operational achievement in moving from separate currencies to common payments and cash, but its political economy remained unsettled. A historian should distinguish the reasons for initial adoption, institutional performance in its first decade, and the policy conflicts that followed. Understanding the original balance between a single monetary policy and national fiscal authority is necessary for Chapter 138, but the details of crisis management, sovereign borrowing problems, assistance programs, austerity and their social consequences belong there rather than in the present chapter.

Section 134.20

Maastricht, Monetary Union, Sovereignty, and the European Project: Historical Synthesis

The history from the Single European Act to Lisbon began with an economic problem and culminated in a debate about political authority. In the mid 1980s the European Community had removed most internal tariffs but still faced national regulations and administrative borders that hindered trade and investment. The Single European Act strengthened procedures for removing those obstacles. Currency volatility and the liberalization of capital movements then encouraged governments to consider a monetary union that would be more binding than earlier exchange-rate arrangements. The Delors Report supplied a staged institutional design, but the decisive choices were made by governments through treaty negotiations. Maastricht committed them to a new framework whose implications extended beyond the internal market. This was a political achievement shaped by economic interests, not an automatic result of economic integration.

The European Union established in 1993 was neither a sovereign state replacing its members nor a mere secretariat for agreements between governments. The three-pillar structure made that compromise visible. The European Communities retained developed supranational legal and administrative instruments, while foreign policy and justice cooperation operated through different procedures with stronger governmental control. The development of Union citizenship broadened individual rights and symbolic membership, but national citizenship remained the condition of Union citizenship. These arrangements reflected the different expectations that had converged on Maastricht. The Union was made by states willing to share some authority and reluctant to surrender other authority.

The monetary union revealed how extensive an institutional commitment could be. Participating governments abandoned independent national monetary policy and, beginning in 1999, irrevocably fixed their currencies' conversion rates under the new euro. For three years national notes and coins still circulated, and the 2002 cash changeover turned a technical monetary transformation into an event visible in ordinary shops and households. Central banking responsibilities were shared through a new institutional framework in Frankfurt. Yet the euro-area countries retained different fiscal policies and economic structures. The creation of one currency did not imply the creation of one national economy or eliminate choices about employment, taxation, spending, and distribution.

Political opposition and uncertainty formed part of the process from the beginning. Danish voters rejected Maastricht before a later vote under clarified conditions approved it; the French referendum was closely contested; Britain obtained negotiated arrangements concerning monetary participation. Currency crises exposed the costs of trying to maintain fixed exchange rates before monetary union. Later treaty negotiations revealed disputes over institutional influence, voting rights, social policy, national autonomy and the public's role in authorizing changes. In 2005 the proposed European Constitution failed after rejection in France and the Netherlands. Its failure does not prove that integration ceased, but it demonstrates that governments could not simply treat political consent as an inevitable consequence of expanding markets.

Amsterdam, Nice and Lisbon responded to different institutional pressures. Amsterdam widened cooperation and brought Schengen rules into the treaty framework. Nice attempted to adapt representation and decision-making to enlargement. Lisbon reorganized legal personality, institutional responsibilities and legislative procedures after the constitutional treaty failed. It gave the Charter of Fundamental Rights binding legal value and increased Parliament's position in legislation, while maintaining special rules in sensitive areas. Its entry into force required resolution of renewed ratification disputes, including Irish referendums. Change therefore resulted from repeated agreements and adjustments rather than one final founding moment.

European Governance from 1987 to 2009

Continuities: National governments remained indispensable to treaty reform and to the Council; the Union operated only within powers conferred by states; member states differed in political priorities; questions of democratic consent continued throughout.

Changes: Community law expanded beyond the old customs union, Maastricht established Union citizenship and the EMU framework, the euro entered monetary use in 1999 and cash use in 2002, successive treaties expanded Parliament's legislative role, and Lisbon ended the formal three-pillar framework.

Several models help explain the result, but each is incomplete when used alone. A federal interpretation highlights common legal principles, central banking and the growing authority of directly elected representatives. An intergovernmental interpretation emphasizes negotiated treaty change, national vetoes and the continuing importance of leaders meeting in the European Council. A system-of-shared-governance interpretation notes that powers vary by policy: the Union could exercise substantial authority over monetary policy in the euro area while states retained major roles in taxation, welfare and defense. Historical analysis should not begin by choosing a label and forcing all evidence into it. The diversity of institutions and the unequal pace of participation were themselves characteristic features of European integration.

Finally, the story should not be treated as a straight line from 1986 to a settled future. Politicians who signed Maastricht could not know the precise form of later treaty reforms, the outcome of the constitutional referendums or the subsequent financial and geopolitical crises. National differences survived, the effects of integration remained uneven, and institutions still required political approval for significant changes. Chapter Summary

European integration acquired a different scope in the years from the Single European Act to Lisbon. Chapter 133 traced the construction of the European Communities through treaties, a customs union, agricultural agreements and the legal development of a common market. Chapter 134 explained why the completion of that market encouraged further decisions about money, political institutions and individual rights. These decisions were not inherent in the first European treaties. They emerged through negotiations over the costs of separate national currencies and the possibilities of exercising authority in common.

The Single European Act, signed in 1986 and effective in 1987, sought to eliminate non-tariff restrictions that continued to obstruct economic exchange. It expanded qualified-majority voting in specified areas and gave the European Parliament additional influence. Jacques Delors and his Commission made the completion of the internal market a principal political objective. The removal of capital controls and the resulting pressures on exchange-rate arrangements encouraged a renewed search for monetary cooperation.

Earlier efforts at monetary integration provided experience but not a single currency. The Werner Plan had been interrupted by international monetary instability, and the European Monetary System preserved national currencies within negotiated exchange-rate margins. The Delors Report of April 1989 proposed stages toward Economic and Monetary Union and an independent system of central banks. Governments chose to give parts of that proposal legal effect only through the Maastricht negotiations and subsequent treaty ratification.

German reunification and the end of the Cold War altered the circumstances in which European leaders negotiated. Germany, France, Britain and the smaller Community members brought distinct political and economic priorities to the negotiations. The Maastricht Treaty was signed on February 7, 1992, and entered into force on November 1, 1993. The dates are separate, because signature alone did not confer legal effect. Maastricht established the European Union and gave formal expression to a three-pillar settlement combining Community powers with intergovernmental cooperation in other areas.

European Union citizenship supplemented, rather than replaced, nationality of a member state. It provided rights relating to movement, residence, political participation and access to selected institutions. The creation of Union citizenship did not erase national laws on nationality, differences in welfare policy or administrative conditions affecting the exercise of rights. Ratification disputes in Denmark, France and Britain demonstrated that expanded legal and political cooperation could provoke powerful disagreements about sovereignty and democratic authorization.

Monetary union was designed as a staged process. The first stage began in 1990, the European Monetary Institute prepared institutions in the second stage from 1994, and the third stage began on January 1, 1999. The Maastricht convergence criteria covered inflation, government finances, interest rates and exchange-rate stability. Their reference values were governed by legal procedures rather than by a simple automatic formula. The Exchange Rate Mechanism crisis of 1992 and 1993 exposed how exchange commitments could become unstable under diverging national conditions and open capital markets.

The European Central Bank, founded in June 1998, became the core of a monetary system designed to operate independently within treaty-defined responsibilities. On January 1, 1999, eleven member states adopted the euro as their common currency for monetary and accounting purposes. Greece joined in 2001, and banknotes and coins entered circulation in twelve countries on January 1, 2002. The separation between monetary and cash changeovers illustrates how a major institutional decision could precede the visible everyday transformation by several years.

The Union's policy fields and institutions also expanded. The Amsterdam Treaty revised employment and justice provisions and incorporated Schengen cooperation into the treaty framework. The Treaty of Nice addressed the balance of representation and voting in a Union preparing to enlarge. These measures did not resolve every institutional disagreement. Member states continued to differ over national discretion, public accountability and the division of responsibilities among the Commission, Councils, Parliament and courts.

The Charter of Fundamental Rights, proclaimed in 2000, gathered rights in a single document but was not initially a treaty instrument with binding legal force. Governments then proposed a Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe. Signed in 2004, it did not enter into force after its rejection in French and Dutch referendums in 2005. Those outcomes showed that reforms could be negotiated among governments but still fail during the process of national authorization. The Union's institutional evolution was neither a continuous sequence of uncontested victories nor simply a succession of failures.

The Lisbon Treaty, signed in 2007 and effective on December 1, 2009, reorganized the Union's institutional and legal structure. It strengthened the European Parliament's legislative role, recognized the Charter as legally binding within its treaty scope, provided mechanisms for national parliamentary scrutiny of subsidiarity and ended the formal Maastricht three-pillar framework. Irish voters rejected Lisbon in 2008 and approved it in 2009 after additional assurances. The episode again demonstrated the importance of national constitutional procedures in the construction of common institutions.

The euro's economic achievements were accompanied by continuing structural tensions. Member states that shared money no longer controlled national monetary policy or exchange rates, but they retained extensive responsibility for taxation, budgets and domestic economic arrangements. Their economic structures remained different, and a common monetary decision could affect members unevenly. The Stability and Growth Pact expressed an attempt to coordinate national fiscal behavior without establishing a fully centralized treasury. Debates about the adequacy of this design continued into the financial shocks at the end of the period.

The historical conclusion is that the European Union became an unusually developed system of shared authority whose political and economic functions varied by policy field and by member state. Its institutions reflected treaties negotiated by sovereign governments, judicial developments, direct elections and public participation. Those features created important opportunities while also complicating responsibility and political consent. The later European stories of war, enlargement, migration, financial crisis and geopolitical rivalry should be understood against this institutional background, without assuming that their outcomes were inevitable when Maastricht was signed.

Chapter Reflection: Shared Authority and the Problem of Consent

Between 1986 and 2009 European governments built institutions through which they exercised powers that had often been identified with the nation-state. The results touched money, movement, legislation, rights and external affairs. Some of those developments, especially the common currency, required decisions that could not simply be reversed by national policy-makers. Others preserved considerable room for differences among states.

That history presents a problem at the heart of the European project. Countries may obtain benefits from common rules precisely because those rules limit what any one of them can decide alone. Yet the transfer or pooling of authority also makes democratic accountability more complex. Who should determine the limits of a common policy, and what kinds of consent should be required when an earlier settlement is revised?

Maastricht, the euro, the rejected Constitution and Lisbon illustrate different answers. Their historical significance lies not in demonstrating that deeper integration was inevitable or that its critics were necessarily mistaken, but in revealing how European societies negotiated and renegotiated the terms of governing together. Those negotiations form the institutional setting for the conflicts and transformations examined in the next chapters.
