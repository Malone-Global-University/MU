Chapter Opening

Between 1935 and 1939 the instruments meant to preserve European peace, treaties, guarantees, a League of Nations and the diplomacy of great powers, were tested in Africa, in Spain, in Austria, in Czechoslovakia and in Poland. Each test left the next one more dangerous. War came in September 1939 not because it was fated, but because governments with unequal intentions and unequal power made choices that narrowed the room for peace.

In January and February 1939, roads in Catalonia leading north toward the French frontier filled with civilians, wounded soldiers, and retreating Republican units. Roughly half a million people crossed the Pyrenees in a matter of weeks, and many were held on arrival in improvised camps on the beaches of southern France. These columns were the human result of nearly three years of civil war in Spain, a war that Europeans of every political persuasion had watched, debated, and in some cases fought in. A photographer such as David Seymour, whose 1939 images of Spanish refugees are held by the Library of Congress, could record the scene, but a photograph cannot by itself explain why a legally elected government fell, or why other European governments declined to prevent its fall.

Only a few months earlier, in late September 1938, the mood in other European capitals had been different but not unrelated. In London, workers dug air-raid trenches in public parks and the government distributed gas masks, while in Paris and Prague reserves were called up and people waited to learn whether a quarrel over the German-speaking border districts of Czechoslovakia would become a general war. The settlement signed at Munich on September 29 and 30 spared Europe a war that month. It also transferred territory from a sovereign democratic state to Germany without that state's participation, and within six months Germany occupied the rest of the Czech lands. The same governments that had signed at Munich then offered a guarantee to Poland, and in September 1939 they declared war.

This chapter studies the interplay of these events as a single historical problem: how Europe's international order broke down between 1935 and 1939. It has three connected subjects. The first is the failure of collective security, visible in the League's response to Italy's invasion of Ethiopia and in the muted reaction to German rearmament and the reoccupation of the Rhineland. The second is the Spanish Civil War, which was a Spanish conflict with deep domestic roots before it became an international ideological struggle and a laboratory for the diplomacy of non-intervention. The third is the series of crises over Austria, Czechoslovakia, and Poland, in which German territorial demands, Anglo-French concessions, Soviet strategic calculation, and the decisions of threatened states combined to produce war.

The chapter avoids two familiar simplifications. One treats the Second World War as inevitable from the moment Hitler took power, as though every diplomatic decision were merely a delay of a fixed outcome. The other treats the war's origins as a story of the single failure of one policy, appeasement, as though a different British or French decision at a single moment would have averted catastrophe. Historians who have studied the archives of the period generally find both pictures too tidy. The record shows aggressive regimes with ideological and strategic goals, democratic governments constrained by public opinion, finances, and military weakness, a Soviet Union pursuing its own security, and smaller states exercising choices of their own. The probability of war rose through these years, but at several points governments could have acted differently, and they knew less than later readers know.

The evidence on which this chapter rests is unusually rich and unusually contested. The great published collections of diplomatic documents, including the British, German, French, and Soviet series, allow historians to compare what governments said to one another with what they said privately. Parliamentary debates, newspapers, and the League of Nations records show how policies were justified in public. For Spain, the archives of the Republic and of the Nationalists, volunteers' letters, posters, photographs, and the testimonies of refugees supply evidence of civil war politics and civilian experience. Each type has limits. Diplomatic records show elite calculation but rarely the views of ordinary citizens, memoirs written after 1945 were shaped by knowledge of the war's outcome, and wartime publications such as government white books were selective by design.

This chapter occupies a particular place in the textbook. Chapter 113 explained the treaty settlement of 1919 and the new states it created, Chapter 114 examined the fragility of interwar democracy and the economic crisis, and Chapter 115 explained how authoritarian regimes in Italy, Germany, the Soviet Union, and elsewhere built their systems of power. Chapter 116 draws on all three, but it does not repeat their accounts of domestic institutions. Its task is the international consequences: how those regimes and the democracies confronting them interacted. It closes Part XVII and Volume V's first movement by ending with the outbreak of war in September 1939. The military campaigns that followed belong to Chapter 117, and the occupation regimes, genocide, and resistance belong to Chapters 120 and 121.

Finally, the chapter treats the people affected by these decisions as historical actors and not only as objects of great-power diplomacy. Ethiopians, Spaniards, Austrians, Czechs, Slovaks, Sudeten Germans, Poles, Jews in Vienna and Prague, volunteers in the International Brigades, refugees in French camps, British and French citizens anxious about bombing, and Soviet citizens living under Stalin's rule experienced the same years very differently. The chapter's features, including a multiple-perspectives study, a social-history study of Spanish civilians, and a primary-source study of the Munich Agreement, are designed to keep those differences in view. Its overall aim is to help students understand why the peace failed, with attention to evidence, contingency, and responsibility.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the collective-security system of the interwar period depended on the willingness and capacity of great powers to enforce it, and trace how the crises of 1931 to 1936 exposed that dependence.
  2. Distinguish among treaty revisionism pursued by negotiation, territorial revisionism pursued by coercion, and imperial conquest, using Germany and Italy as cases.
  3. Analyze the origins, course, and consequences of the Italian invasion of Ethiopia and the German remilitarization of the Rhineland for the European balance of power.
  4. Trace the political origins and military course of the Spanish Civil War, distinguishing the diverse coalitions on both sides.
  5. Assess how non-intervention operated in practice and compare German, Italian, Soviet, and volunteer involvement in Spain.
  6. Interpret the strategic logic of appeasement as a contemporary policy, distinguishing what governments knew at the time from later judgments.
  7. Describe the Anschluss and explain its consequences for Austrians, for Czechoslovakia, and for central European security.
  8. Analyze the Munich Agreement as a document and as a diplomatic event, with attention to Czechoslovak sovereignty and the reactions of different groups.
  9. Evaluate the roles of Poland, Britain, France, the Soviet Union, and Germany in the crises of 1939 and distinguish the German invasion of September 1 from the Soviet invasion of September 17.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of why European peace failed, without assigning equal responsibility for initiating aggression to all governments.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the League of Nations and the Locarno system fail to restrain aggression between 1935 and 1936, and which failures belonged to institutions and which to governments?
  2. How did the Spanish Civil War change from a domestic conflict into an international ideological contest, and what did intervention and non-intervention reveal about European politics?
  3. What problems was appeasement designed to solve, and how should historians weigh its contemporary reasoning against its results?
  4. How did the evidence available to British, French, German, Soviet, and Czechoslovak leaders at each decision point shape their choices?
  5. In what ways did smaller states, including Ethiopia, Austria, Czechoslovakia, and Poland, exercise agency, and what limits did they face?
  6. Why did the occupation of Prague in March 1939 change British and French policy when earlier territorial changes had not?
  7. How should historians distinguish diplomatic understandings, ideological partnerships, and formal military alliances in the alignments of 1936 to 1939?
  8. Why do historians argue that war was increasingly probable by 1939 but not mechanically inevitable, and what evidence supports this view?

Historical Context

The diplomatic order examined in this chapter was built on the postwar settlement of 1919 to 1923, described in Chapter 113. The Treaty of Versailles and its companion treaties redrew the map of central and eastern Europe, created or enlarged Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Romania, and others, and imposed military and territorial restrictions on Germany, including the demilitarization of the Rhineland. The League of Nations, established in 1920, was meant to supply a standing forum for the resolution of disputes and a framework for collective action against aggressors. In 1925 the Locarno agreements added a guarantee by Britain and Italy of the Franco-German and Belgian-German frontiers, though not of Germany's eastern borders, a distinction that would matter later to Poland and Czechoslovakia.

That order never enjoyed universal consent. Germany regarded the treaty as a dictated peace and sought revision of its frontiers and its armaments, though Weimar governments sought revision through negotiation and had achieved real results, including the end of Allied occupation of the Rhineland in 1930. Hungary resented the loss of two thirds of its prewar territory, Bulgaria sought outlets to the Aegean, and Italy felt that its wartime sacrifices had not been rewarded. France and the new states of eastern Europe, by contrast, defended the settlement and built a network of alliances: the Franco-Polish alliance of 1921, the Franco-Czechoslovak treaty of 1924, and the Little Entente of Czechoslovakia, Romania, and Yugoslavia. Britain, exhausted by the war and anxious about imperial commitments, supported a moderate revision of the settlement while refusing to guarantee central and eastern European frontiers.

The Great Depression, examined in Chapter 114, damaged this order in several ways. It undermined parliamentary governments and strengthened nationalist and authoritarian movements. It encouraged protectionism and economic nationalism, reduced the willingness of governments to spend on distant commitments, and made economists and treasuries central voices in defense policy. In Germany it contributed to the collapse of the Weimar Republic and the appointment of Adolf Hitler as Chancellor in January 1933. Chapter 115 explained how the Nazi regime consolidated power and how Mussolini's Italy and Stalin's Soviet Union developed their own authoritarian systems. This chapter begins where those domestic transformations began to have international effects.

The international crisis was also a crisis of memory. The First World War, examined in Chapters 108 to 112, had killed millions, and its legacy in Britain and France was a deep reluctance to repeat it. In Britain this showed in the Peace Ballot of 1934 and 1935, a large unofficial poll organized by the League of Nations Union that showed wide support for the League and for arms limitation, though also majority support for economic and, in many answers, military measures against an aggressor. In France the loss of a large proportion of a generation shaped defensive military planning, symbolized by the Maginot Line. Many leaders feared above all the bombing of cities, a fear shaped by the First World War's air raids and by popular predictions of the effects of aerial attack. These memories and fears shaped what governments believed they could ask of their citizens.

Finally, a number of regional crises outside Europe shaped the European strategic environment. Japan's seizure of Manchuria in 1931 and its war with China from July 1937 stretched British imperial defense and made British planners anxious about facing hostile powers in Europe, the Mediterranean, and East Asia at once. The League's failure in Manchuria taught the leaders of Italy and Germany that a determined major power might not meet effective collective resistance. The Soviet Union entered the League in 1934 and, under Foreign Commissar Maxim Litvinov, advocated collective security, even as Stalin's purges damaged the Red Army's leadership and Western confidence in Soviet power. Against this background, Chapter 116 follows the crises in order, with attention to how each one altered the expectations and the options of those that followed.

The Breakdown of Collective Security

Section 116.1

The International Order after Versailles: Security, Revisionism, and the Failure of Cooperation

The international system of the 1920s rested on three overlapping foundations: the territorial and military terms of the postwar treaties, the League of Nations, and a set of regional guarantees of which the Locarno agreements of 1925 were the most important. As Chapter 113 showed, none of these was self-enforcing. The Covenant of the League provided for consultation, arbitration, and in cases of aggression an economic boycott under Article 16, with military measures left to the discretion of members. The League had no army, its Council acted by unanimity, and its two potentially strongest supporters, the United States and, until 1934, the Soviet Union, stood outside it. The Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928, signed by dozens of states, renounced war as an instrument of national policy but contained no mechanism for dealing with a state that broke the promise.

The Locarno system illustrates the same asymmetry. By guaranteeing the Franco-German and Belgian-German frontiers, Britain and Italy created a security commitment in western Europe. They declined to make the same commitment for Germany's frontiers with Poland and Czechoslovakia, which Germany signed only arbitration treaties to respect. Many contemporaries believed that Locarno had pacified the west and left the east open to later revision. The settlement therefore combined firm obligations in one region with ambiguity in another, and the ambiguity was to prove consequential when German policy turned east in the late 1930s.

The Great Depression, after 1929, strained this arrangement in at least three ways. First, it weakened the parliamentary governments that were the system's principal supporters, as Chapter 114 described, and strengthened parties that rejected the postwar order. Second, it encouraged economic nationalism, so that governments in Britain and France prioritized budgets, currency stability, and unemployment over foreign commitments. Third, it supplied revisionist movements with grievances that they could use to build mass support. In Germany the Nazi movement combined demands for treaty revision with a broader ideology of racial and territorial expansion, examined in Chapter 115. The seizure of Manchuria by Japan in September 1931 demonstrated that a major power could defy the League without suffering decisive collective penalties, a lesson that was not lost on Rome or Berlin.

It is essential to distinguish among the governments that wanted change. In the 1920s and early 1930s many states, including democratic ones, sought revision of the peace settlement by negotiation. Britain accepted that some German claims were reasonable, including equality in armaments and adjustment of reparations. Weimar governments pursued revision by diplomacy and achieved results: the evacuation of the Rhineland in 1930 and the end of reparations in practice by 1932. Hungary, Bulgaria, and Italy also sought changes. Revisionism, in other words, was not itself aggression, and demands for treaty change could be pursued within the framework of international law. What marked the policies of Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy after 1933 and 1935 was their readiness to use force or its threat, their rejection of the multilateral process, and in Germany's case an ideological program that went well beyond the restoration of the 1914 frontiers. Italy's attack on Ethiopia was not revision of any treaty at all, but a war of colonial conquest against a fellow League member.

Historians reconstruct this order from the League's published records, the great collections of diplomatic documents, ministers' papers, and the press. They also use unofficial evidence of opinion, such as the Peace Ballot in Britain, though such polls must be read carefully because their questions were framed by organizations with particular aims. The evidence suggests that the international order of 1931 was less a stable system than a set of commitments whose credibility depended on the readiness of the strongest powers to defend them. The problem that this chapter explores is that, as more states tested those commitments between 1935 and 1939, the readiness proved to be uncertain, unequal, and in the end insufficient. International agreements could restrain an aggressive government only if other governments possessed both the capacity and the will to enforce them.

Historical Vocabulary
Collective Security, Revisionism, Deterrence, and Territorial Sovereignty

Collective security was the idea that peace could be preserved if all members of an international organization agreed to treat an attack on one as a matter for all. It differed from an alliance, which is directed at named opponents and pledges mutual aid among specific partners. In the League of Nations the principle was embodied in Articles 10 and 16, but the Covenant left each member to decide how far to take part in enforcement, and in practice the principle worked only when great powers agreed to apply it.

Revisionism refers to efforts to change the terms of the 1919 settlement. It should not be treated as a single phenomenon. Some governments wanted adjustments to specific clauses and were willing to negotiate. Others, including Nazi Germany, sought changes that were not limited by treaty rights and were prepared to use force. Deterrence is the strategy of discouraging attack by threatening unacceptable costs. It depends on two things: capacity, the forces to impose costs, and credibility, the aggressor's belief that they will be used. Historians often analyze the 1930s as a series of failures of deterrence.

Territorial sovereignty is the principle that a state controls its own territory and cannot be deprived of it without its consent. The principle was central to the Covenant, and the crises of the late 1930s repeatedly raised the question of whether sovereignty was compatible with great-power bargaining. Students should note the contrast between sovereignty as a legal right and sovereignty as practical capacity to resist: Ethiopia, Austria, and Czechoslovakia each held the first and lacked, in different ways, the second.

Section 116.2

Italy's Invasion of Ethiopia and the League of Nations Crisis

Ethiopia, also called Abyssinia in the diplomacy of the day, was one of the few African states to retain its independence during the colonial partition described in Chapter 104. It had defeated an Italian army at Adwa in 1896, joined the League of Nations in 1923, and was ruled from 1930 by Emperor Haile Selassie, who sought to modernize its administration and army while using League membership as a shield against colonial pressure. Italy held the neighboring colonies of Eritrea and Italian Somaliland, and Benito Mussolini, whose regime's domestic institutions Chapter 115 examined, regarded the conquest of Ethiopia as a way to avenge Adwa, create a settler and resource empire, and demonstrate Fascist vitality.

The immediate pretext was an incident at Walwal, an oasis in the disputed border region, on December 5, 1934, in which Italian and Ethiopian forces clashed and many Ethiopian soldiers and some Italian colonial troops were killed. Ethiopia appealed to the League, and Italy refused to accept responsibility. Mussolini meanwhile ordered the dispatch of large forces to East Africa, building a force of hundreds of thousands. Britain and France, anxious to preserve Italian goodwill against Germany, sought a compromise that would give Italy economic and territorial advantages without war. At Stresa in April 1935, Britain, France, and Italy had jointly condemned German rearmament, and neither London nor Paris wished to lose Italy from that front. The Ethiopian question was therefore entangled from the start with the German question.

Italian forces crossed from Eritrea on October 3, 1935. The League Council promptly concluded that Italy had resorted to war in breach of the Covenant, and the Assembly endorsed a program of sanctions, applied by most members from November 18, 1935. It would be wrong to say that sanctions did not exist. They included an arms embargo against Italy, a ban on loans and credits, a prohibition on many imports from Italy, and a ban on exports of key materials. But the measures excluded oil, coal, and steel, the items most directly necessary to a modern war effort, and the Suez Canal remained open to Italian shipping. Britain and France feared that an oil embargo might provoke Italy to attack, and neither had planned for war in the Mediterranean. Sanctions imposed real costs on the Italian economy and on public finances, yet they did not stop the invasion.

The Hoare–Laval proposals of December 1935 revealed the underlying hesitation. British Foreign Secretary Samuel Hoare and French Prime Minister Pierre Laval prepared a plan to give Italy large parts of Ethiopia and an economic zone of influence over much of the rest, leaving the Emperor with a reduced state. When the terms were leaked in the French press, public reaction in Britain was fiercely hostile, and Hoare resigned. The proposals were abandoned, but the episode taught different lessons to different observers. It showed that the public in democracies would not accept an open sacrifice of a League member, and it showed that their governments were prepared to consider one.

Italian forces, using aircraft, artillery, and chemical weapons including mustard gas, in violation of the Geneva Protocol of 1925 to which Italy was party, defeated Ethiopian armies and entered Addis Ababa on May 5, 1936. Haile Selassie went into exile, and Italy proclaimed King Victor Emmanuel III emperor of Ethiopia and annexed the country to Italian East Africa. On June 30, 1936, the Emperor addressed the League Assembly in Geneva in person, interrupted by Italian journalists in the gallery, and warned that the fate of Ethiopia would be a warning to other small states. The League voted soon afterward to lift sanctions. The episode was widely understood in Europe and beyond as the failure of collective security.

The analysis of this failure should avoid simple explanations. One cause lay in the reluctance of Britain and France, the League's principal members, to risk a Mediterranean war while Germany rearmed. Another lay in the structure of the League itself, which required consensus and had no independent means of enforcement. Another lay in domestic politics: British opinion supported the League but also feared war, and French governments were divided. Ethiopia, finally, was not a passive victim. It mobilized as fully as it could, appealed to international law, and carried on resistance in some regions even after the fall of the capital, and Italian rule remained contested. The war had profound consequences for European diplomacy: it destroyed the Stresa Front, strained Britain's and France's relations with Italy, and drew Mussolini toward Germany. Section 116.3

German Rearmament and the Remilitarization of the Rhineland

Nazi Germany's early foreign policy combined public declarations of peaceful intent with a rapid program of rearmament. On October 14, 1933, Hitler announced Germany's withdrawal from the Disarmament Conference and from the League of Nations, citing the refusal of other powers to grant Germany equality in arms. The decision was ratified by a plebiscite in November. In January 1935 the Saar, administered by the League since 1920, voted overwhelmingly to return to Germany, an outcome that reflected the wishes of a German-speaking population but which Nazi propaganda treated as a triumph for the regime. On March 16, 1935, the government announced the reintroduction of conscription and the creation of an army of thirty-six divisions, together with the open existence of an air force, in direct breach of the Treaty of Versailles.

The reactions of the major powers were hesitant. Britain, France, and Italy met at Stresa in April 1935, reaffirmed Locarno, and condemned unilateral treaty breaches, and the League Council passed a resolution of censure. But no state proposed to enforce the military clauses. Britain then took an independent step: in June 1935 it concluded the Anglo-German Naval Agreement, which allowed Germany to build a fleet up to thirty-five percent of the Royal Navy's tonnage, with a larger share of submarine tonnage and a right to claim parity in certain circumstances. British officials regarded this as a realistic limitation on a program that Germany would pursue regardless. France and Italy, who had not been consulted, saw it as British acceptance of German rearmament and a blow to common action. The agreement illustrates the way British policy often preferred bilateral bargains to collective measures.

The reoccupation of the Rhineland came on March 7, 1936. The Treaty of Versailles had forbidden German military forces west of the Rhine and in a fifty-kilometer zone to its east, and Germany had reaffirmed the arrangement at Locarno. Hitler, who used the French ratification of the Franco-Soviet pact in February 1936 as justification, ordered German troops into the zone while declaring Germany's willingness to negotiate a new security system. The force that crossed the Rhine bridges was small, only a few battalions, though troops and police were already present in the region. Hitler later said that he would have withdrawn had France resisted, and some contemporaries and historians accept that German commanders were nervous; but the evidence is incomplete, and it would be a mistake to assume that German withdrawal or defeat would have followed automatically.

The French government of Albert Sarraut denounced the act, as did the League Council, which declared Germany in breach of Versailles and Locarno. But France did not mobilize. The army command, under General Maurice Gamelin, advised that effective action would require general mobilization, because France lacked a rapidly deployable force and was committed to a defensive doctrine. A national election was due within weeks, the economy was fragile, and politicians feared the risks of unilateral action without British support. Britain, whose government under Stanley Baldwin and Foreign Secretary Anthony Eden condemned the violation, declined to support military action. Many British commentators argued that Germany was merely entering its own territory. Legally, however, Article 1 of the Locarno Rhine Pact guaranteed observance of the demilitarization provisions in Versailles Articles 42 and 43 as well as the western frontiers. Article 4 established League procedures and conditions for assistance after a breach, including urgent action in cases of unprovoked aggression. British ministers did not judge the March 1936 breach to require military intervention. The result was diplomatic protest rather than coercion.

The significance of the Rhineland crisis was military, diplomatic, and psychological. Militarily, it allowed Germany to fortify its western frontier, begin planning western fortifications, and eventually reduce France's capacity for offensive action in support of Poland or Czechoslovakia, since a French advance across the Rhine would meet prepared defenses. Diplomatically, it showed small states in eastern Europe and the Balkans that French and British guarantees might not be reliable, and Belgium announced a policy of independence in October 1936, distancing itself from French military alignment; the associated diplomatic adjustments continued in 1937 rather than constituting an immediate legal return to its pre-1914 neutral status. Psychologically, it encouraged Hitler's confidence that the western powers would not resist, and it increased the stature of the regime within Germany at a moment when some military leaders had been cautious. Whether early firmness would have led to the collapse of the regime or merely to war on less favorable terms remains a counterfactual question that historians should treat with caution.

Section 116.4

From Collective Security to Competing Strategic Alignments

By the middle of 1936 the League system had failed in two consecutive tests, and governments began to look for other strategies. This shift was gradual and did not produce two fixed blocs. It is better described as a series of overlapping and sometimes contradictory attempts by governments to ensure their own security under conditions of uncertainty. For some states that meant seeking protection through bilateral alliances; for others it meant accommodation with the stronger power; for others it meant neutrality; and for the regimes in Rome, Berlin, and Tokyo it meant closer cooperation based on shared hostility to the existing order and, in part, to communism.

French policy illustrates the dilemma. France's strategy had been based on the Versailles settlement and alliances with Poland and Czechoslovakia, but it was constrained by demographic weakness, a defensive military doctrine, and a divided politics. After Hitler's arrival, French foreign minister Louis Barthou sought a closer connection with the Soviet Union and Italy, and this effort produced the Franco-Soviet Treaty of Mutual Assistance, signed on May 2, 1935. Its terms were guarded: mutual assistance was linked to League procedures and to the Locarno guarantors, and no military convention followed. The French government delayed ratification until early 1936, partly because conservative politicians distrusted the Soviet Union. The treaty was followed by a Czechoslovak–Soviet pact in May 1935 that tied Soviet aid to Czechoslovakia to prior French action, a condition that would matter in 1938.

The Soviet Union had changed course. After joining the League in 1934, Moscow, under Foreign Commissar Maxim Litvinov, proposed collective security and the indivisibility of peace. The Seventh Congress of the Communist International in 1935 endorsed the Popular Front tactic, encouraging communists to cooperate with socialists and liberal parties against fascism. Historians disagree about how far these changes represented a lasting reorientation or a tactical response to German power; the evidence, including Soviet documents, suggests that Stalin kept open the possibility of an arrangement with Germany while pursuing security with the West. Western suspicion of Soviet intentions was reinforced by the purges, which in 1937 and 1938 destroyed much of the Red Army's senior leadership.

Meanwhile, relations between Italy and Germany improved. Mussolini had opposed German absorption of Austria in 1934, when he moved troops to the Brenner after the Nazi murder of Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss. After Ethiopia, Italy was isolated from Britain and France by sanctions and mutual hostility, and Germany had refused to join them. In the summer of 1936, both governments intervened in Spain. On October 25, 1936, the Italian foreign minister Galeazzo Ciano and Hitler reached an understanding on common policies, and on November 1 Mussolini spoke of an axis around which other European states could revolve. A month later, Germany and Japan signed the Anti-Comintern Pact, a publicly anticommunist agreement whose secret protocol promised consultation if either were attacked by the Soviet Union. Italy joined the Pact in November 1937.

It is vital to separate ideological sympathy from formal alliance. The Anti-Comintern Pact bound its signatories to share information and consult; it did not commit them to fight together. The Rome–Berlin Axis was an informal understanding; the formal military alliance, the Pact of Steel, came only in May 1939. The differences between Germany's and Italy's aims also remained substantial: Italy sought dominance in the Mediterranean and Africa, while Germany's goals centered on central and eastern Europe. The relationship was characterized by mutual advantage, rivalry over Austria, and unequal power. Contemporaries and later writers sometimes treated these alignments as an already completed Axis camp, but in 1936 diplomatic positions were still fluid. Britain and France explored accommodation with Italy, Poland balanced between Berlin and Moscow, and Moscow kept diplomatic channels open to several capitals.

The new alignments came into focus in Spain, where an internal conflict broke out in July 1936. The positions that governments had taken over Ethiopia and the Rhineland influenced their choices: Germany and Italy saw Spain as an opportunity to weaken France, gain a friendly regime, and test their forces, while France and Britain, wary of widening the conflict, sought containment, and the Soviet Union aimed to support a government under attack from fascist powers, within limits set by its own priorities. To understand how Spain became an international conflict, however, one must first understand its domestic origins, which Division II examines.

Cause and Effect

The Weakening of Collective Security and the Changing Diplomatic Order

Long-term causes

A League Covenant that depended on great-power cooperation and had no enforcement mechanism. The absence of the United States. Disagreement between France, which wanted enforcement of the treaties, and Britain, which preferred moderate revision. Wartime losses and public aversion to renewed conflict. The economic damage of the Depression and the rise of authoritarian regimes.

Short-term causes

Japan's conquest of Manchuria. Germany's withdrawal from the League and open rearmament. The Ethiopian invasion, and the choice of limited sanctions. The Rhineland move in March 1936 and the absence of coordinated Anglo-French response. The competing priorities of Britain in the Mediterranean and imperial defense and of France in continental security.

Effects

The League lost credibility and sanctions were lifted. Belgium returned to neutrality. Italy moved away from Stresa and toward Germany. France and the Soviet Union tested a limited alliance. Smaller states sought accommodation with Germany or neutrality. The Spanish conflict became a point of contest among the competing diplomatic strategies.

Historical complexity

The weakening of collective security was not the result of a single decision. Italian aggression, German revision, British caution, French weakness, and Soviet distrust each contributed. The same crisis taught different lessons to different governments, and none of the changes made a general war certain.

The Spanish Civil War and Europe's Ideological Conflict

Section 116.5

The Spanish Republic, Political Polarization, and the Military Uprising of 1936

The Spanish Civil War grew from Spanish conditions before it became a European one. Spain in 1931 was a largely agrarian society with deep regional differences, a very unequal distribution of land in the south, a powerful Catholic Church, an army that had intervened repeatedly in politics, and strong regional identities in Catalonia and the Basque Country. After municipal elections on April 12, 1931 returned republican majorities in the cities, King Alfonso XIII left the country and the Second Republic was proclaimed on April 14. The new regime was greeted with enthusiasm by many workers, intellectuals, and middle-class reformers, who hoped to modernize the state, and with suspicion or hostility by landowners, much of the clergy, and many army officers.

The Constituent Cortes drafted a democratic constitution in 1931 that guaranteed civil rights, granted women the vote, separated church and state, restricted religious orders in education, and permitted regional autonomy. Catalonia received a statute of autonomy in 1932. The reforming governments of Manuel Azaña attempted to reduce the size and political role of the officer corps and to begin agrarian reform in the south. These measures aroused strong opposition. Catholic conservatives believed that the state was attacking the church, landowners resisted redistribution, and some officers regarded the reforms as a threat to Spain's unity. General José Sanjurjo attempted a coup in August 1932 that failed. At the same time the left was divided: socialists, who participated in government, were often criticized by anarchists of the CNT, whose rejection of state politics produced insurrections, and by the small Communist Party. Landless laborers pressed for faster reform than governments delivered.

The elections of November 1933 brought a center-right majority, in which the Catholic confederation CEDA became the largest party, and the government reversed or suspended parts of the reform program. In October 1934, when CEDA ministers entered the cabinet, the socialists and others called a general strike. In Catalonia the regional government declared a Catalan state within a Spanish federal republic, and the rising was quickly suppressed. In Asturias, miners seized towns and held out for about two weeks before army forces, including Moroccan colonial troops and the Foreign Legion under the overall direction of General Francisco Franco, crushed the rising with great force. The repression, with thousands of arrests and many killed, left a legacy of bitterness. For the left, Asturias became a symbol of the violence of the right; for the right, it demonstrated the threat of revolution.

The Spanish political landscape by 1936 was therefore highly polarized but not binary. On the left were republican parties, the Socialist Party (itself split between moderates and followers of Francisco Largo Caballero), the Communist Party, the anarchist CNT and FAI, and the dissident Marxist POUM. On the right were monarchists, Carlists in Navarre, the CEDA, and the Falange of José Antonio Primo de Rivera, which was small before 1936 but grew rapidly afterward. Regional parties in Catalonia and the Basque Country pursued autonomy and did not fit either camp neatly. The Popular Front, formed by republican, socialist, and communist parties, with the support of the POUM and anarchist voters who abstained less than usual, won the elections of February 16, 1936 with a narrow popular margin but a larger parliamentary majority under Spain's electoral law.

The months after the election saw widespread political violence, with strikes, church burnings, clashes between Falangists and leftist militants, and assassinations. Historians debate how far the government was capable of controlling these events, but there is no doubt that they strengthened the case of officers who had begun conspiring against the Republic. General Emilio Mola organized the plot in the north, Sanjurjo was in exile in Portugal, and Franco, who had been removed from Madrid to the Canary Islands, joined the conspiracy only late. The murder of the monarchist politician José Calvo Sotelo on July 13, 1936, by police officers seeking revenge for the killing of a republican officer, provided a powerful pretext.

The rising began on July 17 in Spanish Morocco and spread to the mainland on July 18. It was not a single coherent coup. In some regions, including Navarre, Old Castile, Galicia, and parts of Andalusia such as Seville, the insurgents prevailed quickly. In Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, and the Basque industrial areas, loyal officers, police, and armed workers prevented success. Sanjurjo died in an air crash on July 20, and the rebels had no unified command. The country was therefore divided, and the conflict became a long war. The Republic was the elected government, but its authority dissolved temporarily as workers' committees and militias took over functions in many places; the insurgents, who soon called themselves Nationalists, were a coalition of army officers, Falangists, Carlists, monarchists, and conservative Catholics whose goals differed. Historians treat the war as the outcome of a breakdown of consensus, not a simple conflict between democracy and fascism, though the character of the Nationalist regime and its foreign support made the latter framing powerful at the time and afterward.

Section 116.6

The Spanish Civil War: Military Fronts, Political Coalitions, and the Struggle for Control

The first phase of the war turned on the Nationalists' attempt to join their northern and southern holdings and take Madrid. The most important insurgent force was the Army of Africa, composed of Spanish Legionaries and Moroccan regulars, stranded in Morocco by the Republican navy's control of the Strait of Gibraltar. In late July and August 1936, German and Italian transport aircraft airlifted these troops across the strait, an early demonstration of the importance of foreign assistance. Advancing north through Extremadura under Colonel Juan Yagüe, they took Badajoz in August and approached the capital. In November Nationalist forces reached the outskirts of Madrid, where the Republican government had moved to Valencia and General José Miaja organized the defense. With the help of militias, the first International Brigades, and Soviet aircraft and tanks, Madrid held.

Franco's rise to command was gradual. After the deaths of Sanjurjo and, in 1937, Mola, he emerged as the most prominent Nationalist general, and on September 29, 1936, the Nationalist junta named him generalísimo and head of government, a position he formalized in the following days. The unification decree of April 1937 merged the Falange and the Carlists into a single state party under his leadership, an act that suppressed internal dissent and created a political structure for the regime. Nationalist unity was therefore constructed during the war, not given at its start. Franco's preference for slow, thorough conquest, sometimes criticized for prolonging the war, also suited his aim of eliminating opponents throughout territory he occupied.

The war on the main fronts followed several phases. In February 1937 Nationalist and Italian forces took Málaga, and the Republic held on at the battles of Jarama, south-east of Madrid, in February, and at Guadalajara in March, where Republican forces and the International Brigades defeated the Italian expeditionary corps, a setback that Italian propaganda found hard to explain. Franco then shifted to the north, where the Basque Country, Santander, and Asturias fell between April and October 1937; Bilbao fell in June, and the Basque industries and mines passed to the Nationalists. Republican offensives at Brunete near Madrid in July 1937 and at Belchite in Aragon in August and September gained little. The battle of Teruel, from December 1937 to February 1938, ended with a Nationalist recapture, and in April 1938 Nationalist troops reached the Mediterranean at Vinaroz, cutting the Republic in two.

The final phase began with the Republic's Ebro offensive. On July 25, 1938, Republican forces under Juan Modesto and Enrique Líster crossed the Ebro river in a bold operation intended to relieve pressure on Valencia and to show the democracies that the Republic could still fight. The battle lasted until mid-November and was the longest and bloodiest of the war, ending in a Republican retreat with very heavy losses of men and matériel. Nationalist forces then attacked Catalonia in December, took Barcelona on January 26, 1939, and sent a flood of refugees toward the French border. The remaining Republican zone around Madrid, Valencia, and Alicante collapsed in March 1939 after a coup by Colonel Segismundo Casado against Prime Minister Juan Negrín, who favored continued resistance. Madrid surrendered on March 28. Franco announced the end of the war on April 1, 1939.

Republican Spain was not a unified camp. After July 1936, the central government lost control of large parts of the territory to militias and workers' committees, particularly in Catalonia and Aragon, where anarchists and the POUM carried out collectivizations of industry and land. Largo Caballero's government, formed in September 1936 with socialist, communist, republican, and later anarchist ministers, sought to rebuild a regular army and restore state authority. Communist influence grew because of Soviet arms and the party's organizational discipline, and because it argued that winning the war must precede revolution. Anarchists and the POUM held that the war and the revolution were inseparable. These tensions broke into open fighting in Barcelona on May 3 to 8, 1937, the May Days, when government forces and communist-aligned police fought anarchist and POUM militants. Several hundred people died. The crisis led to the fall of Largo Caballero and the formation of a government under Negrín, and in the following weeks the POUM was suppressed and its leader Andreu Nin was murdered by agents of the Soviet secret police. These events are known from government documents, the memoirs of participants such as George Orwell, and later archival research, which has qualified both Republican and anti-communist accounts.

Section 116.7

Foreign Intervention, Non-Intervention, and the International Brigades

In the first weeks of the war the Spanish Republic asked the French government of Léon Blum for aircraft and arms, and Blum initially agreed in principle. The decision aroused strong opposition from French conservatives and the press, and from Britain, whose government feared that involvement would spread the conflict and divide France domestically. On August 2, 1936, France proposed an international agreement against intervention, and over the following weeks twenty-seven European states, including Germany, Italy, the Soviet Union, and Portugal, joined. A Non-Intervention Committee met in London from September 1936 under British chairmanship and attempted to monitor the embargo. The policy was presented as a means of containing a dangerous conflict, and it reflected the hope that a European war could be avoided by isolating the Spanish one.

In practice the policy operated unequally. The Republic was an internationally recognized government that, in normal circumstances, would have had the right to purchase arms; the embargo removed that right while the insurgents received supplies from governments that formally accepted the agreement. Germany provided aircraft, anti-aircraft guns, tanks, and trainers, and from November 1936 the Condor Legion, a force of several thousand airmen and ground personnel, rotated through Spain. Italy sent aircraft and, from December 1936, a large volunteer force, the Corpo Truppe Volontarie, with tens of thousands of men at its peak, along with submarines that attacked shipping. Portugal under António de Oliveira Salazar allowed Nationalist use of its territory and supplies and sent volunteers. The Non-Intervention Committee noted violations but lacked means to stop them. The Republic, by contrast, received arms mostly by purchase on the black market and from Mexico and, after October 1936, from the Soviet Union.

The Soviet role was substantial but limited. The Soviet government sent aircraft, tanks, artillery, and small arms, along with several hundred advisers and pilots, and the Comintern organized volunteers. The Republic paid with a large part of its gold reserves, shipped to the Soviet Union in late 1936. Historians differ on Stalin's motives. Some stress a desire to defend a fellow antifascist government and to keep France and Britain engaged against Germany; others emphasize the aim of extending Soviet influence in the Republican government and avoiding a commitment that might provoke a wider war. Soviet aid was important in the defense of Madrid, but it declined after 1937 as supply routes became harder and as Soviet priorities shifted. The Soviet secret police also operated in Spain against opponents on the left, which added to Republican divisions.

The International Brigades were formed in the autumn of 1936 from volunteers recruited through communist parties and other networks, with a base at Albacete. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum, among other sources, documents that approximately 35,000 to 40,000 volunteers served, from more than fifty countries, including France, Poland, Germany and Austria (many of them exiles), Italy, Britain, the United States, and Yugoslavia. Their motives varied. Many were communists or socialists who saw the war as a struggle against fascism; others were trade unionists, intellectuals, Jewish refugees from central Europe, and adventurers, and some acted from solidarity with a besieged democracy. They fought at Madrid, Jarama, Guadalajara, Brunete, and on the Ebro, and suffered heavy casualties. The Negrín government announced their withdrawal in September 1938 in the hope of persuading the Nationalists to send away their foreign forces, but the Italians remained. Smaller numbers of foreign volunteers also served with the Nationalists, including Irish, French, and Romanian volunteers, and Portuguese troops, though fewer in number and less organized.

Multiple Perspectives

One War, Five Viewpoints on Intervention and Non-Intervention

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions of positions that historians have identified in the documentary record. They are not verbatim testimony, and none of them is a quotation from a named individual.

Republican authorities: Elected government officials argued that the Republic was the lawful government of Spain and had been attacked by army officers supported by foreign powers. They regarded non-intervention as a denial of their legal right to buy arms and appealed to Britain and France, and to the League of Nations, with little success. Their dependence on Soviet aid brought material help but also increased the influence of the Communist Party and deepened divisions with anarchists and socialists.
Nationalists: Nationalist leaders presented the rising as a defense of religion, order, and national unity against revolution and disorder, and they stressed the violence against the Church and property in Republican territory. They welcomed German and Italian assistance as essential, and they sought to portray foreign volunteers on the Republican side as an alien communist invasion, while treating their own foreign aid as help from friendly governments.
Foreign volunteers: Volunteers from many countries often described their service as an act of solidarity with a democratic government and as a way of resisting fascism when their own governments would not. Many were communists following party direction; others were not. They faced danger and often prosecution at home, and some, such as exiles from Germany, Italy, and Austria, had no home to return to. Their experience included heroism, heavy casualties, and disillusionment with communist control.
Non-intervention governments (Britain and France): British ministers believed that the Spanish war could ignite a European war and feared that Britain was not prepared to fight Germany and Italy. French ministers, especially in Blum's government, sympathized with the Republic but feared civil conflict at home and the loss of British support. Both governments saw non-intervention as realistic, though critics at the time argued that it was one-sided in effect.
Soviet policymakers: Soviet leaders presented their aid as proletarian internationalism and antifascist solidarity, while using it to influence the Republican government and to demonstrate Soviet usefulness to potential Western partners. They were also constrained by their wish to avoid a direct confrontation with Germany and by the cost and difficulty of supplying a distant front.

Caution: These groups did not possess equal power, equal legal standing, or equal responsibility. German and Italian forces, for example, intervened with state-organized military units while formally professing non-intervention.

Section 116.8

Civilian Violence, Guernica, Refugees, and the Defeat of the Republic

The Spanish Civil War produced mass violence behind the lines on both sides, although the organization, duration, and scale differed. In Republican territory, the collapse of state authority in July and August 1936 was followed by a wave of killings by militias and committees, directed against priests, landowners, officers, and suspected sympathizers with the rising, together with the burning or closure of many churches. Thousands of clergy, including bishops, priests, monks, and nuns, were killed. The Republican government gradually reasserted control and curbed the worst of the violence, but not before large numbers had been murdered, and the killing damaged the Republic's image abroad. In Nationalist territory, the killings were carried out by the army, Falangists, and civil guards as a deliberate instrument of policy to eliminate political opponents, teachers, trade unionists, and officials associated with the Republic, and it continued through the war and long afterward, with prison camps, summary trials, and forced labor. The overall numbers remain disputed, but most historians conclude that Nationalist killings exceeded those in Republican territory, and that they were systematic and sustained by the authorities, while Republican killings were most intense in the first months. They should not be treated as automatically equivalent in organization or scale.

The bombing of Guernica on April 26, 1937, occurred during the Nationalist offensive against the Basque country. German Condor Legion aircraft, with Italian participation, attacked the town, a market and symbolic center of Basque identity located behind the front lines, over several hours on a market day. Reports by journalists, including the correspondent of The Times, brought the event to world attention within days, and the Nationalist authorities initially denied responsibility and blamed Republican forces. Casualty estimates differ widely: the Basque government soon reported 1,654 dead, while later archival work has produced much lower figures, ranging from the low hundreds upward; historians therefore caution against precise claims. Guernica was not the first aerial attack on civilians, as earlier colonial wars and the First World War had shown, but it became an emblem of the vulnerability of cities, strengthened fears in Britain and France, and inspired Pablo Picasso's painting for the Spanish pavilion at the 1937 Paris Exposition. It also illustrates how air attack on urban targets would shape later warfare, discussed in Chapters 117 and 122.

The war's end brought a mass exodus. After the fall of Barcelona on January 26, 1939, hundreds of thousands of civilians, wounded soldiers, and fighters moved toward the French frontier. France, which had closed the border for much of the war, opened it at the end of January for civilians and in early February for soldiers. Roughly half a million people crossed within weeks, among them women, children, and elderly people. French authorities, overwhelmed, interned many on beaches at Argelès-sur-Mer, Saint-Cyprien, and Le Barcarès, where shelter, sanitation, and food were initially inadequate and disease spread. Soldiers were disarmed and held in camps under guard. In the following months many refugees returned to Spain, others were recruited as laborers in France, and thousands emigrated to Mexico and elsewhere. Some who remained in France later served in the French army or in the resistance during the Second World War, and some were deported to German camps. The Retirada was therefore a major European displacement in its own right, preceding the larger wartime movements examined in Chapter 123.

The end of the war came in stages. Britain and France recognized Franco's government on February 27, 1939. In Madrid, the Casado coup of March 1939 sought to negotiate a settlement but failed; the Nationalists accepted only unconditional surrender, and the capital fell on March 28. On April 1, Franco announced that the war was over. Victory did not bring reconciliation. The regime imprisoned or executed many Republicans, imposed censorship and the dominance of the Catholic Church in education, suppressed regional languages and autonomy, and punished former Republicans through purges and labor battalions. Tens of thousands of Republicans remained in prison or exile. The Spanish war should therefore be treated as a major European conflict with its own causes and consequences, and not simply as a rehearsal for the Second World War: its dynamics were shaped by Spanish society, though foreign involvement affected the outcome and the European order.

Everyday Life and Social History

Spanish Civilians, Refugees, and Families during the Civil War

The war reached ordinary Spaniards in many forms. In cities such as Madrid and Barcelona, residents endured bombardment, food shortages, queues, and rationing, and the requisitioning of buildings for hospitals, schools, and offices. Rural communities saw their young men taken for military service, land seized or collectivized, and livestock requisitioned. Families were separated by the front line, sometimes with brothers or fathers in opposing armies. Women took on new roles in Republican territory as factory workers, nurses, and members of political organizations and, for a time, as militia fighters, though by 1937 most were withdrawn from combat. In Nationalist territory, the regime emphasized traditional roles, and women's organizations such as the Falange's Sección Femenina were mobilized for relief and propaganda.

Children were evacuated in significant numbers, some to Soviet Union, Mexico, France, Belgium, and Britain, where about four thousand Basque children arrived in May 1937 on the steamer Habana. Many of them did not see their families again for years; some never returned. Relief organizations, including the Red Cross, Quaker groups, and trade unions abroad, organized food and medical aid, and both sides used photographs and posters to appeal for support. Historians use a wide range of evidence to reconstruct this social experience: posters, letters, oral history collected decades later, photographs, hospital records, and refugee registers. Each has biases. Propaganda posters show what organizers wished audiences to feel, oral histories reflect later memory and political change in Spain, and archives of the victorious side were shaped by a regime that long controlled the narrative.

Division III · Sections 116.9 to 116.12 · 1935 to 1938

Appeasement, Rearmament, and the Changing Balance of Power

Section 116.9

The Policy of Appeasement: Origins, Objectives, and Strategic Logic

The word appeasement has become a term of reproach, but in the 1930s it described a respectable and widely supported approach to foreign policy. It meant resolving international disputes by identifying legitimate grievances and meeting them through negotiation, so as to remove causes of conflict and avoid war. Its roots lay in a belief held by many British officials and politicians that the Treaty of Versailles had been in some respects too harsh, that Germany's claims to equality in arms and to some frontier adjustments had merit, and that a satisfied Germany would be a stable neighbor. Appeasement was thus a policy of negotiated concessions intended to prevent war, not simply surrender, and it was not the same as ideological sympathy with Nazism, although a small number of public figures did sympathize with Germany.

The experience of the First World War shaped British and French thinking. Britain had lost nearly 900,000 dead, and France had lost about 1.4 million, with large areas of the north devastated. The memory of the trenches made leaders and electors reluctant to risk another continental war, and the fear of aerial bombardment, widely believed to be capable of destroying cities within days, intensified it. Public opinion was not uniformly pacifist: the Peace Ballot of 1934 and 1935 showed support for collective action, and many people recognized the threat that Germany posed. But there was strong opposition to commitments that might lead to war over distant causes, and the evidence suggests that governments believed they had to bring their publics along carefully.

Economic and imperial considerations reinforced these attitudes. The Depression had left Britain with high unemployment and a Treasury that regarded financial stability as the foundation of national power; successive Chancellors resisted heavy arms spending, fearing inflation and damage to exports. The Royal Navy and the army were required to defend a vast empire, including India, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia, and the Chiefs of Staff repeatedly warned that Britain could not fight Germany, Italy, and Japan at the same time. Dominion governments, such as Canada and South Africa, were reluctant to be drawn into a European war. These calculations led British planners to seek to reduce the number of potential enemies by diplomacy, and in particular to detach Italy and Japan from Germany if possible.

Stanley Baldwin, who led the British government until May 1937, was cautious, preferring to avoid foreign commitments and to build up defenses gradually, in line with his reading of public opinion. His successor Neville Chamberlain, who had been a long-serving Chancellor of the Exchequer, was more activist. Chamberlain believed that European problems could be settled by direct negotiation among leaders, and he distrusted both the League and the Foreign Office's caution. He wished to deal with Hitler and Mussolini personally, to explore what they wanted, and to address what he saw as legitimate grievances, while rearming as a safeguard. French leaders, including Édouard Daladier, who became Prime Minister in April 1938, took a more pessimistic view of German intentions but depended on Britain and feared that France alone could not defeat Germany. French politics were also fractured: the Popular Front government of Léon Blum from June 1936 faced economic troubles, labor unrest, and a rapid succession of ministries thereafter.

To judge appeasement fairly, historians distinguish what policymakers knew at the time from what later generations know. In 1937 and 1938, British and French leaders did not know that Hitler would occupy Prague, nor that the war would involve the Holocaust. They had imperfect intelligence about German military strength, sometimes overestimating the Luftwaffe's capacity and sometimes underestimating German economic strain. Some believed that Hitler's aims could be limited to uniting German speakers, an assumption that the documentary record, including the later Hossbach memorandum, complicates. At the same time, historians also note that critics at the time, such as Churchill, parts of the Foreign Office, and some French officials, warned that concessions would encourage further demands, so that policymakers had alternatives available even if they did not command majority support. The question is therefore not whether appeasement failed, which is clear, but why governments chose it, what alternatives were realistic, and how far it was shaped by miscalculation as distinct from constraint.

Historical Vocabulary
Appeasement, Non-Intervention, Rearmament, and Strategic Concession

Appeasement originally meant to pacify or to settle. In 1930s Britain it described a method of diplomacy by negotiated concessions, associated particularly with Neville Chamberlain, and used by supporters as a positive description. After 1939 it became an insult, and historians now try to recover the original meaning without defending or condemning the policy by hindsight alone.

Non-intervention was the 1936 policy of refusing arms and forces to both sides in Spain. It appeared neutral but operated unequally because some signatories violated it. Rearmament meant expanding military forces and production. In Britain it was constrained by financial limits and public debate over priorities, with air defense and the navy given priority over a large continental army. Strategic concession is an analytical term for the deliberate surrender of a position or claim to gain time, avoid a larger conflict, or improve alliances. Historians debate whether British and French concessions bought useful time or gave Germany more advantages than the democracies gained.

Section 116.10

Britain and France: Rearmament, Public Opinion, and the Critics of Appeasement

British rearmament began in earnest after 1934, when the government recognized the German threat and abandoned the assumption that no major war would occur within ten years. Priority went to the Royal Air Force, which expanded its planned strength under a series of schemes, and to the development of modern fighters, the Hurricane and the Spitfire, and a chain of coastal radar stations. The Royal Navy maintained global commitments, and the army, assigned a limited role, received less funding for a continental expeditionary force until early 1939. The Treasury's insistence on limits, and Chamberlain's own belief that economic strength was a fourth arm of defense, restricted the pace. Industrial capacity, skilled labor shortages, and bargaining with trade unions also slowed production. By 1938 British air production was growing rapidly, but the country remained vulnerable to bombing and did not have an army capable of intervening on the continent.

French military doctrine and planning were defensive. The Maginot Line, built in the 1930s along the German frontier but not extended along the Belgian border, reflected a belief that fortifications and a large army of conscripts could defend France at manageable cost. The French army was large but slow to mobilize, and its leaders doubted that France could attack Germany without British help. Demographic weakness, with smaller age cohorts reaching military age in the mid-1930s, increased fears of another prolonged war. French industry, affected by the Depression and by labor disputes, produced aircraft and tanks slowly, though rearmament accelerated from 1936 and especially after 1938. Alliances with Poland and Czechoslovakia existed on paper, but the French general staff did not plan an offensive to relieve them. The challenge of defending eastern European states from the west, across territory that Germany could now defend with fortifications, was a central fact of French strategy.

Critics of appeasement came from several quarters. Winston Churchill, a Conservative backbencher excluded from office, argued from 1933 that Britain was neglecting its air defenses and underestimating Germany, and he later opposed concessions over Czechoslovakia. Anthony Eden resigned as Foreign Secretary in February 1938, ostensibly over Chamberlain's overtures to Mussolini, though the disagreement also concerned the style of policy. Within the Foreign Office, senior officials including Robert Vansittart warned about German ambitions. The Labour Party supported the League and opposed Chamberlain's foreign policy but had also voted against many arms estimates until 1937, and it was divided between pacifists and those who wanted to resist fascism. Liberals, Communists, and some trade unionists campaigned for collective security and for aid to Spain. In France, communists, many socialists, and some conservatives such as Paul Reynaud criticized concessions, while others, including parts of the right and some pacifist socialists, argued that France should avoid war over eastern Europe.

Public opinion offers a more complicated picture than later memory suggests. Evidence from by-elections, the Peace Ballot, press commentary, and early opinion polls shows that many people feared war and welcomed Chamberlain's efforts, but also that support for firmness grew as German demands expanded. The memory of the First World War, which had produced both pacifism and, among others, the belief that defense of the country required readiness, affected everyone. Governments assessed the costs of intervening immediately and the risks of waiting. Ministers could not know when or whether Germany's strength would exceed that of the democracies, and the picture changed with every estimate. Some believed time favored Germany, because its rearmament was ahead; others, including Chamberlain's advisers, believed time favored Britain, because the radar chain and fighter production were improving.

The argument that appeasement was justified because it bought time for rearmament requires careful handling. It had real force in some areas: by September 1938 Britain's air defense was still weak, whereas by September 1939 it was considerably stronger, and radar had been deployed. But Germany also used the interval to rearm, to absorb Austria and the Czech lands with their industries and weapons, and to improve its strategic position; Czechoslovakia's army and fortifications were lost to the potential coalition. Historians therefore disagree on whether the balance shifted in favor of the democracies. In addition, the time-buying argument, though invoked by some officials in 1938, was more prominent in later justifications, so contemporary reasoning must be distinguished from retrospective defense. What the evidence suggests is that strategic judgment, domestic constraints, and hopes for lasting settlement all played roles that varied among ministers and across time.

Section 116.11

The Rome–Berlin Axis, Anti-Comintern Diplomacy, and the Changing Alliance System

The Rome–Berlin Axis was a product of circumstance and calculation rather than a long-planned alliance. After Italy's invasion of Ethiopia, sanctions and British hostility pushed Mussolini toward Germany, whose government had refused to join sanctions and offered recognition of the conquest. During a visit by Foreign Minister Ciano to Berlin in October 1936, the two governments reached a series of understandings on Spain, the Danube basin, and other matters, and on November 1 Mussolini publicly described the relationship as an axis. This was a diplomatic alignment, not a treaty of alliance. The two governments committed themselves to no common military action, and each regarded the other as both a partner and a rival. Italy sought to preserve a balance between Germany and the western powers, and in 1937 and early 1938 Mussolini remained interested in better relations with Britain, leading to an Anglo-Italian agreement in April 1938.

The Anti-Comintern Pact of November 25, 1936 between Germany and Japan, which Italy joined a year later, was likewise limited. Its public text pledged cooperation in countering the Communist International; a secret protocol promised that neither party would assist the Soviet Union if it attacked the other, and that they would consult on measures. It did not bind them to fight together. For Germany, the agreement helped to link its ideological hostility to communism with a potential strategic partner in the Far East, though Hitler also kept relations with China for a time. For Japan, it provided diplomatic support against the Soviet Union during its war in China. Historians therefore describe the Pact as an ideological and diplomatic statement more than a military arrangement, and they note that it reassured neither partner of the other's real commitments.

The shift in Italy's attitude had a direct consequence for Austria. In 1934, Mussolini had been Austria's principal protector, sending troops to the Brenner Pass when Austrian Nazis murdered Chancellor Dollfuss. By 1936 and 1937, however, Italy was increasingly drawn toward Germany through cooperation in Spain, the aftermath of League sanctions lifted in July 1936, strained relations with France and Britain, and growing reliance on German coal and diplomatic support. In 1937, Mussolini visited Germany and was impressed by German power. Italy therefore no longer served as a counterweight to German pressure on Vienna, and Austria lost its most important external guarantor. Hitler recognized this and in a meeting on November 5, 1937, recorded in the Hossbach memorandum, outlined to military and foreign policy leaders a range of possible scenarios for expansion in central Europe, including action against Austria and Czechoslovakia. Historians debate its significance, since it is a record by an adjutant, but it is generally accepted as evidence of Hitler's expansionist thinking, though not of a fixed timetable.

It is important not to project the camps of 1939 or 1941 backward. In 1936 and 1937 Britain still hoped to detach Italy from Germany, France was divided on how to handle Mussolini, the Soviet Union remained a possible partner of the West, and Japan's future orientation was uncertain. Even Germany did not regard Italy as a reliable military ally: German planners did not plan for Italian cooperation in war. The diplomatic alignment of this period was evolving and sometimes contradictory, and it did not become an alliance system until the Pact of Steel in 1939 and the later Tripartite Pact of 1940.

Section 116.12

The Anschluss: Germany's Annexation of Austria, March 1938

Austria, the German-speaking rump of the Habsburg Empire, emerged from 1918 as a small republic whose Social Democrats and many others favored union with Germany, a union forbidden by the peace treaties. The republic was politically divided, and in 1933 Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss suspended parliament and built an authoritarian regime based on the Christian Social Party and Catholic corporatism, which suppressed the Social Democrats in a brief civil war in February 1934. The Austrian Nazis, supported from Germany, attempted a coup in July 1934 in which Dollfuss was murdered. The coup failed, and Dollfuss's successor Kurt Schuschnigg continued the authoritarian regime and sought to preserve independence with Italian backing. Austrian opinion was divided, and a significant minority, including many Nazis and other pan-Germans, favored union with Germany, while others, including Catholics, monarchists, Jews, and socialists, opposed absorption by the Nazi state.

After the Italian shift, Schuschnigg's room for maneuver narrowed. In July 1936 he concluded an agreement with Germany in which Berlin recognized Austrian sovereignty while Austria promised to follow a foreign policy consistent with its identity as a German state and to admit some Nazi sympathizers into its government. On February 12, 1938, Hitler summoned Schuschnigg to Berchtesgaden and, with military threats, demanded further concessions, including the appointment of the Austrian Nazi Arthur Seyss-Inquart as Minister of the Interior and the release of imprisoned Nazis. Schuschnigg agreed but, returning to Vienna, announced on March 9 a plebiscite for March 13 on Austrian independence, with conditions favorable to a yes vote. Hitler, alarmed, demanded its cancellation on March 11, then Schuschnigg's resignation and the appointment of Seyss-Inquart, backing the demands with an ultimatum and with the threat of invasion, relayed in part through Hermann Göring.

Schuschnigg resigned on the evening of March 11, announcing that Austria yielded to force and ordering the army not to resist. Seyss-Inquart became Chancellor, and German troops crossed the frontier on March 12 to widespread crowds in many places and some acts of enthusiasm, though also arrests and fear. A law of March 13 proclaimed Austria a province of the German Reich. A plebiscite on April 10, held under Nazi control, with open voting in some places, exclusion of Jews and many political opponents, and intimidation, produced an officially reported approval of more than 99 percent. Historians generally agree that there was considerable real support for union among Austrians, but that the plebiscite itself was not a free expression of opinion and that the number was manufactured. The annexation also violated the peace treaties, the Austro-German agreement of 1936, and international law.

The international response was weak. Britain protested, but Chamberlain's government concluded that it could not prevent the annexation by force and that Austria lay outside areas of vital interest. France was in the middle of a government crisis. Italy, as Mussolini accepted the outcome, was thanked by Hitler. The Soviet Union proposed a conference, which the western powers declined. The annexation had immediate strategic effects. It gave Germany control of Austria's resources, army, and railways, and opened a southern frontier with Hungary and Yugoslavia, and it put German forces on three sides of Czechoslovakia. The effect on southeastern Europe, where Hungary, Romania, and Yugoslavia now faced German power directly, was to encourage accommodation with Berlin.

The Anschluss was accompanied by immediate persecution. Within days, the Gestapo and SA and local Nazis arrested thousands of political opponents, including monarchists, socialists, communists, and Catholic activists, and transported prominent prisoners, including many who had served in the Schuschnigg government, to Dachau. Jews in Vienna, one of Europe's largest Jewish communities, were subjected to public humiliation, robbery, and violence, and Nazi authorities applied the Nuremberg Laws and began the confiscation of property and enforced emigration under the Central Office for Jewish Emigration, set up in August 1938. These events, which accelerated the pace of Nazi persecution and expelled many thousands, are reserved for fuller treatment in Chapter 115 on the regime's racial policy and in Chapter 121 on the Holocaust, and they are noted here as a consequence of the annexation and as evidence that annexation meant subjugation for many Austrians.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Austria before and after the Anschluss

What Changed

  • Austria ceased to exist as a sovereign state and became a province of the German Reich, with its institutions, army, and currency absorbed.
  • Political parties and organizations other than the Nazi Party were banned, and political opponents were arrested or exiled.
  • Jewish residents of Vienna and other cities lost civil rights, property, and security within weeks.
  • Austria's economy was integrated into German rearmament, and its workers and resources were mobilized for German purposes.

What Stayed the Same

  • Authoritarian government continued, though in a more intense and totalitarian form, after the authoritarian Ständestaat of 1934 to 1938.
  • Many Austrians, including some who had supported union before 1938, remained divided about the regime, and local administrators, churches, and villages continued some routines.
  • Economic ties with the German lands, which were strong before 1938, persisted and deepened.

Who experienced change most directly: Jews, socialists, monarchists, Catholic activists, and officials of the previous regime experienced abrupt and violent change. Who experienced greater continuity: Some Austrians, including rural people and sympathizers of union, initially experienced the change as less disruptive, although later years of war and occupation altered this. Why the pattern matters: It shows that annexation combined real support among some Austrians with coercion, and that formal continuity in institutions masked fundamental changes in rights and security.

Division IV · Sections 116.13 to 116.16 · 1938 to March 1939

Czechoslovakia, Munich, and the Failure of Appeasement

Section 116.13

Czechoslovakia, the Sudeten Germans, and the Crisis of National Sovereignty

Czechoslovakia, proclaimed in October 1918 and recognized by the peace treaties, was a multinational democracy and the most industrialized state of east-central Europe. Its population of roughly fourteen to fifteen million included about seven million Czechs, over two million Slovaks, around three million German speakers, and substantial Hungarian, Ruthenian (Rusyn), Polish, and Jewish communities. The state was governed by a coalition of parties dominated by Czech politicians, with a constitution that guaranteed civil rights and minority language rights in districts where minorities were numerous. It had a strong army, built on the industrial resources of the Škoda works and other firms, and constructed a system of fortifications along its mountainous western and northern frontiers, partly modeled on French practice. Its security rested on its alliances with France and the Little Entente and on the 1935 treaty with the Soviet Union.

The Sudeten Germans lived mostly in the border districts of Bohemia and Moravia, regions that had belonged to the Austrian half of the Habsburg Empire and never to Germany, though they had been part of the Holy Roman Empire and German Confederation. The term Sudetenland, popularized after 1918, grouped diverse districts. The Sudeten Germans were not a single political bloc: before 1933, German Social Democrats, Agrarians, and Christian Socials participated in Czechoslovak governments. The Depression hit the export-oriented glass, textile, and machine industries of the border districts severely, producing unemployment that exceeded that in Czech regions, and grievances arose over civil service employment, language use, state contracts, and land reform. These were real, though historians disagree about how far discrimination was systematic and how far Prague's reforms in the later 1930s addressed them.

Konrad Henlein, a gymnastics teacher, founded the Sudetendeutsche Heimatfront (Sudeten German Homeland Front) on October 1, 1933. Renamed the Sudetendeutsche Partei (Sudeten German Party) in April 1935, the movement received about two thirds of the German vote in the May 1935 elections, becoming the largest single party in the country. Henlein presented his party as loyal to the state while demanding autonomy, but from 1935 and especially from 1937 it received funding and direction from Berlin. After the Anschluss, which put German forces on the borders of Bohemia on the south as well as the north and west, Henlein was instructed by Hitler on March 28, 1938 to make demands that Prague could not accept. In April 1938, at Karlsbad, the party issued a program demanding full autonomy and freedom to profess National Socialist ideology. Nazi propaganda exaggerated the plight of the Sudeten Germans and produced charges of persecution that were generally not supported by independent observers, though some of the grievances had a basis.

The Czechoslovak government, led by President Edvard Beneš, who had been a founder of the state, and by Prime Minister Milan Hodža, tried to address minority claims through a succession of proposals while maintaining the integrity of the state. They knew that granting autonomy to a party controlled by Berlin would risk the state's dissolution. On May 20 and 21, 1938, after reports of German troop movements, which were later shown to have been exaggerated or misinterpreted, the Czechoslovak government ordered a partial mobilization. Britain and France warned Berlin, and Hitler, angered, ordered the military to prepare for an attack on Czechoslovakia in the autumn, with the date of October 1 in a directive of May 30. The incident left the impression, which Hitler resented, that Germany had been forced to back down.

It is essential to treat Czechoslovakia as an independent state with its own government and strategic interests, and not as an object of great-power diplomacy. Its army of about thirty-five divisions, among the best equipped in Europe, along with fortifications and a strong armaments industry, made it a formidable opponent, and its leaders were prepared to fight if attacked. They hoped for help from France, the Soviet Union, and the Little Entente, though Romania and Yugoslavia were unlikely to take sides actively. Czechoslovak leaders were also aware that their position depended on France and, through France, on Britain, and in the summer of 1938 they were pressed by both to make concessions. The question of what they could accomplish alone, and what their allies would do, shaped the crisis that followed.

Section 116.14

The Sudeten Crisis of 1938: Escalation, Diplomacy, and Threats of War

British policy in the summer of 1938 aimed at persuading Prague to concede enough to satisfy German demands and at warning Hitler that Britain might not stand aside if Germany used force. In July, Chamberlain proposed a mediator, and the former cabinet minister Lord Runciman, a Liberal peer, led a mission to Czechoslovakia from August 3, to investigate and encourage negotiations between the government and the Sudeten Germans. The Czechoslovak government made successive offers, culminating in the Fourth Plan in early September, which went far toward granting autonomy. Henlein's party, acting on instructions from Berlin, broke off talks after incidents in mid-September. Runciman reported that the main grievances were justified in part and recommended that border regions with a German majority be transferred, though historians note his report reflected the views of those he met and the pressure of the circumstances.

The crisis moved to a new level when Hitler, speaking at the Nuremberg Party Rally on September 12, denounced Czechoslovakia and demanded self-determination for the Sudeten Germans. Riots followed in the border districts, martial law was declared, and Henlein fled to Germany. On September 15, Chamberlain, flying for the first time in his life, met Hitler at Berchtesgaden. Hitler demanded the transfer of districts with a German majority, and Chamberlain, returning to London, persuaded his cabinet and the French government to accept the principle of cession. On September 19, Britain and France presented Prague with a proposal to cede areas where Germans formed more than half the population, together with an international guarantee of the new frontier. The Czechoslovak government, under heavy pressure, and after being told that France would not fight, accepted on September 21.

When Chamberlain returned to meet Hitler at Bad Godesberg on September 22, he found the demands had increased. Hitler now insisted on German military occupation of the Sudetenland by October 1 and on satisfying Polish and Hungarian claims, and he wanted the transfer to happen without a plebiscite in the territory or an international commission. Chamberlain regarded the new terms as unacceptable in form and the Czechoslovak government rejected them. On September 23, Prague ordered general mobilization. In the following days, Britain mobilized its fleet, France called up reservists, and civil defense measures were taken in London, including the digging of trenches. On September 26, in a speech at the Berlin Sportpalast, Hitler said that this was his last territorial demand in Europe and gave Prague a deadline. Chamberlain on September 27, in a radio broadcast, said it was horrible, fantastic, and incredible that Britons should be digging trenches because of a quarrel in a far-away country between people of whom they knew nothing; the remark has been widely cited, as evidence either of his reluctance or of his failure to appreciate Czechoslovakia's position.

The decisions made in these days were complex. Hitler, determined to occupy at least the Sudetenland and probably to destroy the Czechoslovak state, was nonetheless confronted by signs of reluctance in Germany: the military leadership, including Chief of Staff Ludwig Beck, who resigned in August, doubted that Germany could win a general war, and the reception of troops in Berlin on September 27 was reportedly cool. Mussolini, informed by the British, intervened on September 28 and urged Hitler to accept a conference, and Hitler agreed to postpone mobilization and invited Chamberlain, Daladier, and Mussolini to Munich. Meanwhile, Beneš and his government faced choices with no good outcome: fighting alone, or perhaps with Soviet help if France acted, against a much larger army, or accepting terms imposed by powers they had expected to be allies. The Soviet Union stated its readiness to honor its treaty obligations if France did so, but the conditions of the pact, the refusal of Poland and Romania to allow Soviet troops to cross, and uncertainty about Soviet capacity and intentions meant that historians still debate how far Moscow's offers were practicable.

Section 116.15

The Munich Agreement, September 29 to 30, 1938

The conference opened at the Führerbau in Munich on September 29, 1938, with Hitler, Mussolini, Chamberlain, and Daladier present. The Czechoslovak government was not invited to participate, and its representatives were kept waiting in a hotel, receiving the outcome from Chamberlain's and Daladier's aides rather than taking part. The agreement was signed in the early hours of September 30. It was based in large part on a proposal that Mussolini presented, but which was in substance a German draft prepared in Berlin. The settlement thus decided the fate of a sovereign state in the absence of its government, and Czechoslovakia was informed that it should accept or face Germany alone.

In its main terms, the agreement provided that Germany would occupy the Sudetenland in stages, beginning October 1 and finishing by October 10, with the evacuation of the areas to be carried out under the supervision of an international commission, with representatives of the four powers and Czechoslovakia. The commission would decide on the remaining areas of predominantly German population, to be occupied by October 10, and possibly the holding of plebiscites in some disputed areas. Czechoslovakia was obliged to release Sudeten German prisoners and soldiers, and inhabitants were given a right to opt for one state or the other, with a time limit for exchanging populations. Britain and France promised an international guarantee of the new frontiers, which Germany and Italy would join once the Polish and Hungarian minority questions had been settled. An annex and subsequent declarations prepared the way for further negotiation.

The consequences for Czechoslovakia were immediate and severe. The transfer deprived it of about a third of the territory of the Czech lands, around three million German speakers but also several hundred thousand Czechs and others, and of the mountainous frontier on which its fortifications stood, so that the remainder of the state became militarily indefensible. Industries in the border districts, including coal mines, textile and glass manufacturers, and armaments plants, passed to Germany, and railway lines were cut, so that the remainder of the economy was disrupted. Many Czech and Jewish residents and anti-Nazi Germans fled or were expelled from the ceded districts. Beneš resigned on October 5 and left the country, and the new government under President Emil Hácha accepted that Czechoslovakia must cooperate with Germany.

Reactions varied sharply. Chamberlain, on returning to Britain on September 30, was greeted by large and relieved crowds and waved a paper signed with Hitler, the Anglo-German declaration promising consultation; he spoke of peace with honor and, outside 10 Downing Street, of peace for our time. Daladier, who is reported to have expected a hostile reception in Paris, was cheered and was privately dismayed. Churchill told the House of Commons on October 5 that Britain had sustained a total and unmitigated defeat, a judgment that many in the House rejected; the Commons approved the agreement by a large margin, with Labour opposing, and some Conservatives, including Eden and Duff Cooper, abstaining or resigning. In Czechoslovakia, officials and citizens expressed bitterness at what they saw as betrayal, though some also accepted the government's argument that war would have been unwinnable. Sudeten Germans in the border districts largely welcomed the arrival of German troops, and Czech and Jewish residents and social democrats experienced fear and flight. Hitler himself was privately dissatisfied that he had not obtained a war, according to some accounts, and aimed to destroy the rest of Czechoslovakia.

The Munich Agreement, September 29 to 30, 1938, and Czechoslovak Sovereignty

Creator: The governments of Germany, Italy, the United Kingdom, and France, represented by Adolf Hitler, Benito Mussolini, Neville Chamberlain, and Édouard Daladier · Date: signed in the early hours of September 30, 1938, dated September 29 · Place: Munich · Type: A multilateral diplomatic agreement with annexes, published in the original languages and in translation, including by the Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs

Context: The agreement ended the Sudeten crisis by providing for the transfer of the German-majority districts to Germany. It was negotiated by four heads of government in one day and a night, and the Czechoslovak government was not a party to it. The text is short, consisting of a set of numbered provisions and supplementary declarations and annexes.

Paraphrase of the principal provisions (not a verbatim translation) The signatories state that, having agreed on the terms and conditions of the cession of the Sudeten German territory already agreed in principle, they set out the procedures for it. Evacuation is to begin on October 1 and to be completed by October 10, without destruction of installations, and Czechoslovakia is held responsible for carrying it out without damage. Four stages of German occupation are specified by zone. An international commission of representatives of the four powers and Czechoslovakia is to determine the remaining territory and may recommend modifications, including plebiscites. A right of option into and out of the transferred territories is provided, with a time limit, and a German-Czechoslovak commission is to settle details of population transfers. Czechoslovakia is to release Sudeten German prisoners within four weeks. Annexed declarations address the guarantee of the new frontiers and the Polish and Hungarian claims.

Analysis: The text reveals how the agreement treated territorial transfer as a technical question, with timetables and zones, and how the stipulations imposed obligations on Czechoslovakia, a state that had no voice in drafting them. It shows the use of the language of orderly procedure and international supervision, which gave the transfer a legal appearance, though the timetable gave Czechoslovakia very little time. The text does not tell us how people in the transferred districts felt, what the Czechoslovak government might have done differently, or what the signatories privately expected. It also does not explain why the German side was able to dictate the pace. Historians must therefore read it alongside diplomatic records, memoirs, and the reports of the international commission, and note the perspective of the four governments that wrote it. The perspectives of Czechs, Slovaks, Sudeten German democrats, and Jews are missing from the document.

Section 116.16

From Munich to the Destruction of Czechoslovakia, 1938 to 1939

Munich did not settle the competing claims on Czechoslovak territory. Poland, which had long-standing disputes with Prague over the Těšín (Teschen, Cieszyn) district, delivered an ultimatum on the night of September 30 and occupied the district on October 2, a decision that was welcomed in Poland and condemned in Czechoslovakia and by many observers as opportunistic. Hungary claimed the Hungarian-majority districts of southern Slovakia and Carpathian Ruthenia. Direct negotiations failed, and on November 2, 1938, Germany and Italy, as arbiters, awarded Hungary southern Slovakia and part of Carpathian Ruthenia, including the towns of Košice and Uzhhorod, in the First Vienna Award, a transfer of over ten thousand square kilometers and roughly a million people. Britain and France, which had promised a guarantee, did not provide one, and Germany declined to give its guarantee while Poland and Hungary claims remained.

The rump state, known after November as the Second Czecho-Slovak Republic, was a federation of autonomous Czech lands, Slovakia, and Subcarpathian Rus. Slovakia gained autonomy in October 1938 under the Slovak People's Party of Father Jozef Tiso, which was authoritarian and nationalist, and the central government in Prague under Hácha and his ministers operated under constant German pressure, with the army reduced, press censorship, and the suppression of left-wing and Jewish activity. The November pogrom in Germany of November 9 and 10, 1938, which damaged synagogues and Jewish property across the Reich, intensified Jewish flight from the region, and refugees from the Sudetenland encountered difficulties in finding safe havens. Economic disruption and the loss of industry left the state dependent on German trade.

In March 1939 Hitler moved to complete the destruction of the state. German agents encouraged Slovak separatists, and on March 9 Prague dismissed Tiso's government in Slovakia, hoping to preserve unity. Tiso was summoned to Berlin on March 13, where Hitler urged him to proclaim independence, warning that otherwise Slovakia might be left to Hungary. On March 14 the Slovak Diet declared independence under German protection. The same day, Hácha and Foreign Minister František Chvalkovský were summoned to Berlin and, in a meeting in the early hours of March 15, were told by Hitler and Göring that German troops would enter Bohemia and Moravia at dawn and that resistance would be crushed, including by air raids on Prague. Hácha, reportedly fainting and revived by Hitler's doctor, signed a statement placing the fate of the Czech people in Hitler's hands. German troops entered on March 15 and Hitler arrived in Prague that evening. On March 16 he proclaimed the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, with Konstantin von Neurath as Reich Protector and a Czech government subordinate to him; Slovakia became a nominally independent client state, and Hungary occupied Carpathian Ruthenia between March 14 and 16, despite brief resistance by Ruthenian forces.

The occupation was a turning point for the interpretation of German aims. Nazi propaganda and many in Britain and France had accepted that German claims in 1938 were based on the principle of self-determination, which supported union of German speakers in the Sudetenland and Austria. The seizure of Prague and the Czech lands, whose population was predominantly Czech, contradicted that justification, and it also violated the Munich assurances. German forces took control of the Czechoslovak army's equipment, including tanks and arms factories, adding to German military strength. Hitler's own words and documents, such as his speeches and the later Hossbach record, suggested that he had always envisaged a larger program. In its immediate effects, the occupation demonstrated to many leaders in London and Paris that further concessions were unlikely to bring a lasting settlement.

British reaction developed over several days. On March 15 Chamberlain told the Commons that Britain's guarantee did not apply since the state had dissolved from within, and he expressed regret, while avoiding strong condemnation. On March 17, in a speech at Birmingham, after pressure from his cabinet, press, and public opinion, he said that the occupation raised the question whether Germany sought to dominate the world by force, and that Britain would resist such an attempt. Within two weeks, Britain and France began to prepare commitments to eastern Europe, accelerated rearmament, and, in April, introduced conscription in Britain. The shift was not immediate or total: some in the government still hoped for a settlement, and the change in policy reflected both the shock of Prague and a recalculation of the German threat. On March 22 and 23, Germany pressed Lithuania to return the Memel territory, which it did, and on April 7 Italy invaded Albania, further heightening alarm. Parliamentary statements from these weeks provide an important documentary record of the transformation, and they point toward the Polish crisis examined in Division V.

Poland and the Collapse of European Peace

Section 116.17

Poland, Danzig, the Polish Corridor, and the Crisis of 1939

The Polish question had been embedded in the peace settlement since 1919. The restored Polish state received from Germany the province of Posen and much of West Prussia, giving it a strip of territory, the Polish Corridor, that reached the Baltic and separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany. The nearby port of Danzig, whose population was overwhelmingly German-speaking, was made a Free City under League supervision, with a High Commissioner appointed by the League, with Polish rights over customs and foreign representation, and with Poland's access to the sea secured through the neighboring Polish-built port of Gdynia. Almost all German governments regarded the Corridor and Danzig as injustices, and Weimar Germany never accepted the eastern frontiers as final. Poland, for its part, considered the access to the sea essential to its economic and political independence, and it had a substantial German minority in the Corridor and in Upper Silesia, as well as large Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Jewish communities.

Polish foreign policy under Józef Beck, who served as foreign minister from 1932 under the dominant influence of Marshal Józef Piłsudski until 1935 and thereafter in the circle of the successor regime, was based on an attempt to balance between Germany and the Soviet Union. Poland signed a non-aggression declaration with Germany in January 1934 and renewed its non-aggression treaty with the Soviet Union, and it sought to avoid dependence on either neighbor while maintaining its alliance with France. Beck believed that Poland's independence depended on avoiding subordination, and he was skeptical of collective security and of Soviet intentions. The policy brought Poland short-term gains: in October 1938 Poland occupied the Těšín district, an action that damaged its reputation in the west and earned Berlin's tacit approval. It also reduced cooperation with Czechoslovakia, a decision whose consequences became apparent when Germany turned against Poland.

After Munich, the German government approached Poland with a package of proposals. On October 24, 1938, Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop proposed to the Polish ambassador, Józef Lipski, the return of Danzig to Germany and an extraterritorial road and railway across the Corridor, in exchange for a guarantee of the Polish frontier and the extension of the non-aggression pact. Poland offered alternatives, such as a joint guarantee of Danzig's status and facilities for transit, but refused to cede the city or to grant extraterritorial rights, fearing that concessions would lead to the loss of its independence, as had occurred in Czechoslovakia. German-Polish relations deteriorated through the winter. The local Nazi regime in Danzig, led by the Gauleiter Albert Forster, meanwhile increased pressure on the Polish minority and on the city's remaining institutions.

The German occupation of Prague changed the situation. Germany had now surrounded Poland to the south, west, and north, since the Slovak state was a German client, and Hitler's demands to Poland on March 21, 1939, were renewed with greater force. British policy in the same period shifted from reluctance to commitment. Receiving reports, some inaccurate, of an imminent German attack on Poland, the British government announced on March 31, 1939, in a statement by Chamberlain to the Commons, that in the event of any action that clearly threatened Polish independence, and which the Polish government considered it vital to resist with its national forces, Britain would lend Poland all support in its power; France associated itself with the statement. Beck accepted a reciprocal arrangement in London in early April, and the Anglo-Polish Mutual Assistance Agreement was signed formally on August 25. Britain and France also extended guarantees to Romania and Greece on April 13, after the Italian invasion of Albania.

The guarantee was a major break with the policy of avoiding commitments in eastern Europe, but it also had limits. Britain lacked the ability to defend Poland directly, and the British and French general staffs expected that Poland could hold out for some months and that the western powers would win a long war by blockade and attrition. Poland, for its part, remained unwilling to accept Soviet troops on its territory. On April 3, Hitler issued a directive for Case White, the preparation of an attack on Poland, with a possible date after September 1, and on April 28 he denounced the German-Polish non-aggression declaration and the Anglo-German Naval Agreement. Beck's speech in the Polish parliament on May 5 rejected the German demands as incompatible with Polish sovereignty and honor. It is important to recognize that Poland possessed its own strategic interests and choices, and that some of its earlier decisions, including its stance toward Czechoslovakia and the Soviet Union, were criticized then and since. But the primary immediate threat to its independence was Nazi Germany, and the decision to use force was Germany's.

Section 116.18

The Failure of Collective Diplomacy and the Nazi–Soviet Pact

After the British guarantee, the question became whether Britain and France could construct a coalition that included the Soviet Union. The Soviet government, which had been excluded from the Munich conference and which had experienced the failure of collective security repeatedly, proposed in April a tripartite alliance with military conventions and a guarantee of the states between Germany and the Soviet Union. British leaders hesitated. Chamberlain distrusted Soviet motives, doubted the effectiveness of the Red Army after the purges, and feared that an alliance would provoke Germany and alienate Poland and Romania, which had no wish to be defended by Soviet forces. In early May, Stalin replaced Litvinov, who was Jewish and associated with collective security, with Vyacheslav Molotov as Foreign Commissar, a change that observers in Berlin and London read as a signal of possible Soviet flexibility.

Political negotiations continued through the summer, with disagreements over the definition of indirect aggression, which the Soviet side wanted to include internal changes of government in the Baltic states, and over the Baltic states themselves. Military talks began in Moscow in mid-August, when a British and French mission, led on the British side by Admiral Sir Reginald Drax and traveling by slow ship, arrived without full negotiating authority. The critical issue was passage: Soviet military leaders, headed by Marshal Kliment Voroshilov, asked whether Soviet troops would be allowed to enter Poland and Romania to fight Germany. The Polish government refused, fearing that Soviet troops would not leave, and Romania was similarly cautious. The British and French delegations had no authority to override them. The talks reflected deep mutual distrust: the Soviet side suspected that the West wanted to turn Germany eastward, while the British and French suspected that Moscow sought to stay out of a war or to profit from it.

Hitler's strategic motives were simple and urgent. He had decided to attack Poland, probably before the autumn weather, and he wished to avoid a two-front war with the Soviet Union and the western powers, as in 1914. German diplomats, who had made contact through trade negotiations, indicated to the Soviet side from May onward that Berlin was ready for improved political relations. In August the offers became explicit. On August 20, Hitler appealed directly to Stalin to receive Ribbentrop, and Stalin agreed. Stalin's reasoning has been interpreted in different ways, and the debate continues. Soviet documents and later studies suggest he sought to avoid fighting Germany alone, to gain time to rebuild the army, to regain territory lost after 1917, and to secure a buffer, while expecting that Germany and the western powers would be drawn into a long and exhausting war. He was also affected by distrust of Britain and France after Munich and by the military crisis with Japan in the Far East, where fighting at Khalkhin Gol in Mongolia continued through the summer.

Ribbentrop arrived in Moscow on August 23, and the German–Soviet Non-Aggression Pact was signed in the early hours of the following morning, dated August 23, 1939. Its public terms were brief. The parties undertook to refrain from any act of force or aggression against each other, whether alone or with other powers; to refrain from supporting any third power that attacked either party; to consult on common interests; and to settle disputes peacefully. The pact was to last ten years. The secret additional protocol, whose existence was long denied by the Soviet authorities and was officially acknowledged by the Soviet Union only in 1989, divided eastern Europe into spheres of influence. In the north, Finland, Estonia, and Latvia fell within the Soviet sphere and Lithuania in the German sphere. In Poland, the line of division was drawn roughly along the Narew, Vistula, and San rivers, and the question of whether an independent Polish state should remain was left for later settlement. Germany declared its lack of interest in Bessarabia, in Romania, where the Soviet Union claimed an interest.

The original August protocol must be distinguished from later modifications. After the invasion of Poland and the Soviet entry on September 17, the two governments signed on September 28 the German–Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty, with secret protocols that moved Lithuania into the Soviet sphere in exchange for a larger share of Polish territory for Germany, including the Lublin district and part of Warsaw province. The treaty and its secret protocol are identifiable documents, available in the German diplomatic archives captured after the war and published, and in the Soviet record. Studying them directly, not only through later interpretations, shows how explicitly the two regimes divided the territory of third states. The pact had immediate consequences: it removed the threat of Soviet opposition to a German attack on Poland, it shocked communists and antifascists across Europe, who were required by Moscow's changed line to reverse their positions, and it persuaded Hitler that Britain and France might back down, although in fact it strengthened Britain's resolve to honor its guarantee.

Historical Vocabulary
Non-Aggression Pact, Secret Protocol, Spheres of Influence, and Alliance Commitments

A non-aggression pact is a treaty in which the parties promise not to attack one another or to support an attacker of the other. It is not an alliance, which would oblige them to assist each other in war. Non-aggression pacts were common in the 1930s, including those between Germany and Poland in 1934 and between Poland and the Soviet Union in 1932, and what made the German–Soviet pact distinctive was the context and the secret protocol attached to it.

A secret protocol is a confidential supplement to a public treaty. In international law and diplomacy secret agreements were not unusual, but they raised questions of legitimacy, since they allowed governments to announce one policy while committing to another. Spheres of influence are areas in which a power claims the right to predominate. The protocol did not say that Germany and the Soviet Union would annex the territories in question, but in practice the two governments occupied and incorporated them. Finally, alliance commitments, such as the Anglo-Polish agreement and the Franco-Polish alliance, differed from guarantees and non-aggression treaties in the specificity of the obligations they created. Students should use these terms carefully, avoiding the assumption that every diplomatic arrangement was an alliance.

Section 116.19

The Invasion of Poland and the Beginning of World War II in Europe

The last week of August 1939 combined desperate diplomacy with military preparation. Hitler told his generals on August 22, in a speech on which accounts differ in wording, that war with Poland would begin soon and that Britain and France would probably not intervene, and he fixed the attack for August 26. The signing of the Anglo-Polish treaty on August 25 and news that Italy, whose leaders told Hitler it was not ready for war, would not join immediately caused him to postpone the date by several days. Meanwhile, Swedish businessman Birger Dahlerus and others carried messages between Berlin and London, and Britain sought to persuade Poland to negotiate, while Germany presented demands to Poland through a narrow window of time that made acceptance impossible. Poland ordered general mobilization on August 30 after earlier postponing it under British and French pressure, which meant that part of its forces were not in position when the attack came.

The invasion was preceded by staged incidents designed to give the appearance that Poland was the aggressor. On the evening of August 31, SS men in Polish uniforms staged an attack on a German radio station at Gleiwitz in Upper Silesia, leaving the body of a murdered concentration camp prisoner as evidence, in an operation commanded by the SS and given the code name Himmler. At about 4:45 in the morning of September 1, German forces crossed the frontier, while the old battleship Schleswig-Holstein, visiting Danzig, opened fire on the Polish garrison at the Westerplatte. Hitler told the Reichstag that Germany was returning fire. The attack involved well over a million German soldiers, supported by armor and aircraft, against a Polish army that was numerically smaller, less mechanized, and facing an enemy on three sides. Danzig's Nazi authorities proclaimed the city's incorporation into Germany the same day.

The British and French governments responded cautiously. On September 1, they warned Germany that unless it withdrew they would fulfill their obligations, and hesitated for two days partly because the French sought time to mobilize and to await Italian mediation proposals. In London, the cabinet and the House of Commons showed strong impatience at delay. At 9 a.m. on September 3, the British ambassador in Berlin delivered an ultimatum that expired at 11 a.m., and Chamberlain announced in a radio broadcast that Britain was at war with Germany. France's ultimatum expired at 5 p.m., and the French government declared war the same day. The Dominions followed, with Australia and New Zealand declaring war at once, Canada on September 10, and South Africa on September 6 after a political struggle. Britain's and France's declarations transformed the conflict from a German-Polish war into a general European war, though neither could provide immediate military relief to Poland. Italy declared non-belligerence, while Mussolini waited to see who prevailed.

The Soviet invasion of eastern Poland began on September 17, when the Red Army crossed the frontier in the early morning, after the Polish government and high command had already begun to retreat toward the Romanian border and when Polish forces were heavily engaged with the Germans. The Soviet government justified the invasion by saying that the Polish state had collapsed and that it was protecting the Ukrainian and Belarusian populations of the region. This explanation was contradicted by the continued existence of Polish resistance and by the prearranged character of the operation under the August agreement. The Soviet invasion must be distinguished from Germany's initial campaign: it came more than two weeks later, in implementation of a secret agreement with Germany, and led to the occupation of territory that had belonged to Poland since 1921. The Polish government crossed into Romania on September 17 and 18, and Polish forces continued to resist, with Warsaw surrendering on September 28. The September 28 treaty then revised the line of partition. The treatment of the military campaign here is deliberately concise: Chapter 117 examines the Polish defeat, the Soviet occupation, the western campaigns, and the Battle of Britain in detail, and the occupation and its crimes are examined in Chapters 120 and 121.

Section 116.20

Why European Peace Failed: An Assessment of the Road to World War II

The preceding sections suggest that the failure of European peace between 1935 and 1939 was produced by the interaction of several factors that cannot be reduced to one. The first was the aims and methods of expansionist regimes. Nazi Germany sought revision of the 1919 settlement and, behind it, a program of racial and territorial expansion; Fascist Italy sought empire in Africa and dominance in the Mediterranean; the Soviet Union, though it was not an expansionist power in the same sense, pursued security and territorial advantage by methods that included an agreement with Germany to partition neighboring states. These were distinct policies, and their relationship was one of changing convenience and not a single plan. Responsibility must distinguish the acts involved: Italy invaded sovereign Ethiopia; Germany breached demilitarization provisions within its own Rhineland in 1936, then coerced the annexation of Austria, dismembered and occupied Czechoslovakia, and invaded Poland; the Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland in September 1939.

The second factor was the weakness of the collective-security machinery. The League was ineffective against major powers, the United States stayed outside, and Britain and France, which together might have enforced the settlement, disagreed on priorities and lacked the military means or political will to act decisively until 1939. The third was domestic constraint. Democratic governments in Britain and France faced publics that remembered the last war, treasuries that restricted spending, and political parties divided about rearmament and foreign policy, while authoritarian regimes could mobilize resources more freely. The fourth was strategic misjudgment. British and French leaders overestimated the effect of German bombing, misjudged Hitler's aims, and underestimated the possibility that concessions would encourage rather than moderate demands, while Hitler overestimated how long the western powers would stay passive and how far the pact with Stalin would deter them.

War became increasingly probable by 1939, but it should not be interpreted as mechanically inevitable. At several points, including the Rhineland in 1936, the Czechoslovak crisis in 1938, and the negotiations with Moscow in the summer of 1939, different decisions were possible, and leaders acted on imperfect information and under competing pressures. It is unclear whether different choices would have averted war or produced a war on better or worse terms. But the existence of alternatives does not make the outcome any less the responsibility of those who chose aggression. Historians therefore try to describe the narrowing of options without treating the outcome as fated.

This chapter closes Part XVII, which began with the July Crisis of 1914. The treaty system analyzed in Chapter 113, the democratic and economic instability analyzed in Chapter 114, and the authoritarian transformations analyzed in Chapter 115 together created the setting in which the crises of 1935 to 1939 unfolded. The settlement of 1919 had created states and borders that were contested and difficult to defend; the Depression undermined the political and economic basis of international cooperation; and the new authoritarian regimes were committed to challenging the existing order by force. By September 1939 the interwar settlement had been destroyed. The next chapter, Chapter 117, begins with the German campaign in Poland and examines how the war that began in September 1939 spread to western Europe, the fall of France, and the Battle of Britain.

Historical Debate

Was Appeasement a Strategic Necessity, an Avoidable Failure, or a Combination of Both?

The question: Did British and French concessions to Germany between 1936 and 1938 reflect realistic assessments of military weakness and domestic constraints, or did they squander opportunities to deter Hitler when the balance of forces was more favorable?

Interpretation A, appeasement as strategic necessity: Some historians argue that Britain and France were not prepared for war in 1938. They point to the Chiefs of Staff's assessments, the state of the Royal Air Force and radar, the weakness of the British army, Dominion reluctance, the risk of simultaneous threats from Italy and Japan, and public opinion. They argue that Chamberlain gained time and demonstrated to the British public and the Dominions that every effort had been made to preserve peace, so that in 1939 the country entered the war united. Some stress the work of scholars such as R. A. C. Parker on the constraints Chamberlain faced.
Interpretation B, appeasement as avoidable failure: Others argue that the policy misread Hitler, that the military balance in 1938 was more favorable to the democracies than leaders believed, and that Czechoslovakia's army, fortifications, and industry, possible Soviet help, and German generals' doubts offered opportunities for deterrence. They stress that Germany gained more from the interval than the democracies did, since it absorbed Austria and the Czech lands, and that earlier firmness over the Rhineland or Czechoslovakia might have altered Hitler's calculations. Winston Churchill's later account has influenced this view, though modern scholars treat it critically.
Combined interpretations: Much recent scholarship, including the work of Zara Steiner and others, combines these views: appeasement was shaped by real constraints, but its execution, its misreadings of Hitler, and its neglect of potential allies such as the Soviet Union and Czechoslovakia were choices. A. J. P. Taylor's 1961 argument that Hitler was an opportunist responding to openings, and not executing a fixed plan, was provocative and is now widely rejected in its strongest form, though it prompted scholars to study contingency more carefully. The historian should avoid false balance: the debate concerns the responses of the democracies, not whether Germany bore primary responsibility for aggression.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on military appraisals, cabinet and Foreign Office records, German documents including the Hossbach memorandum, estimates of relative strength, public opinion evidence, and Soviet and Czechoslovak records.

Why it matters: How historians judge appeasement shapes understanding of the causes of the war, the lessons later drawn from it, and the use of Munich as a political analogy, which historians treat with caution because each situation differs.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the European international order broke down between 1935 and 1939. Its central argument is that the collapse of peace resulted from the interaction of aggressive territorial ambitions, weakened collective-security institutions, military calculations, domestic political constraints, and a succession of consequential diplomatic decisions. No single mistake, whether the failure to respond in the Rhineland, the abandonment of Czechoslovakia at Munich, or the delay in approaching the Soviet Union, explains the outcome, and no account that treats war as predetermined fits the evidence.

Division I traced the breakdown of collective security. The settlement of 1919 and the Locarno agreements established commitments that were firm in western Europe and ambiguous in the east, and the League of Nations lacked the means to enforce its Covenant. Italy's invasion of Ethiopia in October 1935 tested the sanctions system, which imposed real but limited penalties that excluded oil and left the Suez Canal open, and the Hoare–Laval proposals showed how far Britain and France were willing to compromise. Germany's open rearmament in 1935 and its reoccupation of the Rhineland on March 7, 1936 further weakened the credibility of French and British guarantees, and by the middle of 1936 governments had turned to competing strategies, including the Franco-Soviet pact, the Rome–Berlin understanding, and the Anti-Comintern Pact, none of which amounted to a fixed alliance system.

Division II examined the Spanish Civil War as a conflict with deep Spanish roots that became an international confrontation. The Second Republic's reforms, the polarization of the early 1930s, the Asturias rising, the Popular Front victory of February 1936, and the military rising of July 1936 produced a divided country and a long war. Both camps were coalitions: the Republic included republicans, socialists, communists, anarchists, and regional autonomists who clashed violently in Barcelona in May 1937, and the Nationalists included army officers, Falangists, Carlists, monarchists, and Catholic conservatives whom Franco brought under a single political structure. Non-intervention restrained arms supplies to the Republic while Germany, Italy, and Portugal supported the Nationalists, and the Soviet Union and the International Brigades supported the Republic. Civilians suffered terribly, from political killings in both zones, from air attack at Guernica, from hunger and displacement, and from the flight of roughly half a million refugees into France in early 1939.

Division III turned to appeasement and rearmament. The policy was a considered effort to resolve grievances through negotiation and avoid another general war, shaped by the memory of the First World War, fear of bombing, financial limits, imperial overstretch, and divided opinion. Its supporters, including Neville Chamberlain, believed that Germany's grievances were partly legitimate and that rearmament and diplomacy could be combined. Its critics, including Winston Churchill and some officials, warned that concessions would encourage further demands. The Rome–Berlin Axis, by drawing Italy away from the defense of Austrian independence, prepared the way for the Anschluss of March 1938, which brought Austria under German rule with coercion, a manipulated plebiscite, and immediate persecution of Jews and political opponents.

Division IV analyzed the Czechoslovak crisis. Czechoslovakia was a functioning democracy with a strong army and a multinational population, and the Sudeten German question was real but was exploited and radicalized by Nazi Germany through the Sudeten German Party. During the crisis of September 1938, Britain and France pressed Prague to accept cession, Hitler escalated his demands at Bad Godesberg, and Europe came to the brink of war. The Munich Agreement of September 29 and 30 transferred the Sudetenland to Germany without Czechoslovak participation, stripped the state of its defenses, and was followed by Polish and Hungarian territorial gains. The German occupation of the Czech lands on March 15, 1939 destroyed the claim that Germany sought only the union of German speakers and led Britain and France to abandon concession as their principal policy.

Division V followed the final crisis. German demands concerning Danzig and the Polish Corridor met Polish refusal, and Britain's guarantee of March 31 committed it to Poland's defense. Negotiations among Britain, France, and the Soviet Union failed because of mutual distrust and the refusal of Poland and Romania to admit Soviet troops, while Germany and the Soviet Union concluded the Non-Aggression Pact of August 23 with a secret protocol dividing eastern Europe into spheres of influence. Germany invaded Poland on September 1, Britain and France declared war on September 3, and the Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on September 17 in implementation of the agreement with Germany. These events must be distinguished: the German attack began the war, the declarations made it a general European war, and the Soviet invasion carried out the secret arrangement.

Several patterns stand out. Responsibility for initiating aggression lay with the governments that used force or threatened it, though other governments' decisions shaped the conditions in which they acted. Smaller states, including Ethiopia, Austria, Czechoslovakia, and Poland, exercised agency within harsh constraints. Regional variation mattered: the war in Spain, the Danubian crises, and the confrontation in the Baltic region each had distinct dynamics. Continuities with earlier history include the persistence of the Rhine and Danube corridors as strategic spaces, and the long-standing problem of defending eastern European states from the west.

The chapter's historical significance lies in showing how an international order that had appeared stable in the late 1920s was dismantled in less than a decade. It also illustrates the difficulty of judging decisions made under uncertainty, and the importance of distinguishing contemporary reasoning from later knowledge. Chapter 117 now turns to the war that began in September 1939, which would extend across Europe and, as examined in later chapters, bring occupation, genocide, and destruction on a scale that few contemporaries anticipated.

Review Materials

Key Terms

The thirty definitions below correspond exactly to the Core Vocabulary Preview.

Collective security
The principle, embodied in the Covenant of the League of Nations, that aggression against one member should be met by the combined diplomatic, economic, and in extremity military response of the others. In the 1930s it depended on great powers willing to bear the costs of enforcement.
Revisionism
The political aim of altering the territorial, military, or financial terms of the 1919 to 1923 peace settlements. Revisionist states ranged from governments seeking negotiated adjustment to regimes prepared to use force.
Deterrence
The attempt to prevent aggression by convincing a potential aggressor that the costs of attack would exceed the gains. It required credible military capacity and a visible willingness to use it.
Territorial sovereignty
The recognized right of a state to exercise exclusive authority within its borders, including the right to be consulted about any change to them. The Munich settlement of 1938 violated this principle in practice for Czechoslovakia.
League sanctions
Economic and financial penalties applied to Italy by League members from November 1935 after its invasion of Ethiopia. They covered arms, credit, and many imports and exports but excluded oil, coal, and steel, and the Suez Canal remained open to Italian shipping.
Non-intervention
The policy adopted in August and September 1936 by most European governments of refusing to supply arms or forces to either side in Spain, administered through a Non-Intervention Committee in London. In practice Germany, Italy, and the Soviet Union continued to supply forces or matériel.
Popular Front
A coalition of left and centre-left parties formed to oppose fascism and the right. Popular Front governments took office in Spain in February 1936 and in France in June 1936.
Nationalists
The insurgent coalition that rebelled against the Spanish Republic in July 1936 and ultimately won the war, comprising army officers, Falangists, Carlists, monarchists, conservative Catholics, and landowners.
Republicans
The supporters of the Spanish Republic during the civil war, including republican parties, socialists, communists, anarchists, and Basque and Catalan autonomists, who often disagreed sharply with one another.
International Brigades
Units of foreign volunteers, organized largely through the Communist International from autumn 1936, who fought for the Republic. Roughly 35,000 to 40,000 volunteers served, drawn from many countries.
Condor Legion
The German air and ground force sent to support the Nationalists from November 1936. It served as a testing ground for aircraft, tactics, and coordination with ground forces.
Guernica
A Basque town bombed on April 26, 1937 by the German Condor Legion and Italian aircraft in support of Nationalist operations. It became an international symbol of aerial attack on civilians.
Retirada
The Spanish term for the mass retreat of Republican soldiers and civilians into France in January and February 1939, when roughly half a million people crossed the Pyrenees.
Appeasement
In 1930s Britain and France, a policy of resolving international disputes by negotiated concessions to grievances thought partly legitimate, in order to avoid war. It was initially a respectable term for conciliation rather than an accusation of cowardice.
Rearmament
The expansion of armed forces and arms industries. Germany rearmed openly from 1935, while Britain and France expanded their forces more slowly and under financial and political constraints.
Demilitarized zone
An area in which a state may not station armed forces. The Treaty of Versailles and the Locarno agreements prohibited German military forces in the Rhineland west of the Rhine and in a strip east of it.
Rhineland
The German territory along the Rhine, occupied by Allied forces until 1930 and demilitarized under the postwar settlement. Its reoccupation on March 7, 1936 removed a key French strategic buffer.
Rome–Berlin Axis
The informal understanding between Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany announced in the autumn of 1936. It was a diplomatic alignment, not yet a formal military alliance, which came with the Pact of Steel in May 1939.
Anti-Comintern Pact
An agreement signed by Germany and Japan on November 25, 1936 directed publicly against the Communist International. Italy adhered in November 1937. It did not create a binding military alliance.
Anschluss
The German annexation of Austria, begun by the entry of German troops on March 12, 1938 and proclaimed in law on March 13. The term means union or joining, a euphemism that obscures coercion.
Sudetenland
The border districts of Bohemia and Moravia with large German-speaking populations. The name, popularized after 1918, covers territory that had never been part of Germany.
Munich Agreement
The settlement signed in the early hours of September 30, 1938 by Germany, Italy, Britain, and France providing for the transfer of the Sudetenland to Germany. Czechoslovakia was not a party to the negotiations.
Territorial cession
The transfer of territory from one state to another by agreement or compulsion. At Munich and afterward, cessions from Czechoslovakia went to Germany, Poland, and Hungary.
Protectorate
A dependent political arrangement in which a stronger state controls foreign policy and security while preserving local administration in form. Germany proclaimed the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia on March 16, 1939.
Danzig
The Free City of Danzig (Gdańsk), a League-supervised city-state created in 1920 with a predominantly German-speaking population and Polish customs and port rights. It became a focus of German pressure on Poland in 1938 and 1939.
Polish Corridor
The strip of territory in Pomerania given to Poland in 1919 to provide access to the Baltic Sea, which separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany and was a persistent target of German revisionism.
Security guarantee
A public commitment by one government to support another if its independence is threatened. Britain's guarantee to Poland of March 31, 1939 was followed by guarantees to Romania and Greece.
Non-aggression pact
A treaty in which signatories promise not to attack one another. The German–Soviet pact of August 23, 1939 was formally of this kind, but its secret protocol turned it into a division of eastern Europe.
Secret protocol
A confidential supplement to a published treaty that records understandings the signatories do not wish to disclose. The protocol attached to the German–Soviet pact assigned eastern European territories to spheres of influence.
Spheres of influence
Regions in which a great power claims preponderant political or economic interests, often without annexing them. In 1939 Germany and the Soviet Union used the term to partition parts of eastern Europe between them.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an international order can dissolve through a series of decisions, each explicable at the time, that cumulatively narrow the options for peace. The crises from Ethiopia to Poland did not follow a predetermined script. They were shaped by the choices of aggressive governments, by the hesitations of democracies, by the calculations of the Soviet Union, and by the efforts of threatened societies to survive, and understanding them requires attention to all of these.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the history of Europe in the twentieth century: the fragility of the settlement of 1919, the political consequences of economic crisis, the radicalization of nationalism, and the weakness of international institutions without enforcement. Developments in Spain, in the Danubian lands, and around the Baltic show that European history in these years cannot be told from the vantage point of a few western capitals alone, and that regions on the periphery of great-power attention were central to the unfolding crisis.

Many of its consequences extended well beyond 1939. The partition of Poland, the German occupation of the Czech lands, the Soviet absorption of territories under the August protocol, the fate of Spanish exiles, and the later use of Munich as a political analogy all shaped Europe's history through the war and after, as later chapters examine. The experience of failed deterrence also influenced the design of postwar security institutions, including those discussed in Volume VI. Students should treat such analogies with caution, since the circumstances of each later crisis differ.

Within Volume V, the chapter brings the interwar crisis to its end and opens the era of total war. It does not conclude the story of European catastrophe but marks a transition: the diplomatic history of the breakdown of peace gives way to the military, social, and moral history of occupation, resistance, and genocide. The questions raised here about responsibility, agency, and the limits of negotiation remain central as the textbook proceeds to Chapter 117.
