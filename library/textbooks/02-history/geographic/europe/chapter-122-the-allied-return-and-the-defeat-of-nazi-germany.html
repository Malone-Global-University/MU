Chapter Opening

By the spring of 1945 the war that Germany had begun in 1939 was ending in the ruins of its own cities. Soviet guns were firing into Berlin from the east, American and British columns were driving across the German plain from the west, and a regime that had promised a thousand years of dominion was reduced to improvised courts, schoolboy soldiers, and a leader in a bunker. The defeat of Nazi Germany was not a single event but a coalition achievement assembled over three years of production, planning, attrition, and bloodshed.

Consider two documents prepared within days of each other in the first week of June 1944. One was a public message of confidence addressed to the soldiers, sailors, and airmen of the Allied Expeditionary Force, announcing that the great crusade for which they had trained was about to begin. The other was a private note, drafted on the possibility that the invasion would fail, in which the Supreme Commander accepted personal responsibility for the failure. Both were written by Dwight D. Eisenhower, and together they capture the condition in which the western Allies returned to northwestern Europe: enormous material strength, meticulous preparation, and genuine uncertainty. Chapter 122 examines how that uncertainty was overcome, and how the coalition of which Eisenhower was one servant brought about the surrender of the German armed forces in May 1945.

This chapter is the culminating military chapter of Part XVIII. The preceding chapters have followed Germany's early victories (Chapter 117), the Mediterranean and Balkan wars (Chapter 118), Operation Barbarossa and the Eastern Front (Chapter 119), the systems of occupation, collaboration, and resistance (Chapter 120), and the Holocaust (Chapter 121). Chapter 122 draws these interconnected developments together. Its question is how the Allied coalition transformed advantages in industrial production, shipping, aviation, intelligence, and manpower into the destruction of the German military machine, and how the final campaigns reshaped Europe's political and human landscape. The detailed consequences of that destruction, including displacement, trials, occupation, and reconstruction, belong to Chapter 123.

The chapter argues that Germany's defeat resulted from cumulative pressure applied across interconnected fronts. Soviet armies destroyed the greater part of German ground strength in the east at a cost in Soviet lives that no other Allied power approached. The western Allies, together with Canadian, Polish, French, Commonwealth, and other contingents, won the Battle of the Atlantic, built an overwhelming air force, fought up the Italian peninsula, and opened a second continental front in France. Strategic bombing, though morally and militarily controversial, helped drain German fuel, aircraft, and transport. Hitler's strategy and the regime's ideological priorities compounded these disadvantages. At the same time, the chapter insists that victory was not inevitable, that Allied cooperation was frequently strained, and that the end of Nazi rule did not resolve the consequences of genocide, mass death, and territorial upheaval.

The chapter's geography is continental. It moves from the conference tables at Casablanca and Tehran to the mountains south of Rome, the beaches and hedgerows of Normandy, the Scheldt estuary, the Ardennes forest, the Vistula and Oder valleys, the Rhine, the streets of Berlin, and the schoolhouse in Reims where the German surrender was signed. Its actors include American, British, Canadian, Polish, French, Soviet, Indian, New Zealand, Brazilian, Romanian, Yugoslav, and Italian forces, as well as German soldiers, conscripted adolescents, civilians in bombed cities, forced laborers, and the survivors of concentration camps encountered by advancing armies. Treating the end of the war as only an Anglo-American story, or only a Soviet one, would distort the evidence.

Historians reconstruct these events from an unusually rich and unusually contested body of sources. Allied headquarters records, operational orders, signals intelligence, and unit war diaries survive in great quantity. The U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey and its British counterpart produced contemporary assessments of air power. German evidence includes the war diary of the Armed Forces High Command, captured records, and the testimony of generals produced largely after the war, which often shifted blame onto Hitler and must be read critically. Soviet operational records, long closed, have become more accessible since 1991, though they remain shaped by official narratives. Letters, diaries, photographs, newsreels, and oral histories supply the experience of soldiers and civilians. Wartime communiqués, in particular, were instruments of morale and diplomacy as well as reports, and students must distinguish them from conclusions supported by later archival scholarship.

Within Volume V, this chapter marks the end of the second European catastrophe as a military conflict. Its position at the close of Part XVIII means it must conclude the military narrative without pretending to conclude the history. The war against Japan continued until September 1945, and the war in Europe left behind a continent of displaced people, destroyed cities, occupied states, and growing disagreement among the victors. Those consequences carry the textbook into Volume VI and the Cold War examined from Chapter 124 onward. Chapter 122 therefore ends with victory and with unresolved questions, which is how its participants experienced the first days of May 1945.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Grand Alliance formulated strategy, including the Europe First priority, the Casablanca policy of unconditional surrender, and the commitments made at Tehran, while identifying the disagreements that persisted among the Allies.
  2. Assess the objectives, methods, and disputed effects of the Combined Bomber Offensive, distinguishing strategic bombing doctrine from operational accuracy and area bombing.
  3. Analyze the roles of shipping, production, the Battle of the Atlantic, and logistics in making continental invasion possible.
  4. Trace the Italian campaign from Salerno to the German surrender of 1945 and evaluate its military and political significance.
  5. Describe the planning, deception, and execution of Operations Overlord and Neptune, and distinguish D-Day from the wider Battle of Normandy.
  6. Compare the autumn 1944 campaigns at Antwerp, Arnhem, Aachen, and the Ardennes, and explain how logistics and German resistance slowed the western advance.
  7. Evaluate the contribution of Soviet operations in 1944 and 1945, including the political implications of Soviet advances across eastern and southeastern Europe.
  8. Interpret the final campaigns for Germany, from the Rhine crossings to the Battle of Berlin and the meeting at Torgau, and the encounters of Allied armies with concentration camps.
  9. Distinguish the surrender signed at Reims on May 7 from the Berlin ceremony of May 8 and the differing commemorations of Victory in Europe.
  10. Evaluate competing explanations for Germany's defeat, and connect the end of the European war to the political and human conditions of postwar Europe.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a coalition of states with differing political systems and priorities coordinate strategy, and what limits did political disagreement place on cooperation?
  2. What did the policy of unconditional surrender mean in practice, and why have historians disagreed about its effects?
  3. To what extent were air power, naval supremacy, and industrial production decisive, and how can historians measure their effects?
  4. Why did the Italian campaign continue until 1945, and what did it contribute to the defeat of Germany?
  5. What made the Normandy invasion succeed, and why did the breakout from the beachhead take far longer than the landings themselves?
  6. Why did the Allied advance slow in autumn 1944 despite the collapse of German positions in France?
  7. How should historians weigh the contribution of Soviet armies against that of the western Allies, and what evidence is required for such a comparison?
  8. In what ways did the military end of Nazi Germany shape the political and humanitarian conditions of postwar Europe?

Historical Context

By the end of 1942 the strategic situation that had favored Germany in 1940 and 1941 was reversing. Germany had conquered much of the continent, but it had failed to defeat Britain, had brought the Soviet Union and the United States into the war, and had committed its best forces to a campaign in the east that its logistics and manpower could not sustain. The German Sixth Army's encirclement at Stalingrad, completed in November 1942 and ending in surrender in early February 1943, and the Anglo-American landings in French North Africa in November 1942 (Operation Torch) marked the beginning of a long shift in initiative. These events are treated in Chapters 118 and 119; what matters here is that from 1943 Germany fought a defensive war on several fronts against states whose combined industrial capacity far exceeded its own.

That combined capacity had been built into an alliance only gradually. The United States entered the war in December 1941 after Pearl Harbor and Germany's declaration of war, and early Anglo-American staff discussions had already adopted the principle that Germany, the most dangerous enemy, would be defeated first. The Soviet Union, invaded in June 1941, bore the main weight of the land war but received substantial British and American supplies under Lend-Lease, including vehicles, rail equipment, food, and raw materials. The three principal powers were never a formal alliance with a unified command. They coordinated through summit conferences, military missions, and, between the British and Americans, a standing Combined Chiefs of Staff. Their cooperation rested on a shared enemy rather than a shared vision of the postwar order.

The European theater into which the Allies would advance had been transformed by occupation. German control extended from the Atlantic coast to the Volga, supported by satellite and allied states including Italy until 1943, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Slovakia, and Croatia. Chapter 120 examined the systems of occupation and resistance, and Chapter 121 the genocide of European Jews, Roma, and others. Allied armies therefore entered territories that were simultaneously battlefields, sites of forced labor and mass killing, zones of partisan warfare, and societies in which loyalties had been divided by collaboration, coercion, and survival. Military liberation always involved political questions about who would govern once German authority collapsed.

Germany's own position was shaped by structural constraints that its leaders only partly acknowledged. The German economy had been mobilized for total war later and less completely than Britain's or the Soviet Union's, and from 1942 under Albert Speer production of weapons rose sharply, drawing on millions of forced laborers and prisoners. Yet the supply of oil, steel, transport, skilled labor, and trained pilots could not match Allied output. Strategic direction was concentrated in Hitler, who from December 1941 also commanded the army directly, and the regime's ideological commitments, including the continuation of the genocide while military resources were scarce, shaped priorities in ways that professional officers could rarely contest. Many German commanders disagreed with specific decisions, but the political system offered few channels for effective dissent, as the failed bomb plot of July 20, 1944 would demonstrate.

Students should also recall the problems of evidence and memory that surround this period. Victory generated competing national narratives: a Soviet account of the Great Patriotic War, an American and British account centered on D-Day and the liberation of the west, a French emphasis on resistance and national recovery, and a German postwar tradition that stressed Hitler's responsibility and the army's supposed professional honor. Each contains truths and distortions. The chapter's approach is to reconstruct operations from contemporary records, to give each Allied contribution its documented weight, and to treat later memory as a historical subject in its own right.

Section 122.1

Allied Grand Strategy: The Grand Alliance and the Decision to Defeat Germany

The coalition that defeated Germany was never a single organization. The United States, Great Britain, and the Soviet Union fought the same enemy but through different institutions, with different resources, and for overlapping rather than identical purposes. On January 1, 1942, twenty-six governments signed the Declaration by United Nations, pledging to use their full resources against the Axis and not to make a separate peace. Britain and the United States went further, creating at the Arcadia Conference of December 1941 and January 1942 the Combined Chiefs of Staff, a committee in Washington that coordinated Anglo-American strategy and allocated shipping and supplies. The Soviet Union was not a member. It cooperated through summit meetings, military missions, and Lend-Lease deliveries, and Soviet commanders did not share operational plans with the same openness as their western counterparts.

The foundation of Anglo-American strategy was the decision, reached in staff talks before American entry into the war and reaffirmed afterward, that Germany would be defeated first. This Europe First priority did not mean that the Pacific war was neglected, since the American navy and marine corps fought major campaigns there throughout 1942 and 1943, but it governed the allocation of the largest ground and air forces. Within the priority lay a persistent disagreement. American planners, led by George Marshall, favored a direct cross-Channel invasion at the earliest practicable date, concentrating force against the German army in northwestern Europe. British leaders, led by Churchill and Alan Brooke, the Chief of the Imperial General Staff, preferred to wear Germany down through Mediterranean operations, bombing, blockade, and support for resistance before risking a landing in France, recalling the casualties of 1914 to 1918 and the narrow evacuation from Dunkirk in 1940. The compromise sequence of Operation Torch in November 1942, the invasion of Sicily in July 1943, and the landings in Italy in September 1943 reflected British preferences and the practical fact that in 1942 the Allies lacked the shipping and trained divisions for a cross-Channel assault.

Stalin regarded Mediterranean operations as insufficient. From 1941 Soviet officials demanded a western front that would draw German divisions away from the eastern war; postponements deepened mistrust. The Soviet government also broke relations with the Polish government-in-exile in April 1943 after the Katyn graves were publicized and Warsaw sought an independent investigation. Military cooperation continued amid serious political disagreements.

At Casablanca, held January 14 to 24, 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill agreed to continue Mediterranean operations after Tunisia, to build up forces in Britain, and to intensify the bombing of Germany through what became the Combined Bomber Offensive. On January 24, at a press conference, Roosevelt announced that the Allies would demand the unconditional surrender of Germany, Italy, and Japan, and Churchill supported the statement. The formula echoed Ulysses Grant's Civil War usage and was intended partly to reassure Stalin that the western powers would not negotiate a separate peace, and partly to avoid the perceived mistake of 1918, when German nationalists later claimed that the army had not been truly defeated. It did not specify what terms would follow, and the actual instruments of surrender and the Allied assumption of supreme authority in Germany, issued in May and June 1945, were separate documents. Historians have long debated whether the demand stiffened German resistance by discouraging opposition to Hitler. Most scholars find little evidence that a different formula would have produced an early German capitulation, since the regime's leaders were committed to continuing the war, but the policy did leave Allied planners without a negotiated exit and committed them to occupying the country.

The Tehran Conference, from November 28 to December 1, 1943, brought Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin together for the first time. The leaders confirmed that the cross-Channel invasion, Overlord, would take place in 1944, supported by a landing in southern France. Stalin undertook to coordinate a major Soviet offensive with the invasion and, in private discussion, indicated that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan after Germany was defeated. Conversations about the postwar treatment of Germany and the shifting of Poland's frontiers westward were inconclusive but revealing. Soon afterward Eisenhower was named Supreme Commander of the Allied Expeditionary Force, with Marshall remaining in Washington as Army Chief of Staff and Brooke representing British military views. The system that resulted combined unified command in the field with national governments retaining control over political goals, an arrangement that worked best when the interests of the partners overlapped.

Conference minutes, diplomatic papers, staff memoranda and Alan Brooke's diaries preserve distinct views of Allied strategy. Public communiqués emphasized unity, while private accounts reveal sustained disagreements; postwar memoirs require separate critical examination.

Historical Vocabulary
Grand Alliance, Europe First, Unconditional Surrender, and Coalition Warfare

Grand Alliance is Churchill's phrase, later adopted by historians, for the wartime partnership of Britain, the United States, and the Soviet Union, with other states joined through the Declaration by United Nations. It was a coalition of necessity rather than a formal treaty system or unified command. Europe First names the strategic priority that Germany, the most powerful enemy, would be defeated before Japan, shaping the allocation of troops and aircraft though not excluding the Pacific. Unconditional surrender was the Casablanca announcement that the Allies would accept no negotiated armistice with the Axis powers, and it should be distinguished from the later written instruments that implemented German surrender. Coalition warfare refers to the conduct of war by allied states with separate governments, requiring negotiation over strategy, shared logistics, and political aims. The term matters because it prevents students from treating Allied victory as the product of a single will.

Section 122.2

The Combined Bomber Offensive: Air Superiority, Industry, and Civilian Destruction

The air war over Germany was the one campaign through which the western Allies directly struck the German homeland before 1944, and it became one of the most debated episodes of the war. The Casablanca directive of January 1943 instructed the British and American air forces to pursue the progressive destruction and dislocation of the German military, industrial, and economic system, and to undermine the morale of the German people. The resulting Combined Bomber Offensive combined two different methods. The Royal Air Force's Bomber Command, directed from 1942 by Arthur Harris, bombed at night, accepting that precision against specific targets was beyond its navigational and bombing technology, and attacked industrial cities as a whole. This practice is usually called area bombing, and British policy explicitly included the destruction of housing and the disruption of civilian life. The United States Army Air Forces, principally the Eighth Air Force flying from Britain and the Fifteenth from Italy, bombed by day, with the aim of destroying specific targets such as aircraft factories, ball-bearing plants, and oil refineries. This doctrine of strategic bombing, or precision bombing, was based on the belief that a modern economy had critical nodes whose destruction would paralyze the whole.

Doctrine and results differed sharply. Cloud cover, flak, smoke, and the limits of the Norden bombsight meant that American "precision" attacks frequently scattered bombs over wide areas, and by late 1944 American forces were also bombing by radar through overcast, effectively area attacks on city centers. In 1943 neither air force could protect its bombers over Germany. The Eighth Air Force's raids on Schweinfurt and Regensburg in August and October 1943 resulted in heavy losses, and the attrition of crews threatened the daylight campaign. Bomber Command's winter 1943 to 1944 attacks on Berlin, which cost it heavily in aircraft and men, did not break German resistance. Hamburg in July and August 1943 (Operation Gomorrah) produced a firestorm that killed tens of thousands of civilians, with estimates commonly placed between about 35,000 and 45,000, and destroyed large parts of the city, but German industrial output continued to rise through 1944 under Speer's reorganization.

The turning point came with the arrival of long-range escort fighters. The P-51 Mustang, fitted with the Rolls-Royce Merlin engine and drop tanks, could accompany bombers to Berlin and back by early 1944, and together with P-47 and P-38 fighters it transformed the daylight offensive. The Big Week attacks of February 20 to 25, 1944 on the German aircraft industry, though they did not eliminate production, drew the Luftwaffe into a war of attrition it could not win. German fighter pilots were lost faster than they could be trained, and fuel shortages, which cut flying hours for new pilots, deepened the problem. By the time of the Normandy landings the Allies possessed air superiority over western Europe, a precondition for the invasion. From the spring of 1944 the American air forces and, increasingly, the RAF attacked German synthetic fuel plants and the transport system, particularly railways and marshaling yards. The oil campaign reduced the fuel available to the German army, air force, and navy, and by the autumn of 1944 shortages restricted training and operations, a constraint visible during the Ardennes offensive. Allied bombing also forced Germany to divert enormous resources to air defense: antiaircraft guns, ammunition, radar, and hundreds of thousands of personnel, many of whom might otherwise have served at the front, including in the east.

The human costs were immense on both sides. Bomber Command lost roughly 55,000 aircrew killed, a casualty rate comparable to the heaviest losses of the infantry war, and the American air forces also suffered severe losses. German civilian deaths from Allied bombing are commonly estimated in the range of several hundred thousand, with figures from about 300,000 to 600,000 appearing in the scholarship, and many millions lost their homes. The attack on Dresden on February 13 to 15, 1945, in which British and American aircraft destroyed much of the historic city center, has become the emblem of this debate. For decades casualty claims ranged from about 25,000 to well over 100,000, partly because Nazi propaganda and later Cold War polemics inflated the figures. The Dresden historians' commission, in its 2010 final report, concluded that up to approximately 25,000 people died in the attacks. Dresden was a rail junction and contained industrial plants, but it was crowded with refugees from the east, and critics have questioned its military necessity so late in the war. Defenders, including some contemporary planners, pointed to its role in transport behind the eastern front at a time when the Soviet offensive was under way.

The record does not support either claim that bombing alone defeated Germany or that it had no significant military effect. Its impact varied by target, date and countermeasures. Students should weigh wartime directives against the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey, British official histories, German records and survivor testimony, particularly when evaluating area bombing's civilian cost.

Cause and Effect

The Bombing of Germany: Air Superiority, Industry, and Civilian Destruction

Long-term causes

Interwar air power doctrines promised that bombers could paralyze industrial states; Britain lacked another means of striking Germany after 1940; the Casablanca directive committed both air forces to a combined campaign.

Short-term causes

Navigation and radar advances, the arrival of long-range escort fighters in early 1944, and the Allied decision to target oil and transport enabled sustained, more effective attacks.

Immediate effects

The Luftwaffe lost experienced pilots and control of the sky; fuel and rail capacity shrank; German cities suffered mass casualties and displacement; Allied aircrews bore very heavy losses.

Long-term consequences

Air superiority made Normandy possible and German movement in 1944 hazardous; the destruction of cities complicated postwar reconstruction; the ethics of area bombing shaped later laws and debates on civilian protection.

Historical complexity: Industrial output continued to grow until late 1944, yet effects on fuel, training, and transport were cumulative, and not all can be measured by production statistics alone. The offensive cannot be reduced to one cause or one effect.

Section 122.3

The Battle of the Atlantic, Allied Production, and the Logistics of Continental Invasion

No Allied army could have landed in France without first solving a problem of maritime supply. Britain lived on imports, and from 1942 it became the forward base for the American armies that would cross the Channel. Every soldier, tank, aircraft, and gallon of fuel had to cross the Atlantic, largely in convoy, while German submarines operating from Atlantic ports in France attempted to sink merchant ships faster than they could be replaced. The Battle of the Atlantic, which had begun in September 1939, reached its crisis in the spring of 1943, when losses in the North Atlantic convoys were severe in March but the balance shifted in May, a month the Germans themselves called one of disaster after a large number of U-boats were destroyed.

Several developments explain the shift. Escort groups grew in number and quality, with Canadian, British, and American destroyers, frigates, and corvettes cooperating, and the Royal Canadian Navy took a large share of North Atlantic escort duty by 1943. Very-long-range Liberator aircraft closed the mid-Atlantic gap where convoys had lacked air cover, and small escort carriers accompanied convoys and hunting groups. Improved radar, shipborne high-frequency direction-finding, better depth-charge tactics, and signals intelligence, including the reading of German naval Enigma traffic, allowed convoys to be rerouted and submarines located. Meanwhile American shipyards, producing the standardized Liberty ship in huge numbers, built tonnage faster than the U-boats could sink it. The submarine threat did not end, since the Germans developed new tactics and technologies, but after 1943 it ceased to be strategically decisive.

Allied production made this logistical effort possible. The United States, with a continental economy undisturbed by invasion or bombing, became what Roosevelt called the arsenal of democracy, supplying not only its own forces but Britain and, through Lend-Lease, the Soviet Union. Britain mobilized its labor and industry more completely than any other belligerent, while Canada produced ships, vehicles, aircraft, and food for the common effort. Soviet industry, evacuated eastward in 1941 and 1942, produced tanks, guns, and aircraft in large numbers, assisted by Allied deliveries of trucks, locomotives, aluminum, and food that eased the strain on Soviet transport and agriculture. Behind this output stood the labor of factory workers, shipbuilders, dockers, and merchant seamen, among them millions of women employed in industries from aircraft assembly to munitions. Merchant seafarers suffered high casualties and were often drawn from many nations and colonial territories.

The invasion of France required a specialized logistical system. Landing craft of many types, built in large numbers and in competition with other priorities, were the scarce item that governed the timing of operations from 1943 onward. Britain became a vast depot, with American troops, vehicles, and supplies accumulating over months, and training exercises, including some that went disastrously wrong, such as the German E-boat attack on an exercise convoy at Slapton Sands in April 1944, tested amphibious procedures. Planners could not assume that a port would be captured quickly, so they devised artificial harbours, designated Mulberries, whose caissons and breakwaters were towed across the Channel and assembled off the Normandy coast, together with a pipeline for fuel under the sea that became known as Pluto. Specialized armor, including amphibious and flail tanks associated with the British 79th Armoured Division, was prepared to clear obstacles on the beaches. The scale of organization, involving thousands of ships and aircraft, millions of tons of supplies, and the movement of hundreds of thousands of personnel, anticipates the central lesson of the campaigns that follow: victory depended on sustaining armies as well as defeating enemy forces in battle.

Evidence for logistics lies in supply records, shipping returns, engineering reports, and the planning documents of SHAEF and the services, which are less dramatic than battle narratives but essential for explaining why campaigns succeeded or stalled. As the next sections show, these same constraints would later slow the Allied advance, when supply lines stretched across France faster than ports and railways could be repaired.

Section 122.4

The Italian Campaign: Monte Cassino, Anzio, Rome, and the Gothic Line

The Allied landings on the Italian mainland began on September 3, 1943, with British forces crossing the Strait of Messina, followed on September 9 by the main American and British landing at Salerno, as described in Chapter 118. Italy's armistice with the Allies, announced on September 8, led the German command to disarm Italian forces and occupy the country, and German troops under Albert Kesselring then fought a skillful defensive withdrawal. The mountainous spine of the peninsula, cut by rivers flowing across the line of advance, favored the defender. Roads were few, bridges easily destroyed, and weather in autumn and winter turned valleys into mud. The Germans organized a series of defensive lines, of which the most formidable was the Gustav Line, anchored on the Garigliano and Rapido rivers and the mountains around the town of Cassino.

The Allies in Italy were an international force. The Fifth Army, under the American Mark Clark, included American, British, French, and later Brazilian formations, and the British Eighth Army contained British, Canadian, Indian, New Zealand, South African, and Polish troops, as well as a Jewish Brigade formed in 1944. The French Expeditionary Corps under Alphonse Juin included Moroccan, Algerian, and Tunisian soldiers recruited from France's North African territories. The Polish II Corps, under Władysław Anders, was composed largely of Polish soldiers who had been deported to the Soviet Union in 1939 and 1940 and later released, and its presence reflected the distinctive politics of Polish exile.

To break the stalemate south of Rome the Allies attempted an amphibious outflanking at Anzio on January 22, 1944. The landing achieved surprise but the force commander did not exploit it quickly, and German reinforcements sealed the beachhead, which was then subjected to counterattacks and shelling for four months. Meanwhile four successive battles were fought around Monte Cassino between January and May. During the second, on February 15, American bombers destroyed the Benedictine abbey, a decision justified by Allied commanders who believed it was used for German observation, though the Germans had not occupied the abbey itself; German paratroopers then fortified the ruins. After the failures of the winter, the spring offensive, Operation Diadem, began on May 11 with an attack along the whole front. French mountain troops advanced through the Aurunci Mountains, outflanking the Gustav Line, and on May 18 the Polish II Corps took the Cassino heights after heavy fighting. The Anzio forces broke out from May 23, and Rome, declared an open city, was entered by American troops on June 4, two days before the Normandy landings. The French advance was accompanied by documented mass rapes and violence against civilians by some soldiers in the Lazio region, an episode remembered in Italy as the Marocchinate, which belongs in any honest account of the campaign.

Rome's capture did not end the fighting. German forces fell back to the Gothic Line in the northern Apennines, and Allied divisions were withdrawn for Operation Dragoon, reducing the strength of the Italian armies. The Gothic Line was attacked in late August and September 1944, but autumn rains and exhaustion halted the advance, and the Allies spent the winter in the mountains. Final offensives began in April 1945, when the Allies broke into the Po Valley, outflanked German positions, and drove across the plain. The German commanders in Italy signed a surrender at Caserta on April 29, effective on May 2.

The campaign also had a civil dimension. After the German occupation, Mussolini was installed as head of the Italian Social Republic in the north, under German control, and a civil war developed between his supporters, German forces, and the partisans of the Committee of National Liberation. Italian partisans sabotaged German transport, liberated towns, and suffered reprisals, including massacres of civilians such as those at Sant'Anna di Stazzema and Marzabotto in 1944. In late April 1945 insurrections in Milan, Turin, and Genoa preceded Allied arrival, and Mussolini was captured and shot by partisans on April 28. Liberation therefore coincided with intense political conflict over the future of the Italian state and the treatment of fascists, as well as with famine conditions and destroyed infrastructure.

The Italian campaign has been criticized as a costly sideshow and defended as an indispensable drain on German strength. Italy never provided a rapid route into central Europe, since the Alps and the demands of other theaters limited its possibilities, but it tied down roughly a score of German divisions, forced the Germans to maintain armies far from the main fronts, provided air bases for attacks on southern Germany and the Romanian oil fields, and contributed to the collapse of Mussolini's regime. Its costs fell upon Italian civilians as much as upon the soldiers.

Section 122.5

Planning Operation Overlord: Intelligence, Deception, and the Invasion of France

Operation Overlord was the name given to the Allied campaign to establish and expand a lodgement in northwestern Europe, and Operation Neptune to its initial assault phase, the naval and amphibious crossing and landings. The distinction matters, because Neptune ended once the beachheads were secured and supplied, while Overlord continued through the breakout and the advance to the Seine. Planning began under a British officer, Frederick Morgan, and was taken over in early 1944 by Eisenhower's headquarters, which became SHAEF. The principal commanders were Montgomery for the ground forces in the assault, Admiral Bertram Ramsay for naval operations, and Air Chief Marshal Trafford Leigh-Mallory for the Allied air forces, with Arthur Tedder as Eisenhower's deputy. Montgomery insisted on widening the assault from three to five beaches and on adding airborne divisions, changes that required additional landing craft and delayed the invasion from May to June.

Normandy was chosen over the Pas-de-Calais, the shortest crossing and the more obvious target, for several reasons. Its beaches were within range of fighter cover from English airfields and were less heavily fortified than the Calais coast, it lay near the port of Cherbourg, and the hinterland permitted an expansion of the beachhead. The Pas-de-Calais, by contrast, was the area the Germans expected to be attacked, which made it valuable to the Allies as the object of deception. Operation Fortitude, the cover plan for Overlord, created a notional First United States Army Group under George Patton in southeastern England, with dummy equipment, false radio traffic, and double agents controlled by British counterintelligence, who passed misleading reports to Berlin. The deception was reinforced by Allied reading of German ciphers, which showed how the German command assessed the information. It persuaded many German leaders that Normandy might be a diversion and that a larger landing would come at Calais, a belief that influenced the commitment of reserves for weeks after June 6.

The Allies also prepared the battlefield. From spring 1944 bombers and fighters attacked the French and Belgian railway system, bridges, and airfields in the transportation plan, a controversial program because it killed French civilians, and French resistance networks, coordinated with the Special Operations Executive and the Office of Strategic Services, cut rail lines and telephone cables on the eve of the landings. The BBC broadcast coded messages to the resistance, as discussed in Chapter 120. Weather forecasting was critical. The required combination of a low tide at dawn, a moon for airborne operations, and calm seas occurred only on a few days of each month. Eisenhower provisionally fixed June 5, but a storm forced a postponement, and on the night of June 4 the chief meteorologist, James Stagg, forecast a temporary improvement, leading Eisenhower to order the operation for June 6.

The German defenders faced their own uncertainties. Rommel, who commanded Army Group B, believed the invasion had to be defeated on the beaches, before Allied air power could prevent the movement of reserves, and he supervised the construction of obstacles, minefields, and fortifications that formed part of the Atlantic Wall, a system much more developed in some sectors than in others, and often manned by static divisions that included older soldiers and non-German volunteers. His superior, Rundstedt, commander in the west, favored holding armor inland for a mobile counterattack. Hitler kept control of the principal panzer reserves, so that they could not be released without his authority, which on the morning of June 6 was delayed. The compromise left the German armored divisions neither forward nor centralized, and this unresolved command dispute is often cited as a factor in the German failure to contain the landings.

The commitment to the operation involved genuine uncertainty. On or about June 5 Eisenhower wrote a note to be released if the landings failed, and on June 6 his Order of the Day addressed to the Allied Expeditionary Force was issued. These two documents, both preserved by the U.S. National Archives, show a commander making a decision under conditions in which weather, intelligence, and German reaction could not be predicted.

Together they illustrate the burden of command in coalition warfare, in which one person's decision committed forces of several nations. They do not tell us what the troops felt, how the plan actually worked, or how the French civilians in the landing zone viewed the invasion, and they are the words of the senior officer rather than of the men who carried out the operation. Section 122.6

D-Day, June 6, 1944: Airborne Operations and the Five Landing Beaches

The assault began in darkness. In the hours after midnight the British 6th Airborne Division landed by parachute and glider east of the Orne River to secure the left flank, including the bridges over the Orne and the Caen canal, and the American 82nd and 101st Airborne Divisions dropped in the Cotentin Peninsula behind Utah Beach. Airborne assault, the delivery of infantry by parachute and glider behind enemy lines, was a high-risk method. Navigation errors, cloud, antiaircraft fire, and flooded fields scattered the American paratroopers widely, many drowned, and units fought as improvised groups. The disorder also confused the Germans, who could not identify a single main thrust, and the paratroopers seized crossroads and bridges that mattered for the beach landings. The British also captured the bridges over the Orne and canal, the first Allied objectives achieved on French soil.

Naval bombardment by battleships, cruisers, and destroyers began before dawn, and the assault troops landed from the early morning, with the exact time depending on tides. Five beaches were designated. Utah, on the east coast of the Cotentin, and Omaha, further east, were American assault sectors, with the 4th Infantry Division landing at Utah and the 1st and 29th Infantry Divisions at Omaha, accompanied by Rangers who scaled the cliffs at Pointe du Hoc to attack coastal guns. Gold and Sword were assigned to British divisions, Gold to the 50th Division and Sword to the 3rd, supported by commandos, while Juno, between them, was assaulted by the 3rd Canadian Infantry Division with British support. Forces from other nations, including Free French commandos, Norwegians, Poles, and others, took part in the naval and air operations, and sailors from many allied navies manned the fleet.

The experience differed by beach. At Utah, strong currents carried the landing craft south of the planned area to a lightly defended stretch, and the assault suffered relatively few casualties. At Omaha, the defenses were strongest. German infantry occupied bluffs above a narrow beach, the preliminary bombardment had largely missed its targets, amphibious tanks were lost at sea, and the first waves were pinned down, suffering heavy casualties, including roughly 2,000 total American casualties according to commonly cited estimates. Small groups of soldiers, supported by destroyers firing at close range, eventually led an advance up the bluffs. At Gold, Juno, and Sword, British and Canadian troops, supported by specialized armor, overcame the beach defenses, though they suffered substantial casualties too, including at Juno, where rough sea and obstacles delayed the landing and the Canadians fought fiercely at the sea wall and in the villages behind. Allied losses were lower than some planners had feared, but this was not an easy or uncontested landing at any beach.

By the end of the day Allied forces had landed more than 150,000 men by sea and air and established lodgments, but the beachheads were not linked, and several first-day objectives were unfulfilled. British forces had not captured Caen, a city that would resist for weeks, and the American beaches were not joined until after the first days. The Germans had mounted limited counterattacks, including by the 21st Panzer Division near Caen. Casualty figures must be used carefully. Later research has confirmed more than 4,000 Allied dead on June 6, and total Allied casualties on the order of 10,000 including wounded and missing, but counts depend on how and when casualties are tallied and on which units are included, and German losses on the day are less precisely documented. D-Day was a successful foothold on an invaded continent, not the liberation of France, which would take months of fighting.

The operation depended on many who are less often remembered: sailors who navigated landing craft in the Channel swell, medical personnel who treated the wounded under fire, combat engineers who cleared obstacles, and logistics staff who sustained the beachheads. French civilians, especially in the villages of the landing zone, were caught between the fighting and Allied bombing, and several thousand died in the first days and weeks as towns such as Caen and Saint-Lô were bombarded. Evidence includes unit war diaries, naval logs, photographs, German reports, and the testimony of participants, which vary in reliability and in the scale of what each witness could observe.

Section 122.7

The Battle of Normandy: Caen, the Bocage, Operation Cobra, and Falaise

The weeks after D-Day were not a triumphant march. The Allies landed supplies and reinforcements across the beaches, including at the British Mulberry at Arromanches, though a severe storm from June 19 to 22 wrecked the American artificial harbour at Omaha and halted unloading for days. The Germans moved reinforcements, including several panzer divisions, to Normandy, and the Allies had to fight through country that favored the defender. Much of the American sector lay in the bocage, small fields surrounded by earth banks topped with thick hedgerows, which concealed antitank guns and infantry, restricted tank movement, and turned each field into a separate engagement. American units adapted, using improvised plows on tanks to cut through hedgerows and closer coordination between infantry, armor, and artillery.

The American First Army cleared the Cotentin and took Cherbourg on June 26 to 30, but the Germans had demolished the port, and it took weeks to restore it to limited operation. The British Second Army and the First Canadian Army attacked around Caen, on the open ground south and east of the city, in a series of costly operations, among them Epsom in June, Charnwood in July, and Goodwood in mid-July, which involved a massive armored attack preceded by heavy bombing. The Anglo-Canadian operations did not at first capture the whole city or achieve a breakthrough, and Montgomery's critics, including some in Eisenhower's headquarters, charged him with delay. Montgomery maintained that the Caen fighting attracted German armor to the eastern sector, making an American breakout possible on the west. Historians have debated how far this was planned from the outset, but there is general agreement that the British and Canadian attacks pinned the largest share of German panzer strength. The two Allied wings thus conducted different but strategically interconnected operations.

The breakthrough came with Operation Cobra, launched on July 25, 1944 near Saint-Lô. Heavy bombers dropped thousands of tons of bombs on a narrow stretch of German positions, with some bombs falling on American troops and causing casualties, including Lieutenant General Lesley McNair. American infantry and armor then pushed through the weakened line, and by the end of July the breakthrough at Avranches opened the way into Brittany and across the Loire. Patton's Third Army became operational on August 1, and Bradley's 12th Army Group took command of American forces. The German counterattack at Mortain on August 7, ordered by Hitler against the advice of his commanders, aimed to cut the American breakthrough at its base but was halted with the help of Allied aircraft, leaving German forces in a salient exposed to encirclement.

The result was the encirclement in the area of Falaise and Argentan. American forces advancing from the south and Canadian and Polish units advancing from the north attempted to close a gap through which German Seventh Army and Fifth Panzer Army were retreating. The pocket was closed in its final form around August 21, after fierce fighting, including by the Polish 1st Armoured Division at Hill 262, and the area was devastated by air attack and artillery. Estimates of German losses vary, but it is generally accepted that tens of thousands of men were captured and thousands killed, while a significant number of troops, estimated in the tens of thousands, escaped across the Seine without most of their heavy equipment. The Falaise Pocket was therefore a catastrophic defeat for Germany in Normandy but not a total annihilation of the German forces there. Overall, German losses in the Normandy campaign were enormous, with hundreds of thousands killed, wounded, or captured by the end of August, and the Allies also suffered heavily, including tens of thousands of dead.

Several factors explain the outcome. Allied air power constrained German movement by day and attacked reinforcements en route, and Allied naval gunfire supported the flanks. The Allies had superiority in material, notably artillery, ammunition, and vehicles, though German tanks such as the Panther and Tiger had qualitative advantages that Allied tactics had to overcome. Coordination between the American and British-Canadian armies, however imperfect, allowed the Allies to apply pressure at several points. German leadership decisions mattered too, including Hitler's refusal to permit withdrawals and the ordering of the Mortain counterattack. The breakout was not inevitable after June 6. For several weeks the campaign remained in the balance, and the Allied command worried about a stalemate comparable to the First World War, in which attrition might have outweighed advantage. Civilians in Normandy suffered extensively from bombing, artillery, and combat, and cities such as Caen, Saint-Lô, and Le Havre were largely destroyed.

Section 122.8

Operation Dragoon, the Liberation of Paris, and the Restoration of French Authority

While the Normandy battle culminated, the Allies launched a second landing on the Mediterranean coast of France. Operation Dragoon, begun on August 15, 1944, delivered the American Seventh Army and, under it, the French Army B commanded by Jean de Lattre de Tassigny onto the coast between Toulon and Cannes, preceded by an airborne landing and supported by naval forces. The landing met much lighter resistance than Normandy, as the Germans were withdrawing most of their forces northward, and the Allies advanced rapidly up the Rhône valley. French troops liberated Toulon and Marseille in late August, giving the Allies two important ports, which handled significant supplies in later months, though their distance from the front limited their usefulness. French Army B included a large number of soldiers recruited from France's North African colonies, among them Algerian, Moroccan, and Tunisian troops, and from sub-Saharan Africa, and these contributed disproportionately to the French fighting strength. Their later treatment, including the replacement of colonial troops in front-line units in the winter of 1944 and 1945, and disputes about recognition and pensions, is part of the history of the colonial empire examined in Chapter 128.

Eisenhower had intended to bypass Paris, to avoid the burden of supplying and governing a large city and the risk of urban fighting, but events overtook this plan. On August 19 the Paris police went on strike and resistance fighters of the Forces Françaises de l'Intérieur began an uprising, with communist and Gaullist resistance leaders divided over the wisdom of rising before the Allies arrived. The German military governor, Dietrich von Choltitz, had orders to defend the city and destroy its bridges, but he negotiated local truces and did not carry out the destruction. De Gaulle pressed Eisenhower to send the French 2nd Armored Division under Philippe Leclerc, and Eisenhower agreed. Leclerc's troops entered the city on the night of August 24, followed by the American 4th Infantry Division, and Choltitz surrendered on August 25. De Gaulle arrived the same day and walked down the Champs-Élysées on August 26 to cheering crowds.

The liberation of Paris was a military event and a political one. De Gaulle intended to establish the authority of the Provisional Government of the French Republic, formed in June 1944, and to prevent rival claims, whether from the communist-influenced resistance or from an Allied military administration, which Roosevelt had earlier envisaged. In a speech at the Hôtel de Ville he emphasized the role of the French people in their own liberation. Resistance leaders and the Free French were brought together in public ceremonies, and the Allies recognized the Provisional Government as the de facto authority in France on October 23, 1944. Restoring authority meant replacing the wartime prefects, courts, and police, reorganizing the economy, and dealing with collaborators. Some of the latter were punished through legal channels, while others suffered informal violence in the weeks after liberation, including summary executions and the public head-shaving of women accused of relations with Germans. Estimates of extrajudicial killings vary and are disputed, and the matter requires care, as it belongs to the contested memory of liberation. The military liberation of Paris did not end the war in France, as fighting continued in Alsace, around Atlantic ports such as Lorient and Saint-Nazaire, and elsewhere into 1945.

Everyday Life and Social History

Civilians during the Liberation of Paris

For Parisians the summer of 1944 was a time of shortage and uncertainty before it became a time of celebration. Food rations had been inadequate since the occupation, and in August the city's supply of bread, vegetables, gas, and electricity was disrupted by transport strikes, bombing of rail lines, and the German retreat. Many families had little to eat other than what could be obtained on the black market, and queues for food were long. When the uprising began, residents built barricades from paving stones, furniture, and overturned vehicles, and fighting broke out around police headquarters on the Île de la Cité and other buildings, with civilians among the casualties. Rumors of what the German garrison would do spread rapidly, and many feared that the city would be destroyed.

The arrival of Leclerc's and the American troops on August 24 and 25 was followed by public celebration, including crowds in the streets and church bells ringing, but also by sniper fire on August 26 from remaining German or collaborationist forces, which frightened crowds at the Place de la Concorde and Notre-Dame. The days after the liberation brought different experiences for different people. Resistance members emerged from hiding and took public posts. Jews who had survived in hiding, many of them foreign-born, learned of the fate of deported relatives in the months that followed, a subject treated in Chapter 121. Women accused of consorting with Germans were humiliated in public, while many of those who had worked for the occupiers or profited from them were quietly absorbed into postwar life. Municipal workers had to restore transport and utilities, and food shortages continued through the following winter. Evidence comes from diaries, photographs, newsreels, police reports, and memoirs, and it often reflects the perspective of those who wrote or filmed rather than of the full range of the population.

Section 122.9

The Allied Advance toward Germany: Supply Lines, Ports, and Strategic Limits

After the destruction of German forces in Normandy the Allied armies advanced with a speed that astonished both sides. Paris was liberated on August 25, British armor reached Brussels on September 3, and the British 11th Armoured Division captured Antwerp on September 4, taking much of its port facilities intact. In a few weeks the Allies covered distances that planners had expected to cover in months, and some optimistic officers believed the war might end before Christmas. On September 1 Eisenhower assumed direct command of the Allied ground forces in France, a change that placed Montgomery, newly promoted to field marshal, under his authority and aroused British resentment, since it removed Montgomery from the role of overall ground commander.

The advance outran its supplies. The original plan had assumed a methodical advance to the Seine, with a pause to build up logistics, and the pursuit across France strained a system designed for a slower pace. Armies consumed vast quantities of fuel, ammunition, and food, which had to be brought from the Normandy beaches and from Cherbourg, more than 500 kilometers from the front by September. Railways had been destroyed by Allied bombing before the invasion and by German demolition, and while engineers rebuilt them, trucks had to fill the gap. The most famous response was the Red Ball Express, a one-way, closed-loop trucking route established in late August and continued until mid-November, which at its peak employed thousands of vehicles and carried several thousand tons of supplies daily. A large proportion of its drivers, perhaps about three quarters, were African American soldiers in segregated service units, whose labor was essential to the campaign but whose contribution was long underrecognized in a military that excluded most Black soldiers from combat roles. The system was also costly, as trucks consumed much of the fuel they carried, and it could not sustain all armies at their maximum rate of advance.

Antwerp illustrates the difference between possessing a port and operating it. The city lay some eighty kilometers from the sea on the Scheldt estuary, and the estuary's banks, including the Breskens pocket on the south side and the islands of South Beveland and Walcheren on the north, remained in German hands. German coastal guns and mines meant that Allied ships could not reach the docks until the approaches were cleared. The Germans, moreover, were allowed to withdraw much of their Fifteenth Army across the estuary, since the British did not at once press the advantage after seizing the city. Clearing the Scheldt was therefore a precondition for supplying the Allied armies in the north, but it did not receive the priority it required in September, as commanders concentrated on pushing forward.

The commanders disagreed about how to proceed. Montgomery urged a single concentrated thrust, under his command, through the Netherlands and into the Ruhr, the industrial heart of Germany, with priority in supplies, arguing that it could end the war quickly. Bradley and Patton wanted priority for their own advances toward the Saar and Frankfurt. Eisenhower rejected both extremes, preferring to advance on a broad front, keeping pressure on the Germans everywhere and avoiding the risk of a narrow thrust, while giving Montgomery limited priority for an attempt to cross the Rhine. Critics, including Montgomery, later argued that this decision prolonged the war, while defenders contended that the supply system could not support a single deep thrust and that a broad front preserved the Allied advantage in numbers and mobility. Historians generally find that no solution would have eliminated the logistical problem, which arose from the speed of the advance. Patton's Third Army, for example, was halted near Metz in September partly for lack of fuel, and then faced prolonged fighting at fortified positions.

German defenses regained cohesion behind prepared positions and shorter supply lines, assisted by deteriorating weather. The Allied pause reflected a combination of logistics, terrain and German resistance, not a simple loss of combat effectiveness. These constraints shaped the campaigns that followed.

Section 122.10

Operation Market Garden, the Scheldt, and the Struggle for the German Frontier

Montgomery's proposal to seize a crossing over the Lower Rhine before the Germans could recover took the form of Operation Market Garden, launched on September 17, 1944. The plan had two components. Market was an airborne operation by the First Allied Airborne Army, in which the American 101st Airborne Division was to seize bridges and roads near Eindhoven, the American 82nd Airborne Division the crossings at Grave and Nijmegen, and the British 1st Airborne Division, with the Polish 1st Independent Parachute Brigade, the bridge at Arnhem on the Lower Rhine. Garden was the ground advance of the British XXX Corps along a single road through the Dutch countryside, which was to link up with each airborne division in turn and establish a bridgehead beyond the Rhine from which Allied forces could outflank the Westwall and threaten the Ruhr.

The operation achieved partial success. The 101st secured much of its objective and the road north of Eindhoven, though German attacks cut the highway repeatedly. The 82nd captured the bridge at Grave and, after a costly and daring assault crossing of the Waal river in boats on September 20, the great bridge at Nijmegen. But at Arnhem the British paratroopers, dropped some distance from the bridge, met stronger resistance than expected, including elements of II SS Panzer Corps that were refitting nearby, and only a small force reached the northern end of the bridge, where it was cut off. Radios failed, aircraft could not provide effective support in poor weather, and XXX Corps was delayed by the single road and German counterattacks. The British 1st Airborne was withdrawn across the river on the night of September 25 to 26, after losing thousands of men killed, wounded, and captured, with commonly cited figures indicating that only a fraction of the roughly 10,000 who landed returned. The Allies held a salient in the Netherlands but had failed to secure the intended bridgehead.

Intelligence and assumptions played their part. Allied planners had received indications of German armored units near Arnhem, and some officers voiced doubts, but the plan went ahead on the assumption that German resistance would be weak and that the ground forces would advance rapidly. Logistical limits also meant that the airborne divisions could be delivered only in successive lifts over several days. Historians dispute how far the failure reflected an inherently risky plan, as opposed to specific errors in execution, and there is general agreement that the operation required many things to go well in a short time. Dutch civilians paid a heavy price. A railway strike called by the Dutch government in exile to aid the operation led to German reprisals, and the western Netherlands endured the Hunger Winter of 1944 to 1945, during which many thousands died of starvation and cold.

The failure of Market Garden left the more mundane but essential task of opening Antwerp. This was the campaign of the Scheldt, assigned to the First Canadian Army, which also included British, Polish, and other Allied formations. From early October Canadian divisions cleared the Breskens pocket against determined German resistance, then advanced along the isthmus to South Beveland in flooded and mined terrain. The final stage was the assault on the island of Walcheren, whose coastal batteries covered the estuary. After the RAF bombed the dikes, flooding much of the island, British commandos and Canadian and other forces landed on November 1 and fought for about a week, and major German resistance in the estuary ended on November 8. Minesweepers then cleared the channel, and the first convoy reached Antwerp on November 28. The delay between the capture of the city in September and the use of its port in late November shaped the supply situation of the Allied armies through the winter.

Aachen fell in October, while the Hürtgen Forest campaign consumed American casualties for limited territorial gains. By December Allied forces faced the Westwall along an extended front, confronting shortages of replacements and ammunition and a German army still able to mount resistance.

Historical Vocabulary
Airborne Operation, Bridgehead, Estuary, and Logistical Corridor

An airborne operation delivers troops by parachute and glider to seize objectives behind enemy lines, normally to be relieved by ground forces. A bridgehead is a defensible position seized on the far side of a river or obstacle, from which a force can build up and continue its advance. An estuary is the widening tidal mouth of a river, such as the Scheldt, where navigation depends on controlling both banks and clearing channels of mines. A logistical corridor is a route, usually a road or rail line, along which supplies and reinforcements move to an army. In Market Garden the single road served as both an axis of advance and a logistical corridor, and in the Scheldt the estuary determined whether Antwerp was usable. The terms matter because they explain why the possession of a city or a bridge could not by itself determine operational success.

Section 122.11

The Ardennes Offensive: Germany's Final Major Attack in the West

In the autumn of 1944 Hitler decided to launch a major counteroffensive in the west. His reasoning combined strategic hope and ideological conviction. He believed that the Anglo-American coalition was fragile, that a decisive blow could split the British from the Americans, and that a defeat in the west would free German forces for the east or lead the western powers to negotiate. The plan, code-named Wacht am Rhein and later Herbstnebel (Autumn Mist), called for an attack through the lightly held Ardennes, the wooded hill country of Belgium and Luxembourg through which German forces had advanced in 1940, across the Meuse and on to Antwerp, cutting the Allied armies in two and depriving them of their port. German generals, including Rundstedt and Model, thought the goals unrealistic and proposed more limited objectives, but Hitler insisted.

The Germans achieved surprise. They concentrated some twenty-odd divisions, including the Sixth SS Panzer Army, the Fifth Panzer Army, and the Seventh Army, with the help of strict radio silence, movement by night, and the belief of Allied intelligence that the Germans were too weak to attack. Allied signals intelligence, which had been so valuable, was less useful because the Germans used landlines and couriers. The offensive began on December 16 in the early morning under a heavy cloud cover that kept Allied aircraft grounded, striking thinly held American positions, including several inexperienced divisions. The Germans penetrated deeply, creating the bulge in the Allied lines that gave the battle its popular name.

Allied resistance was uneven at first but then solidified. American units, often in small groups, delayed the German advance at key points, notably at St. Vith, at Elsenborn Ridge and the twin villages of Rocherath and Krinkelt, and at the crossroads town of Bastogne, which was surrounded from about December 20. Its defenders, the 101st Airborne Division reinforced by elements of an armored division, held out despite shortages of ammunition and medical supplies. Eisenhower moved reserves and placed the northern shoulder of the bulge under Montgomery's temporary command, a decision that was controversial among Americans but practical, since German advances had cut communications between Bradley's headquarters and the northern armies. Patton redirected the Third Army northward with unusual speed, and his 4th Armored Division broke through to Bastogne on December 26. From December 23 clearing weather allowed Allied aircraft to attack German columns and supply lines, and the Germans, lacking fuel, found their armored spearheads stalled. Many tanks were abandoned for want of gasoline, and the plan to capture Allied fuel depots had failed.

The offensive involved documented atrocities. On December 17 soldiers of a German battle group associated with the Waffen-SS killed about eighty American prisoners of war near Malmedy, an event that became known as the Malmedy massacre, and German units also killed Belgian civilians in several villages. The crimes, which resembled those committed elsewhere by SS formations, reinforced Allied views of the nature of the enemy and were later the subject of war crimes trials, as discussed in Chapter 123. Belgian and Luxembourg civilians suffered displacement, destruction, and shelling in what was for many a second invasion in four years.

German and Allied casualties are estimated differently, and figures should be treated as approximate. Commonly cited estimates suggest that American casualties, including killed, wounded, and missing, were in the vicinity of 80,000 to 90,000, with about 19,000 killed, which made the Ardennes the costliest single battle in American military history. German casualties are often estimated at between about 60,000 and 100,000, together with the loss of hundreds of tanks and many aircraft, notably in the Luftwaffe's large but costly attack on Allied airfields on January 1, 1945. The Allies, however, could replace their losses, and the Germans could not. The fighting continued into January, and January 25 is commonly used as the closing date of the battle, although other dates are also used by historians.

Allied commanders, surprised by the offensive, recovered quickly, but the Germans had no realistic means of capturing Antwerp. The operation exhausted the fuel, tanks, and trained personnel that might have been used to defend the Rhine or the eastern front, and it brought the Allied armies into a more compact line for the invasion of Germany. The Ardennes offensive thus did not reverse Germany's strategic decline. It accelerated it, even though it did delay Allied operations by some weeks and showed that German resistance would be prolonged and bitter.

Section 122.12

The Eastern and Southeastern European Fronts in Late 1944

The Allied achievements in the west took place against the background of the Red Army's summer and autumn offensives, which Chapter 119 treated in detail. Operation Bagration, begun on June 22 and 23, destroyed Army Group Centre, and the Soviet advance in the Baltic region, in Belarus, and in Ukraine drove German forces back to the Vistula and the Carpathians. This section is concerned not with those operations but with their consequences for Germany's strategic position in the second half of 1944. The Germans had to rush reserves eastward, could not reinforce Normandy, and lost hundreds of thousands of men. Soviet superiority in armor, artillery, aircraft, and transport, built on the production described in Section 122.3, was joined by improved planning, deception, and coordination between fronts.

In southeastern Europe, Soviet advances shifted the alignment of German allies. On August 23, 1944, King Michael of Romania, supported by opposition politicians and military officers, arrested the dictator Ion Antonescu and announced Romania's withdrawal from the war with Germany. Romania then declared war on Germany, and Romanian forces fought alongside the Red Army in Transylvania and Hungary. The change cost Germany the Ploiești oil fields, its principal source of natural petroleum, and exposed the Balkans. Bulgaria, which had not been at war with the Soviet Union, saw the Soviet declaration of war on September 5, followed by a coup on September 9 that brought a Fatherland Front government to power and moved Bulgaria into the Allied camp. Finland signed an armistice with the Soviet Union on September 19, leaving Germany without its northern partner. In Yugoslavia the Partisan movement under Josip Broz Tito, supported by Soviet and Bulgarian forces, liberated Belgrade on October 20, though Tito's forces also fought alongside rival movements in a civil conflict. The Germans withdrew from Greece and southern Yugoslavia, abandoning positions they could no longer supply.

Hungary, which had been a German ally, was occupied by German forces in March 1944. When Regent Miklós Horthy sought to leave the war in mid-October, the Germans forced him out and installed Ferenc Szálasi and the Arrow Cross movement, whose rule brought a new wave of violence against Budapest's Jewish population, as discussed in Chapter 121. The Red Army, with Romanian participation, reached the outskirts of Budapest by late 1944, and the city was encircled in late December, beginning a siege that would last until February. Hitler diverted substantial armored reserves to the relief of Budapest even as his forces were needed elsewhere, a decision that illustrates his priorities.

The relationship between military operations and political authority was complex. In Poland, the Soviet advance to the Vistula in the summer of 1944 coincided with the Warsaw Uprising of the Polish Home Army, which began on August 1 and lasted until October 2. The Soviet armies halted on the east bank of the Vistula and gave little effective assistance, for reasons that historians debate, including exhaustion and the German counterattack, and Soviet political hostility to the London-aligned Home Army. The Germans suppressed the rising and destroyed much of Warsaw. Meanwhile, the Soviet-backed Polish Committee of National Liberation had been established at Lublin in July, creating a rival authority that the western Allies had to consider. In Romania and Bulgaria, Soviet forces were followed by Allied control commissions, and in Moscow in October 1944 Churchill and Stalin discussed informal spheres of influence in the Balkans, a conversation whose meaning has been much debated. The Allied governments differed over Poland's future frontiers and government, and in the Slovak National Uprising of August 1944, the Warsaw Uprising, and elsewhere, the question was not only how the German army would be defeated but who would govern afterward.

For Germany, the cumulative effect was a collapse of its southeastern and northern flanks. It lost resources, oil, territory, and allies, and its defensive positions had to be rebuilt from depleted forces. By the end of 1944 the Soviet armies had reached the Vistula, the Carpathians, and the outskirts of Budapest, and the Baltic States were largely lost, with German forces cut off in Courland. The tensions among Soviet strategic priorities, Polish sovereignty, Western Allied interests, and the emerging political future of eastern Europe were the background to the Yalta Conference and to the Cold War examined in Volume VI.

The 1945 Soviet campaigns are examined next; Chapter 119 covers Soviet operations through 1944 in detail.

Section 122.13

Crossing the Rhine: The Western Allied Offensive into Germany

The Rhine was Germany's last great natural barrier in the west, a wide, fast river with few bridges and a long record as a strategic frontier. Behind it lay the Ruhr, the industrial region whose coal and steel had sustained German armament. After the Ardennes, the Allies resumed their advance in February 1945 by first clearing the land west of the river, the Rhineland. In Operation Veritable, begun on February 8, British and Canadian forces of the First Canadian Army attacked southeast from the Nijmegen area through the Reichswald forest, fighting in flooded and muddy terrain, and in Operation Grenade, begun on February 23, the American Ninth Army attacked from the Roer. By early March the Germans had been driven back to the river, losing tens of thousands of men as prisoners, and the retreating forces blew up bridges as they went. The Allies' supply system, now supported by Antwerp, was able to sustain the offensive.

On March 7, 1945, a unit of the American 9th Armored Division discovered that the Ludendorff railway bridge at Remagen had not been destroyed. German demolition charges had failed to bring it down completely, and American soldiers crossed under fire, establishing a bridgehead on the east bank. The capture was not part of a major plan, and Eisenhower, who recognized its value, authorized reinforcement of the bridgehead. The bridge collapsed on March 17, but by then pontoon bridges had been built, and the position tied down German reserves. Hitler responded by having several officers held responsible for the failure tried and executed, an illustration of the regime's reliance on terror in the last months. The Remagen crossing should be distinguished from the larger, coordinated assaults later in the month, which were the product of detailed preparation.

The principal crossing in the north, Operation Plunder, was planned by Montgomery's 21st Army Group with the American Ninth Army and was launched on the night of March 23 to 24. It involved a massive artillery bombardment, the use of amphibious vehicles and assault boats, and a supporting airborne landing, Operation Varsity, in which the British 6th and American 17th Airborne Divisions were dropped east of the river on March 24, in daylight and close to the ground forces, a lesson learned from Arnhem. Though casualties were heavy in the airborne units, the bridgeheads were quickly linked and expanded. Further south, Patton's Third Army made a surprise crossing at Oppenheim on the night of March 22, and the American First and Seventh Armies and the French First Army, which crossed in the Speyer area at the end of the month, established more bridgeheads. The Allies, in short, crossed the Rhine at multiple locations, and no single German response could contain them all.

The Allied armies then encircled the Ruhr. American armies advancing from the south and north joined near Lippstadt on April 1, trapping Army Group B, under Walter Model, in a pocket that included the Ruhr industrial region. German resistance in the pocket ended around April 18, with more than 300,000 prisoners reported by Allied commanders, and Model committed suicide on April 21. Meanwhile other armies drove into central and southern Germany, taking Kassel, Nuremberg, Leipzig, and other cities in April, and the British advanced to the Elbe and toward Hamburg and the Baltic. Decisions on the final objectives were influenced by political as well as military considerations. Eisenhower decided in late March to direct the main advance toward Leipzig and Dresden and to stop at the Elbe rather than race for Berlin, partly because the Soviets were closer to the capital, partly because of anticipated casualties, and partly because the zones of occupation had been agreed upon. His message to Stalin about his plans, sent directly, annoyed Churchill and British officials, who thought the capital's political importance should have been weighed more heavily.

The nature of German resistance changed. In many sectors units surrendered en masse, and white flags appeared in towns, but fanatical local defenses persisted, particularly where SS or Nazi Party officials enforced fighting, and mobile courts-martial hanged soldiers and civilians accused of defeatism. Allied troops advancing into Germany found cities devastated by bombing and artillery, ministries and factories without power, and roads filled with refugees, prisoners of war, and foreign forced laborers, many of whom had been liberated and sought food and transport home. Allied policy on contact with the German population was initially restrictive, with a non-fraternization order, and soldiers who had been told of the nature of Nazism met people who often claimed they had known nothing. Evidence for the experience of this period comes from military records, letters and diaries of soldiers, German civilian diaries, and photographs, which together show a society in collapse.

Section 122.14

The Soviet Vistula–Oder Offensive and the Battle for Eastern Germany

The Soviet winter offensive began on January 12, 1945, when Ivan Konev's 1st Ukrainian Front broke out of the Sandomierz bridgehead, followed on January 14 by Georgy Zhukov's 1st Belorussian Front, attacking from bridgeheads south of Warsaw. Soviet accounts later asserted that the offensive was advanced to aid the Western Allies during the Ardennes fighting, but historians contest the extent of any acceleration, given months of planning, operational readiness and winter weather. A mass of artillery, tanks, and infantry broke through thinly held German defenses, the German command having moved reserves to Hungary and denied Army Group A permission to withdraw. In a short time the Soviet armored columns advanced up to thirty kilometers a day, and Warsaw fell on January 17, Kraków on January 19, and Łódź soon after. Within three weeks the Red Army had advanced some 500 kilometers, seized Silesia's industrial region, and established bridgeheads on the Oder, in places less than eighty kilometers from Berlin. The Soviet forces were extremely large, numbering well over a million men, with overwhelming superiority in guns and armor.

Soviet success rested on more than numbers. Commanders such as Zhukov and Konev used concentrated artillery barrages, careful deception, and combined-arms operations, and the Red Army had learned lessons from earlier campaigns in the conduct of deep operations, the exploitation of breakthroughs by mobile formations. Supplies were brought forward by trucks, many American, and by railways whose gauge had to be converted. German defenses were weakened by the diversion of reserves and by shortages of fuel and ammunition. Soviet casualties nevertheless remained heavy, and the advance had left flanks exposed, particularly in Pomerania, where Zhukov was compelled to halt the drive on Berlin to deal with a threat from the north. In February the front stabilized along the Oder and Neisse. Meanwhile Konstantin Rokossovsky's 2nd Belorussian Front and other forces attacked in East Prussia, trapping German forces, and Königsberg, then the capital of East Prussia and now Kaliningrad, fell on April 9 after a bitter siege. Budapest, besieged since late December, fell on February 13 after a fight in which both soldiers and civilians suffered terribly, and the Soviet offensive in Austria brought the capture of Vienna on April 13.

The advance had devastating consequences for the German civilian population of the east, with effects that complicate any simple account of liberation. Fear of the Red Army was fed by Nazi propaganda, but it also had real foundations: German atrocities in the Soviet Union and Poland had been immense, Soviet troops had seen the devastation, and in October 1944 the East Prussian village of Nemmersdorf had been briefly taken by Soviet forces, an episode that propaganda exaggerated but that also involved real killings. Nazi officials had delayed evacuations, regarding them as defeatist, and in January 1945 millions of civilians fled in freezing conditions, on roads clogged with military traffic, or across the Baltic by sea in a large evacuation. The sinking of the Wilhelm Gustloff by a Soviet submarine on January 30 killed roughly 9,000 people, most of them civilians, in one of the largest maritime disasters in history. Many other civilians died in the cold, from exhaustion, and in the fighting.

Soviet troops also committed widespread violence against civilians, including killing, looting, and sexual violence. Estimates of the number of women raped in Germany vary greatly and are uncertain, with historians offering figures from several hundred thousand to far higher, and the issue was long suppressed in Soviet and, later, in some German memory. These acts were committed by individuals and units, and Soviet authorities tried at times to restrain them, though with limited consistency. Soviet soldiers also committed violence against Poles and other populations, and the ethnic German expulsions that followed are examined in Chapter 123. None of this alters the historical significance of the Red Army's victory over Nazi Germany, which ended a regime responsible for genocide and a war of annihilation in the east, but historians insist on distinguishing the crimes of individuals and units from the overall character of that victory, and on distinguishing the suffering of German civilians from the responsibility of the regime that had started the war.

The Soviet advance had political as well as military results. Soviet armies occupied Poland, Romania, Hungary, much of Czechoslovakia, and the eastern part of Germany, and their presence shaped the political order that followed. At the Yalta Conference in February, the leaders confirmed zones of occupation in Germany and made declarations about free elections in liberated Europe, but the reality on the ground favored the Soviet Union. The advance therefore produced both the defeat of Nazi rule and the foundations of new Soviet influence in central and eastern Europe, a pattern studied further in Chapters 124 and 125.

Historical Vocabulary
Operational Depth, Offensive, Encirclement, and Strategic Breakthrough

Operational depth refers to the Soviet doctrine of striking not only at the enemy's front line but at reserves, communications, and rear areas in succession, using mobile forces to exploit a breach. An offensive, in Soviet usage, was a large-scale operation by one or more fronts, planned over weeks, rather than a single battle. Encirclement is the surrounding of enemy formations so that they are cut off from supply and retreat, as at Falaise, the Ruhr, and Berlin. A strategic breakthrough occurs when an attacker penetrates the entire defensive system and can advance freely, as the Red Army did in January 1945. These terms matter because they explain the difference between gaining ground and defeating an enemy's army, and why the Vistula–Oder offensive achieved results in weeks that earlier operations had taken months to approach.

Section 122.15

The Battle of Berlin: Encirclement, Urban Warfare, and Hitler's Final Days

The Battle of Berlin began on April 16, 1945, when Zhukov's 1st Belorussian Front attacked across the Oder toward the Seelow Heights, the last defensive line before the capital, while Konev's 1st Ukrainian Front crossed the Neisse to the south. Stalin had set the two commanders in competition, and the boundary between their fronts was drawn so that Konev could swing north toward Berlin if Zhukov's advance faltered. The German defenders included the Ninth Army and the remains of other formations, supplemented by police units, SS, Hitlerjugend, and the Volkssturm, the militia of older men and boys. The forces were unequal: the Soviets had roughly two and a half million men in the operation, with thousands of tanks and guns, while the Germans had perhaps a million in the broader region, many poorly equipped. Zhukov's attack on the Seelow Heights, which opened with a huge artillery bombardment and the use of searchlights, was slowed by the terrain, German fortified positions, and congestion, and the heights were taken only after several days of costly fighting. Konev's forces advanced more quickly to the south and west.

By April 25 the Soviet fronts had encircled Berlin and the Ninth Army had been cut off to the southeast. The city then became a battlefield. Defenders fought from buildings, cellars, and subway tunnels, using Panzerfaust antitank weapons against Soviet armor, and the Soviets responded with massed artillery and assault groups of infantry and tanks that fought from building to building. The fighting included the heavily defended Tiergarten, government quarter, and the Reichstag, which Soviet troops reached on April 30, raising flags there on April 30 and May 1, a moment of great propaganda value. Civilians, numbering in the millions, sheltered in cellars and bunkers with scant food and water, as the supply of electricity, gas, and water failed. Thousands died in the shelling and street fighting, and thousands more committed suicide, and sexual violence by Soviet soldiers was widespread. German forces also killed civilians who tried to surrender, hanging some from lampposts. Soviet losses were high, with commonly cited figures of more than 80,000 killed and over 300,000 casualties in the Berlin operation as a whole, though precise numbers remain disputed.

Hitler remained in the bunker beneath the Reich Chancellery garden, increasingly detached from reality. On April 20 he celebrated his fifty-sixth birthday, and on April 22 in a meeting he acknowledged that the war was lost, but still ordered attacks by units that did not exist, such as Steiner's army detachment, having ordered the destruction of infrastructure in the Nero Decree of March, which Speer and others partly evaded. Hermann Göring and Heinrich Himmler sought separate contacts with the Allies, and Hitler dismissed both from their offices. On April 29 he dictated a political testament in which he blamed the Jews for the war and designated Karl Dönitz as president and Joseph Goebbels as chancellor, and married Eva Braun. On April 30, as Soviet troops fought nearby, Hitler killed himself, and Braun also died by suicide. Goebbels and his wife killed their six children and themselves on May 1.

The encirclement of Berlin should be distinguished from its surrender. General Hans Krebs attempted to negotiate on May 1, but Soviet commanders demanded unconditional surrender, and fighting continued. On May 2 General Helmuth Weidling, commander of the Berlin Defense Area, ordered his troops to lay down their arms, and the garrison surrendered, though some units fought on or tried to break out to the west. This ended organized resistance in the capital but not the war. German forces still held areas of northern and southern Germany, Czechoslovakia, Austria, and Norway, and Soviet and Allied forces were still engaged elsewhere. The strategic importance of Berlin's fall was considerable, as it eliminated the seat of the regime and symbolized defeat, though the importance of the city for the military outcome was less than its symbolic value. It also became a source of controversy, with arguments, both then and later, over whether the western Allies should have tried to take it.

Section 122.16

The Allied Convergence: The Elbe, Concentration Camp Liberation, and the End of Organized Resistance

On April 25, 1945, patrols of the American 69th Infantry Division and the Soviet 58th Guards Rifle Division met at Torgau on the Elbe, with earlier contacts at nearby Strehla, and the meeting was celebrated in both countries as a symbol of cooperation. Its military significance was practical: the German-held territory was split into northern and southern pockets, and neither could be effectively coordinated by the remains of the high command. The linkup had been anticipated by Allied commanders, who arranged recognition signals, and the Elbe was agreed upon as a line to avoid accidental clashes. Within days other Allied forces advanced to the Baltic at Wismar, where British troops reached the coast shortly before the Soviets, closing off Denmark and the Schleswig-Holstein area, and the American Third and Seventh Armies advanced into Bavaria and Austria, and into Czechoslovakia in the west. The meeting at Torgau also marks the beginning of the division of Germany into zones, a process examined in Chapter 124.

The advancing armies also encountered the camps. The Soviet army had come upon Majdanek in July 1944 and Auschwitz on January 27, 1945, as examined in Chapter 121. In the west, American forces reached Buchenwald on April 11, 1945, shortly after SS guards had begun to evacuate prisoners, and the British entered Bergen-Belsen on April 15, followed by the Americans at Dachau on April 29 and Mauthausen on May 5, and the Soviets at Ravensbrück and Sachsenhausen in late April, while Theresienstadt was liberated on May 8. The soldiers who arrived, few of whom had been prepared, found dead and dying prisoners, unburied bodies, and evidence of starvation, disease, forced labor, and murder. They also found survivors, among them Jews, Roma, political prisoners, prisoners of war, and others, some of whom had been driven on death marches, which are discussed in Chapter 121. Allied commanders ordered photographs, film, and eyewitness testimony to be recorded, and at some camps civilians from nearby towns were taken to see the sites, in part to prevent later denial. These records became important evidence for postwar trials and for the historical understanding of the crimes.

Liberation did not end suffering. At Bergen-Belsen, the memorial estimates that some 53,000 prisoners remained when British troops arrived on April 15, 1945. Disease and starvation were widespread, and approximately 14,000 liberated prisoners died from the effects of their imprisonment by June 1945, despite organized medical relief. Prisoners who were given large quantities of rich food sometimes suffered further harm, as their weakened bodies could not digest it, and medical staff developed more careful feeding routines. The armies had not been organized to care for such numbers, and relief depended on improvised measures, army medical units, and eventually on civilian organizations. Many survivors faced questions of where to go, since their homes, families, and communities had been destroyed, and they were housed in the same camps or in displaced persons centers, a problem treated in Chapter 123. Liberation, therefore, was a military event, followed by a long process of recovery that many did not complete.

Boundary note: Chapter 121 examines the Holocaust, the death marches, and the camp system systematically. This section addresses the encounter of advancing armies with these crimes and the emergency problems they posed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Military Liberation, Civilian Survival, and Emergency Administration

What changed?

  • SS authority ended and prisoners were no longer subject to guards, forced labor, or selection for death.
  • Armies, which had prepared for combat, were compelled to assume medical, administrative, and humanitarian tasks.
  • Visual and testimonial records of the camps began to circulate, shaping public knowledge and later trials.

What remained continuous?

  • Hunger, disease, and exhaustion continued to kill thousands of survivors after the guards had gone.
  • Survivors faced the loss of families, property, and communities, and uncertainty about where they could go.
  • The wider war continued, so that military priorities often competed with relief efforts.

Who experienced change most directly? Camp survivors who could be treated and moved, and soldiers who encountered the camps. Who experienced greater continuity? The sickest prisoners, those who had no home to return to, and, in some cases, those who were moved from one camp to another in the confusion of the last weeks.

Why the pattern matters: The distinction between liberation as an event and survival as a process prevents students from treating the arrival of armies as the end of the story, and it connects the military history of this chapter to the displaced-persons crisis and postwar reckoning of Chapter 123.

The Collapse of Nazi Germany and the End of the European War

1945. How the Nazi state disintegrated, Germany capitulated, and Europe entered an uncertain postwar order.

Section 122.17

The Disintegration of Nazi Rule: Military Collapse and Political Breakdown

By early 1945 the German state was losing the material basis of war. The Allied advance had overrun the Rhineland, the Silesian industrial region, and the eastern provinces, and the air offensive had crippled fuel production and rail transport. Coal could no longer be delivered to factories, finished weapons accumulated at plants for want of transport, and units at the front lacked fuel, ammunition, and replacements. Divisions existed on paper with a fraction of their nominal strength, and the army relied on improvised formations, headquarters staff, and rear-area personnel thrown into the line. Productive capacity did not fall everywhere at the same time, which helps explain why the war continued, but the system on which it depended was breaking down.

The regime responded by widening compulsion. The Volkssturm, a national militia established in autumn 1944 and directed through the Nazi Party, called up men of sixteen to sixty who were not already in service, and organizations including the Hitler Youth sent adolescents into antiaircraft batteries and the front line. Such formations were poorly armed and trained, and many suffered heavy casualties in the fighting, notably in Berlin and the east. Violence against Germans themselves increased. Decrees in early 1945 established summary courts-martial in threatened areas, and thousands of soldiers and civilians were executed or hanged for desertion or defeatism, sometimes by officers or party officials acting on their own initiative, sometimes on the basis of placards hung around the necks of the dead. Officials also attempted to implement scorched-earth policies, though Speer and others, as well as many local officials and officers, evaded or modified them to preserve infrastructure for postwar survival. Himmler's SS also continued to murder prisoners and evacuated camps in forced marches, while in the last weeks Nazi leaders spoke of resistance to the end, of wonder weapons, and of a final redoubt in the Alps that existed largely in propaganda.

German responses were varied, and no single image captures them. Some soldiers and civilians continued to fight out of ideological commitment, fear of Soviet vengeance, loyalty to comrades, or coercion. Others surrendered at the first opportunity, particularly to western forces, and large numbers deserted or tried to make their way home. Many civilians fled westward, hoping to reach the American or British lines instead of the Soviet, and many tried to protect their families by hanging white sheets from windows, despite the risk of reprisals. Isolated acts of opposition occurred, such as an uprising in Bavaria on April 28 by a group of officers and civilians who tried to surrender Munich, which was put down with killings. The July 1944 plot to assassinate Hitler had been suppressed and its participants executed, and the subsequent purge discouraged further attempts. The evidence of diaries, letters, and postwar testimony indicates that German society was neither universally loyal to Hitler nor universally opposed to Nazism, and that responses depended on region, age, class, proximity to the front, and the pressure of the regime's terror.

At the top, authority dissolved. Hitler's suicide on April 30 left the question of succession, and under his political testament Admiral Karl Dönitz became head of state and was based first at Plön and then at Flensburg, near the Danish border, with a cabinet led by Lutz Graf Schwerin von Krosigk. The Dönitz government had no real power. Its aim was to end the war in a way that allowed as many German soldiers and civilians as possible to reach the western Allies, and to preserve some semblance of a German government, goals that conflicted with the Allied demand for unconditional surrender on all fronts. The government was dissolved and its members arrested on May 23. The collapse of the regime thus involved both military defeat and a political breakdown in which the institutions of the party and state ceased to function.

Section 122.18

Germany's Unconditional Surrender: Reims, Berlin, and Victory in Europe

The surrender of Germany took place in stages. In Italy, representatives of the German commander, Heinrich von Vietinghoff, signed an instrument at Caserta on April 29, effective on May 2. On May 4, at Lüneburg Heath, Admiral Hans-Georg von Friedeburg signed the surrender to Montgomery of German forces in northwestern Germany, the Netherlands, and Denmark, effective the next morning, and on May 5 the commander of Army Group G surrendered in southern Germany and Austria. These were local or regional capitulations by commanders in the field, concluded at army-group level and distinct from a general German surrender.

The Dönitz government tried to surrender selectively. Friedeburg went to Eisenhower's headquarters at Reims, in northeastern France, on May 5 to offer the capitulation of forces facing the western Allies while continuing to fight the Soviets, so as to evacuate soldiers and refugees from the east. Eisenhower refused, insisting on a simultaneous surrender of all German forces to all Allied powers and threatening to close the western front to Germans fleeing the Soviets. He held that the Allies had agreed not to make a separate peace, and had communicated with the Soviet high command. After Dönitz recognized that no alternative existed, he authorized General Alfred Jodl, the chief of the operations staff of the Armed Forces High Command, to sign a general capitulation.

The act of military surrender was signed at Reims on May 7, 1945, at 2:41 a.m., in a school building that housed Eisenhower's headquarters. Jodl signed for the German High Command, and Walter Bedell Smith, Eisenhower's chief of staff, for the Supreme Command of the Allied Expeditionary Force, while Ivan Susloparov signed on behalf of the Soviet High Command and French General François Sevez signed as witness. Soviet authorities subsequently said that Susloparov had not been authorized to conclude the agreement. The instrument provided that all forces under German control would cease active operations at 23:01 Central European Time on May 8, a delay that Eisenhower's headquarters accepted to allow word to reach all units. Soviet leaders, who had not been fully consulted and regarded Berlin as the proper place for the capitulation of Germany, objected to a ceremony conducted in the west. A second signing was therefore arranged in Berlin, at Karlshorst, on the evening of May 8, in the presence of Zhukov, the British air marshal Arthur Tedder representing Eisenhower, and French and American representatives. Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel, with Friedeburg and a Luftwaffe officer, signed for Germany. By Moscow time it was already May 9 when the document was completed.

These facts account for the different dates of commemoration. Churchill and Truman proclaimed Victory in Europe on May 8, and May 8 became VE Day in Britain, the United States, and France, and in many other countries. The Soviet Union announced the victory on May 9, and Russia and several successor states observe Victory Day on that date, while some post-Soviet states have changed their practices in more recent years. The differences also reflect different memories of the war. The documents must be treated as historical evidence rather than anecdote: they show who was authorized to sign, which forces were bound, and how the Allies defined the end of hostilities, and variations in the texts, languages, and times show how coalition politics shaped the ending.

The surrender did not mean that every shot was fired on May 8. German units in Czechoslovakia, notably Army Group Centre under Ferdinand Schörner, continued to fight into May 11 against Soviet and Czech forces, and the Prague uprising that began on May 5 was supported by dissident German formations and the Soviet advance. Isolated groups resisted elsewhere, and weapons clearance and the return of prisoners took months. The capitulation of the armed forces was also different from the establishment of political authority. The Allied governments formally assumed supreme authority in Germany on June 5, 1945, in a separate declaration, which belongs to the discussion of occupation in Chapter 123, and the war with Japan, in which the Allies were still engaged, continued until September 1945.

Section 122.19

The Human Cost of Victory: Destruction, Displacement, and the Immediate Crisis

The end of the fighting revealed the scale of destruction across Europe, though it cannot be measured with precision. Historians commonly estimate that the Soviet Union lost on the order of 25 to 27 million people, including both military and civilian dead, a very large share of all European war deaths, and Poland lost roughly five to six million, including about three million Polish Jews murdered in the Holocaust. Rüdiger Overmans' demographic study estimates approximately 5.3 million German military deaths, including deaths in captivity and missing personnel reconstructed as dead. Estimates of German civilian deaths are less directly comparable: bombing and fighting during the war must be distinguished from the losses associated with flight and forced expulsion during and after 1945. These totals are estimates and each depends on who is counted, and students should keep apart battlefield casualties, civilian deaths, victims of genocide, deaths in captivity, and demographic losses from lowered births and migration.

Cities lay in ruins. Many German cities, including Cologne, Hamburg, Dresden, Berlin, and Würzburg, had lost a large share of their housing, and a substantial fraction of the country's dwellings was destroyed or damaged. Rail lines, bridges, ports, and canals were broken, and water supply and sanitation systems failed in many urban areas. Elsewhere, towns in Normandy, the Netherlands, Poland, Belorussia, and Ukraine had been wrecked, and in western Europe famine conditions affected the Netherlands in the Hunger Winter and parts of Greece and Italy. Food production and distribution had collapsed in many regions. Public health was precarious, with outbreaks of typhus, tuberculosis, and venereal disease, and hospitals were overwhelmed.

Population movement was vast. At the war's end, Allied authorities estimated that millions of foreign forced laborers, prisoners, and concentration camp survivors were in Germany, and they struggled to feed, house, and repatriate them. Millions of German soldiers became prisoners of war, held in camps in the west, in the Soviet Union, and elsewhere, some for years. Germans also moved in great numbers, as refugees from the east, as evacuees from bombed cities, or as people searching for relatives, and the flight and expulsion of ethnic Germans from eastern Europe, which was already beginning, is a major subject of Chapter 123. Women, children, and the elderly made up much of the civilian population in the ruined cities, as many men were dead, wounded, or in captivity, and women cleared rubble, bartered for food, and cared for families, while families searched for missing members through notices, lists, and Red Cross tracing services.

Allied military authorities faced immediate emergency tasks, including clearing roads, restoring minimum utilities, distributing food, controlling epidemics, and keeping order, while still trying to complete the military occupation. Victory therefore did not restore normal life, and the effects of Nazi persecution continued in the lives of survivors, who had lost families and property. Some German civilians were also victims of the war's later stages, including bombing and Soviet violence. But the suffering of German civilians has to be understood in context. Nazi Germany had started a war of aggression, occupied and exploited much of Europe, and carried out genocide, and an account of the destruction of Germany that ignored those responsibilities, or portrayed Germans as victims only, would distort the historical record. It is equally important not to deny the reality of civilian suffering. The history of 1945 must hold both together.

Section 122.20

Why Germany Lost: Allied Victory and the Transformation of Europe

Explaining the German defeat requires attention to many factors interacting over time. The first was the sheer extent of Allied resources. The United States, Britain with its empire, and the Soviet Union together commanded much larger populations, industrial output, and access to raw materials than Germany and its allies, and the German economy could be strained to its limit only by exploiting occupied Europe and millions of forced laborers. By 1944 Allied production of aircraft, tanks, ships, and trucks far exceeded Germany's, and Allied control of the seas ensured that these resources could be moved. The advantage was not merely numerical. Allied production was increasingly integrated, and logistics, intelligence, and air power multiplied its effect.

The second factor was the land war in the east. The great majority of German military casualties in the war were inflicted by the Red Army, with many estimates placing the share on the Eastern Front at about two thirds to four fifths, and the destruction of German divisions at Stalingrad, Kursk, Belorussia, and in the winter offensive of 1945 depleted the army that might otherwise have defended the west. The Soviet contribution cannot be reduced to numbers. It rested on evacuated industry, Lend-Lease supplies, improved command, intelligence and deception, a ferocious commitment to defeating an enemy that had invaded and devastated the country, and enormous casualties, which were among the heaviest of any state. The western Allies contributed in other ways: by controlling the Atlantic, by the air offensive that destroyed fuel, transport, and the Luftwaffe, by the Mediterranean and Italian campaigns, and by opening a second front in France that, with the Soviet offensives of 1944, forced Germany to fight on multiple fronts. The relative importance of these contributions changed over time, and any attempt to compare them needs to consider manpower, casualties, industrial support, geography, and the period considered.

The third factor was German strategy and the nature of the regime. Hitler launched a war against powers whose combined resources he could not match, invaded the Soviet Union before defeating Britain, and declared war on the United States. His direction of military operations, including refusals to allow withdrawals, the diversion of reserves to peripheral objectives, and the Ardennes gamble, accelerated the loss of trained manpower and material. The regime's ideological priorities also shaped the war, including the pursuit of genocide while transport and manpower were scarce, the brutal occupation policies that fueled resistance in occupied territories, and the loss of potential allies. Germany lost its European partners in 1944, and by 1945 the army depended on conscripting the very young and the old. At the same time, German military professionalism, technology, and tactical skill meant that defeat required prolonged and costly Allied effort. Defeat was a product of strategic structure rather than inevitable from the outset, and Allied commanders at the time did not regard it as inevitable.

Victory shaped politics immediately. At Yalta, from February 4 to 11, 1945, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin agreed on the division of Germany into occupation zones, with a zone for France, on the joint control of Berlin, and on a Soviet declaration of war against Japan, and they issued a declaration on liberated Europe promising free elections, whose implementation would prove contested. Disagreements over Poland, reparations, and the interpretation of the declaration were already visible. The conference is treated further in Chapters 123 and 124. As Allied armies converged in the spring, differences over the treatment of Germany and eastern Europe grew, and Roosevelt's death on April 12 brought Harry Truman to the presidency in the last weeks. The coalition that had defeated Germany would not survive intact, though its collapse was not predetermined.

The war in Europe ended with Germany occupied and divided, much of the continent devastated, and millions dead, displaced, or bereaved. The Holocaust had destroyed most of the Jewish communities of central and eastern Europe, and the Roma and others had suffered terrible losses. Chapter 123 will examine displacement, reparations, trials, denazification, reconstruction, and memory in detail. Military victory had ended Nazi rule, but it left unresolved the problems of political authority, justice, and security, and it created the conditions for the division of Europe between the Soviet Union and the Western Allies.

Historical Debate

Which Factors Best Explain Germany's Defeat?

The question: Historians agree that Germany was defeated by the Allied coalition, but disagree about the relative weight of the factors that produced defeat, including Soviet land power, Allied economic strength, air and naval power, coalition coordination, and German strategic and ideological failures.

Interpretation A: The Eastern Front was decisive. Historians who study the Red Army, among them David Glantz and Jonathan House, emphasize that the bulk of the German army was engaged and destroyed in the east, and that Soviet operational learning, mobilization, and sacrifice, rather than western intervention, broke the German military. From this view the western campaigns, however important, operated against an army already bled white.
Interpretation B: Allied material and coalition power explains the outcome. Richard Overy, in Why the Allies Won, stresses the combination of economic capacity, air and naval superiority, technology, and the organization of production and fighting power across the coalition, in which each partner's contribution depended on the others, together with the Allies' ability to adapt. On this view the war was won by an integrated system rather than by one front.
Interpretation C: German strategy and the nature of the regime. Others, including Ian Kershaw in The End, examine why the German state continued to fight on until it was destroyed, pointing to the regime's structure, the leadership's refusal to accept defeat, terror, and the ideological commitments that shaped decisions. This emphasis explains the duration and destructiveness of the end, as well as the defeat itself.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on casualty statistics, production figures, operational records, intelligence, and German command documents. These are compatible rather than mutually exclusive, because most historians combine them while differing in emphasis. Each also depends on difficult measurement, since contributions overlapped: Soviet success relied on Lend-Lease supplies, the air offensive diverted German resources, and the second front affected the allocation of German reserves.

Why it matters: How historians weigh the factors affects how national memories of the war are constructed, how Soviet and western contributions are valued, and how strategic lessons are drawn. It also shapes whether defeat appears inevitable, or the product of decisions that could have been different.

Student task: Select two sections of this chapter and identify evidence that supports each of the three interpretations. Then explain which combination you find most persuasive, and what additional evidence, such as archival records of production, losses, or decision-making, would help resolve the disagreements.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 122 has argued that the defeat of Nazi Germany was the product of a coalition that, despite deep political differences, applied cumulative military, economic, and organizational pressure on interconnected fronts. The Grand Alliance of the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union committed itself to defeating Germany first, announced a policy of unconditional surrender at Casablanca in January 1943, and confirmed the plan for a cross-Channel invasion at Tehran in November and December of that year. Cooperation was real but never harmonious, and disagreements over strategy, Poland, and the shape of postwar Europe persisted throughout.

The Combined Bomber Offensive, the Battle of the Atlantic, and the vast production of the Allied economies created the conditions for invasion. The British area offensive and the American daylight campaign, whatever their moral and operational controversies, helped win air superiority, undermined German fuel and transport, and forced Germany to divert resources to defense. The defeat of the U-boats secured the Atlantic supply line, and the organization of shipping, landing craft, artificial harbours, and supply depots made the invasion of France possible. The chapter stressed that victory depended on sustaining armies as well as defeating enemies.

In the Mediterranean, the Italian campaign, fought by a multinational army, tied down German forces, produced the capture of Rome on June 4, 1944, and continued through the Gothic Line and the Po Valley until the German surrender in Italy took effect on May 2, 1945. It also drew Italians into civil conflict and brought heavy civilian suffering. The campaign should be assessed neither as a decisive route into central Europe nor as an irrelevant sideshow.

The invasion of Normandy on June 6, 1944 was the result of extensive planning, deception, and air and naval preparation. D-Day established a foothold at five beaches and by airborne landings but fell short of some first-day objectives, and the subsequent Battle of Normandy was long, costly, and uncertain until Operation Cobra in July and the Falaise encirclement in August destroyed much of the German force in the west. The landings in southern France in August, the liberation of Paris on August 25, and the restoration of French authority under de Gaulle completed the first phase of the return to the continent, though the political and social consequences of liberation remained unresolved.

The autumn of 1944 showed the limits of Allied speed. Supply lines, the unusable port of Antwerp, the failure at Arnhem in Market Garden, and the costly fighting at Aachen and in the Hürtgen Forest slowed the advance, while the Battle of the Scheldt opened the port only at the end of November. Germany's Ardennes offensive of December 16 achieved surprise but could not realistically reach Antwerp, and it consumed reserves that Germany could not replace. Meanwhile Soviet offensives in 1944 destroyed Army Group Centre, drove Romania, Bulgaria, and Finland out of the German alliance, and brought the Red Army to the Vistula, the Carpathians, and Budapest, with deep political consequences for eastern Europe.

The final campaigns of 1945 brought the collapse of the Reich. The Soviet Vistula–Oder offensive of January 12 carried the Red Army to the Oder, while the western Allies cleared the Rhineland, crossed the Rhine at Remagen and in Operation Plunder, and encircled the Ruhr. The Soviet assault on Berlin from April 16, the meeting at Torgau on April 25, and Hitler's suicide on April 30 destroyed the Nazi state, and the encounters of Allied armies with concentration camps exposed the scale of its crimes and the limits of military liberation. German civilians experienced bombing, flight, Nazi coercion, and Soviet violence, but these experiences must be understood in the context of the regime's responsibility for the war and the genocide.

The surrender was concluded in stages: regional capitulations in Italy, northwestern Germany, and Bavaria; the instrument signed at Reims at 2:41 a.m. on May 7, to take effect at 23:01 Central European Time on May 8; and the Berlin ceremony of May 8. These explain the different dates on which victory is commemorated. The war in Europe ended, but the war against Japan continued, and the capitulation of the armed forces was not yet the establishment of an Allied political order.

Assessments of why Germany lost must combine several factors: the great material strength of the Allies, the Red Army's destruction of the German land forces, western maritime and air power, coordination and logistics, German strategic overextension, and the nature of Hitler's regime. Historians differ over the relative weight of these factors, but not about their interaction. The chapter has also shown that the outcome was not inevitable, and that victory left Europe transformed by mass death, genocide, occupation, displacement, and the growing confrontation between former allies, themes carried forward in Chapters 123 and 124 and into Volume VI.

Glossary of Key Terms

The thirty terms below match the Core Vocabulary Preview in wording and sequence.

1. Grand Alliance
The wartime partnership of the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union against Germany, a coalition of necessity without a formal unified command.
2. Europe First strategy
The Anglo-American decision to defeat Germany before Japan, governing the allocation of the largest forces without ending the Pacific war.
3. Casablanca Conference
The Roosevelt–Churchill meeting of January 14 to 24, 1943, which set Mediterranean and air strategy and produced the unconditional surrender announcement.
4. Tehran Conference
The first meeting of Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin, held from November 28 to December 1, 1943, which confirmed the cross-Channel invasion for 1944.
5. Unconditional surrender
The Allied declaration that no negotiated armistice would be accepted with the Axis, distinct from the later written instruments by which Germany surrendered.
6. Combined Bomber Offensive
The coordinated Anglo-American air campaign against Germany ordered at Casablanca, combining British night attacks with American daylight raids.
7. Strategic bombing
Air attack aimed at an enemy's industrial, fuel, and transport systems in order to destroy war-making capacity, in doctrine directed at specific targets.
8. Area bombing
Attacks on whole urban districts, as practiced by RAF Bomber Command, aimed at destroying housing and disrupting industry and civilian life.
9. Battle of the Atlantic
The long struggle between Allied convoys and German submarines over the sea lanes between North America and Britain, decided in the Allies' favor in 1943.
10. Italian Campaign
The Allied military operations on the Italian mainland from September 1943 to May 1945, fought by a multinational army against German forces.
11. Gothic Line
The German defensive position across the northern Apennines, attacked from August 1944 and broken only in the spring offensive of 1945.
12. Operation Overlord
The overall Allied campaign to establish and expand a lodgement in northwestern Europe in 1944, including the Normandy battle.
13. Operation Neptune
The naval and amphibious assault phase of Overlord, covering the crossing of the Channel and the initial landings.
14. Operation Fortitude
The Allied deception plan that suggested a larger invasion at the Pas-de-Calais to mislead German commanders about Normandy.
15. Mulberry harbour
One of two prefabricated artificial harbours towed to Normandy to supply the beachheads, of which the British one at Arromanches remained in use.
16. D-Day
June 6, 1944, the first day of the Normandy landings, on which the Allies landed more than 150,000 troops by sea and air.
17. Airborne assault
The delivery of troops by parachute and glider behind enemy lines to seize objectives in advance of or in coordination with ground forces.
18. Bocage
The Norman countryside of small fields enclosed by earth banks and hedgerows, which favored German defenders and slowed Allied advances.
19. Operation Cobra
The American breakout offensive launched on July 25, 1944 near Saint-Lô, preceded by heavy bombing.
20. Falaise Pocket
The August 1944 encirclement in Normandy that destroyed or captured many German troops, though substantial numbers escaped.
21. Operation Dragoon
The Allied landing in southern France on August 15, 1944, which opened Mediterranean ports and involved large French forces.
22. SHAEF
Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force, the integrated Anglo-American command under Eisenhower for operations in northwestern Europe.
23. Red Ball Express
The one-way truck supply route operated from late August to mid-November 1944 to carry supplies to the advancing armies, manned largely by African American drivers.
24. Battle of the Scheldt
The fall 1944 campaign, chiefly by the First Canadian Army, to clear the estuary so Antwerp could be used as a port.
25. Operation Market Garden
The September 1944 combined airborne and ground attempt to seize bridges in the Netherlands and cross the Lower Rhine, which failed at Arnhem.
26. Siegfried Line (Westwall)
The German frontier fortifications along the western border of Germany, reinforced in autumn 1944.
27. Battle of the Bulge
The German Ardennes offensive beginning December 16, 1944, named for the salient it created, and generally regarded as ending in late January 1945.
28. Vistula–Oder Offensive
The Soviet offensive begun January 12, 1945, that advanced from Poland to the Oder River in about three weeks.
29. Battle of Berlin
The Soviet assault on the German capital from April 16, 1945, ending with the surrender of the garrison on May 2.
30. Victory in Europe (VE) Day
The commemoration of the end of the war in Europe, observed on May 8 in much of the West and on May 9 in the Soviet Union and several successor states.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 122 matters because it explains how the Second World War in Europe was won, and in doing so reveals that victory was a coalition process built from production, shipping, intelligence, air power, and the willingness of millions of soldiers to endure heavy losses. It also shows that the coalition was a partnership of states with different priorities, a fact that shaped the war's conduct and anticipated the political divisions that followed.

The chapter connects different regions of Europe. The same war that was fought on the Normandy beaches was fought on the Vistula and in the Balkans, in the Italian mountains and in the Dutch polders, and the experiences of French, Polish, Dutch, Italian, Romanian, Hungarian, Yugoslav, and German populations differed greatly. National traditions of memory have often separated these experiences, but the historical record requires them to be considered together.

In the larger argument of Volume V, the chapter completes the history of the military catastrophe that began in 1914. It shows how industrial warfare, ideological regimes, and imperial rivalries produced destruction of a scale that earlier European history had not known, and how the defeat of Nazism came at the price of mass death and displacement. The end of the war did not close that history, since the questions of justice, memory, and political order passed to Chapter 123.

The continuities that survived 1945 include the occupation of Germany, the division of the continent along the line where the armies met, and the memory of genocide and resistance. The new European order that emerged, examined in Volume VI, was neither the inevitable result of the war nor separable from it. Understanding how Germany was defeated is therefore a prerequisite for understanding how Europe was rebuilt and divided.
