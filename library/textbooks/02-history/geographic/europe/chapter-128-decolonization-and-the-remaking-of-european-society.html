Chapter Opening

In 1945, European states still ruled territories on every inhabited continent. By 1975, most of those territories had become independent states, and the former imperial powers had become home to millions of people whose families, languages, laws, and loyalties were bound up with the empires that had ended. Decolonization was not something that happened only overseas. It remade Europe.

On 22 June 1948, passengers disembarked from the former troopship Empire Windrush at Tilbury on the Thames. The ship had docked late the preceding evening, and the surviving passenger list records 1,027 passengers, more than 800 of whom gave a Caribbean location as their last place of residence, principally Jamaica. In the summer of 1962, in the months surrounding the end of the Algerian War, several hundred thousand people crossed the Mediterranean to French ports, most of them European settlers leaving the country in which they or their families had lived for generations. In 1975, in the aftermath of the Carnation Revolution, an air and sea evacuation brought hundreds of thousands of Portuguese nationals from Angola and Mozambique to Lisbon. These movements differed enormously in their causes, their legal circumstances, and the welcome that awaited the people involved. Yet each belongs to a single, connected historical process: the dismantling of European overseas empires and the consequences of that dismantling within Europe itself.

Chapter 127 examined the reconstruction of democratic government in Britain, France, Italy, and West Germany after 1945. This chapter explains a transformation of comparable significance. Within roughly three decades, European states lost much of their overseas imperial authority, while simultaneously becoming home to increasingly diverse populations connected to those former empires. The central question is how the dismantling of European colonial empires transformed European politics, citizenship, migration, national identity, and everyday society after World War II. The chapter argues that decolonization did not simply change political boundaries overseas. It changed the governments, armies, laws, cities, schools, workplaces, and public memories of Europe.

The chapter also insists on a principle of historical agency. Independence was not a gift conferred by European governments that had decided, after reflection, that empire had become unnecessary. Anticolonial parties, trade unions, armed movements, diplomats, intellectuals, soldiers, women's organizations, and ordinary people forced the issue, sometimes through negotiation, sometimes through prolonged war, and often through a combination of both. European governments responded unevenly. Some attempted to reform empire in order to preserve it; some fought long colonial wars; some negotiated hurriedly under pressure; and some were overtaken by revolutions at home. The routes to independence taken by India, Indonesia, Algeria, Congo, Kenya, and Angola were therefore very different, and so were the postcolonial societies that resulted in Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Portugal.

The consequences for Europe were profound but varied. The Algerian War brought down the Fourth Republic and shaped the institutions of the Fifth. Portugal's wars in Africa helped provoke a military revolution that ended Europe's longest-lived right-wing dictatorship. Britain's retreat from empire coincided with a reappraisal of its international role, while the arrival of migrants from the Caribbean and South Asia produced new debates about who counted as British. In France, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Portugal, decolonization set in motion population movements, legal arguments about nationality, and contests over memory that continued long after the last flags had been lowered.

Migration, however, cannot be told only as a story of former colonial subjects. Western European economies recruited labor from Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Yugoslavia, and Turkey as well, and migrants from the former empires entered receiving societies under very different legal conditions. Some held the nationality of the state they entered, others did not; some were recruited, others were refugees; some had served the colonial state and feared reprisals if they remained. Nationality law, immigration law, labor markets, housing markets, and racial prejudice all shaped what migration meant in practice, and the chapter treats legal equality and social equality as separate questions.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. It includes statutes and treaties, parliamentary debates, colonial and ministerial archives, ship passenger lists, census data, press coverage, police and court records, oral histories, memoirs, novels, photographs, and newsreels, as well as the records of anticolonial movements and migrant organizations. Each has limitations, and the chapter asks repeatedly whose perspective a source represents and whose is missing. The chapter belongs to Part XIX, on Cold War Europe, and to Volume VI, which traces the movement from postwar division to integration and the contemporary order. It must therefore be read alongside the reconstruction and welfare-state histories of Chapters 126 and 127, and it prepares the discussion of social transformation in Chapter 130, southern European democratization in Chapter 131, and European integration in Chapter 133. It does not attempt to reproduce the national histories of the former colonies themselves, which belong to other works in the MGU History Series.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why European overseas empires became increasingly difficult to sustain after 1945, distinguishing long-term anticolonial mobilization from the changes produced by World War II and the postwar international order.
  2. Compare the British, Dutch, Belgian, French, and Portuguese routes to imperial withdrawal, distinguishing negotiated transitions from prolonged colonial wars.
  3. Assess the role of colonized peoples, anticolonial movements, and international pressure in shaping the timing and terms of independence.
  4. Analyze how the Algerian War transformed French politics, including the collapse of the Fourth Republic and the establishment of the Fifth.
  5. Trace the connection between Portugal's African wars, military discontent, and the Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974.
  6. Distinguish among forms of postwar migration to Europe, including labor recruitment, migration under imperial nationality, family reunification, forced displacement, and the movement of settlers and colonial auxiliaries.
  7. Interpret changes in nationality and immigration law in Britain, France, and other states, and explain why nationality, citizenship, and the right of entry are not interchangeable concepts.
  8. Evaluate the roles of discrimination, racist violence, antiracist campaigns, and equality legislation in shaping postwar European societies.
  9. Contextualize the experiences of repatriates, harkis, retornados, and Indo-European and Moluccan communities, and explain why states treated them differently.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether independence ended empire or transformed the forms of unequal relationship between Europe and its former colonies.
  11. Interpret primary sources relating to decolonization, migration, and nationality, identifying authorship, purpose, perspective, and limitation.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did European states that had won or survived World War II find it so difficult to restore their overseas empires, and why did some nonetheless try to do so by force?
  2. How should historians weigh the roles of anticolonial movements, metropolitan politics, economic calculation, and international pressure in explaining the end of empire?
  3. Why did Algeria, the Congo, and Portuguese Africa produce political crises so much more severe than many other transfers of power?
  4. How did colonial wars change the institutions, political parties, and military establishments of the European states that fought them?
  5. Why did migration between former colonies and Europe take such different forms in Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Portugal?
  6. What is the difference between nationality, citizenship, and the right of residence, and why did that difference matter so much to postcolonial migrants?
  7. Why did legal equality not automatically produce social equality, and how did migrants and their descendants shape the struggle for equal belonging?
  8. Did formal independence end European imperial influence, or did it change the forms through which unequal relationships operated?
  9. How and why have the former imperial powers remembered, and forgotten, the end of empire in different ways?

Historical Context

The overseas empires that existed in 1945 were the product of several centuries of European expansion. The commercial and settler empires created after 1492 are examined in Part XI, while the rapid late nineteenth century partition of Africa and the consolidation of European rule in Asia are examined in Chapters 104 and 105. By 1914 European states, together with their settler offshoots, controlled much of Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and the Caribbean. The two world wars did not create anticolonial politics, but they altered the conditions in which it operated. Colonial soldiers and laborers served in both conflicts, colonial economies were mobilized for European war needs, and the promises made in wartime about self-determination and reform raised expectations that European governments could not easily satisfy.

The chapters immediately preceding this one in Part XIX provide the setting. Chapter 124 described the division of Germany and the origins of the European Cold War, Chapter 125 the formation of the opposing alliance systems, Chapter 126 the economic recovery of western Europe, and Chapter 127 the political reconstruction of Britain, France, Italy, and West Germany. Western European governments were thus attempting to rebuild their economies and legitimacy at the same moment that they confronted claims for self-government overseas. Many believed that colonial resources and markets would assist reconstruction, and some policymakers expected empire to restore international standing. The Cold War then complicated every calculation. The United States generally favored eventual self-government but also feared that abrupt withdrawal might benefit communist movements, while the Soviet Union publicly condemned colonialism while pursuing its own strategic interests.

A word about terminology is necessary. Decolonization is the conventional term for the end of colonial rule, but it can mislead if it implies a single process with a single cause. This chapter uses metropole or metropolitan for the European homeland of an empire, and distinguishes colonies, protectorates, mandates and trust territories, and overseas departments, because legal status shaped both the path to independence and the rights of people who later moved to Europe. The chapter also avoids treating colonial populations as a single group. In 1945 the inhabitants of European empires included settlers, officials, soldiers, indigenous elites, peasants, urban workers, students, merchants, and religious communities, whose interests and loyalties often diverged.

This chapter relies on evidence quite different from the diplomatic and parliamentary sources usually emphasized in political history. Colonial archives record the viewpoint of administrators and soldiers, and some were destroyed or removed at independence, while others remain restricted. Anticolonial movements produced manifestos, newspapers, and correspondence, but under conditions of repression and war. Statistics on migration derive from censuses, immigration records, and administrative categories that often define people by nationality or place of birth rather than by their own identities, so numerical estimates must be read with care. Oral history, memoir, literature, photography, and film preserve the experiences of migrants, soldiers, and settlers whose voices are otherwise missing, though memory is shaped by later political argument. Historians must therefore compare sources from European governments, colonial movements, and migrant communities rather than rely on any single one.

The Crisis and Dismantling of European Overseas Empires

European imperial retreat occurred through different combinations of nationalist mobilization, warfare, negotiation, international pressure, and metropolitan political change.

Section 128.1

World War II, Anticolonial Nationalism, and the Crisis of Empire

In 1945 European empires still covered an extraordinary share of the globe. Britain administered India, Burma, Ceylon, Malaya, much of Africa, the Caribbean, and numerous strategic islands and ports, and held the Palestine Mandate until 1948. France ruled Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia, French West and Equatorial Africa, Madagascar, Indochina, and Pacific territories. The Netherlands governed the East Indies and Suriname. Belgium ruled the Congo and the trust territory of Ruanda-Urundi. Portugal held Angola, Mozambique, Guinea, Cape Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe, Macau, and Timor, and Spain retained territories in Morocco, the Sahara, and the Gulf of Guinea. Italy had lost its colonies in the war, and their disposition was settled later. These empires had acquired their modern form during the processes examined in Chapters 104 and 105, and in 1945 most European governments assumed that they would continue in some form.

The war damaged that assumption in several ways. Japan's conquest of British Malaya and Singapore in February 1942 and of the Dutch East Indies in March 1942 shattered the image of European invincibility, whatever the brutality of the Japanese occupation that followed. In French Indochina, Japan had worked through the Vichy administration until it removed French authority in March 1945. Japanese occupiers encouraged and armed some nationalist groups in Indonesia and Burma for their own purposes, and many nationalists collaborated tactically while pursuing their own aims. European states also depended heavily on colonial resources. Roughly two and a half million Indians served in the British Indian Army, the largest volunteer force in history, and colonial soldiers from Africa and the Maghreb fought in the Free French and Allied armies. Colonial economies were mobilized through requisitioning, forced labor, price controls, and shortages, which produced inflation and hardship and politicized populations that had previously stood aside from nationalist organizations.

The international environment also changed. The Atlantic Charter of 1941 endorsed the right of peoples to choose their form of government, and although Winston Churchill argued that it did not apply to the British Empire, colonial nationalists quoted it repeatedly. The United Nations Charter of 1945 committed colonial powers to promote the well-being of the inhabitants of non-self-governing territories, and its trusteeship system placed former League of Nations mandates and some former enemy colonies under international supervision. The United States tended to favor eventual self-government, though Cold War strategic concerns and alliance with European partners moderated its pressure, and the Soviet Union condemned colonialism while expanding its own influence. The Fifth Pan-African Congress, held in Manchester in October 1945 with participants including Kwame Nkrumah, George Padmore, and W. E. B. Du Bois, demonstrated that anticolonial thinking was now organized internationally.

It would nevertheless be a serious error to present anticolonial movements as spontaneous consequences of European exhaustion. The Indian National Congress had existed since 1885, Egypt's Wafd mobilized a mass movement after 1919, the Étoile Nord-Africaine was founded among North African workers in France in 1926, and Vietnamese, Indonesian, Caribbean, and African intellectuals had organized for decades, often in European universities and imperial capitals. What 1945 changed was the balance of forces. Violent episodes at the war's edge revealed both the strength of colonial grievance and the willingness of European authorities to use force, among them the killing of Senegalese soldiers at Thiaroye in December 1944, the repression that followed demonstrations at Sétif and Guelma in Algeria in May 1945, and the suppression of the Malagasy uprising of 1947, whose death toll has been estimated in a very wide range, from tens of thousands upward.

European governments responded by trying to reform and develop empire in order to preserve it. Britain's colonial development legislation and France's investment fund for overseas territories, created in 1946, financed schools, clinics, and infrastructure and sought greater economic output from colonial territories, a policy some historians describe as a "second colonial occupation" because it increased state intervention in colonial life. Colonial exports earned dollars and supported the sterling and franc areas at a time of severe balance-of-payments difficulty, which made empire seem economically valuable during reconstruction (Chapter 126). But reform raised expectations and often provoked the opposition it was meant to forestall. By the late 1940s the central problem was clear: European states wished to retain influence with reduced resources and declining international legitimacy, while organized nationalist movements demanded sovereignty.

Historical Vocabulary
Decolonization

Decolonization refers to the end of colonial rule and the transfer of political sovereignty to formerly subordinate societies. The word became common in the mid-twentieth century and is useful, but it can suggest a single orderly process directed from Europe. Historians now stress that it comprised many distinct histories, including negotiated transfers, wars of liberation, partial settlements, and cases where empire was replaced by new forms of influence. British officials often used the phrase "transfer of power," which emphasizes the European side of the transaction. This chapter uses decolonization for the whole process and tries to make visible the political action of colonized peoples. The term also describes cultural and intellectual change, for instance challenges to racial hierarchy and colonial education, which continued long after flags changed. Students should not equate decolonization with the achievement of full economic independence.

Section 128.2

Britain, Indian Independence, Partition, and Imperial Retreat

Britain's withdrawal from India in 1947 was the pivotal event of British imperial retreat, and the detailed history of South Asian independence belongs to other works in the series. This section concentrates on its implications for Britain and the empire. By 1945, the war had left Britain financially dependent on the United States and, for the first time, in debt to India. Congress and the Muslim League mobilized millions, and the Quit India movement of 1942 and the trials of Indian National Army officers in 1945–1946 had shown that Indian loyalty, including that of the army, could no longer be assumed. The Labour government elected in July 1945 was committed in principle to Indian self-government, but it faced rising communal violence, the League's demand for Pakistan, and the breakdown of the Cabinet Mission plan in 1946.

In February 1947 Prime Minister Clement Attlee announced that Britain would transfer power by June 1948. The new viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, arrived in March and advanced the date to August 1947 under mounting violence. The Indian Independence Act, which received royal assent on 18 July 1947, created two dominions, India and Pakistan, with effect from 15 August, and boundary commissions chaired by Sir Cyril Radcliffe drew the new frontiers through Punjab and Bengal. The speed of the timetable, the lack of preparation for dividing provinces, and the withdrawal of British authority produced catastrophic violence. Estimates of deaths range widely, from a few hundred thousand to as many as two million, and some twelve to fifteen million people were displaced, one of the largest forced migrations of the twentieth century. Historians still debate how far British haste and boundary policy, as opposed to Indian political choices and communal mobilization, were responsible. What is clear is that Britain chose its timetable under pressure and then largely withdrew from responsibility for the consequences.

For Britain, the loss of India transformed the Commonwealth. The 1949 London Declaration allowed India to remain a member as a republic, which converted the Commonwealth from a club of states loyal to the Crown into a looser association of sovereign states. Burma, which had been separated from India administratively and had its own nationalist movement, became independent on 4 January 1948 and left the Commonwealth, while Ceylon became a dominion on 4 February 1948. Palestine was a different case, with the Mandate ending in May 1948 amid violence and an uncompleted settlement. The loss of the empire's largest territory encouraged the belief among some officials that the future lay in Africa, where colonial rule might be extended through development and gradual political reform.

That expectation was soon overtaken. In the Gold Coast, Nkrumah's Convention People's Party won the elections of 1951 and 1954 and pressed for self-government, and Ghana became independent on 6 March 1957. Nigeria followed on 1 October 1960, and Kenya on 12 December 1963. British transitions were largely negotiated, but not all were peaceful. In Malaya, the government declared an Emergency in June 1948 against a communist insurgency recruited largely among the Chinese minority, and fought it using resettlement, detention, and military force until 1960, though Malaya became independent on 31 August 1957 under a conservative Malay-led coalition. In Kenya, the Mau Mau rebellion, which began among Kikuyu communities that had lost land and political voice, provoked a state of emergency declared in October 1952. The British suppressed the revolt with mass detention, forced villagization, and abuses recorded in colonial and later court records, including the killing of eleven detainees at Hola camp in 1959. In 2013, the British government acknowledged that Kenyan claimants had been tortured and agreed compensation.

The Suez crisis of 1956, in which Britain and France colluded with Israel against Egypt and were forced to withdraw under American financial and diplomatic pressure, demonstrated the limits of independent imperial action. It did not cause decolonization, but it clarified the situation. Harold Macmillan's speech in Cape Town in February 1960 recognized that African nationalism was a force to be accommodated, and a rapid sequence of independences followed, including Sierra Leone and Tanganyika in 1961, Jamaica, Trinidad and Tobago, and Uganda in 1962, and Kenya in 1963. For British politics, the significance lay less in a dramatic domestic crisis, as in France, than in a gradual reorientation. Governments of both parties accepted that Britain's international position would rest on alliances, the Commonwealth, and eventually Europe, rather than on empire, but the migration and nationality questions raised by empire (Divisions III and IV) would prove harder to settle than the transfer of flags.

Context: The Act implemented the decisions announced in the 3 June 1947 plan for partition and the transfer of power. It was drafted rapidly in London after consultation with Congress and League leaders and the viceroy.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation The Act established two independent dominions, India and Pakistan, from an appointed day in August 1947. It ended British responsibility for governing the territories concerned and the Crown's suzerainty over the princely states, leaving those states to decide on accession. It gave the new constituent assemblies power to frame constitutions, allowed the Government of India Act 1935 to operate temporarily with adaptations, and provided for the division of provinces and assets. The imperial title for the monarch was to be dropped.

Analysis: The Act is evidence of the legal form British withdrawal took: the transfer of sovereignty to dominions, which kept the new states formally within the Crown's realm until they chose otherwise. It shows how the British framed independence as the culmination of constitutional development. It cannot show the negotiating strength of Indian parties, the pressure of violence and finance, or the experience of the millions displaced, and its silence about the consequences of partition is part of what historians must interpret.

Section 128.3

The Netherlands, Indonesia, Belgium, and the End of Colonial Rule

Two days after the Japanese surrender, on 17 August 1945, Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta proclaimed Indonesian independence in Jakarta. The proclamation preceded the arrival of Allied forces, and Indonesian republicans organized a government, an army, and a constitution before the Dutch could return. The Netherlands, which had been occupied by Germany and economically devastated, regarded the East Indies as essential to recovery and national standing, and Dutch officials and much of public opinion initially assumed that the Indonesian Republic was a Japanese creation that could be set aside. British forces under Allied command occupied Java and Sumatra in late 1945 and became involved in fighting, and the period of violence known in Indonesian history as the Bersiap, in which Indonesians attacked Indo-Europeans, Dutch internees, and Chinese civilians, caused many deaths and coloured Dutch perceptions of the revolution.

Negotiations produced the Linggadjati Agreement (November 1946, signed March 1947), which proposed a federal United States of Indonesia in union with the Netherlands, but disagreement over its meaning led to Dutch military offensives in July 1947 and December 1948, which the Dutch called "police actions." The second offensive captured Yogyakarta and the republican leadership. International reaction was sharp. The United Nations Security Council intervened, the United States, which had been supporting Dutch reconstruction through Marshall aid, signalled disapproval, and many Asian states pressed the issue. Under this pressure, and after the Indonesian Republic had survived a communist revolt at Madiun in 1948 and so presented itself as anticommunist, the Netherlands agreed at a Round Table Conference at The Hague in 1949 to transfer sovereignty. The transfer took place on 27 December 1949, and Western New Guinea remained Dutch until 1962, when it was transferred under United Nations arrangements and then to Indonesia.

Distinctions of date matter because they encode different understandings. Indonesians date independence from 17 August 1945, the moment of proclamation. The Netherlands dated Indonesian sovereignty from the transfer of 27 December 1949, and for decades did not accept the earlier date. In 2005 the Dutch foreign minister stated that the Netherlands accepted the de facto start of Indonesian independence in 1945 in a moral and political sense. A large research program by Dutch institutes, published in 2022, concluded that the Dutch military used structural and excessive violence in a conflict that cost Indonesians many lives, a conclusion that departed from the older Dutch view of the actions as largely proportionate. Dutch prime minister Mark Rutte apologized on 17 February 2022 on behalf of the government for systematic, widespread extreme violence during the war of independence. King Willem-Alexander had expressed regret during a visit to Indonesia in March 2020; these were distinct statements, made on different occasions. In the Netherlands the war was long remembered chiefly through the experience of Dutch soldiers and Indo-European civilians, while in Indonesia it is remembered as a national revolution, so the same events were understood in markedly different ways.

Belgium's colonial experience differed in time and character. The Belgian Congo, taken over from Leopold II's personal rule in 1908 after the exposure of atrocities in the Congo Free State, was administered through an alliance of the colonial state, the Catholic missions, and large companies such as the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga. The system offered Africans schooling and wages but allowed almost no political participation and no higher education for most. Belgium expected a long, gradual transition. But urban African associations, riots in Léopoldville in January 1959, and the independence of neighboring French territories made that timetable untenable, and a round table in Brussels in January 1960 agreed to independence on 30 June 1960. The Congo became independent with an inexperienced political class, an army, the Force Publique, still commanded by Belgians, and strong regional divisions.

The consequences were swift. Patrice Lumumba became prime minister and Joseph Kasa-Vubu president. Within days the Force Publique mutinied, Belgian civilians fled or were evacuated, Belgium sent troops, and Moïse Tshombe declared Katanga's secession with Belgian commercial and military support. The United Nations deployed a peacekeeping mission. Lumumba was dismissed in September, arrested, and killed in January 1961 in Katanga, with the involvement of Belgian officers and officials and the complicity of Congolese rivals, and with American and Belgian hostility to him documented in later inquiries. A Belgian parliamentary commission in 2001 concluded that some Belgian ministers and officials bore moral responsibility for the circumstances of his death, and the government expressed regret in 2002. Mobutu Sese Seko took power in 1965. The Congo crisis shows how abrupt transfer, unresolved internal divisions, Cold War rivalry, and commercial interests combined to make independence extremely precarious. Belgium also gave independence to Rwanda and Burundi, former League mandates administered as a United Nations trust territory, on 1 July 1962.

Italy's case was brief but distinctive. Defeated in 1943, Italy lost its colonies under the 1947 peace treaty. Libya became independent in 1951 under United Nations auspices, Eritrea was federated with Ethiopia in 1952, and Somalia was placed under Italian administration as a United Nations trust territory from 1950 until its independence on 1 July 1960, when it united with the former British Somaliland, which had become independent on 26 June.

Section 128.4

France, Indochina, and the Transformation of the French Empire

France emerged from the war convinced that empire had contributed to its liberation and was essential to its status. The Brazzaville Conference of 1944, which de Gaulle opened, had promised reform within the empire but ruled out independence. The constitution of the Fourth Republic created the French Union in 1946, which sought to combine the republican tradition of universal citizenship with continued imperial authority. Subjects of overseas territories received French citizenship in 1946 and the forced labor and legal regime of the indigénat were abolished, but political participation remained unequal, with separate electoral colleges and no real federal power-sharing. Many colonial leaders initially sought equality within the Union rather than secession, a demand France found as difficult to meet as independence, since genuine equality would have transformed metropolitan politics. This contradiction between republican universalism and colonial subordination lay at the heart of the French crisis.

In Indochina, the Việt Minh under Ho Chi Minh seized power in August 1945 and on 2 September proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. France returned, negotiated with Ho in 1946, and then went to war in December 1946. The First Indochina War combined colonial reconquest with the Cold War, since the communist victory in China in 1949 gave the Việt Minh sanctuary and supplies, and the United States, anxious to secure French cooperation in Europe, increasingly funded the French effort. The war was fought largely by professional troops, including colonial and Foreign Legion soldiers, which kept it from becoming a mass conscript conflict, but its costs mounted. In 1954 the French garrison at Điện Biên Phủ was besieged and surrendered on 7 May. At Geneva in July 1954, a settlement under the premiership of Pierre Mendès France divided Vietnam provisionally at the seventeenth parallel and ended French rule in Indochina, though not the conflict.

North Africa and sub-Saharan Africa followed different paths. In Morocco and Tunisia, which were protectorates rather than French territory, nationalist movements, the Istiqlal and the Neo-Destour, resisted. France exiled Sultan Mohammed V in 1953 and faced increasing violence, and Mendès France offered Tunisian internal autonomy in 1954. Both countries became independent in March 1956, Morocco on 2 March and Tunisia on 20 March, in part because France sought to concentrate its forces on Algeria. In sub-Saharan Africa, the 1956 framework law (loi-cadre) devolved powers to territorial assemblies, and in 1958 de Gaulle's constitution offered a French Community. In the referendum of September 1958 only Guinea, under Sékou Touré, voted to refuse, and became independent in October. Within two years the others negotiated independence, and around fourteen former French territories, including Cameroon, Togo, Senegal, Mali, Côte d'Ivoire, and Madagascar, became sovereign in 1960. France thereby shed most of its African empire with relatively little warfare, though it preserved close political, military, and monetary connections discussed in Section 128.19. The earlier violence in Madagascar in 1947 shows that this peaceful image is incomplete.

Algeria was different. Conquered from 1830, it had been organized as departments of France, and about a million European settlers lived among some nine million Muslim Algerians in a society of deep inequality. Algeria was therefore not a protectorate to be released but, in French law and in the view of most politicians of all parties in 1954, part of France. This status, the size and political weight of the settler community, and the associations of Algeria with French national identity made withdrawal extraordinarily explosive. The Algerian problem, examined in Division II, was what turned French decolonization from a series of reluctant concessions into a national crisis.

Historical Debate

Why Did European Empires End?

The question: To what extent did European empires end because European governments chose to withdraw, and to what extent because colonized peoples and the international system left them no alternative?

Interpretation A: Metropolitan calculation and decline. Some historians emphasize the decisions of European governments. They argue that war had weakened Britain, France, and the Netherlands financially and militarily, that governments concluded that the costs of empire outweighed its benefits, and that withdrawal was often a policy chosen to preserve influence through informal means, as in the British approach to the Commonwealth.
Interpretation B: Anticolonial agency and international change. Others stress the organized pressure of nationalist movements, popular mobilization, and armed resistance, together with a changed world order shaped by the United Nations, the United States and the Soviet Union, and Asian and African diplomacy. In this view, European governments conceded what they could no longer hold, and withdrawal was often forced rather than chosen.

Evidence: Interpretation A relies on cabinet papers, treasury records, and military assessments. Interpretation B relies on nationalist archives, colonial police reports, and the sequence of strikes, revolts, and negotiations. Most current scholarship combines both, and also emphasizes differences among empires: Portugal's withdrawal clearly followed metropolitan revolution, whereas Indonesia's followed a failed war.

Why it matters: If decolonization is a European decision, imperial withdrawal appears as a gift or a rational retreat, and colonized peoples become spectators. If it is the product of anticolonial agency, European policy appears as a response to pressure, and responsibility for violence shifts accordingly.

Section 128.5

Algeria and the Struggle against French Colonial Rule, 1954–1962

Algeria's relationship with France was unlike that of any other French dependency. After the conquest that began in 1830, the northern territory was divided into departments and treated administratively as part of France. A settler population of European origin, including French, Spanish, Italian, and Maltese families, and Algerian Jews, who were collectively granted French citizenship in 1870, held full political rights. Most Muslim Algerians, by contrast, were French nationals but not ordinary citizens, because they were governed by a separate personal status unless they renounced it. The 1947 Statute of Algeria created an assembly with two electoral colleges of equal size, one for a million Europeans and one for roughly nine million Muslims, a system that preserved settler dominance. Settlers controlled the best agricultural land and the main economic and political institutions, and Muslim Algerians suffered from poverty, rural dispossession, unemployment, and unequal access to schooling.

Algerian political life was varied. Messali Hadj's movement demanded independence, Ferhat Abbas's moderates sought equality and autonomy, the Muslim reformist ulama emphasized religious and cultural identity, and the Communist Party worked among workers. After the repression around Sétif, Guelma, and Kherrata in May 1945, many younger activists concluded that peaceful reform was impossible. The death toll cannot be established precisely: French official accounts initially minimized the killings at roughly 1,500, scholars have advanced estimates including 6,000 to 8,000 and 15,000 to 20,000, while Algerian commemorative accounts have often cited 45,000. These figures come from different sources and methodologies, and should not be treated as equally verified counts. The Front de libération nationale (FLN) emerged in 1954 from a split in Messali's movement. On 1 November 1954, a series of coordinated attacks across Algeria marked the beginning of the insurrection, and the FLN issued a proclamation calling on Algerians to join the struggle for national independence.

The war grew over several years from scattered guerrilla actions into a general conflict. The FLN's strategy combined rural guerrilla warfare, attacks on the colonial administration, political organization, and diplomacy abroad. An important turning point came in August 1955 near Philippeville, where FLN-led attacks killed European and Muslim civilians and French reprisals killed many more, which hardened communal divisions. The Soummam conference of August 1956 created a political structure, and the FLN worked to eliminate rivals, notably Messali's Algerian National Movement, in violent conflict that extended to Algerian workers in France. The French response was to escalate. Conscripts and reservists were sent to Algeria, so that around two million French men served over the course of the war, and the army adopted counterinsurgency doctrine that treated the civilian population as the battlefield. Villagers were forcibly relocated into regroupment camps, with estimates of the number displaced of the order of two million, and areas were declared forbidden zones.

The Battle of Algiers in 1957 became the emblem of this form of war. FLN bombings in the city were answered when the government gave police powers to General Jacques Massu's paratroopers, who used mass arrests, disappearances, and systematic torture. Torture was widely discussed in France through the writing of Henri Alleg, the testimony of conscripts, and the protests of intellectuals, and a French general later admitted in 2001 to having used it on orders. These practices damaged France's moral standing and divided opinion at home. The FLN set up a provisional government in 1958 and fought a diplomatic battle for recognition at the United Nations and among newly independent states, while France argued that the conflict was an internal matter.

Distinctions among Algerians must be preserved. The FLN claimed to represent the nation, but it fought other nationalists, killed Algerians it regarded as collaborators, and imposed discipline on civilians. Many Algerians served France, whether as soldiers, auxiliaries, officials, or local councillors, for reasons that included family security, wages, coercion, loyalty, and distrust of the FLN. Settlers likewise varied: many wealthy landowners and urban businessmen were joined by many lower-income Europeans in Algerian cities. The war killed Algerians, Europeans, and French soldiers in very large numbers. Published historical estimates of Algerian war-related deaths vary substantially, with some scholarly analyses around 300,000 and others roughly 400,000 to 600,000. The frequently repeated official Algerian figure of 1.5 million is a commemorative national claim rather than an established demographic count. Definitions also differ as to whether civilian, combatant, indirect, and post-ceasefire deaths are included. French defense records give approximately 25,000 military dead, including about 8,000 who died through accidents or illness; that figure is different in scope from estimates of Algerian civilian and combatant mortality. French authorities long called the conflict "events" and "pacification," and only in 1999 did the National Assembly officially recognize it as a war.

Section 128.6

The Algerian War and the Political Crisis within France

The Algerian War was both an overseas anticolonial conflict and a crisis within France. The Fourth Republic's unstable coalitions, whose governments rarely lasted a year, struggled to formulate a settlement that could satisfy the settlers, the army, the public, and international opinion. The Suez failure in 1956 and the military's sense of having been abandoned in Indochina deepened the conviction among officers that politicians would not back them. By 1958 the army and the settlers distrusted a succession of ministries that appeared ready to negotiate. In Algiers, on 13 May 1958, European demonstrators seized the government building and formed a Committee of Public Safety supported by General Massu and, soon afterwards, General Raoul Salan, who appealed for the return of Charles de Gaulle. Fearing civil war and a military landing in metropolitan France, the National Assembly invested de Gaulle as prime minister on 1 June 1958, with powers to draft a new constitution.

The constitution approved by referendum in September 1958 created the Fifth Republic, with a strong president, and it is difficult to understand the later French political system without recognizing that it emerged from this colonial crisis. De Gaulle visited Algiers in June and told the crowd he had understood them, a phrase settlers took as support for French Algeria. But de Gaulle gradually moved. In September 1959 he announced that Algerians would have the right of self-determination, a statement that infuriated settlers and many officers. The "week of the barricades" in Algiers in January 1960 was an insurrection by settlers against this policy. Referendums in France in January 1961, in which a large majority approved the principle of self-determination, and in April 1962 expressed metropolitan opinion that was increasingly weary of the war.

The conflict between supporters of French Algeria and advocates of negotiation became violent. In April 1961 four retired and serving generals, including Maurice Challe and Raoul Salan, attempted a putsch in Algiers. It failed within days because most conscripts and officers did not support it and de Gaulle appealed publicly to the nation, but it left the army purged and embittered. A number of conspirators then formed the Organisation armée secrète (OAS), which waged a terrorist campaign in Algeria and France, killing Muslims and French officials, attacking those it saw as traitors, and attempting to assassinate de Gaulle. OAS violence in 1961 and 1962 was among the most serious threats to the republic, and it fed hostility to the settlers as a whole.

The war also reached metropolitan France in other ways. Hundreds of thousands of Algerian workers lived there, and the FLN's French federation raised funds and organized them, while the French police surveilled and repressed them. In October 1961 the Paris police prefect, Maurice Papon, imposed a curfew on Algerians. On 17 October, tens of thousands of Algerians demonstrated peacefully in response to an FLN call, and police arrested thousands, beat demonstrators, and killed an unknown number, some of whose bodies were thrown into the Seine. The official death toll initially was three, but historians' estimates range from several dozen to well over a hundred, and the event was long suppressed in public memory. On 8 February 1962 nine demonstrators against the OAS were killed by police at the Charonne metro station. Intellectuals also divided the country. The "Manifesto of the 121" in 1960 defended the right to refuse to fight, and the state responded with prosecutions and censorship. In October 2021, President Emmanuel Macron acknowledged that crimes committed that night under Papon's authority were inexcusable.

This episode demonstrates that decolonization could undermine a state's political order from within. The Fourth Republic fell, a new constitutional order was established, the army's political role was restricted, and the metropolitan left and right reorganized around positions on the war. It also demonstrates that conflicts over empire reached into French streets and police stations and affected the lives of Algerians in France, a connection that returns in Section 128.11.

Section 128.7

The Évian Accords, Algerian Independence, and the Human Consequences of Withdrawal

Secret and public negotiations between the French government and the FLN's provisional government culminated in agreements concluded at Évian on 18 March 1962. A ceasefire came into effect on 19 March. The accords provided for a transitional provisional executive, a referendum on self-determination, the protection of persons and property, a period during which Europeans could choose their nationality, French cooperation in economic and technical matters, and a continuing French military presence, including the naval base at Mers el-Kébir for a transitional period, and some Saharan facilities. Voters in France approved the accords in a referendum on 8 April 1962 by a very large majority. On 1 July 1962 an Algerian referendum produced an overwhelming vote for independence. France recognized Algerian independence on 3 July. Algerian authorities, however, proclaimed independence on 5 July, the anniversary of the French capture of Algiers in 1830, and that date is commemorated in Algeria today. The difference between 3 July and 5 July reflects different understandings of who conferred sovereignty and when.

The ceasefire did not end the violence. The OAS campaigned to prevent the accords from taking effect, setting off bombs and killing civilians, and the shooting of unarmed European demonstrators by French troops in Algiers on 26 March 1962 (the rue d'Isly incident) killed dozens. In the spring and summer, abductions and killings of Europeans occurred, and on 5 July 1962 in Oran, a number of European civilians were killed in violence whose scale and responsibility remain disputed among historians, with estimates running to several hundred. Fear spread quickly. Roughly 800,000 Europeans, most of the settler population, left for France in a few months, mostly in the summer of 1962. These departures were not planned by the French government, which had expected that many would stay under the guarantees of the accords. The settlers called themselves pieds-noirs, a term of uncertain origin that became their collective identity in France.

The harkis, Muslim Algerians who served France as auxiliaries, were in a particularly vulnerable position. The term is used broadly for various categories of auxiliaries, and estimates of the number of Muslims who served France in various roles run to well over 200,000, though the number of harkis proper was smaller. The Évian Accords prohibited reprisals, but after independence many were killed, imprisoned, or tortured in violence whose total is disputed, with published estimates extending from approximately 10,000 to 150,000. This range demonstrates the scale of uncertainty, not a measured tally of victims. The French government instructed military authorities not to organize the transfer of harkis to France en masse, and some officers disobeyed and arranged transport for those they had commanded. Around 90,000 harkis and family members eventually reached France by the mid-1970s, many of them housed in isolated camps and forest hamlets, such as Rivesaltes, Bias, and Saint-Maurice-l'Ardoise, where conditions were poor, schooling limited, and social isolation severe. The French government's own historical documentation records the difficult reception and inadequate protection many received. The French state acknowledged its responsibility in stages: the compensation laws of the 1970s, a recognition law in 2022, and an apology by President Macron in September 2021.

The war thus ended in a mixed outcome. The independence for which Algerians had fought was won at an extraordinary human cost, and the new state faced reconstruction and political conflict between leaders such as Ahmed Ben Bella and the army. Algerians who had supported France, European settlers, and conscripts who had served in Algeria all returned or remained with bitter memories. The treatment of displaced populations became a lasting question for the French state, which, as Section 128.17 shows, had to decide what obligations it owed to those whom it had in some cases classified as French but in practice treated as outsiders.

Section 128.8

Portugal's Colonial Wars and the Final Major Wave of African Decolonization

Portugal's route was the longest and the most closely tied to the nature of its regime. Under António de Oliveira Salazar, who led the Estado Novo from 1932 until incapacitated in 1968, and his successor Marcello Caetano, Portugal defended a vision of a multi-continental nation. In 1951 its colonies were renamed "overseas provinces," a legal device intended to rebut United Nations criticism, and official ideology promoted the claim that Portuguese colonization was uniquely benign. Portugal was a poor country, and empire provided it with markets, raw materials, remittances, and prestige. When other European powers withdrew, Portugal's authoritarian government, which tolerated little political opposition, resisted. India's seizure of Goa in December 1961 reminded the regime that its position could be challenged.

Wars began in Angola in 1961, in Guinea in 1963, and in Mozambique in 1964. In Angola, a revolt in Luanda in February 1961 was followed in March by attacks in the north by the Union of the Peoples of Angola, and the Portuguese responded with massive reprisals and reinforcements. Three movements emerged, the MPLA, the FNLA, and UNITA, which differed in their regional bases, ideologies, and foreign backers. In Guinea-Bissau, Amílcar Cabral's PAIGC fought an effective guerrilla war and controlled much of the country by the early 1970s, and in Mozambique FRELIMO, founded under Eduardo Mondlane and later led by Samora Machel, gradually extended operations. Cabral was assassinated in January 1973, and the PAIGC proclaimed independence unilaterally in September 1973, a claim recognized by many states. Portugal maintained its forces through conscription, and at the peak had some 150,000 troops in Africa, a very large burden for a country of under ten million. A large proportion of the state budget went to defense, thousands of Portuguese soldiers died, and many more were wounded or suffered lasting trauma, while military service pushed hundreds of thousands of young men to emigrate to France and elsewhere. Reports of massacres, including the one at Wiriyamu in Mozambique in December 1972, damaged Portugal's reputation abroad.

The war's greatest effect was on the Portuguese armed forces. Many career captains resented a decree of 1973 that allowed conscript officers faster promotion, seeing it as an insult to their professional standing, and more broadly a number of officers concluded that the wars could not be won by military means. General António de Spínola, a former commander in Guinea, published Portugal and the Future in February 1974, arguing for a political rather than military solution. A secret organization of junior officers, the Armed Forces Movement (MFA), carried out a coup on 25 April 1974, and the largely bloodless Carnation Revolution, named for the flowers placed in soldiers' rifles by crowds, overthrew the regime. The new government's program called for democratization, decolonization, and development, and the fate of the empire became inseparable from the revolution's course (the transition to democracy is examined in Chapter 131).

Decolonization then proceeded quickly and unevenly. Portugal recognized Guinea-Bissau's independence on 10 September 1974. In Mozambique, FRELIMO took power at independence on 25 June 1975. Cape Verde became independent on 5 July and São Tomé and Príncipe on 12 July 1975. In Angola, the Alvor Agreement of January 1975 envisaged a transitional coalition of the three movements, but it collapsed into civil war, with foreign intervention by Cuba and South Africa, among others, and the MPLA proclaimed independence on 11 November 1975 in Luanda. Portuguese Timor was invaded by Indonesia in December 1975. The United Nations admitted most of the new states in 1975, but admitted Angola only in 1976 because of its contested situation. Spain, meanwhile, withdrew from Equatorial Guinea, which became independent on 12 October 1968, and from the Western Sahara in 1975–1976 under an arrangement with Morocco and Mauritania that left the territory's status disputed.

Cause and Effect

Colonial Warfare, Military Discontent, and Political Change in Portugal

Long-term causes

An authoritarian regime that tied national identity to empire, a poor economy that relied on colonial markets and emigration, and a lack of legal political channels for dissent.

Short-term causes

Thirteen years of war in three theaters, officers' grievances over the 1973 promotion rules, Spínola's published criticism, and the failure of Caetano to reform.

Event or transformation

The Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 overthrew the Estado Novo and committed the new authorities to ending the wars.

Immediate effects

Ceasefires, rapid recognition of Guinea-Bissau and then of the other territories, civil war in Angola, and a mass movement of people to Portugal.

Long-term consequences: Portugal became a democracy, absorbed several hundred thousand returnees, and later sought a new European role through integration (Chapters 131 and 133). Former colonies faced civil wars and Cold War interventions.

Historical complexity: The wars did not cause the revolution alone. Economic change, emigration, domestic opposition, and the example of other decolonizations also mattered, and historians debate how far the officers acted for professional or political reasons. The anticolonial movements, meanwhile, were not merely background: their military pressure created the conditions in which the Portuguese army lost confidence.

Division III · Sections 128.9–128.12 · Principal period 1945–1980

Postcolonial Migration and the Transformation of European Populations

Postcolonial migration was a historically varied process linking imperial institutions, labor demand, family networks, citizenship status, and the movements of people across formerly imperial spaces.

Section 128.9

Empire, Labor Demand, and the New Geography of Migration

Migration between colonies and Europe long preceded decolonization. Sailors, students, soldiers, servants, and merchants from Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean had lived in European port cities for centuries, and both world wars brought colonial soldiers and workers to Europe. What changed after 1945 was the scale and the character of movement. Empires had created shared languages, educational systems, legal statuses, shipping lines, and family connections, and when the postwar boom produced severe labor shortages (Chapter 126), these links made former colonial territories an obvious source of workers. Decolonization then changed the legal framework within which people moved, sometimes widening the channels for a time, and sometimes closing them.

Several kinds of movement must be distinguished. Labor migration was the most important numerically: workers recruited by employers or governments, or moving through networks of kin and fellow villagers, to work in transport, construction, manufacturing, mining, and public services. Migration under imperial nationality allowed some colonial subjects to travel to the metropole as of right, as in the case of Commonwealth citizens in Britain, Algerians in France, and Surinamese in the Netherlands, though the legal rights attached to nationality varied. Family reunification followed as workers sent for wives and children. Political displacement included those who fled violence or reprisals, such as harkis, Moluccan soldiers, and later Ugandan Asians. Finally, there was the movement of settlers and colonial officials who returned or relocated, whose cases are examined in Section 128.17. These categories overlapped, and one person might pass through several of them.

Postwar migration must not be equated with former colonial subjects alone. Western European states also recruited workers from Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Yugoslavia, and Turkey. Belgium signed a coal-for-labor agreement with Italy in 1946, and the Marcinelle mining disaster of 1956, in which 262 men died, among them 136 Italians, became a symbol of the risks borne by migrant workers. West Germany signed recruitment agreements with Italy in 1955 and Turkey in 1961. Britain drew workers from Ireland, which stood outside the Commonwealth after 1949 but within a Common Travel Area, and from Poland and eastern Europe through resettlement schemes after the war. France received very large numbers of Portuguese and Spanish workers, and by 1975 Portuguese nationals formed one of the largest foreign groups in the country, comparable to Algerians. These flows are the subject of wider discussion in later chapters, and their inclusion here prevents a misleading association of all immigration with decolonization. They also show that European governments treated different groups differently, often preferring workers perceived as culturally closer or temporary.

Migration was thus shaped by the interaction of demand, law, and networks. Employers and states set the demand, but migrants made choices based on information from kin, villages, and religious or political associations, and chain migration produced communities concentrated in particular towns and trades. Remittances, return plans, and the "myth of return" shaped migrants' expectations. Governments, for their part, often described migration as temporary, a premise that proved increasingly inaccurate.

Historical Vocabulary
Postcolonial Migration

Postcolonial migration refers to movement of people between former colonies and the former imperial states, shaped by the institutional, linguistic, legal, and economic ties of empire. The term should not be read to mean that all migrants to Europe were postcolonial, nor that migrants chose Europe merely because of colonial history. It also should not be reduced to a single type, since it includes workers, students, refugees, settlers, and soldiers. In British usage, "New Commonwealth" referred to the non-white Commonwealth countries of Asia, Africa, and the Caribbean, in contrast to the "Old Commonwealth" dominions such as Canada and Australia. The category itself, as the later history of immigration law shows, embodied racial assumptions. The related word postcolonial is used by historians both in a chronological sense, to mean after colonial rule, and in an analytical sense, to describe the continuing effects of colonial relationships.

Section 128.10

Britain and Commonwealth Migration: The Windrush Generation and Beyond

HMT Empire Windrush, a former German liner that had become a British troopship, docked at Tilbury late on 21 June 1948, and its passengers disembarked the following day. The National Archives passenger list records 1,027 people, more than 800 of whom gave a Caribbean location as their last place of residence. This is not the same measure as the number who boarded at a particular Caribbean port. Many were former servicemen who had served in the Royal Air Force or other forces, others were skilled workers and young people seeking work. Some were temporarily housed in a wartime shelter at Clapham South and looked for work through the Brixton labour exchange. The ship has become a symbol of the beginning of postwar Caribbean migration. It was not, however, the beginning of Black British history, nor the first arrival of Caribbean people in Britain, since people of African and Caribbean descent had lived in Britain for centuries and Caribbean volunteers and workers had come in the Second World War. Its importance in public memory owes much to the press attention of the time, to the newsreel footage, and to later commemoration, especially since the 1980s and the Windrush scandal of 2018, which revealed that some long-resident Commonwealth migrants had been wrongly denied rights.

Migration from the Caribbean increased through the 1950s as the United States tightened its own immigration restrictions and British employers sought workers. London Transport recruited directly in Barbados from 1956, and the National Health Service recruited nurses and ancillary staff from the Caribbean and, later, South Asia, while workers entered textile mills, foundries, car plants, and construction. From the 1950s, migration from India, Pakistan, and later Bangladesh developed, in large part through chain migration from particular districts such as Punjab, Gujarat, Sylhet, and Mirpur, where the construction of the Mangla Dam in the 1960s displaced many families. Migrants, usually men at first, found work in textile towns of Lancashire and Yorkshire, the foundries of the Midlands, and the service sector in London. In 1968 and 1972, the arrival of East African Asians, many of them British passport holders from Kenya and Uganda, added a new group, discussed in Section 128.13.

Communities formed in particular cities. Caribbean settlement centered on Brixton, Notting Hill, and other districts of London, as well as Birmingham, Bristol, Manchester, Nottingham, and Leeds, while South Asian communities developed in Southall, Leicester, Bradford, Birmingham, and the East End. Many Caribbean migrants joined or founded churches, including Pentecostal congregations, that provided worship, support, and leadership training, and saving schemes known as "pardners" enabled the purchase of homes when banks refused loans. Migrants also created newspapers, social clubs, and cultural events. Although the majority of migrants worked and lived quietly, the existence of a "colour bar" in housing and some employment, which Section 128.14 examines, shaped their experience.

Official attitudes were ambivalent from the start. Ministers in 1948 welcomed the labor but worried about the social consequences. A group of Labour members of Parliament wrote to the prime minister in June 1948 urging limits on the arrival of Black colonial subjects, and Cabinet committees in the early 1950s discussed restrictions, but concluded that controls would be difficult to justify for subjects of the Crown. British officials thus preferred to rely on informal discouragement and employer practice, and the 1948 legal framework meant that, until 1962, Commonwealth citizens could enter freely.

Everyday Life and Social History

Arrival, Work, and Home in 1950s Britain

For many newcomers, the first challenge was housing. Landlords frequently refused to let to Black tenants, so migrants rented rooms in overcrowded houses, often from other migrants or from exploitative landlords, and shared beds in shifts. Work was often below migrants' qualifications, since skilled tradesmen, clerks, and teachers found themselves in unskilled jobs. Women, a large share of Caribbean migrants, worked as nurses, seamstresses, and factory hands and generally combined paid work with household responsibilities, often leaving children with relatives in the Caribbean until they could send for them. Food was a constant link to home, and markets and shops selling Caribbean ingredients appeared in Brixton and elsewhere. Religion, music, sound systems, dances, and domestic gatherings created social space apart from hostile public settings. These ordinary practices also show migrants as active builders of new communities rather than as problems for the receiving society.

Section 128.11

France, North African Migration, and the Postcolonial Workforce

Migration from North Africa to France began before 1945, notably with Algerian workers recruited in the First World War and the interwar period. After 1945, it expanded rapidly. The 1947 Statute of Algeria recognized the freedom of movement between Algeria and France, and rural poverty, land scarcity, and the war in Algeria drove many Algerians to seek work in French industry. The number of people counted as Algerian nationals in the French census rose from 211,675 in 1954 to 350,484 in 1962 and 710,690 in 1975. These are nationality-based counts, not all people of Algerian ancestry, and their meaning changed with independence, naturalization, and shifting legal categories. Morocco and Tunisia, after independence, signed labor agreements with France in the 1960s, and migration from West Africa, particularly from the Senegal River valley and Mali, increased from the late 1950s.

Migrants entered a labor market that placed them in the least desirable jobs. Algerians and later Moroccans worked in car factories at Billancourt and Poissy, steelworks, mines, construction, and municipal cleaning, mostly as unskilled workers, and they were often hired through informal networks. Housing was a grave problem. Many lived in shantytowns (bidonvilles) at the edge of cities, notably at Nanterre, in overcrowded hotels, or in hostels run by organizations such as Sonacotra, created in 1956 to house Algerian workers and later extended to other migrants. These hostels (foyers) offered cheap beds but imposed restrictive rules and often supervision that reflected police concerns. Migrants regularly lived in single-sex groups tied to home villages, sending remittances to relatives in Algeria, Morocco, or Mali.

Legal status shaped experience. Until Algerian independence, Algerians were French nationals with the right to enter France, though they were frequently subject to special police surveillance, and the war led to heightened control, as the events of October 1961 demonstrated. After 1962 they became foreign nationals, but a 1968 Franco-Algerian agreement gave them a special regime of residence and work that was more favorable than the general rules in some respects. Algeria itself suspended emigration to France in September 1973 after a wave of racist violence in Marseille that summer, in which several Algerians were killed. By then the economic context was changing. After the oil shock of 1973, the French government decided in July 1974 to suspend most new labor immigration, and the measure ended the era of large-scale recruitment of workers.

The 1974 suspension did not end immigration. Family reunification continued, and in 1976 the government adopted rules for it, though a 1977 attempt to restrict it was annulled by the Conseil d'État in 1978, which recognized a right to a normal family life as a general principle of law. Migrants who had thought of staying temporarily brought wives and children, and the population of North African origin became more settled, more family-based, and more diverse. Women and children appear in the record as workers, students, and community organizers, though migrant women have been less visible in the sources. Housing conditions improved slowly through public housing projects (grands ensembles) on the outskirts of French cities, which later became associated with social segregation.

Section 128.12

The Netherlands, Belgium, and Portugal: Different Postcolonial Migrations

The Netherlands received several distinct groups. After Indonesian independence, some 300,000 people left the former East Indies for the Netherlands between the late 1940s and the 1960s. Most were Indo-Europeans, people of mixed Dutch and Indonesian ancestry who had held privileged but ambiguous positions in colonial society, along with Dutch-born officials and soldiers. Many had never lived in the Netherlands and arrived in a cold, poor, and unprepared country, where authorities sometimes expected them to assimilate quickly, and where they met mixed reception. A particular group were the Moluccans: some 12,500 soldiers of the Royal Netherlands East Indies Army and their families, who were brought to the Netherlands in 1951 on what both sides saw as a temporary basis, and who were housed in camps, including the former Westerbork camp, while Moluccan hopes for an independent South Moluccan republic faded. The isolation and frustration of the second generation contributed to episodes of political violence in the 1970s, including train hijackings, which were followed by reassessment of Dutch policy.

Suriname, a colony with a population of African, South Asian, Javanese, indigenous, and other origins, became a constituent country of the Kingdom in 1954 and fully independent on 25 November 1975. Surinamese were Dutch nationals, and migration to the Netherlands grew in the 1960s and 1970s. Fear of the consequences of independence, and the expectation of restrictions, led to a large movement in the mid-1970s, and a transitional arrangement allowed free entry until 1980, after which a visa requirement was imposed. Dutch statistical research estimates that the Surinamese-origin community in the Netherlands increased from fewer than 30,000 in 1970 to approximately 165,000 by the beginning of 1981, including people born in the Netherlands with Surinamese family origins. This population stock differs from annual immigration flows: Statistics Netherlands records approximately 39,700 arrivals from Suriname in 1975 and 18,990 in 1980. Migration from the Netherlands Antilles, whose people remained Dutch citizens within the Kingdom, was continuing. Dutch cities, notably Amsterdam, thus became increasingly diverse earlier than many had expected.

Belgium's colonial migration was smaller. Congolese had been officially discouraged from migrating, and before 1960 only small numbers, mostly students and elites, lived in Belgium. After independence some Congolese came as students and political exiles, and a community developed in Brussels, notably in the Matongé district, while the Belgian migrant population as a whole was dominated by Italians, Spaniards, Moroccans, and Turks, the last two recruited from 1964 under bilateral agreements. At the same time, the crisis of 1960 forced tens of thousands of Belgians to leave the Congo, and the later difficulties of Rwanda, Burundi, and Zaire (the name given to the Congo under Mobutu from 1971) created further links.

Portugal is the reverse case. For most of the postwar period it was a country of emigration, and large numbers of Portuguese workers, many of them avoiding military service or fleeing poverty, went to France, West Germany, and elsewhere. From the 1960s, workers from Cape Verde began coming to Lisbon to work in construction. Then the revolution brought a sudden inflow. Between 1974 and 1976, hundreds of thousands of people, estimated at between roughly 500,000 and 800,000, moved to Portugal from Angola, Mozambique, and other territories, in what became one of the largest population movements in modern Portuguese history, an addition of several percent to the population of a country of fewer than ten million. They included settlers, officials, soldiers, and those of mixed or African descent who had held Portuguese nationality. Portugal also later became a destination for African immigrants, notably from Cape Verde, Angola, and Guinea-Bissau, as its economy changed.

These cases demonstrate that postcolonial migration was highly dependent on nationality, timing, and the nature of colonial society. People who had served in colonial armies, people who had never lived in Europe but held European nationality, workers recruited under labor schemes, and refugees from civil war all moved under different legal and social conditions. The Division IV discussion of citizenship and race relations explores how states responded to these arrivals.

Division IV · Sections 128.13–128.16 · Principal period 1948–1980

Citizenship, Race Relations, and the Struggle for Equal Belonging

Imperial citizenship structures were dismantled or redefined, legal equality did not automatically produce social equality, and migrants and minorities actively shaped Europe's changing civic institutions.

Section 128.13

Nationality, Immigration Law, and the Redefinition of Citizenship

European empires had created layered categories of subjects, nationals, citizens, and protected persons. Decolonization forced governments to decide who belonged to the nation once empire ended, and the right to enter, live, work, and vote in the metropole proved a different matter from holding a particular nationality. In Britain, the British Nationality Act of 1948, which took effect on 1 January 1949, created the status of citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies and affirmed that citizens of independent Commonwealth countries, along with those of the United Kingdom and Colonies, were British subjects. The Act was driven partly by Canada's decision to create its own citizenship, and by a desire to preserve the idea of a common imperial status. It did not set out to promote immigration, but it left the right of entry of all these subjects unrestricted.

That freedom did not last. As migration from the Caribbean and South Asia increased and domestic hostility grew, especially after the unrest of 1958 (Section 128.15), the government turned to control. The Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962 introduced employment vouchers for Commonwealth citizens, with the Irish exempt through the Common Travel Area, and it came into force on 1 July 1962. The act was explicitly racialized in effect, although not in wording, because Commonwealth migrants who were not white formed the large majority of those affected. In 1968, a second act was passed in great haste in response to the movement of Kenyan Asians who held British passports and feared Africanization policies in Kenya. It required that those entering have a close connection, through a parent or grandparent, with the United Kingdom, which excluded many British passport holders in Africa. In the same year the Conservative politician Enoch Powell delivered a speech in Birmingham, on 20 April, attacking immigration and predicting social conflict, and was dismissed from the shadow cabinet, but his speech attracted substantial public support and demonstrated the political force of the issue.

The Immigration Act of 1971, which came into force on 1 January 1973, replaced the earlier system with one based on work permits and a new concept of "patriality," under which the right of abode depended on birth, descent, or other close ties to the United Kingdom. In practice, the concept favored people of British descent in the white Commonwealth. Critics at the time saw it as a legal restatement of racial preference, while supporters regarded it as a way to simplify and regulate entry. In 1972, Britain accepted some 27,000 Ugandan Asians expelled by Idi Amin, in an operation that reflected both moral obligation and the limits of the new regime. The process thus moved from imperial nationality toward territorial citizenship, completed with the British Nationality Act of 1981, which is outside this chapter's main period.

France's dilemma was different. French law distinguished nationality, civil status, and political rights. Subjects of overseas territories obtained French citizenship in 1946, but Muslim Algerians remained French nationals of local personal status, and political equality was never realized before independence. After Algerian independence, the ordinance of July 1962 provided that people of Algerian origin with local civil status had to formally declare their wish to retain French nationality within a limited period, whereas people of common-law civil status, mostly Europeans and some Jewish Algerians, remained French. This created legal complexity and exclusion, and some harkis who failed to complete formalities found themselves classified as foreigners. A 1968 agreement set special terms for Algerian migration. As in Britain, a shift from imperial status to territorial control resulted, and race was embedded in practice even where the law spoke of origin or status.

The Dutch, Belgian, and Portuguese cases differed again. Surinamese held Dutch nationality until independence in 1975, after which a transitional agreement governed their right of entry and nationality. In Belgium, Congolese had been colonial subjects rather than citizens and lost any claim at independence. In Portugal, a 1975 decree-law removed Portuguese nationality from many people born in the former colonies who did not meet criteria of residence or descent, a decision that left some who had been Portuguese in 1974 without that nationality. These variations show that the connection between empire and nationality was not stable, and that new states and old metropoles each redefined the boundaries of belonging.

Historical Vocabulary
Nationality, Citizenship, and the Right of Abode

Nationality describes a person's legal membership in a state for international purposes. Citizenship usually refers to the bundle of civil and political rights, such as voting and standing for office, that a state grants. The right of abode is the right to enter and remain in a territory without immigration control. In modern nation-states these usually coincide, but in empires they often did not. A colonial subject could hold the nationality of the imperial state without full political rights or any assurance of entry to the metropole, and a person could possess a passport yet face immigration control. Much of the history in this division turns on the distinction. The terms must be used carefully: describing colonial subjects as "British" or "French" can conceal the unequal rights that came with the label.

Section 128.14

Employment, Housing, and Racial Discrimination in Postwar Europe

Governments and employers wanted workers, but they were often reluctant to accept the people who came. Migrants faced discrimination in employment, housing, education, and access to public services, and the contrast between labor demand and unequal treatment is among the defining features of the period. In Britain, workers from the Caribbean and South Asia were commonly given unskilled or shift work, found their qualifications unrecognized, and were passed over for promotion. Some trade unions and workplaces operated informal colour bars, and some employers openly preferred white workers. A 1967 report by the research organization Political and Economic Planning, based on tests in which applicants of different backgrounds sought jobs, housing, and services, found substantial discrimination, and the evidence it gathered was influential in the passage of the 1968 Race Relations Act.

Housing was the most visible area of exclusion. Landlords and estate agents often refused Black and Asian tenants, and signs such as "No Coloureds" or "No Blacks" are documented in the record. The longer phrase "No Irish, no Blacks, no dogs" is widely remembered, but historians point out that its documentary evidence is thinner than its place in memory suggests, and that the wording of actual signs varied, which illustrates the distinction between memory and evidence. Because mortgages and council housing were hard to obtain, many migrants bought or rented run-down houses in inner-city districts, sometimes subdividing them, and exploitative landlords such as Peter Rachman in Notting Hill became notorious. Council housing allocation rules, including residence requirements, also disadvantaged newcomers. In political campaigns, such as the 1964 general election contest at Smethwick in the West Midlands, hostility to immigration was openly exploited, and the visit of the American activist Malcolm X to Smethwick in February 1965 drew international attention.

In France, the central problems were housing and employment segregation. Algerian and later Moroccan, Tunisian, and West African workers were concentrated in unskilled industrial jobs, often through contractors, and in shantytowns and hostels. The Nanterre bidonvilles, with their lack of running water and sanitation, were a prominent example, and the slow rate of rehousing made them lasting symbols. Official discourse labelled the "problem" of North African migrants in terms of social order and public health, and policing remained harsh. The racist violence of 1973, particularly in Marseille, was met with inadequate response, and the killing of the teenager Djellali Ben Ali in Paris in October 1971 sparked solidarity protests in which intellectuals such as Jean-Paul Sartre and Michel Foucault took part.

Careful distinctions are necessary. Institutional discrimination, meaning practices built into the rules and routines of organizations, differs from overt racist violence by individuals or crowds, and both differ from socioeconomic disadvantage, which also affected many white workers. They interacted, since poor housing and low pay could be justified by prejudice and reinforced by it, but they should not be treated as interchangeable. Differences also existed among countries. Britain's colonial legacy and its comparatively open nationality law produced a politics centered on race and immigration control, while French republican ideology, which officially recognized no distinctions of origin, often made discrimination harder to name and measure, and Dutch policy long assumed that migrant groups would eventually return or integrate.

Section 128.15

Racist Violence, Antiracist Campaigns, and the Politics of Equality

In late August and early September 1958, racist attacks and disturbances took place in Nottingham and in Notting Hill, London. In Notting Hill, groups of white youths attacked Black residents and their homes over several nights, and Caribbean residents organized to defend themselves, while the police and the press often treated the events as a question of immigration rather than of racist violence. In May 1959, the killing of Kelso Cochrane, a Black carpenter, in Notting Hill, with no one convicted, deepened anger. The events also prompted political organization. Claudia Jones, a Trinidad-born activist, founded the West Indian Gazette in 1958 and was among those who organized a cultural event in January 1959, an ancestor of the Notting Hill Carnival, to affirm Caribbean culture in the face of hostility.

The Bristol Bus Boycott of 1963 was a landmark of organized protest. The Bristol Omnibus Company refused to employ Black and Asian conductors and drivers, a policy supported by elements of the transport union, and a group of local activists, among them Paul Stephenson, Roy Hackett, Guy Bailey, Owen Henry, and Audley Evans, organized a boycott of the buses in April 1963. After weeks of publicity and political pressure, the company ended its colour bar on 28 August 1963, the same day as the March on Washington in the United States, a coincidence that helped link British and American civil rights narratives, although British campaigners had their own context. Other campaigns followed, such as strikes by South Asian workers at Mansfield Hosiery in 1972, Imperial Typewriters in Leicester in 1974, and the Grunwick film processing plant in London from 1976 to 1978, which involved mainly Asian women and raised questions about trade union support for immigrant workers.

The law developed in stages. The first government Race Relations Bill was presented to the House of Commons by Home Secretary Sir Frank Soskice in April 1965, and Soskice introduced its second reading in May. The resulting Race Relations Act of 1965 outlawed discrimination in specified public places and made incitement to racial hatred an offence, and it created a Race Relations Board to conciliate complaints, but it had weak enforcement and did not cover housing or employment. The 1968 Act extended coverage to housing, employment, and services, and set up a Community Relations Commission. The 1976 Act, passed by a Labour government, introduced the concept of indirect discrimination, which prohibits practices that appear neutral but disadvantage a racial group without justification, allowed individuals to bring complaints to tribunals and courts, and created the Commission for Racial Equality. Many activists argued that these laws were accompanied by tighter immigration control, an approach in which equality for those already present was balanced by restriction on those still to come. The law and its enforcement remained, nonetheless, an important recognition that discrimination was a public wrong.

Policing and the far right shaped the climate. The "sus" law, which allowed arrest on suspicion under the Vagrancy Act of 1824, was used disproportionately against young Black men. The Mangrove restaurant in Notting Hill became a focus of conflict, and the 1970–1971 trial of the Mangrove Nine ended in acquittals on the most serious charges, with the judge remarking on evidence of racial hatred. The National Front gained support in the 1970s, and anti-fascist mobilization, including the movement Rock Against Racism from 1976, grew. In April 1979, the teacher Blair Peach died during an anti-National Front protest in Southall, for which no police officer was prosecuted.

Elsewhere in Europe, mobilization took different forms. In France, migrant workers' organizations, trade unions, Catholic and Protestant groups, lawyers, and intellectuals campaigned against expulsions, for housing rights, and for the legal status of migrants. The Mouvement des travailleurs arabes, rent strikes in Sonacotra hostels from 1975, and campaigns following racist killings expressed a growing sense that migrants were not temporary guests. In the Netherlands, Moluccan and Surinamese associations pressed their claims, and in all these countries local activists, lawyers, churches, and unions played a role. The antiracist struggle was not simply a British story, but it took forms shaped by national traditions of citizenship.

Section 128.16

Families, Education, Religion, and the Formation of New European Generations

Many governments and many migrants initially regarded migration as temporary. Workers expected to return after saving money, and employers and ministries planned for rotation. By the 1960s and 1970s this assumption was weakening, as workers brought wives and children, formed families in Europe, and children were born and raised there. The restrictions of 1962 in Britain, which allowed entry of dependants but curtailed new work arrivals, and the French suspension of 1974, which left family entry open, both accelerated family settlement, since migrants feared that doors would close and chose to bring relatives while they still could. The settled community thus emerged partly as an unintended result of control.

Women and children had distinct experiences. Many Caribbean women had migrated as workers in their own right, and many left children behind with relatives, with painful separation that sometimes lasted years. South Asian and North African women often arrived to join husbands, and might be isolated by language, housing, and prevailing gender norms, though many also worked in factories or at home, and some became active in community and labor organizing. Children faced schools that were frequently unprepared. In Britain a 1965 official circular recommended dispersal of immigrant children when they exceeded roughly a third of a class, and many Caribbean children were placed in schools for the "educationally subnormal," a practice the Grenada-born educator Bernard Coard attacked in a 1971 pamphlet. Black parents and teachers responded by organizing supplementary Saturday schools. In France, children entered a school system committed to laïcité and the French language, and in the 1970s agreements with some countries of origin provided limited instruction in home languages.

Religious institutions became central. Mosques, gurdwaras, temples, and Pentecostal and other churches provided worship, community support, and a space for young people, and in many cases began as rooms in houses or former factories. Practical questions tested pluralism. In 1967 a Sikh bus conductor in Wolverhampton, Mohan Singh Gill, contested a rule against wearing a turban, and a 1976 law exempted turban-wearing Sikhs from motorcycle crash-helmet requirements. In France, prayer rooms in hostels and workplaces raised issues of public space and religion. These conflicts show the working out of the relation between inherited cultural tradition and national institutions.

Debates over integration followed. Roy Jenkins, then British Home Secretary, said in 1966 that he understood integration not as a flattening process of assimilation but as equal opportunity accompanied by cultural diversity in an atmosphere of mutual tolerance, a formulation often cited as an early British version of multiculturalism. The Netherlands and France worked from different models, the Dutch from a tradition of separate communal institutions and the French from a republican insistence on assimilation to a common citizenship. The chapter does not follow these debates beyond 1980, and the wider social movements of the 1960s and 1970s, including feminism and youth culture, are examined in Chapter 130, while the longer history of migration and identity returns in Chapter 137.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Schools, and Worship

Home life combined the practices of the country of origin with the realities of European cities. Families shared houses, pooled wages, cooked food from home, and maintained links through letters, tapes, and visits. Children often translated for parents at offices and shops, learning to move between languages and institutions. Schools could be places of opportunity, but also of low expectations and racist taunts, and parents sought supplementary classes in language, religion, and the home culture. Community associations organized sport, music, celebrations, and mutual aid, and local shops and restaurants made migrant cuisines part of everyday urban life. These routines, often invisible in political history, were the means through which new generations came to see themselves as both from and of Europe.

Section 128.17

Repatriates, Veterans, Former Settlers, and the Obligations of European States

Decolonization produced movements of people toward Europe who were not labor migrants: settlers, officials, soldiers, and auxiliaries. Their experiences differed markedly, and so did their reception. The French pieds-noirs, roughly 800,000 people, arrived chiefly in 1962 and were legally French citizens. The French state classified them as repatriates (rapatriés), a term that implies a return, even though many, whose families had lived in Algeria for generations, had never lived in France. A law of December 1961 established a framework of reception, and a compensation law of 1970 provided some indemnification for lost property, though settlers frequently regarded it as inadequate. Most settled in southern France, especially Marseille, Montpellier, and Provence, and the community developed strong associations and political influence, which shaped the memory of the war.

The harkis, though French nationals by status, received a different treatment. The state placed many in camps and isolated settlements, did not consistently recognize their service, and offered fewer opportunities than other repatriates. Their descendants have long contested the narrative of the war and the state's obligations. The Dutch case differs again. Indo-Europeans and Dutch from the East Indies were also classed as repatriates, though many were in fact born in Indonesia, and authorities in the early years offered limited housing and employment assistance and expected rapid assimilation. Compensation for pay and property lost in the war and under Japanese occupation was granted only slowly, and Indo-European organizations campaigned for decades. The Moluccan soldiers, by contrast, were treated as temporary guests whose military contracts had been terminated, and so were denied the rights of other repatriates.

In Portugal, the retornados were received by a state in revolutionary turmoil. The government created an institute to assist their return, and the large number arriving in 1975 strained housing and employment, with many families initially living in hotels, boarding houses, and makeshift accommodation. Some were of settler origin, some had been officials or soldiers, and others, born in Africa, were of African or mixed descent. Many retornados experienced resentment, because they were associated with the old regime, but the Portuguese economy and society absorbed them within a few years, and they have often been described as an important force in the country's later economic and political life. For Belgium, the evacuation of 1960 brought tens of thousands of Belgians home from the Congo, and for former military personnel of various colonial armies, rights to pensions and recognition were frequently disputed for decades.

Several general questions arise. What obligations does a state have to people who served it or lived under its authority overseas? Who counts as a returnee when many had never lived in the territory to which they "returned"? Why were some groups treated as heroes or victims and others as burdens? The answers depended on legal status, political utility, race, class, and public sympathy. They also depended on the story each country wished to tell about its colonial past, since to acknowledge the suffering of harkis or of colonial veterans was to acknowledge the injustice and betrayal of colonial promises.

Section 128.18

The Transformation of European Cities and Cultural Life

Migration changed European urban life visibly. In London, Brixton and Notting Hill became centers of Caribbean life, Southall of Punjabi life, and the East End, with Brick Lane, of Bangladeshi settlement, adding to older Jewish, Irish, and other layers. In Paris, the Goutte d'Or, Belleville, and suburbs on the outskirts, including industrial districts north and east of the city, became centers of North and West African settlement. In Amsterdam, the Bijlmermeer, a modernist housing estate completed in the late 1960s and early 1970s, housed many Surinamese migrants after its middle-class appeal faded. In Brussels, the Matongé district became a center of Congolese life, and Lisbon's outskirts developed neighborhoods of Cape Verdean and other African workers and retornados, often in informal settlements. These neighborhoods were shaped by discrimination and limited housing choices as much as by choice, but they also provided mutual aid, businesses, and social networks.

Cultural life was transformed. Food is the most obvious example: Indian, Pakistani, and Bangladeshi restaurants, many run by migrants from Sylhet, spread through British towns, couscous and other North African dishes entered French eating, and Indonesian and Surinamese cooking became part of Dutch life. Music provided another meeting point, from Caribbean calypso and later reggae and sound-system culture in Britain to North African and West African music in France. Notting Hill Carnival grew from the 1959 event and the street festivals of the 1960s into one of Europe's largest popular festivals. Religious buildings altered skylines. Migrant-owned shops, markets, and small businesses revitalized declining commercial streets, while public institutions, schools, and hospitals employed workers of immigrant origin.

Intellectually, the end of empire produced a body of thought that shaped European debate. Aimé Césaire, poet and politician from Martinique, helped develop the idea of négritude and published his Discourse on Colonialism in 1950, arguing that colonialism brutalized colonizers as well as colonized. Frantz Fanon, also from Martinique and trained as a psychiatrist, wrote Black Skin, White Masks (1952) on the psychology of racism and The Wretched of the Earth (1961), written during his engagement with the Algerian revolution, on colonial violence and liberation. The journal and publishing house Présence Africaine, founded in Paris in 1947, and the 1956 Congress of Black Writers and Artists gathered writers from Africa and the diaspora. In Britain, Caribbean writers such as George Lamming and Sam Selvon described migrant life in London in novels of the 1950s, and intellectuals such as Stuart Hall, who came from Jamaica in 1951, and C. L. R. James shaped British cultural thought. These figures differed in politics and context, and their influence in Europe grew over time.

The emphasis here remains on the connection between decolonization and metropolitan transformation, and not on a general history of popular culture. Cultural change was also contested: some residents welcomed it, while others reacted with hostility, and the far right built support by claiming to defend the nation against it. By the end of the 1970s, European cities were visibly more diverse, and a generation had grown up regarding European cities as home.

Section 128.19

Postcolonial Relationships: Economic Influence, Diplomacy, and Historical Memory

Independence ended formal rule but not the relationships that empire had created. Former colonies remained linked to Europe through trade, investment, aid, currency arrangements, military cooperation, education, and the continuing presence of European firms. France maintained especially dense ties with its former African territories, through defense agreements, military bases, and interventions, a network of personal and political relationships sometimes called Françafrique, and the CFA franc, a currency tied to the French treasury, whose reform was announced only in 2019. Britain maintained the Commonwealth and the sterling area, trade preferences, and military arrangements, though its role was weakened by the Rhodesian crisis after 1965 and by entry into the European Community (Chapter 133). Belgian companies retained major interests in the Congo, Dutch ties with Suriname were bolstered by a large aid agreement in 1975, and Portugal's links with Lusophone Africa were reshaped by civil war and migration.

European integration also shaped these ties. The Treaty of Rome of 1957 associated overseas territories with the European Economic Community, the Yaoundé Conventions of 1963 and 1969 extended arrangements to newly independent African states, and the Lomé Convention of 1975 linked the Community with dozens of African, Caribbean, and Pacific countries. These agreements provided preferential trade and aid and also entrenched particular patterns of dependence on primary commodity exports. Cold War politics offered some postcolonial states room to maneuver, and the Non-Aligned movement, which began at the Bandung Conference of 1955, provided a platform for resisting alignment with either bloc.

The nature of these relationships is contested. Memory of empire also differs. In Britain, public memory has often emphasized a narrative of orderly transfer of power and the Commonwealth, with the violence in Kenya or Malaya largely forgotten until litigation and research in the twenty-first century, while nostalgia and criticism coexist. In France, the Algerian War remained a subject of silence and conflict, with official recognition of the term "war" only in 1999 and a 2005 law, whose clause on the "positive role" of colonization was repealed in 2006, illustrating the continuing political stakes. In the Netherlands, the 2022 research and apology reopened debate. In Belgium, a parliamentary commission on the colonial past began work in 2020, and the Royal Museum for Central Africa in Tervuren reopened in 2018 after a renovation intended to address its colonial origins. In Portugal, the idea of a benign, multiracial colonization, influenced by the Brazilian writer Gilberto Freyre, long shaped public discourse. In former colonies, memory has centered on liberation struggles, losses, and unresolved inequalities. Because these memories differ, they are themselves historical evidence.

Historical Debate

Did Independence End Empire?

The question: Did formal independence end European imperial domination, or did it change the forms through which unequal relationships operated?

Interpretation A: Neocolonial continuity. Kwame Nkrumah, in a 1965 book, argued that newly independent states were subject to indirect economic and political control by former rulers and other powers. Some historians, drawing on the idea of "informal empire" developed by John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson in 1953, argue that economic dependence, military agreements, and political interference preserved European influence after sovereignty, as in some French and Belgian cases.
Interpretation B: Real transformation and postcolonial agency. Others stress that independent governments pursued their own interests, diversified partners, nationalized assets, joined international organizations, and exploited Cold War rivalry, and that European influence varied widely and often declined. They caution against assuming that every postcolonial relationship reproduced colonial domination, and note the responsibility of postcolonial elites for domestic outcomes.

Evidence: Both interpretations use trade and investment data, treaties and defense agreements, aid flows, and diplomatic records, as well as the accounts of postcolonial leaders and publics. Cases differ considerably, so generalization is difficult.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians describe the chronology of empire, whether 1960 is an ending or a transition, and how responsibility for postcolonial economic and political difficulties is assigned.

Section 128.20

Decolonization and the Remaking of Europe: Historical Synthesis

The end of European empires changed Europe in several connected ways. Politically, imperial withdrawal altered the standing of European states and the legitimacy of governments. Britain reoriented from empire toward alliance and Europe, France fought a war that brought down one republic and shaped the next, the Netherlands lost the territory it had regarded as vital to recovery, Belgium suffered a crisis in the Congo, and Portugal's dictatorship was overthrown by officers exhausted by colonial war. Demographically, migration brought people connected to former empires into European industries, cities, and families, as part of a larger movement of labor that also included southern Europeans and Turks. Legally, nationality and immigration law moved from imperial categories to territorial ones, often with racial effect. Socially, discrimination, racist violence, and antiracist organization pushed European societies toward debates about equality that continued for decades.

A distinction is essential between formal independence, which occurred on a specific date, and the social and political transformations that unfolded over decades. A flag could be lowered in a day, but a nationality law took years to be written, a neighborhood generations to form, and a public memory is still disputed. The chapter has also emphasized that the history is not simply one of European decision. Colonized peoples forced the issue through political organization and armed struggle, migrants shaped the societies they entered, and postcolonial states pursued their own strategies. At the same time, the chapter has avoided portraying postcolonial European society solely through conflict: migrants and their descendants contributed to economies, institutions, culture, and civic life.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the end of European overseas empires was also a transformation of Europe. In 1945 European states still governed a large part of the world, and most expected to continue doing so in some form. The war had weakened them financially and militarily, damaged their prestige through defeat and occupation, and mobilized colonial soldiers, workers, and economies in ways that politicized colonial populations. At the same time, the United Nations, the United States, the Soviet Union, and a growing network of Asian and African leaders created a changed international environment. Yet anticolonial nationalism did not arise spontaneously from European exhaustion. It had long roots in organizations such as the Indian National Congress and in movements in North Africa, Indochina, Indonesia, West Africa, and the Caribbean, and in 1945 it found new strength.

European governments responded in different ways. Britain withdrew from India in 1947 under pressure and in haste, with catastrophic violence during Partition, and then negotiated most subsequent transfers, though it fought brutal counterinsurgencies in Malaya and Kenya. The Netherlands attempted to reestablish control in Indonesia through two military offensives, and withdrew in 1949 after Indonesian resistance and international pressure, leaving a legacy of contested memory that was formally reexamined in 2022. Belgium moved from expectation of long rule to abrupt independence in the Congo in 1960 and a crisis that involved Belgian, Congolese, United Nations, and Cold War actors. France fought in Indochina until 1954, negotiated independence in Morocco, Tunisia, and most of sub-Saharan Africa, and fought a prolonged war in Algeria, where settler society, legal status, and national identity made withdrawal exceptionally difficult.

The Algerian War was the most consequential colonial conflict for metropolitan politics. It brought down the Fourth Republic in 1958, returned Charles de Gaulle to power, and established the Fifth Republic. It produced the practices of counterinsurgency and torture that divided French opinion, an attempted military putsch in 1961, and the terrorism of the OAS, and it extended into Paris through the police violence of 17 October 1961. The Évian Accords of March 1962 ended the war, but they could not guarantee the safety of settlers or harkis. Around 800,000 Europeans left within months, and harkis, many of whom were killed in Algeria, were treated inadequately by the French state when they reached France.

Portugal's case showed how colonial war could transform a regime. A dictatorship that tied national identity to empire fought in Angola, Guinea, and Mozambique from 1961, and a conscript army and professional officers grew weary and disillusioned. The Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 overthrew the Estado Novo, and the new government accepted decolonization, recognizing Guinea-Bissau in 1974 and granting independence to Mozambique, Cape Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe, and Angola in 1975. Angola then fell into civil war, and several hundred thousand people moved to Portugal. Spain's withdrawals from Equatorial Guinea and the Western Sahara completed the principal wave of European withdrawal from Africa, though some territorial questions remained unsettled.

Decolonization also reshaped European populations. Postwar boom conditions created demand for labor, and imperial connections of language, law, and kinship made former colonies an important source of workers. Britain received Caribbean and South Asian migrants, with the Empire Windrush of 1948 serving as a symbol; France drew workers from Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia, and West Africa; the Netherlands received Indo-Europeans, Moluccans, and Surinamese; Belgium attracted fewer colonial migrants; and Portugal absorbed the retornados. But postwar migration was not only postcolonial. Europe also recruited workers from Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Yugoslavia, and Turkey, and states treated these groups differently according to legal status, race, and perceived permanence.

Nationality and immigration law were central. The British Nationality Act of 1948 preserved a common status for subjects of the Crown, but a series of laws in 1962, 1968, and 1971 narrowed the right of entry, in effect along racial lines. France faced the problem of Algerian nationality after 1962, and other states defined and redefined who belonged. These changes show that nationality, citizenship, and the right of abode were separate concepts that governments could and did pull apart. The suspension of French labor migration in 1974 and the Dutch visa requirement for Surinamese in 1980 further illustrate how states responded to economic change and political pressure.

Equality was a long struggle. Migrants faced discrimination in work and housing, racist violence in places such as Nottingham, Notting Hill, and Marseille, and unequal policing. They did not simply endure these conditions: the Bristol Bus Boycott, strikes by migrant workers, community organizations, antiracist campaigns, and legal activism pushed governments toward action. Britain's Race Relations Acts of 1965, 1968, and 1976 built a body of anti-discrimination law, though it coexisted with tighter immigration control. Elsewhere, French and Dutch activists worked within different models of citizenship, and in all cases legal equality did not automatically yield social equality.

Families, schools, and religious institutions turned temporary migration into permanent settlement and created new generations born in Europe. Cities were transformed in their neighborhoods, food, music, religious buildings, and cultural life, and writers and thinkers such as Césaire and Fanon, and Caribbean and South Asian authors in Britain, contributed to postcolonial thought and literature. Repatriates, harkis, Indo-Europeans, Moluccans, and retornados raised questions about the obligations of states to those connected to their empires, and their varying fortunes show that legal status, political usefulness, and public sympathy shaped treatment.

Independence did not end every relationship. Economic ties, defense agreements, currency arrangements, educational networks, and personal connections persisted, and scholars debate whether these represent neocolonial domination or the more complex outcome of negotiated relations among unequal but sovereign states. Public memories of empire also diverged: Britain's emphasis on orderly transfer, France's contested memory of Algeria, the Netherlands' reassessment of its Indonesian war, Belgium's debates about Congo, and Portugal's narrative of benign colonization all reflect different national experiences. Because the chapter has distinguished formal independence from longer social and political change, it presents decolonization as an evolving set of relationships.

The chapter's governing historical conclusion is that decolonization transformed European identities as well as overseas political structures. Colonized peoples helped determine when and how empires ended, and the consequences came home in the form of constitutional crisis, military revolution, new populations, new laws, and new arguments about who belongs to Europe. These developments connect to later themes in the textbook: the social movements of Chapter 130, the southern European democratization of Chapter 131, European integration in Chapter 133, and migration and identity in Chapter 137. They should not be treated as a closed episode but as a continuing part of European history.

Key Terms

Decolonization
The end of colonial rule and the transfer of sovereignty to formerly subordinate societies, comprising many different histories and continuing in cultural and economic forms after formal independence.
Metropole
The European homeland of an empire, as distinct from its overseas territories.
Anticolonial nationalism
Political movements that organized against foreign rule and demanded self-government or independence, often combining political, cultural, and economic claims.
Self-determination
The principle that peoples should choose their own political status; invoked by colonial movements and accepted selectively by European governments and the United Nations.
Trusteeship
A United Nations system under which former mandates and some former enemy colonies were administered by states accountable to the organization, as with Italian Somaliland and Ruanda-Urundi.
Dominion
A self-governing state within the British Commonwealth; India and Pakistan began as dominions in 1947.
Commonwealth
A voluntary association of former British territories, which after 1949 included republics as well as states recognizing the Crown.
French Union
The framework created in 1946 to combine metropolitan France and overseas territories, which gave limited representation and preserved French authority.
Partition
The division of British India in 1947 into India and Pakistan, accompanied by mass violence and displacement.
Counterinsurgency
Military and political methods used to suppress rebellion, including population relocation, detention, and, in Algeria and Kenya, abuses such as torture.
Putsch
An attempted coup, such as the generals' revolt in Algiers in April 1961.
FLN
The National Liberation Front, the Algerian movement that launched the insurrection of 1954 and negotiated independence.
OAS
The Organisation armée secrète, a terrorist group of French Algeria supporters active in 1961–1962.
Pieds-noirs
European settlers in Algeria and their descendants, most of whom left for France around 1962.
Harkis
Muslim Algerian auxiliaries who served the French army, many of whom faced reprisals after independence or poor treatment in France.
Retornados
People who relocated to Portugal from the former African territories in 1974–1976, including settlers, officials, soldiers, and others.
Carnation Revolution
The military coup of 25 April 1974 that overthrew Portugal's Estado Novo and led to decolonization.
Nationality
Legal membership in a state for international purposes, which in empires did not always carry equal political rights or a right of entry.
Citizenship
The set of civil and political rights a state grants to its members, distinct from nationality in colonial systems.
Right of abode
The right to enter and remain in a territory without immigration control; in British law after 1971 tied to patriality.
Patriality
A concept in the British Immigration Act 1971 that defined the right of abode by birth or descent, favoring people with British ancestry.
Family reunification
The legal entry of spouses and children of migrants, which continued after labor recruitment ended and contributed to permanent settlement.
Chain migration
Migration through networks of kin and fellow villagers, concentrating migrants from particular regions in particular destinations.
Colour bar
Practices of racial exclusion in employment, housing, and services, formal or informal, as in Britain and elsewhere.
Institutional discrimination
Disadvantage built into the rules and routines of organizations, distinct from individual acts of prejudice or violence.
Indirect discrimination
A concept in the British Race Relations Act 1976 covering rules that appear neutral but disadvantage a racial group without justification.
Bidonville
A shantytown, as at Nanterre near Paris, where many North African workers lived in poor conditions.
Multiculturalism
A policy approach and social reality that accepts cultural diversity within a common civic framework, contrasted with assimilation.
Négritude
A literary and political movement, associated with Aimé Césaire and others, that affirmed Black identity and culture against colonial racism.
Neocolonialism
The argument that former colonies remained subject to indirect economic or political control after independence.
Postcolonial
A term describing the period after colonial rule and an analytical approach to its continuing effects.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the end of empire was not a distant event but a transformation within European societies. The governments that reconstructed their economies and democracies in the years after 1945, as earlier chapters of Part XIX have shown, were also managing the loss of overseas authority, and that process shaped their constitutions, armies, laws, and public arguments. The Fifth Republic and the Carnation Revolution cannot be understood without colonial war, and the demography of modern Europe cannot be understood without the movements of people that decolonization set in motion.

The chapter also reveals how varied imperial endings were. Britain's negotiated withdrawal, the Netherlands' failed reconquest, Belgium's abrupt exit, France's divided experience, and Portugal's revolutionary collapse produced different political outcomes and different postcolonial societies. At each stage, colonized peoples were historical actors, whose organization and resistance forced decisions that European governments might otherwise have postponed. Understanding them as actors, rather than as recipients of independence, is essential to an accurate account.

The legacies persisted well beyond the dates of independence. Questions of nationality, discrimination, and belonging, begun in the 1940s and 1950s, continued into later decades and return in Chapter 137, while memories of colonial violence and displacement are still contested. The chapter contributes to Volume VI's argument that postwar Europe was shaped not only by Cold War division and integration but also by the unwinding of empire and the diversification of European society, so that contemporary Europe cannot be treated as the predetermined culmination of a purely continental history.

Chapter 129 turns to the eastern half of the continent, where Soviet domination and the uprisings against it raise other questions of sovereignty, legitimacy, and resistance. Those questions differ in important ways from those of overseas empire, and students should keep the distinctions in view while recognizing that across Europe, in different forms, societies were contesting who held power and who belonged.
