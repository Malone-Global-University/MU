Christianity, Religious Pluralism, and Secularization

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Europe's religious history cannot be told as a single road from pagan antiquity to Christian unity and then to secular modernity. It is better understood as a long sequence of negotiations among belief, institutions, rulers, communities, and consciences, negotiations that produced different results in Rome and Constantinople, Córdoba and Cracow, Geneva and Istanbul, Paris and Lublin, and that are still producing different results today.

Consider five buildings, each standing in a different European religious world. In the Hagia Sophia of Constantinople, completed in the sixth century under Justinian, the Eastern Roman emperor and the patriarch shared a ceremonial space in which imperial power and Christian liturgy were meant to appear as aspects of a single order. In the great synagogue and study houses of the Rhineland towns of the eleventh century, Jewish communities maintained legal, scholarly, and commercial lives under the protection, and the vulnerability, of bishops and emperors. In the Great Mosque of Córdoba, begun in the eighth century, Muslim rulers governed a mixed society in which Christians and Jews held a legally recognized but subordinate status. In a Reformed church in the Dutch Republic of the seventeenth century, the public church was Calvinist, yet Catholics, Lutherans, Mennonites, and Jews worshipped in a patchwork of tolerated, sometimes discreet, sometimes quietly open, settings. And in a mosque, a parish church, a synagogue, or an empty cathedral in a European city today, one may find a believing congregation, a heritage tourist, or simply a building whose religious function has been replaced by a concert hall, a museum, or a shop. No single narrative about "Christian Europe" explains all five.

This chapter opens Part XXI, the final comparative Part of the textbook. Unlike most of the chapters before it, it does not retell events in a continuous chronological narrative. Its task is synthetic: to gather the religious developments examined across all six volumes and to ask how they fit together. The governing question is how Europe moved from diverse ancient religious worlds, through a long era of Christian institutional dominance, toward modern societies characterized by differing combinations of religious continuity, pluralism, and secularization, and why that movement was neither uniform nor inevitable.

The chapter proceeds through five divisions of four sections each. The first examines the religious worlds of antiquity and the formation of Christian Europe. The second examines medieval Christendom alongside the Orthodox East, Jewish communities, and the Islamic presence in Iberia, Sicily, and the Balkans. The third addresses the fragmentation of western Christianity and the confessional systems, conflicts, and arrangements of toleration that followed. The fourth traces the reconfiguration of religious authority under Enlightenment criticism, revolution, nationalism, and the modern state. The fifth carries the story through the catastrophes of the twentieth century, the Cold War, migration, and the contested meanings of secularization in contemporary Europe. These divisions are analytical stages, not a timetable that every region followed simultaneously.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, Christianity is central to European history but is not identical with it. Judaism has been present in Europe for as long as Christianity has existed and, in the Mediterranean, longer. Islam has been a European religion in Iberia, Sicily, the Balkans, the Volga and Crimean regions, and, since the twentieth century, in virtually every European state. Unbelief, indifference, and religious dissent also belong to the European record. Second, the chapter evaluates religious institutions historically, not theologically. It asks what churches, synagogues, mosques, and movements did, how they were organized, and how contemporaries experienced them, without adopting or rejecting the truth claims of any tradition. Third, no religious community was internally homogeneous. "The Church," "the Jews," and "the Muslims" were always collections of differing practices, jurisdictions, and opinions.

The evidence for religious history is unusually varied. Theological treatises, conciliar decrees, and papal letters document institutional doctrine but rarely the beliefs of farmers and townspeople. Parish registers, visitation records, wills, church-court cases, and inquisitorial depositions allow historians to approach lived practice. Inscriptions, church and synagogue architecture, relics, images, and liturgical books provide material evidence. Legal texts, from imperial edicts to modern constitutions, record the boundaries states drew around religious communities. Autobiographies, sermons, pamphlets, and, from the twentieth century, opinion surveys and census categories add further layers. Each type of source reflects the viewpoint of the people who produced it, and the chapter attends throughout to whose voices are missing.

Chapter 140 depends on earlier chapters for detail it does not repeat. Christianity's emergence and doctrinal development are examined in Chapters 23 and 26, the Great Schism in Chapter 40, the Reformation and its conflicts in Chapters 61 through 67, and the Holocaust in Chapter 121. This chapter synthesizes their long-term religious consequences and compares them. It also prepares the ground for Chapter 141, which will examine war, states, law, and sovereignty as comparative problems, and for Chapters 142 through 144, which treat empire, social structure, and Europe's relationship with the wider world.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why pre-Christian Europe had no single religious identity and how Christianity's expansion built on, competed with, and absorbed existing religious practices.
  2. Trace the institutional development of Christianity from persecuted minority to imperial religion and the conversion of post-Roman, northern, central, and eastern European societies as a prolonged social process.
  3. Compare Latin Christianity, Byzantine Orthodoxy, and other eastern Christian traditions in their institutions, relationships to political authority, and liturgical and theological emphases.
  4. Describe the history of Jewish communities in Europe as a history of scholarship, commerce, law, and family life as well as of exclusion and persecution.
  5. Analyze the presence of Islam in Iberia, Sicily, and southeastern Europe, and distinguish coexistence, conflict, and cultural exchange in each setting.
  6. Distinguish medieval reform and dissent from the sixteenth-century Reformations and explain the differences among Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, radical Protestant, and Catholic reform movements.
  7. Assess confessionalization as an interpretation of early modern religious and political change, including its explanatory strengths and its limits.
  8. Differentiate selective toleration, corporate religious privilege, state church establishment, and modern individual religious freedom, and evaluate the religious settlements of 1555, 1598, 1648, and 1689.
  9. Evaluate the roles of Enlightenment criticism, revolution, emancipation, and nationalism in transforming religious authority and the legal status of religious communities between the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
  10. Interpret the varied responses of churches and believers to antisemitism, nationalism, the world wars, and the Holocaust, distinguishing anti-Judaism, modern antisemitism, and Nazi racial ideology.
  11. Compare the experience of religion under communist regimes and in postwar democratic societies, and describe the contemporary religious pluralism produced by migration and by long-established minority communities.
  12. Evaluate secularization as a contested, multidimensional concept and explain why European religious history cannot be reduced to a single movement from belief to unbelief.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what senses was Europe ever "Christian," and how should historians distinguish institutional Christianity from the religious lives of ordinary people?
  2. How did the relationship between religious institutions and political authority differ in Latin western Europe, Byzantium and the Orthodox world, and the Ottoman lands, and why did those differences matter?
  3. Why have Jewish and Muslim communities so often been treated by historians as external to European history, and what changes when they are placed inside it?
  4. What distinguishes toleration as a pragmatic arrangement from religious freedom as an individual right, and when did each appear in European law?
  5. To what extent did the Reformation create religious pluralism, and to what extent did it create new systems of religious coercion?
  6. How did Enlightenment criticism, revolution, and nationalism alter the sources of religious authority without simply eliminating religion?
  7. How did religious institutions respond to antisemitism, totalitarian regimes, and war in the twentieth century, and why did responses differ so widely?
  8. Why did secularization proceed so differently in France, Britain, Poland, the Netherlands, Greece, Estonia, and the former East Germany?
  9. What kinds of evidence allow historians to measure religious belief, practice, and belonging, and what are the limitations of each?
  10. Why is it historically misleading to describe contemporary Europe as simply "secular," and equally misleading to describe it as simply "Christian"?

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established that Europe is a geographic, cultural, and political idea whose boundaries have shifted over time, and Chapter 2 examined the physical environment in which European societies developed. A further distinction is essential for this chapter: Europe and Christendom are not the same thing. Christendom, a term examined in the vocabulary feature below, referred to the community of Christian peoples, a community that extended well beyond the geographic limits of Europe into Anatolia, the Levant, Egypt, Ethiopia, and the Caucasus, and that was itself divided among Latin, Greek, Armenian, Syriac, Coptic, and other churches. The Christendom that medieval Latin writers had in mind was smaller than the Christian world as a whole and larger than any single European state. The modern idea of "Christian Europe" is a historical construction that gained particular force when Latin Christendom's political unity dissolved and when Europeans came into sustained contact with the non-Christian world.

Religion in Europe before Christianity was embedded in civic life, household ritual, agricultural cycles, and the authority of rulers. Greek and Roman religion consisted largely of cult, the sacrifices, festivals, and rites that sustained the relationship between a community and its gods, and relatively little of what later traditions would call creed. It was tied to cities and households and was compatible with the worship of many deities. Judaism was exceptional within this world in its exclusive worship of a single God and in its practice of communal law and scriptural study, and Jewish communities were already scattered across the Mediterranean in the Hellenistic and Roman periods. The religious practices of the Celtic, Germanic, Slavic, Baltic, and Finno-Ugric peoples are known primarily through archaeology, later Christian polemic, and the writings of Greek and Roman outsiders, and historians therefore reconstruct them with considerable caution, as Chapter 6 emphasized for the Iron Age societies of northern and central Europe.

Across two thousand years, five long-term relationships structure the history surveyed here. The first is between religious institutions and political rulers, which varied from close alliance to open conflict and from identification to separation. The second is between a dominant religion and the minority communities that lived alongside it, a relationship usually unequal, often negotiated, and occasionally violent. The third is between official doctrine and popular practice, which never coincided perfectly. The fourth is between religion and knowledge, including scholastic theology, humanist philology, natural philosophy, Enlightenment criticism, and modern science. The fifth is between religious belonging as a matter of community or birth and religious belief as a matter of personal conviction. The long history of European religion is in large part the history of how the balance among these relationships changed.

Finally, the chapter relies on a vocabulary that historians use with care. "Paganism" is a Christian category applied to a wide range of non-Christian practices, and it conceals more variety than it reveals. "Heresy" and "orthodoxy" are judgments made by institutions with the power to define them, and the historian must distinguish the contemporaries' labels from the diversity of the beliefs involved. "Toleration" before the eighteenth century usually meant forbearance of a practice the authorities considered mistaken, not recognition of a right. "Secularization" names several different processes, which are separated in Section 140.20. Using these terms precisely, and noting when they are retrospective or contested, is part of the historical method of the chapter.

Core Vocabulary Preview

The following 24 terms are defined briefly for orientation. More detailed definitions and additional terms appear in the end-of-chapter Key Terms section.

Christendom
The community of Christian peoples, and in the medieval Latin west an ideal of a society unified under Christian faith; distinct from the geography of Europe and divided among Latin, Orthodox, and other churches.
Latin Christianity
The Christian tradition of the western Roman Empire and its successors, using Latin liturgy and law and recognizing papal primacy in the medieval period.
Orthodoxy
The Christian tradition associated with Constantinople and the Greek and Slavic churches; also used generally for correct doctrine as defined by an authority, a label that depended on who held power to define it.
Heresy
A doctrine or movement condemned by church authorities as contrary to accepted teaching; the label expressed institutional judgment and often hid considerable diversity among those so labeled.
Conversion
A change of religious adherence or recognized identity, often involving both individual decisions and prolonged communal or institutional change.
Schism
A formal division within a church; the Great Schism between Latin and Greek churches developed gradually, with 1054 as a symbolic date, and the Western Schism of 1378 to 1417 divided the Latin church between rival popes.
Conciliarism
The late medieval theory that a general council held authority within the church above the pope, influential at Constance.
Anticlericalism
Criticism of clergy for wealth, privilege, or misconduct; present in medieval and modern societies and not identical with rejection of religion.
Confession
A formally defined religious body and statement of faith, such as Lutheran, Reformed, or Catholic, in early modern usage.
Confessionalization
A historical interpretation describing how churches and states together shaped confessional identities and discipline in early modern Europe.
Toleration
Forbearance of a religion an authority regards as mistaken; before the eighteenth century usually conditional and revocable, and distinct from a right to religious freedom.
Dissent
Religious disagreement or worship outside an established or officially sanctioned church or confession.
Established church
A church officially recognized and supported by the state, such as the Church of England or, formerly, the Lutheran churches of the Nordic states.
Concordat
An agreement between the papacy and a state regulating the church's legal position, such as Napoleon's of 1801.
Emancipation
The removal of legal disabilities based on religion, as with Catholic and Jewish emancipation in the nineteenth century.
Laïcité
The French principle of state neutrality toward religion, formalized in the 1905 law; related to, but distinct from, secularism in other countries.
Disestablishment
The ending of a church's official status, as with the Church of Ireland under the Irish Church Act of 1869 (effective 1871) and the Church of Sweden in 2000.
Anti-Judaism
Religious and cultural hostility to Jews and Judaism rooted in Christian theology and practice, in which conversion was in principle an exit.
Antisemitism
A modern term, popularized in the late 1870s, for political and racial hostility to Jews that treats them as a biologically defined group.
Deism
The belief in a creator God known through reason and nature, without special revelation or church authority, common among Enlightenment writers.
Secularization
A set of processes including institutional differentiation of religion from other spheres, declining belief or practice, and privatization of religion; not an inevitable disappearance of religion.
Pluralism
The coexistence of several religions or worldviews within a society, which can be legally recognized or only informal.
Ecumenism
The movement for cooperation and unity among Christian churches, institutionalized in the World Council of Churches in 1948.
Freedom of religion
Legal protection of belief, nonbelief, religious change, and religious practice, subject in some systems to narrowly defined restrictions.
Division I · Religious Worlds and the Formation of Christian Europe · Section 140.1

Europe Before Christian Religious Dominance

No religion had been dominant across the whole of Europe before Christianity, and the idea that one should be would itself have seemed unusual. The religious landscape of the first millennium BCE consisted of overlapping local, civic, and household practices. Greek religion, discussed in Chapter 13, centered on the polis: public sacrifice, festivals, oracles such as Delphi, and the cults of particular gods and heroes were responsibilities of the citizen body and its magistrates, and participation expressed membership in the community. Roman religion followed a similar logic. The state maintained correct relations with the gods through priestly colleges and public rites, households honored their own ancestral and domestic spirits, and the Romans readily incorporated foreign deities into their religious system, a pattern that modern scholars sometimes describe as the Roman identification of local gods with their own, the interpretatio Romana.

Alongside civic cult stood movements of more personal religious commitment. The mystery cults, including the Eleusinian rites in Greece and the later cults of Isis, Mithras, and Cybele in the Roman world, offered initiates a closer relationship with a deity and, in some cases, hopes for the afterlife. Philosophical schools offered their own accounts of the divine and of how to live. These forms of religious life were not exclusive. A Roman might participate in public rites, honor domestic spirits, and join a mystery cult without contradiction, since the dominant concept was the proper performance of ritual and not exclusive adherence to a creed.

Beyond the Mediterranean, religious life is known far less directly. Celtic, Germanic, Slavic, Baltic, and Finno-Ugric societies left no extensive written religious texts in the pre-Christian period. Historians rely on archaeological evidence, including votive deposits, sanctuaries, burials, and bog offerings, and on descriptions by Greek and Roman authors such as Caesar and Tacitus, who wrote for Roman audiences and often interpreted northern customs through Roman categories. Medieval Christian writers, including Scandinavian authors of the thirteenth century who recorded older traditions, preserved further material but filtered it through their own concerns. The result is a body of evidence that is rich in fragments and poor in systematic doctrine. It supports the conclusion that these societies had varied, locally rooted religious practices connected to agriculture, kinship, warfare, and rulership, but it does not support the construction of a unified "pagan religion," nor a clear picture of what ordinary people believed.

Jewish communities were already part of this world. After the Babylonian exile and especially in the Hellenistic period, Jewish communities lived in cities from Alexandria to Rome, maintained the worship of one God, the study of scripture, and distinctive legal and dietary observances, and had an uneasy but often workable place within Greek and Roman political systems. Roman authorities generally permitted Jewish worship, though the relationship was marked by episodes of conflict, including the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE after the Jewish revolt and the suppression of the Bar Kokhba revolt in the 130s CE. By the early imperial period, Jewish communities in the Mediterranean diaspora, including Italy, Greece, and the Danubian provinces, were a normal if distinctive part of urban life.

The central point for what follows is that the religious order Christianity would later displace or absorb was not a monolith but a plurality. Religion was bound to political authority, since rulers and magistrates were also ritual leaders, and to community identity. What distinguished Christianity, and in a different way Judaism, was the demand for exclusive worship and the formation of communities defined by belief and practice and not by city or ethnicity. That feature, rather than any simple contrast between "pagan" and "Christian," accounts for much of the friction and much of the transformative energy of the centuries that follow.

Section 140.2

Christianity's Origins and Expansion within the Roman World

Christianity emerged in the first century CE within Second Temple Judaism, in the Roman province of Judaea, among followers of Jesus of Nazareth who understood him as the awaited Messiah. The earliest Christian writings, the letters of Paul and, somewhat later, the Gospels, show a movement still negotiating its relationship to Jewish law and identity. The question of whether non-Jewish converts needed to observe the Torah was a central early controversy. Over the following decades and centuries the relationship between the two traditions became one of increasing separation, though historians now stress that the "parting of the ways" was gradual, uneven, and different from place to place, with overlapping practices persisting in some communities well into late antiquity.

The new movement spread along the networks of the Roman Mediterranean: sea routes, roads, Greek as a common language of the eastern half of the empire, and, above all, the urban Jewish diaspora communities that provided initial audiences and meeting places. Early Christian communities were small, concentrated in cities, and organized around house gatherings, shared meals, scripture reading, and mutual aid. By the second and third centuries they had developed offices of bishop, presbyter, and deacon, a body of authoritative writings, and a practice of exchanging letters and delegates that linked communities across considerable distances. Christianity's appeal drew on its offer of belonging to a community defined by belief and practice, its care for the poor and the sick, and its promise of salvation, though historians continue to debate the relative weight of these factors and caution against explaining a complex social process by any single cause.

Christianity was never without internal diversity. Second- and third-century communities held varied views about the nature of Christ, the status of the Hebrew scriptures, the role of women, the relationship to Roman society, and the meaning of martyrdom. Writers who defended what became the dominant tradition, such as Irenaeus, labeled rival views heresy, and their writings are among our main sources for the movements they condemned. Discoveries of ancient manuscripts, including the Nag Hammadi codices found in Egypt in 1945, have allowed modern scholars to read some alternative traditions directly, though how to categorize them and how widely they circulated remain debated. The important historical point is that "orthodoxy" was constructed through disputes over time, not simply inherited intact.

The relationship between Christians and Roman authorities was episodic rather than continuous. Local hostility, rumors about Christian practices, and refusal to participate in civic cult sometimes led to accusations and punishment, and the emperors Decius in the 250s and Diocletian from 303 ordered empire-wide measures. Earlier persecutions were mostly local and sporadic, and the scale of persecution as a whole, as well as the numbers of martyrs, is a subject on which later Christian tradition tended to magnify and modern scholars tend to be cautious. Nonetheless, the memory of martyrdom became foundational for Christian identity. The sources for early Christianity are overwhelmingly Christian, and the view of outsiders, such as Pliny's letter to the emperor Trajan about Christians in Bithynia, is correspondingly valuable though sparse.

What the evidence shows by the later third century is a church that was numerically significant in many eastern cities, present though less dense in the west, and institutionally capable, with regional synods of bishops and an increasingly developed system of charity and discipline. The expansion of Christianity before Constantine was therefore substantial, but the Christian empire of the fourth century was not an inevitable outcome. Chapter 23 examines this process in detail; the aim here is to emphasize that Christianity entered European history as a movement of urban networks, not as a state religion, and that its subsequent institutional power was acquired through a particular sequence of political decisions.

Section 140.3

Imperial Christianity, Doctrine, and Ecclesiastical Authority

Constantine's rise to sole rule between 306 and 324 CE changed the legal and political position of Christianity. His support for the Christian church, expressed through legal toleration associated with the so-called Edict of Milan of 313 (which scholars now often describe as a joint policy statement with the eastern emperor Licinius rather than a single edict), the return of confiscated property, grants of money and land, and the building of churches, transformed Christianity from a vulnerable movement into a favored institution. Constantine's personal religious convictions, and the relationship between his reported vision before the battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312 and his later policy, remain subjects of scholarly discussion, but the effects of imperial patronage are clear: bishops acquired legal privileges, the church received wealth and public standing, and the emperor became a participant in Christian debates.

That participation was significant. When controversy over the teaching of the presbyter Arius, who argued for a distinction between God the Father and the Son that others considered inadequate, threatened the unity of the eastern churches, Constantine convened the Council of Nicaea in 325. The council's creed, subsequently revised at Constantinople in 381, became the basic statement of Trinitarian orthodoxy for most later Christians. Nicaea's example established the pattern of ecumenical councils, assemblies of bishops that defined doctrine, but it did not end the controversy, and Arian and related views persisted, notably among the Goths and other peoples converted in the fourth century, with consequences for the post-Roman kingdoms discussed in Chapter 27. Later councils, especially Ephesus in 431 and Chalcedon in 451, produced further definitions about the person of Christ that some churches in Egypt, Syria, and Armenia declined to accept. The resulting divisions created enduring non-Chalcedonian traditions, including the Coptic, Syriac, and Armenian churches, whose history belongs largely outside Europe but whose existence demonstrates that Christendom was divided early.

The fourth century also saw the gradual Christianization of public life and legal restrictions on other religions. Emperors increasingly favored Christians for office, banned public sacrifice in various laws, and acted against pagan temples and practices. The edict of 380 attributed to Theodosius I and his colleagues commanded their subjects to follow the faith of the Roman bishop and the bishop of Alexandria, and legislation in the 390s forbade pagan cult more broadly. The enforcement of these laws was uneven, local pagan practice and elite intellectual paganism persisted for generations, and violence against temples and philosophers, as well as conflicts over the removal of the Altar of Victory from the Roman Senate house, show a contested process rather than a swift triumph. Jews retained legal recognition under Roman law but experienced growing restrictions on building synagogues, holding office, and marrying Christians. Chapter 25 and Chapter 26 explore these developments in detail.

Equally important were developments in ecclesiastical authority. The office of bishop, already central, acquired judicial functions, administrative duties, and, increasingly, civic leadership as the imperial administration weakened in the west. The bishop of Rome claimed a primacy based on the tradition of Peter and Paul, a claim accepted in varying degrees; the bishops of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem developed their own patriarchal standing. Monasticism, which began in Egypt and Syria and spread west, created institutions that were at once ascetic and economically significant, and offered an alternative form of religious authority to that of bishops. These institutions provided the framework for the religious organization of post-Roman Europe.

The historical significance of these changes lies in the way doctrinal authority, political power, and institutional development became entangled without becoming identical. Emperors enforced councils, but councils also resisted emperors; bishops accepted imperial support while asserting that spiritual authority was independent. This tension, between a church that depended on state protection and a church that claimed to stand above it, would reappear in the Investiture Conflict, in the Reformation, in the French Revolution, and in the modern debates over church and state. The conversion of the empire, in short, solved a problem for Christians and created a durable set of new ones.

Section 140.4

Conversion, Continuity, and the Christianization of European Societies

The Christianization of Europe was not complete when the Roman Empire became officially Christian. It took many more centuries, and in northeastern Europe the process continued into the late fourteenth century. A useful way to understand it is to compare patterns of conversion across regions, remembering that conversion was a social and institutional process that began, but did not end, with the baptism of a ruler.

In the former western Roman provinces, Christianity survived through episcopal networks that outlived imperial administration. The Frankish king Clovis' baptism, usually placed around 500 CE though its exact date is debated, aligned a powerful ruler with Catholic Christianity rather than with the Arian Christianity of other successor kingdoms, with long-term consequences examined in Chapters 29 and 30. The Visigothic rulers of Iberia moved from Arian to Catholic Christianity by the end of the sixth century. In Ireland, which had never been part of the Roman Empire, Christianity was introduced in the fifth century and developed a distinctive monastic organization and a scholarly culture whose missionaries influenced Britain and the continent. In Anglo-Saxon England, a mission sent by Pope Gregory I under Augustine arrived in Kent in 597, joining and competing with Irish and British Christian traditions; the English kingdoms converted over the following century, with notable reversals when rulers died.

Scandinavia, central Europe, and the Slavic world followed in later centuries, often through the conversion of rulers who saw political advantages in alliance with the church, in access to literate administrators, and in recognition by powerful neighbors. Denmark's royal conversion occurred in the tenth century; Norway and Sweden converted over the tenth to twelfth centuries, with Iceland adopting Christianity by decision of its assembly around the year 1000. In central Europe, Mieszko I of Poland accepted baptism in 966, and Stephen of Hungary established a Christian kingdom around 1000. In the east and southeast, Boris of Bulgaria accepted Christianity from Constantinople in the 860s, and Vladimir of Kyiv adopted Christianity in 988, aligning Rus with the Byzantine tradition, developments examined in Chapters 35 and 38. The last major pagan state in Europe, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, converted under Jogaila in the 1380s through an arrangement connected with the dynastic union with Poland. Sámi and some other northern peoples were Christianized later still, and in some places, Christianization was accompanied by military conquest, as in the Baltic crusades.

Conversion rarely meant immediate replacement of older practice. Missionary strategies, as the correspondence of Gregory I and later accounts such as Bede's history indicate, often included adapting rather than eliminating existing customs: converting sacred sites into churches, reinterpreting festivals, and substituting saints for older protective figures. Modern scholars debate how much "survival" of pre-Christian belief this reflects and how much is a later construction by Christian authors or nineteenth-century folklorists. What seems clear is that the Christianity that took root was shaped by local custom, and that parish structures, pastoral care, and basic religious instruction took centuries to reach most rural populations. Monasteries and cathedral chapters served as centers of literacy and administration, as well as of religion.

Conversion also had social costs and winners. Rulers gained new instruments of governance, including writing, law, and ecclesiastical wealth; aristocratic families founded monasteries; women's roles in religious life, as abbesses, patrons, and ordinary participants, were significant though structured by male clerical authority. For those who resisted, Christianization sometimes meant coercion, the destruction of sanctuaries, and loss of status, and conversion by force, as in Charlemagne's campaigns against the Saxons, remained morally contested even among Christian contemporaries such as Alcuin. The evidence for conversion, drawn mostly from church-authored narratives, tells us much about missionaries and rulers and relatively little about the people who were converted.

Division II · Medieval Christendom and Religious Pluralism · Section 140.5

Latin Christianity and the Organization of Medieval Society

By the High Middle Ages, Latin Christianity had become one of the most extensive and complex institutional systems in Europe. At its apex stood the papacy, whose claims to universal authority, developed in the eleventh-century reform movement and the Investiture Conflict (Chapter 44), were asserted through legates, councils, legal collections, and an expanding bureaucracy. Beneath the pope were archbishops and bishops, who administered dioceses, held courts, controlled substantial property, and in many regions of the German lands exercised princely power directly. At the local level, the parish became the basic unit of religious life, providing baptism, marriage, burial, confession, and weekly instruction to the population. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 required annual confession and communion for all adult Christians, indicating both the ambition of the reform program and the scale of the effort needed to realize it.

Monastic and mendicant orders formed a second great institutional network. Benedictine monasteries, reformed by Cluny and later by the Cistercians, controlled land, labor, and learning. The Franciscans and Dominicans, founded in the early thirteenth century, brought preaching and pastoral care to growing towns and provided many of the university teachers discussed in Chapter 48. Religious women formed communities as nuns, beguines, and anchoresses, although their autonomy was repeatedly constrained by clerical authority. Pilgrimage to Jerusalem, Rome, Santiago de Compostela, and innumerable local shrines, together with the cult of relics, saints, and the Virgin Mary, provided structures of devotion that cut across class and region. The liturgical calendar organized the year through fasts, feasts, and holy days, shaping agricultural and domestic routines.

The church's institutional presence extended well beyond worship. Ecclesiastical courts judged cases involving marriage, wills, tithes, and clerical offenses; canon law became a learned discipline; and the church was a major landowner and employer. Cathedral schools and universities trained clergy and administrators, and the church was the principal patron of building, painting, sculpture, and music. Political legitimacy often rested on religious ceremony, as in coronation and anointing, though rulers and popes contested the limits of each other's authority. The church also took on social functions, from hospitals and almshouses to poor relief, although the scale of these services varied widely.

It would nevertheless be misleading to portray medieval society as uniformly devout. Surviving evidence suggests that attendance, understanding, and belief varied: church-court records show quarrels over tithes, clerical misconduct, absenteeism, and irregular marriage; sermon literature complains of inattention; and anticlerical themes appear in popular stories and literature. At the same time, popular piety was often intense, expressed in confraternities, pilgrimage, and devotion to saints, and many practices combined church teaching with local custom. Historians increasingly emphasize the difficulty of describing the inner beliefs of medieval laypeople, since most surviving sources were produced by clergy and record behavior as the church wished it to be or as it feared it was.

Latin Christianity was also a driver of expansion and exclusion. The same institutional framework that supported schools and hospitals organized crusades, as examined in Chapter 47, established inquisitorial procedures against heresy from the thirteenth century, and defined legal restrictions on Jews and other non-Christians. Regional variation was substantial: the church's wealth, the power of bishops, and the relationship between ecclesiastical and royal authority differed between England, France, the German lands, Iberia, Italy, Scandinavia, and the new Christian kingdoms of central Europe. The uniformity implied by the term "Latin Christendom" was, accordingly, partly an aspiration and partly a function of shared Latin liturgy, canon law, and clerical networks.

Historical Vocabulary
Christendom

Christendom denotes the community of Christian peoples and, in the medieval Latin west, the society ideally united under Christian faith and ecclesiastical and secular leadership. The term had political, religious, and cultural meanings that shifted over time. Medieval writers often used it where later authors would use "Europe," but Christendom also included Christians in the Byzantine, Armenian, Syriac, Coptic, and Ethiopian worlds, and it never coincided with the geography of Europe. Latin Christendom and Orthodox Christendom had distinct institutions, and Jews and Muslims lived within or beside both. Modern appeals to "Christian Europe" are often rhetorical constructions, so students should always ask which Christendom a source means and whom it includes.

Section 140.6

Byzantium, Orthodox Christianity, and the Eastern Christian Traditions

The Christianity of the Eastern Roman Empire developed within a political setting different from that of the Latin west. The empire survived as a Christian Roman state with an emperor who claimed a responsibility for the welfare of the church, while the patriarch of Constantinople led a hierarchy of metropolitans, bishops, and monastic communities. Later western writers sometimes described this relationship as "caesaropapism," implying that emperors controlled the church; historians now regard that label as too simple. Patriarchs could resist emperors, as during the controversies over iconoclasm in the eighth and ninth centuries (Chapter 37), and emperors depended on ecclesiastical legitimacy, so that the relationship was closer to a continuing negotiation within a shared understanding of a Christian empire.

Byzantine religious life centered on liturgy, which in the Great Church of Hagia Sophia and in monastic communities expressed a theology of worship in which icons, incense, chant, and the Eucharist formed a unified experience. Monasticism, including the communities of Mount Athos, which became a major center from the tenth century, was central to spiritual authority, and monks served as advisers, confessors, and sometimes critics of emperors. Theological emphases differed from those of the Latin west: eastern theologians were less inclined to develop systematic scholastic frameworks and more likely to stress the mystical and liturgical dimension of doctrine, though the diversity of both traditions makes such generalizations imperfect.

The conversion of the Slavs and the spread of the Byzantine ecclesiastical model beyond the empire created the Orthodox commonwealth of southeastern and eastern Europe. The mission of Cyril and Methodius in the ninth century and the development of Slavonic liturgical texts, examined in Chapter 38, allowed the Bulgarians, Serbs, and Rus to worship in a language their populations understood, a decision with lasting cultural consequences. Orthodox churches in Bulgaria, Serbia, and Rus acquired varying degrees of ecclesiastical independence, and rulers used church institutions to build political identity, a pattern visible later in Moscow's claims about its own religious role. Chapter 39 traces these political communities.

The relationship with the Latin church was marked by growing estrangement, not a single rupture. Disputes concerned the papal claim of jurisdiction, the addition of the filioque clause to the Latin creed, differences over liturgical practice such as the use of leavened or unleavened bread, and political conflicts, particularly in southern Italy and the Balkans. In July 1054, papal legates pronounced anathema against Patriarch Michael Keroularios and his supporters, and a synod at Constantinople in turn condemned the legates. These actions did not amount to a straightforward excommunication of two entire churches. Although 1054 is conventionally treated as a milestone in the schism, historians stress that contemporaries did not view it as a final, universal rupture and that communion between at least some Latin and Greek Christians continued afterward. The sack of Constantinople by the Fourth Crusade in 1204 and the imposition of a Latin patriarch caused deep and lasting resentment, and later attempts at union, notably at the Council of Florence in 1439, were rejected by much of the Orthodox clergy and laity. Chapter 40 and Chapter 41 provide the detailed narrative.

Eastern Christendom, finally, was larger than the Byzantine tradition. Armenian, Syriac, and Coptic churches, and the Georgian church, followed their own paths, and after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 its patriarch continued to exercise ecclesiastical authority and undertook changing representative and administrative functions under Ottoman rule. Those functions differed across regions and centuries; they should not be equated with an immediately established, uniform empire-wide system of Christian communal government. The long-term consequence of the Latin and Greek divide was that "Christian Europe" always contained at least two major institutional traditions, one of which, for most of the second millennium, lived under non-Christian or Muslim political authority across much of southeastern Europe.

Section 140.7

Jewish Communities, Religious Minorities, and Medieval European Life

Jewish communities lived across medieval Europe in two major cultural spheres. In the Iberian peninsula and the Mediterranean, communities usually known as Sephardic developed within Muslim and later Christian kingdoms and produced scholars, poets, physicians, and administrators. In northern France, the Rhineland, and central Europe, the Ashkenazic communities developed their own legal and educational traditions, associated with figures such as Rashi of Troyes in the eleventh century. Maimonides, born in Córdoba in the twelfth century and active largely in Egypt, wrote philosophical and legal works that influenced both Jewish and Christian thinkers, including scholastic authors discussed in Chapter 48. Jewish communities governed themselves through rabbinic courts and communal institutions, maintained schools and charitable organizations, and combined these with participation in local economies, including trade, moneylending, medicine, crafts, and in some places agriculture.

Their legal position depended on rulers and varied widely. Christian theology had long held that Jews should be preserved as witnesses to the antiquity of scripture, which provided a basis for limited protection, but also that they were in error, which supported restrictions. Kings, bishops, and towns granted charters that defined Jewish rights and obligations and often extracted taxes in return. Canon law restricted Jews' public roles and later, through the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215, required distinctive dress in some forms. Because Christian canon law limited moneylending by Christians at interest, Jews sometimes filled financial roles that rulers and townspeople needed but also resented, and this economic position made them vulnerable to political pressures and debt-related hostility. The common modern notion that Jews were "restricted" to moneylending is an oversimplification: the occupational pattern varied by region and period.

The relationship between Jewish and Christian communities involved both coexistence and violence. Everyday contact in markets, neighborhoods, and workplaces was routine, and scholarly exchange, particularly in Iberia and parts of southern France and Italy, was real. But the era also saw an increase in violence. The attacks on Jewish communities in the Rhineland in 1096, associated with the mobilization for the First Crusade, destroyed communities in Speyer, Worms, and Mainz, though some bishops attempted protection, and Jewish chroniclers recorded both the massacres and acts of martyrdom. Accusations of ritual murder and host desecration, with no factual basis, spread from the twelfth century, and the Black Death of 1348–1349 prompted false accusations of well-poisoning and further massacres, particularly in German lands, despite papal efforts to protect Jews. Rulers expelled Jews from England in 1290, from parts of France in 1306 and again later, and from numerous German towns and territories.

Expulsion and conversion continued in the later Middle Ages. Pogroms in Iberia in 1391 led to mass conversions, producing a population of conversos whose sincerity and descent became the subject of suspicion and, after 1478, of the Spanish Inquisition. The expulsion of unconverted Jews from the Spanish crowns in 1492 and the forced conversion in Portugal in 1497 closed a major chapter of Iberian Jewish history, sending many Sephardic Jews to the Ottoman Empire, North Africa, Italy, and the Low Countries. Meanwhile, Jewish communities settled increasingly in Poland and Lithuania, where rulers offered charters of protection, a pattern examined in Chapter 54 and Section 140.13. Distinguishing the long and varied history of Jewish life from a narrative of persecution does not minimize persecution; it recognizes that Jews were also scholars, merchants, physicians, rabbis, parents, and neighbors, whose agency shaped their communities.

Other minorities occupied a more fragmentary place in the sources. Heretical communities such as the Cathars of southern France, a term that historians debate, and the Waldensians were targeted by crusade and inquisition; lepers and others were subject to exclusion; and Romani groups, who arrived in southeastern and then western Europe in the later Middle Ages, were met with varied hospitality and hostility. Medieval Latin Christendom was a society defined by a dominant religion that created categories of insiders and outsiders, and the status of those on the margins was determined by negotiation, law, and, often, force.

Section 140.8

Islam and the Religious Frontiers of Medieval Europe

Islam has been part of European history since the early eighth century. After the Arab and Berber conquest of most of the Iberian peninsula beginning in 711, the territory that became known as al-Andalus formed a major Muslim-ruled society in western Europe. Under the Umayyad dynasty centered at Córdoba, especially in the tenth century, al-Andalus supported a sophisticated urban society, a flourishing of learning in medicine, mathematics, philosophy, and poetry, and an agricultural economy that introduced or expanded crops and irrigation techniques. Christians and Jews, as "peoples of the book," retained their religion under legal conditions that included payment of a special tax and certain restrictions, an arrangement that offered security and a degree of autonomy but not equality. Over time, a large share of the population converted to Islam, and Arabic became the language of culture, including for many Christians and Jews.

The history of Iberia is therefore not simply one of conflict between Christians and Muslims, and the traditional term "Reconquista" for the Christian advance southward is itself a retrospective label, now often qualified by historians who note that the process was gradual, that alliances crossed religious lines, and that Christian kingdoms sometimes fought one another, using Muslim allies. After the fragmentation of the caliphate in the early eleventh century into smaller taifa kingdoms, Christian rulers expanded, taking Toledo in 1085 and the Guadalquivir valley in the thirteenth century, while North African dynasties, the Almoravids and Almohads, intervened and imposed more rigorous religious policies. Muslims living under Christian rule, called Mudéjars, retained religious and legal institutions for a time, but pressures for conversion increased, and Granada's surrender terms in 1491–1492 initially promised its Muslim inhabitants protection for their religion and customs. Those guarantees were subsequently undermined: coercive conversion campaigns culminated in a Castilian decree of 1502 requiring Muslims to accept baptism or depart, while compulsory conversion was extended to the Crown of Aragon in 1525–1526. Converts from Islam and their descendants, known as Moriscos, faced continuing suspicion and were expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614. The translation of Arabic scientific and philosophical works into Latin, particularly at Toledo, was a major channel by which Greek and Islamic learning reached Latin scholars (Chapter 48).

Sicily offers a second example. Muslim rule began in the ninth century and lasted until the Norman conquest completed in 1091. The Norman kings, notably Roger II in the twelfth century, ruled a multiethnic kingdom of Latin Christians, Greek Christians, Muslims, and Jews, with administration conducted in Latin, Greek, and Arabic and with art and architecture that combined elements of all three traditions. The tolerant image of this kingdom needs qualification, since Muslim communities declined under later pressures, and the emperor Frederick II eventually deported Sicilian Muslims to Lucera on the mainland, but Sicily illustrates how a Christian ruler governed a religiously diverse population using inherited institutions.

Southeastern Europe provides the third major case. The Ottoman advance into the Balkans, beginning with the crossing into Europe in the mid-fourteenth century and the victory at Kosovo in 1389, and culminating in the conquest of Constantinople in 1453 (Chapter 41), created an Islamic empire with a major European province, often called Rumelia. Ottoman rule included a mixed approach to Christian subjects. Orthodox Christians and Jews had legally recognized communities with their own religious leaders and courts, an arrangement modern historians call the millet system, although they caution that the term's later, uniform meaning is partly retrospective and that arrangements developed gradually and varied by place. Christians paid a special tax, faced restrictions on church building and public display, and were subject in some periods and regions to the devshirme, the recruitment of Christian boys for Ottoman military and administrative service. Conversion to Islam occurred on a large scale in some areas, notably Bosnia and Albania, for reasons including social advancement, tax advantages, local conditions, and belief, and not by any single means. The Crimean Khanate and Muslim Tatar communities in the Polish-Lithuanian lands extended Islam's European presence to the north.

These cases show that Muslim and Christian relations over the medieval centuries included warfare, notably in the crusades (Chapter 47), but also trade, diplomacy, intermarriage, intellectual transmission, and the daily coexistence of neighbors under unequal but functioning legal systems. They also show that Muslim history in Europe did not begin with twentieth-century migration. Writing European history with Muslims inside it requires recognizing both cultural contributions and the real hierarchies and coercion that shaped every one of these societies.

Division III · Reformation, Confessional Division, and Religious Coexistence · Section 140.9

Medieval Reform, Religious Criticism, and the Limits of Church Unity

The sixteenth-century Reformation did not begin criticism of the church. Reform was a recurring feature of medieval Christianity, and it took forms that ranged from loyal renewal to open rejection. Monastic reform movements such as Cluny and Cîteaux sought to restore discipline and independence. The papal reform of the eleventh century aimed to end simony and clerical marriage and to free the church from lay control. The mendicant orders responded to urban society with preaching and poverty. Many of these movements were absorbed by the church and became part of its institutions, and the line between acceptable reform and unacceptable dissent was drawn by the authorities, often after the fact. Francis of Assisi's movement was approved; related movements of spiritual Franciscans who insisted on a stricter poverty were condemned.

Some movements were declared heretical. The Waldensians, who began in the late twelfth century as a lay movement of preaching and poverty, were condemned when they continued to preach without authorization, and they survived in the Alps and parts of central Europe. The groups known as Cathars in southern France were targeted by the Albigensian Crusade from 1209 and by inquisitors, though historians disagree about how coherent and how dualist the movement was, and some argue that the label owes much to the polemical framework of its opponents. The inquisitorial procedure developed in the thirteenth century became an instrument for investigating belief, and its records, drawn from interrogations, are both a valuable and a problematic source, since they reflect the questions and assumptions of the inquisitors as well as the replies of the accused.

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries brought a deeper institutional crisis, examined in Chapter 53. The removal of the papacy to Avignon and the Western Schism of 1378 to 1417, when rival popes claimed obedience, damaged the papacy's authority and encouraged conciliarism, the theory that a general council held authority above the pope. The Council of Constance (1414–1418) ended the schism but also condemned John Wycliffe's teachings posthumously and executed Jan Hus in 1415. Wycliffe, an Oxford theologian, had criticized clerical wealth and the doctrine of transubstantiation and promoted vernacular scripture; his followers, called Lollards, survived as a persecuted minority in England. Hus's execution provoked the Hussite movement in Bohemia, which combined religious reform, particularly communion in both kinds for laypeople, with Czech national and social grievances, and which defeated several crusades sent against it. The Bohemian case shows that a dissenting church could survive for a long time in one European kingdom, long before Luther.

Alongside organized dissent, lay devotional movements flourished. Confraternities, devotional reading, the Brethren of the Common Life, and mystical writers encouraged inward piety, and the humanist scholars of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, particularly Erasmus (Chapter 59), subjected clerical practice and scripture to philological and moral criticism. Anticlericalism, the criticism of clergy for wealth, ignorance, or immorality, was widespread, though it should not be confused with rejection of Christianity; it often coexisted with strong piety. The late medieval church also sustained reform from within, through bishops, councils, and religious orders, and in some regions, notably Spain, strong monarchs promoted reform of their own national churches.

These developments matter for interpretation. They suggest that Luther's challenge fell on prepared ground, with an educated laity, printing, discontent with ecclesiastical finances, and existing models of dissent. At the same time, they caution against the older picture of a corrupt and moribund late medieval church awaiting replacement. Historians since the late twentieth century have shown that lay participation in religious life, endowment of chantries and parish churches, and devotional enthusiasm were strong on the eve of the Reformation, and the question of why a movement that began as a reform of the church became a permanent division in it is the subject of the next section.

Section 140.10

The Protestant Reformation and the Fragmentation of Western Christianity

Martin Luther's protest against the sale of indulgences in 1517 grew into a theological challenge to the church's system of salvation. Luther taught that sinners are justified by faith and God's grace, not by their own works, and that scripture, not papal or conciliar tradition, was the ultimate authority. After refusing to retract his views at the Diet of Worms in 1521, he was protected by the Elector of Saxony, translated the New Testament into German, and, with the aid of the printing press, reached an audience that earlier critics had lacked. Chapter 62 details these events. What matters here is that Lutheran reform was implemented in cities and territories where princes and magistrates accepted it, so that political decisions shaped religious geography from the start. By the 1530s and 1540s, Lutheranism had been established in much of northern Germany, in Denmark-Norway, and in Sweden, where monarchs took control of church property and organization.

A second major tradition, the Reformed, emerged in Swiss and south German cities. Huldrych Zwingli led the Reformation in Zurich from 1519, and John Calvin, working in Geneva after 1536, developed a theology emphasizing divine sovereignty, a simplified worship, and a disciplined church governed by pastors and lay elders. The Reformed tradition spread to France, the Netherlands, Scotland, Hungary, and parts of Germany and Poland, and appealed to urban elites and nobles. Reformed and Lutheran Christians disagreed over the Eucharist, worship, and church order, and their disagreements prevented a united Protestant front. The English Reformation (Chapter 64) began with Henry VIII's break from Rome in 1534 and the establishment of royal supremacy, followed by doctrinal change under Edward VI, Catholic restoration under Mary I, and the Elizabethan settlement of 1559, which created a church combining Reformed theology with episcopal structure and ceremony, a compromise that left Catholic and Puritan critics dissatisfied.

The radical Reformation encompassed groups that rejected both Catholic and magisterial Protestant models. Anabaptists, who emerged in the 1520s, held that baptism should be received by adult believers and often separated themselves from the state, rejecting oaths and in many cases violence. The seizure of Münster in 1534–1535 by a radical Anabaptist faction, which attempted a theocratic regime, was crushed and became a justification for persecuting all Anabaptists, though most were pacifists. Their descendants include the Mennonites. Other radicals, including spiritualists and anti-Trinitarians, appeared in Switzerland, the Netherlands, Poland, and Transylvania. Radical groups were persecuted by Catholics, Lutherans, and Reformed alike, and they are important in the history of religious liberty because they argued, sometimes, that conscience should be free from state coercion.

Historians stress that the Reformation was not simply a clerical or princely movement. Printing allowed pamphlets, sermons, and vernacular Bibles to circulate widely; urban magistrates and guild members, many of them literate laypeople, adopted reform through petitions, preaching, and civic decisions; and peasants invoked the new gospel in the demands of the German Peasants' War of 1524–1525, which Luther condemned. Women acted as patrons, writers, and participants, though Protestant church structures typically excluded them from ministry. Rulers' interests, including the prospect of seizing church property and consolidating authority, mattered greatly, and so did genuine religious conviction; separating the two is difficult, and most historians reject both purely cynical and purely idealistic accounts.

The consequence was a permanent fragmentation of western Christianity. Where the medieval church had, in principle, been one institution, Europe now contained several churches, each claiming to be the true church and each tied, to varying degrees, to political authority. This fragmentation was not limited to Protestant and Catholic divisions; it included differences among Protestants, and it extended to the sphere of everyday life, as rival churches defined marriage, burial, worship, and education. Eastern Christianity and Judaism remained outside this particular fragmentation, though they were affected by it, as Protestant reformers developed new attitudes to Jews, some more hostile than others (Luther's later writings on Jews are an example of the violent rhetoric that late twentieth-century scholars have emphasized), and as the Ottoman threat shaped imperial politics in the German lands.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religion in the Rhythms of Ordinary Life

For most Europeans for most of the period covered here, religion was woven into daily routines more than it was a separate sphere of belief. In medieval and early modern parishes, bells marked the hours, the liturgical calendar determined fast days, feast days, and agricultural holidays, and baptism, marriage, and burial were occasions for both religious ritual and community gathering. Women were prominent as household transmitters of devotion, as members of confraternities and religious communities, and, in Protestant regions, as readers and participants in household worship, though formal ministry was largely closed to them. Children were taught prayers and catechism at home, in parish schools, or in confessional schools.

Religion also structured law and economy. Tithes were paid to the church; church courts regulated marriage and probate; Jews and Christians negotiated relations of credit and trade; and in Ottoman towns, mixed neighborhoods organized by religious community shared markets and public life. Reformations altered these routines in different ways: many Reformed communities rejected devotional images, pilgrimage, and numerous saints' days, while Lutheran churches often retained images, altarpieces, and some traditional observances under reinterpreted theology. Catholic reform renewed or intensified selected devotions and pilgrimage traditions. In modern times, religion shaped the school systems, the press, and the social organizations of confessional pillars, and in the twentieth century religious practice under communism was often conducted in secret, in homes. In the present, religious practice may be a weekly obligation, an annual festival, a life-cycle rite, or part of a family or ethnic heritage, and historians must attend to these differences rather than assuming that attendance statistics measure the full range of religious life.

Section 140.11

Catholic Reform, Confessional Institutions, and Religious Discipline

The Catholic response to the Reformation was both reaction and renewal. Catholic reform had roots before Luther, in humanist, monastic, and episcopal movements, and historians often prefer the term "Catholic Reformation" or "early modern Catholicism" to the older "Counter-Reformation," which implies a purely defensive response. The Council of Trent, which met in three periods between 1545 and 1563 (Chapter 65), defined Catholic doctrine on scripture and tradition, justification, the sacraments, and purgatory and the veneration of saints, rejecting Protestant positions. It also enacted disciplinary reforms: bishops were required to reside in their dioceses, seminaries were to be established for the training of clergy, and preaching and catechesis were stressed. Implementation varied across Europe; in Spain, Italy, and the Habsburg lands it proceeded with strong state support, while in France the council's decrees were not formally accepted by the crown, although many of its reforms were adopted in practice.

New religious orders played a central role. The Society of Jesus, founded by Ignatius of Loyola and approved in 1540, combined disciplined spiritual formation, mobility, and a strong commitment to education. Jesuit colleges trained generations of Catholic elites and some Protestants, and Jesuit missionaries traveled to the Americas, Asia, and parts of Protestant and Orthodox Europe, including Poland-Lithuania and Hungary. Other reforming figures and orders, such as Teresa of Avila and the Carmelites, the Capuchins, and the Ursulines, who educated girls, extended Catholic devotion and pastoral reach. Catholic piety emphasized the Eucharist, the Virgin Mary, the saints, and confraternities, and baroque art and architecture became vehicles of religious communication.

In both Catholic and Protestant areas, churches and rulers sought to define and enforce doctrine, train clergy, supervise morals, and instruct the laity. Parish visitations, catechisms, church ordinances, and consistories or similar bodies checked the beliefs and behavior of ordinary people. Marriage was regulated more closely, with Trent requiring that marriages be conducted publicly before a priest, and Protestant territories asserting new magisterial control over marriage and divorce. Education expanded, partly for religious reasons: Lutheran and Reformed territories promoted literacy in order to read scripture, and Catholic schools taught doctrine. Poor relief was reorganized, often under the supervision of church or civic bodies. These measures affected the daily lives of people far more than debates among theologians.

Historians have grouped these developments under the term confessionalization, discussed in the vocabulary feature below, proposed by Heinz Schilling and Wolfgang Reinhard in the 1980s. The concept emphasizes parallels among Lutheran, Reformed, and Catholic territories in the way churches and states cooperated to create disciplined, doctrinally defined communities, and it connected religious change with state formation. It has been very influential. It has also been criticized for overstating the power of states to control belief, for fitting best to German territories, for neglecting regions such as the Netherlands and Poland-Lithuania, where religious plurality persisted, and for underplaying the autonomy of local communities and the persistence of popular practices that clerical reformers disliked. Most historians now treat confessionalization as a useful comparative model that must be tested against local evidence, not as a universal process.

The resulting confessional division produced identities that outlasted the conflicts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Territories became associated with a confession, and the sense of belonging to a church, with its liturgy, saints or lack of them, holidays, and music, shaped regional and, later, national identities. Meanwhile, Protestant and Catholic missionary activity beyond Europe connected religious competition with empire, themes taken up in Chapters 68 through 73 and again in Chapter 142.

Historical Vocabulary
Confessionalization

Confessionalization is a concept proposed by historians in the 1980s, particularly Heinz Schilling and Wolfgang Reinhard, describing the process by which Lutheran, Reformed, and Catholic churches, working with territorial authorities, defined doctrine in confessions of faith, disciplined clergy and laity, regulated education and morals, and built distinct communal identities from roughly the mid-sixteenth to the mid-seventeenth century. It links religious change to state formation. The concept is useful for comparison, but critics argue that it exaggerates state control, neglects regions of religious plurality, and underestimates local resistance and popular religion. A "confession" in this sense is a formally defined religious body and statement of faith, not the act of confessing sins.

Section 140.12

Religious Conflict, Coercion, Toleration, and Dissent

Between the 1520s and 1648, religious division contributed to warfare, rebellion, persecution, and migration across much of Europe, although the extent to which religion was the sole cause of any conflict is disputed. In France, the Wars of Religion from 1562 to 1598 pitted Catholic and Huguenot noble factions and communities against one another; the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of August 1572 killed thousands of Protestants in Paris and other cities. The Dutch Revolt against Habsburg rule (Chapter 66), which combined resistance to taxation and loss of privileges with Calvinist activism and opposition to the Inquisition, produced two states: the Protestant-led northern Dutch Republic and the Catholic southern Netherlands. In the British Isles, religious differences were bound up with the relations among England, Scotland, and Ireland, culminating in the civil wars of the 1640s and the persecution and dispossession of Catholics in Ireland. The Thirty Years' War (Chapter 67), which began in Bohemia in 1618 and drew in most major powers, was shaped by confessional rivalry within the Holy Roman Empire but also by dynastic and strategic goals, and its devastation, due partly to disease, famine, and the movement of armies, was severe in many German regions.

Coercion took different forms: executions for heresy, penal laws against dissenters, exclusion from office, expulsion, forced conversion, and censorship. Millions of people migrated, among them Huguenots, Dutch Calvinists, English Catholics and Puritans, Salzburg Protestants, and Sephardic Jews, and refugee communities often gave rise to economic and intellectual networks in their new homes. At the same time, systems of coexistence emerged, sometimes from exhaustion, sometimes from principle. The Peace of Augsburg (1555) permitted the Lutheran or Catholic confession of each territorial ruler within the Holy Roman Empire, allowing dissenting subjects to emigrate; Calvinists were excluded. The Edict of Nantes (1598) granted French Huguenots freedom of conscience, limited public worship, and a number of fortified places, but Louis XIV's revocation in 1685 ended the arrangement and led to a significant exodus. In Poland-Lithuania, the Warsaw Confederation of 1573 pledged nobles to peace among confessions. In Transylvania, successive diets recognized several confessions, including Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, and Unitarian, in the later sixteenth century. In the Dutch Republic, the Reformed Church was privileged, but other confessions were allowed to worship, with Catholics, Mennonites, and Jews often worshipping in private or semi-private settings.

The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 confirmed the Peace of Augsburg, extended its terms to Calvinists, set a normative year of 1624 for the confessional status of church property and the rights of subjects, and provided for guarantees for minority rights. It is often misrepresented as the origin of religious freedom or of the modern sovereign state, but the settlement was a legal compromise in the Holy Roman Empire that balanced rights of princes and of subjects, and it did not create a universal principle of religious liberty. These distinctions matter. Selective toleration was a policy granted by an authority to particular groups on conditions, and it could be revoked. Corporate religious privilege meant that recognized churches or communities held specific legal rights as groups. The modern idea of freedom of religion, an individual right to believe, worship, change, or abandon religion, was articulated by a minority of thinkers in the seventeenth century, including Roger Williams, Pierre Bayle, and John Locke, and became law gradually and unevenly later.

Debates over toleration were thus conducted in a world where most rulers and many subjects thought religious uniformity was desirable, and where toleration was justified on prudential grounds (to preserve peace and commerce), on theological grounds (that coercion could not produce true belief), or, rarely, on principle. Dissenters themselves were divided: some sought toleration for themselves while rejecting it for others, and some, like the Quakers and Baptists, made arguments for liberty of conscience central. The English Toleration Act of 1689, discussed in the primary source feature, exempted most Protestant dissenters from certain penalties but excluded Catholics and anti-Trinitarians, a reminder that early toleration remained conditional.

Religious Diversity across Early Modern European Empires and Borderlands

The western European model of one state, one confession, whether enforced or tolerated, was not typical of the whole continent. Large composite states and empires in the east and southeast contained substantial religious diversity, and their arrangements differed from those of France, England, or the German principalities. The comparison shows that European religious pluralism had several historical sources and that the western experience was one among many.

The Ottoman Empire governed Muslims, Orthodox Christians, Armenians, Jews, and, after conquest, Catholics in parts of the Balkans and Hungary. Some non-Muslim communities maintained clergy, communal institutions, and rules for aspects of marriage and inheritance, but the extent of such autonomy varied considerably by city, province, community, and period; Ottoman courts and imperial officials also exercised authority in matters involving non-Muslims. The familiar picture of a fixed, empire-wide system of autonomous religious millets operating unchanged from 1453 is a later simplification, not an adequate description of early modern practice. Christian and Jewish subjects had a recognized legal standing, a degree of protection, and also obligations and disabilities. Jewish exiles from Iberia found refuge in Salonica, Istanbul, and other cities, where they developed major communities. The empire's arrangement was not toleration in the modern sense, since it assumed Muslim political superiority, but it contrasted with the western European expulsions of the same period.

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, established by the Union of Lublin in 1569, included Roman Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants of several kinds, Jews, and Muslim Tatars. Noble liberties and decentralized governance produced arrangements, such as the 1573 Warsaw Confederation, that limited the ability of the crown to impose uniformity, and Jewish communities received substantial self-government through institutions such as the Council of the Lands, though they remained subject to restrictions and periodic violence. The Union of Brest in 1596 created a Greek Catholic (Uniate) church that kept Orthodox liturgy while accepting papal authority, and its establishment divided the Ruthenian population and fed tension with Orthodox believers. The Cossack uprising of 1648, in which religious, social, and political grievances combined, led to massacres of Jews and Catholics and weakened the Commonwealth. The Commonwealth's religious system gradually narrowed in the seventeenth century as Catholic reform and political crises hardened confessional lines.

The Habsburg lands combined Catholic dynastic rule with many regions of religious difference. After the Catholic victory at the White Mountain in 1620, Bohemia was re-Catholicized by force, and Protestantism was suppressed in much of the Habsburg territories, while in Hungary, where Ottoman and Transylvanian influences mattered, Protestants survived. Transylvania, as a principality under Ottoman suzerainty, recognized four "received" religions. The Habsburg monarchy later developed a policy of limited toleration under Joseph II, discussed in Section 140.15. The Russian state, under Muscovite and later imperial rule, was founded on Orthodox Christianity and displayed a complex relationship with its non-Orthodox subjects, including Muslims, Buddhists, Jews acquired through the partitions of Poland, and Protestants in the Baltic provinces. The Old Believers, who rejected the liturgical reforms of the mid-seventeenth century, were persecuted, and the Petrine reform of 1721 placed the church under the Holy Synod, a government department. Peter the Great and Catherine II both recruited and tolerated foreign Protestants, and Catherine II regulated Islam through an official body, a reminder that toleration could serve the administrative interests of an empire.

In Scandinavia, the Lutheran state churches were uniform in principle and were enforced through the parishes, with Sámi populations subject to Christianization efforts. In the Dutch Republic, as noted, plurality was pragmatic. In England, the Church of England was established, while dissenting Protestants, Catholics, and Jews were granted differing degrees of tolerance or exclusion. The comparison suggests that the degree of religious plurality in a state depended on the structure of political authority, the strength of the nobility, the presence of borders with other religious systems, and the economic role of minority communities, as much as on the ideas about toleration circulating among intellectuals. The Orthodox world, in particular, shows that the concept of a "western" path of confessional state-building cannot be extended to the whole of European history.

Section 140.14

Enlightenment, Religious Criticism, and the Development of Unbelief

The Enlightenment (Chapters 82 through 84) changed the intellectual terms in which religion was discussed, but it did so in many different ways. The wars of religion and the spread of the new science stimulated reflection on authority and certainty. Some thinkers sought to reconcile religion with reason. Others criticized it. A few rejected it entirely. Treating "the Enlightenment" as a single anti-religious movement misrepresents the evidence, since many leading Enlightenment figures were believers, clergy, or deists, and religious movements of the eighteenth century, such as Pietism in Germany, Methodism in Britain, and Hasidism in Eastern Europe, were vigorous.

Early modern criticism of religious authority built on earlier foundations. Baruch Spinoza, a Jewish thinker expelled from the Amsterdam Jewish community in 1656, argued in his Theological-Political Treatise for critical reading of scripture and for freedom of thought in a state. Pierre Bayle, a Huguenot refugee in the Netherlands, argued that an erring conscience had rights and that atheists could be moral. John Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) made a case that the state should not use coercion in matters of salvation, though Locke excluded Catholics and atheists from his proposed toleration, a limit that later readers have debated. Deism, the belief in a creator God known through reason and nature without special revelation, was developed by writers such as Voltaire, who attacked clerical intolerance and the persecution of Protestants such as Jean Calas in 1762, but who himself held illiberal views toward Jews. David Hume's critique of miracles and natural religion, Denis Diderot and Baron d'Holbach's materialism and avowed atheism, and Edward Gibbon's historical treatment of early Christianity extended the range of criticism.

The relationship between science and religion was complex, not simply oppositional. The Scientific Revolution (Chapter 81) was carried out largely by Christians, including Newton, who held strong theological interests, and many natural philosophers considered the study of nature a way to understand the Creator. Conflicts occurred, most famously the Roman Inquisition's condemnation of Galileo in 1633, but these involved questions of scriptural interpretation, ecclesiastical authority, and personal and political rivalries, as well as science. Historians now caution strongly against the nineteenth-century "conflict thesis," which portrayed the history of science and religion as perpetual warfare, a model that scholarship has substantially revised.

Enlightenment critics also addressed Jews and Judaism, with ambiguity. Lessing's play Nathan the Wise (1779) championed religious tolerance and was inspired in part by his friendship with the Jewish philosopher Moses Mendelssohn, who advocated civil rights for Jews and participated in the Haskalah, the Jewish Enlightenment, which encouraged secular learning and engagement with European culture. Other Enlightenment writers described Judaism as backward, and arguments for emancipation were often conditional on Jewish "improvement." The Enlightenment therefore contributed both to the idea of universal rights and to new categories of prejudice.

Unbelief, in the sense of explicit atheism, remained rare and dangerous in the eighteenth century, and much of what historians call freethought was deism, skepticism, or anticlericalism. Evidence for unbelief among ordinary people is scarce, because it was seldom recorded and because the penalties for blasphemy and heresy continued. Nonetheless, by the end of the century, a public sphere had emerged in which religious claims could be criticized in print, in salons, in coffeehouses, and in Masonic lodges, as discussed in Chapter 86. This public discussion did not destroy religious belief, but it altered the conditions in which belief was held, from an assumption shared by most of society toward a choice that could be examined and, increasingly, rejected.

Section 140.15

Revolution, Emancipation, and the Changing Relationship between Church and State

The reform of church-state relations began before 1789. In the Habsburg monarchy, Joseph II issued the Patent of Toleration in 1781, allowing Lutherans, Calvinists, and Orthodox Christians to worship privately and hold some offices, and later issued edicts for Jews that eased some restrictions while pursuing assimilationist aims. He also dissolved many contemplative monasteries and placed the church under state supervision, policies known as Josephinism. In France, the Edict of 1787 granted civil status to Protestants. These measures reflected the interests of enlightened governments (Chapter 84) in administrative efficiency and economic productivity, as well as ideas about toleration.

The French Revolution (Chapters 88 and 89) changed the scale of the question. In 1789, the National Assembly's Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen included a guarantee, in Article 10, that no one should be disturbed for opinions, including religious ones, so long as their expression did not disturb public order. The Assembly nationalized church property in 1789, abolished tithes, and, in 1790, adopted the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, which reorganized the church as a state institution with elected bishops and priests and required clergy to swear an oath. The pope's condemnation and the division of the French clergy into "juring" and "refractory" priests created a religious schism within the revolution and fed counterrevolution. Jewish communities were granted citizenship in 1790 for Sephardic Jews of the southwest and in 1791 for all Jews in France, with the understanding that they would be citizens as individuals, an approach that conflicted with traditional corporate Jewish autonomy.

The radicalization of the revolution brought the campaigns of dechristianization in 1793 and 1794, in which churches were closed, clergy pressured to resign, and new civic cults, including the Cult of the Supreme Being, promoted. The campaign was uneven, provoked strong resistance in many areas, and was abandoned. Napoleon's Concordat of 1801 with Pope Pius VII restored Catholicism as the "religion of the majority of the French," subordinated the church to state supervision, recognized the sale of church lands, and established arrangements for the appointment of bishops. The Organic Articles added Protestant and later Jewish organization under state recognition, and Napoleon's summoning of a Jewish Assembly of Notables and a Sanhedrin in 1806–1807 represented an effort to integrate Jews into the state. French armies spread these arrangements and the confiscation of ecclesiastical property to the Rhineland, Italy, and other occupied regions, with long-term effects examined in Chapter 90.

The nineteenth century saw uneven extension of civil rights beyond established confessions. In Britain, the Test and Corporation Acts were repealed in 1828, Catholic emancipation was granted in 1829, and Jews gained full access to Parliament by 1858. German states emancipated Jews gradually, with legal equality across the North German Confederation in 1869 and the German Empire in 1871. The Russian Empire, by contrast, maintained special restrictions on Jews, including the Pale of Settlement. Established churches were also altered: the Church of Ireland was disestablished in 1869 (effective 1871), and the Church in Wales in the early twentieth century (effective 1920). Disestablishment, the end of state-church connection, happened in different ways and at different times.

These developments illustrate the need to distinguish several arrangements. A state church receives legal recognition and support from the state; legal toleration allows dissenters to worship under conditions; religious emancipation removes civil disabilities based on religion; separation of church and state ends the institutional connection. Laïcité, the French principle of state secularism and freedom of conscience, is exemplified by the law of 9 December 1905, which generally ended state recognition and direct support of religious bodies while protecting worship. The law nevertheless expressly allows public expenditure for chaplaincies in designated public institutions, and distinct local legal regimes have remained in certain territories, notably Alsace-Moselle after its return to France in 1918. This is one particular form of separation, shaped by French history and conflict between republicans and the Catholic Church. Secular government, in the sense that the state does not base its legitimacy on religion, is a related but different concept, and freedom of religious practice can exist with or without either. Many European states combined features: Germany adopted cooperative arrangements between the state and recognized churches, Britain retained an established church in England alongside broad toleration, and the Nordic states retained Lutheran state churches while becoming among the most secular societies. The history of the church and the state is therefore a history of multiple models and not a single movement toward separation.

Four Legal Approaches to Religious Difference: 1689, 1789, 1905, and 1950

Creators: The English Parliament; the French National Assembly; the French Parliament; the member states of the Council of Europe · Dates: 1689 to 1950 · Type: Statute, declaration of rights, separation law, and international human rights treaty

Context: These four texts come from different political settings. The English Toleration Act followed the Glorious Revolution (Chapter 77) and addressed Protestant dissenters in a country with an established church. Article 10 of the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was adopted by the National Assembly in August 1789 (Chapter 88). The French law of 9 December 1905 concluded a long conflict between republicans and the Catholic Church. The European Convention on Human Rights was signed in Rome in November 1950 and entered into force in 1953. The summaries below are close paraphrases, not verbatim quotations, and students should consult verified translations of the full texts in a documentary collection or the official publications of the institutions concerned before quoting them.

Paraphrased content of the four texts 1689, Toleration Act: Protestant dissenters who took oaths of allegiance and made a prescribed declaration against certain Catholic doctrines were relieved of penalties under several earlier laws for attending their own meetings, subject to conditions such as holding services with open doors. Catholics and those who denied the Trinity were not covered.

1789, Declaration, Article 10: No person should be troubled for opinions, including religious ones, provided that their expression does not disturb the public order established by law.

1905, Law on the Separation of Churches and the State: The Republic guarantees freedom of conscience and the free exercise of worship, subject only to restrictions in the interest of public order (Article 1). The Republic does not recognize, pay salaries to, or subsidize any religion (Article 2), and the law reorganized the ownership and administration of church buildings and property.

1950, European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9: Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, including the freedom to change religion or belief and to manifest it in worship, teaching, practice, and observance. Manifestation of religion may be limited only by measures prescribed by law and necessary in a democratic society for specified purposes, such as public safety, order, health or morals, or the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

Analysis: The four texts represent distinct concepts. The 1689 Act is a conditional toleration: it grants relief from penalties to a defined group while leaving the established church and exclusions in place. The 1789 article states a general principle, but its wording shows that opinions, including religious ones, were protected subject to public order, and it was applied unevenly in the revolution that followed. The 1905 law generally separates the institutions of church and state while guaranteeing freedom of conscience and worship, though its implementation was fiercely contested at the time; its chaplaincy provision and the distinct law later retained in Alsace-Moselle also show the limits of describing French arrangements as a single uniform rule. The 1950 Convention frames religious freedom as an individual right that can be invoked before an international court, including the freedom to change religion, but permits limitations, which the court has interpreted with differing degrees of deference to states. Each text also has limits as evidence. A law does not show how it was enforced, and the texts say little about people who were not covered, the views of the groups affected, or the continuing gap between legal principle and social practice.

  2. Who created each text and for what likely purpose, and what political circumstances shaped it?
  3. What can historians learn from these texts about how conceptions of religious freedom changed between 1689 and 1950?
  4. What can historians not establish from these texts alone about the experience of religious minorities or the enforcement of the laws?
Section 140.16

Religion, Nationalism, Social Reform, and Nineteenth-Century Society

The nineteenth century was not simply an age of declining religion. After the revolutionary and Napoleonic upheavals, many Europeans looked to religion for order, consolation, and identity. Catholic revival, associated with new devotions, pilgrimage sites such as Lourdes after the apparitions of 1858, mass missions, new religious congregations, and the assertion of papal authority, was one expression. The papacy of Pius IX condemned liberal and rationalist positions in the Syllabus of Errors (1864), and the First Vatican Council in 1870 defined papal infallibility, shortly before Italian forces took Rome. Conflicts between the state and the Catholic Church shaped politics in Germany, where Bismarck's Kulturkampf of the 1870s imposed restrictions on Catholic institutions and strengthened a Catholic political party, and in France, where republicans and Catholics struggled over education, culminating in the separation law of 1905.

Protestant activism took the form of evangelical revival, Sunday schools, missionary societies, temperance movements, and philanthropic organizations in Britain, Germany, Scandinavia, and the Netherlands. Nonconformist Protestants in Britain were a major force in reform politics, including the abolition of the slave trade and slavery in the British Empire. In the Netherlands, religious communities organized "pillars," social blocs with their own schools, press, and organizations, a pattern that persisted into the twentieth century. Orthodox churches became tied to the new national movements of the Balkans: the Greek church declared its independence from Constantinople in 1833, the Bulgarian Exarchate was created in 1870, and Serbian and Romanian churches acquired autonomous status, examples of how religious institutions served as bearers of national identity and sources of conflict with the Ottoman state and with the Ecumenical Patriarchate. In the Ottoman Empire itself, the Tanzimat reforms of 1839 and the 1856 decree promised equality for all subjects, though their implementation was uneven and the rise of nationalism complicated them.

Religion and social questions intersected as well. Churches responded to industrialization (Chapter 95) with charity, education, and, increasingly, social theory. The Catholic encyclical Rerum Novarum (1891) addressed labor and property, supporting workers' associations while rejecting socialism, and gave rise to Catholic social movements and trade unions. Protestant and Jewish organizations created welfare institutions. Christian socialists argued for reform, while others viewed religion as a prop for the existing order. At the same time, many industrial workers and urban populations were distanced from the churches, so that urban working-class "de-churching" has been an important topic for historians; the extent differed greatly by country and denomination, with, for example, Catholic Ireland and Poland remaining highly practicing, while parts of industrial Britain, Germany, and France showed lower attendance. Chapters 96 and 97 examine the ideological contests in which religion, liberalism, socialism, and nationalism intersected.

Jewish communities experienced both emancipation and new forms of hostility. Legal equality, the growth of Jewish participation in business, the professions, culture, and politics, and the emergence of reform and neo-Orthodox movements in Judaism marked the era. At the same time, modern antisemitism developed as a political and racial ideology, a subject of Chapter 103: the term itself was popularized in Germany in the late 1870s, and political movements, press campaigns, and events such as the Dreyfus Affair in France and pogroms in the Russian Empire after 1881 revealed hostility. Modern antisemitism drew on older Christian anti-Judaism but differed from it by attributing fixed racial characteristics to Jews, which meant that conversion did not remove the stigma. Zionism, a Jewish national movement, emerged partly in response.

Religion could support existing authority and inspire reform. The established churches of monarchies often legitimized the political order, and conservative thinkers defended "throne and altar." Yet Christian ideas also motivated antislavery campaigns, prison reform, education, and women's participation in public life through charity and mission. The missionary movement connected European religious life to empire (Chapters 104 and 105), sometimes cooperating with colonial rule and sometimes criticizing it. Religion in the nineteenth century was therefore a field of contest where different groups appealed to Christianity, Judaism, or Islam to defend or challenge social and political arrangements.

Cause and Effect

Why Religious Authority Weakened in Some Settings and Persisted in Others

Long-term causes

Confessional fragmentation after the Reformation, the growth of state administrative capacity, Enlightenment criticism, the expansion of literacy and the public sphere, urbanization and industrialization, and the loss of church monopolies in education and welfare.

Short-term causes

Revolutionary confiscation and reorganization, nineteenth-century conflicts over education and the state, the world wars, authoritarian and communist repression, and the cultural changes and expansion of welfare states in the 1960s and after.

Effects of weakening

Reduced attendance and membership, loss of institutional influence, legal separation or neutrality in many states, growth of nonreligious populations, and the emergence of new forms of spirituality.

Effects of persistence

Where churches were linked to national survival, such as Poland, Ireland before the late twentieth century, and Greece, or where they provided protected social space under repression, they retained strong support. Persistence has also been sustained by migration and by the growth of minority religious communities.

Historical complexity: The same factors could have opposite effects. State repression of religion reduced observance in East Germany and Czechoslovakia but strengthened the church's moral standing in Poland. Established status sustained some churches and weakened others by associating them with unpopular regimes. No single cause, such as science or modernization, explains the pattern, and local history, denominational structure, and political alignment matter greatly.

Religion, War, Antisemitism, and the Catastrophes of the Twentieth Century

In the First World War (Chapters 108 through 112), clergy of nearly every belligerent state blessed the national cause, preached to soldiers, and served as chaplains, while the papacy under Benedict XV attempted mediation and condemned the war without being heeded. The war strengthened the links between religion and nationalism in some places and shook faith in others, and historians continue to debate whether it produced a religious revival or accelerated decline. The collapse of the Romanov, Habsburg, Hohenzollern, and Ottoman orders ended several systems in which churches had been tied to dynasties, and new states struggled to define relations with their religious communities. The Russian Revolution (Chapter 111) inaugurated the first state with a program of enforced atheism, a development with consequences examined in the next section.

In the interwar period, churches took varied positions toward authoritarian regimes. The Catholic Church signed concordats with Mussolini's Italy in the Lateran Treaties of 1929 and with Germany in 1933, seeking to protect its institutions. In Spain, the Church was attacked during the Republic and the civil war of 1936 to 1939 (Chapter 116), when anticlerical violence killed thousands of clergy and religious, and many Catholic leaders later supported Franco's regime. In Germany, Protestant churches were divided between the pro-Nazi German Christians, the Confessing Church, which issued the Barmen Declaration in 1934 asserting the church's independence from state ideology, and the majority, who tried to remain neutral or accommodate. Catholic bishops protested particular policies, and in 1937 Pius XI's encyclical Mit brennender Sorge criticized Nazi violations of the concordat and racial ideology, but the institutional church did not oppose the regime as a whole.

Distinguishing three phenomena is central to understanding the Holocaust (Chapter 121). Christian anti-Judaism was a long-standing theological and cultural hostility toward Jews as a religious group, which had sustained discrimination, expulsion, and violence for centuries and in which conversion was a possible exit. Modern antisemitism, which developed in the nineteenth century, defined Jews as a race or nation and sometimes as a political threat, often drawing on and also rejecting Christian themes. Nazi racial ideology made antisemitism central to a worldview of biological struggle and defined Jewishness by descent, so that converted Jews and the descendants of converts were persecuted. Historians agree that older Christian anti-Judaism helped create the cultural conditions in which antisemitism was received, but they also emphasize that Nazi policy was driven by a racial ideology distinct from earlier religious hostility, and that Nazi leaders were often hostile to Christianity itself.

The behavior of churches, institutions, and believers during the Nazi occupation and the Holocaust was varied. Some clergy and laypeople rescued Jews, as in the French village of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, in Denmark, in Italian and Greek communities, and through individual acts across Europe. Orthodox Archbishop Damaskinos of Athens protested the deportation of Greek Jews. The Bulgarian church leadership and politicians contributed to preventing the deportation of Bulgaria's Jewish citizens, though the Jews of Bulgarian-occupied Thrace and Macedonia were deported. Some Catholic and Protestant leaders protested, and some did not. Clergy accepted collaboration in others: Catholic priests played political roles in Slovakia, where Jozef Tiso, a priest, led a regime that deported Jews, and in Croatia, where members of the Catholic clergy were involved with the Ustaša regime's persecution of Serbs, Jews, and Roma, even as others condemned it. Pope Pius XII's role has been intensely debated. The Vatican's wartime archives were opened to researchers in 2020, which has stimulated new research, and historians continue to assess the pope's diplomacy, public statements, and private assistance. The Jehovah's Witnesses, Roma, and other groups targeted for religious or racial reasons also suffered. Silence, indifference, fear, and active participation all existed, and responsibility for the persecution lay first with the perpetrators and their collaborators.

After 1945, Christian churches faced the question of their relationship to antisemitism. The Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt by German Protestant leaders in 1945 acknowledged failure in a general way, and later statements, including the Second Vatican Council's Nostra Aetate in 1965, rejected the charge of collective Jewish guilt for the crucifixion and repudiated antisemitism, initiating a long process of revising teaching and dialogue. These changes were significant but incomplete, and prejudice persisted. The twentieth-century catastrophes demonstrated that religious institutions could be complicit with, resist, or be victims of political violence, and that the history of religious identity in Europe cannot be separated from the destruction of European Jewry.

Section 140.18

Communism, Democracy, and Religious Institutions after 1945

After 1945, Europe's division produced two sharply different religious environments, though both were more varied than that simple contrast suggests. In the communist states (Chapters 124, 125, and 129), Marxist-Leninist ideology treated religion as a product of social conditions that would disappear with socialism, and regimes combined propaganda, education, surveillance, and repression to reduce religion's public role. The Soviet Union had a prewar record of closing churches and persecuting clergy, followed by a wartime concession in 1943 that restored the Moscow Patriarchate under state control, and renewed campaigns thereafter. In 1946, the Greek Catholic Church in Ukraine was forced into the Russian Orthodox Church, and in the following years Greek Catholic churches in Romania and Czechoslovakia were similarly suppressed. Catholic leaders such as Cardinal Mindszenty in Hungary and Archbishop Stepinac in Yugoslavia were put on trial. Albania in 1967 declared itself the world's first atheist state and closed all places of worship.

Yet the experiences differed widely. In Poland, the Catholic Church was deeply rooted in national identity and was never subordinated, though under pressure; it became a space of relative autonomy. In East Germany, the Protestant churches, though under surveillance, provided a shelter for peace and environmental activists, and church-linked prayer meetings in Leipzig in 1989 contributed to the mass demonstrations that preceded the fall of the regime. In Romania, the Orthodox leadership accommodated the regime, and in Bulgaria and Serbia, Orthodox churches also negotiated limited roles. In Czechoslovakia, Catholic and Protestant dissidents were involved in Charter 77. Many people practiced religion privately, and religious observance was often combined with the necessity of public conformity; historians use state security archives and oral history to explore these negotiations, with attention to the fact that records created by secret police must be read critically. The election of the Polish cardinal Karol Wojtyła as Pope John Paul II in 1978, and his visit to Poland in 1979, had major symbolic effects, as discussed in connection with Solidarity in Chapter 132.

In western Europe, churches operated within democratic societies and were often politically significant. Christian democratic parties, led by figures such as Konrad Adenauer in Germany, Alcide De Gasperi in Italy, and Robert Schuman in France, drew on Catholic social thought and, in Germany, interdenominational cooperation to build postwar constitutional democracies and support European integration (Chapter 133). The World Council of Churches was founded in 1948, advancing ecumenism, and the Second Vatican Council (1962 to 1965) brought liturgical reform, the use of vernacular languages in the Mass, engagement with other Christians and with the modern world, and, in Dignitatis Humanae, an affirmation of the right to religious freedom that was a major shift in Catholic teaching. In Spain and Portugal, the church was associated with authoritarian regimes until the 1970s (Chapter 131), and the changes after Franco's death in 1975 included a constitution in 1978 that declared no state religion.

Postwar western Europe also saw institutional decline in religious participation. The social movements of the 1960s (Chapter 130), changes in sexual morality and family life, the expansion of education, urbanization, and the welfare state contributed to declining church attendance, especially in Protestant northern Europe, the Netherlands, Belgium, France, and later Ireland and Spain. In the Netherlands, the "pillars" of confessional society weakened rapidly in the 1960s. In Britain, the Church of England remained established while its attendance fell. In Ireland, the Catholic Church's authority declined after the 1990s with scandals concerning abuse, which also affected the church in other countries, and with referenda in 2015 and 2018 reflecting changed public attitudes. The Nordic churches, with state connections, retained high nominal membership with low attendance.

Therefore, the Cold War did not produce a simple contrast of religious east and secular west. State repression in some eastern countries coincided with high levels of secularization, as in Czechoslovakia and East Germany, and with persistent religiosity, as in Poland. In the west, secular and religious trends coexisted with different outcomes in Italy, Ireland, and Scandinavia. After 1989, as examined in Chapter 136, churches in post-communist societies faced new roles, including claims for restitution of property, debates over religious education, and, in some states, closer relationships with governments and national identities, while others, such as Estonia and the Czech Republic, remained among the least religious societies in the world by survey measures.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Persistence and Transformation of Religious Institutions

What Changed

  • Christianity moved from a persecuted minority to an imperial religion, then from a single western church to multiple confessions, then to one option among several in societies with legal religious freedom
  • Legal status shifted from privilege and toleration by grace toward individual rights and equal citizenship
  • Churches lost many functions in education, poor relief, marriage law, and record-keeping to the state, though they retained some in many countries
  • Religious diversity expanded through Reformation, migration, and the growth of nonreligious populations

What Stayed the Same

  • The tension between religious institutions that claim independent authority and political powers that seek to use or control them
  • The presence of Jewish communities, which persisted through expulsion, persecution, and catastrophe
  • The gap between official doctrine and the practice of ordinary believers
  • Rites of passage, such as baptism, marriage, and burial, which in many societies retained religious forms after attendance declined
  • The connection between religious and communal or national identity, from medieval Christendom to modern Poland, Ireland, Greece, and the Balkans

Who experienced change most directly? Religious minorities, dissenters, and the clergy of established churches felt legal and institutional changes most immediately. Rural communities and many ordinary believers experienced greater continuity in seasonal and life-cycle rituals, though they also experienced confessional campaigns, secular reforms, and, in the twentieth century, state repression. Why the pattern matters: It prevents both the assumption that religion has been an unchanging feature and the assumption that it has steadily disappeared.

Section 140.19

Migration, New Religious Communities, and Contemporary Pluralism

Contemporary Europe's religious diversity results from both old and new sources, and it is misleading to treat it as the product of recent migration alone (Chapter 137 examines migration and identity generally). Muslim communities have deep roots in the Balkans, where Bosnia, Albania, Kosovo, and parts of Bulgaria, North Macedonia, and Greece have long-established Muslim populations from the Ottoman period, and in the Tatar communities of Poland, Lithuania, and Belarus and in the Volga region. Jewish communities, devastated by the Holocaust, survived in reduced numbers; postwar emigration to Israel and elsewhere further reduced many, while the emigration of Soviet and post-Soviet Jews in the 1970s and especially the 1990s reshaped Jewish populations in Germany, where a new community emerged, and in other states. Jewish life continues, with renewed institutions in many cities, and also faces antisemitic incidents, which historians and monitoring bodies document with varying methods.

Postwar labor demand and decolonization (Chapter 128) brought migrants from Turkey, North Africa, South Asia, the Caribbean, sub-Saharan Africa, and southern Europe to the industrial states of the west. West Germany's recruitment of "guest workers," including a bilateral agreement with Turkey in 1961, France's migration from Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia, and British migration from South Asia and the Caribbean created communities that became permanent, though governments had often anticipated temporary stays. Muslim communities grew through family reunification and births, and refugees from conflicts, including the Yugoslav wars, Iraq, Syria, and Afghanistan, added to them. Other religious communities also expanded: Hindus, Sikhs, and Buddhists, notably in Britain; African and Latin American Pentecostal and other Christian churches in many cities; Orthodox Christians from eastern Europe in western states; and Catholic and Orthodox migration within Europe following the enlargement of the European Union (Chapter 136).

These communities have established mosques, temples, schools, charities, and organizations, and the experiences of different generations have varied. First-generation migrants often organized around national origin and language; later generations negotiated religious identity within European societies, with a range of outcomes from strong practice to secularization to new hybrid forms. Muslims in Europe are diverse in nationality, sect, legal school, level of observance, and politics, and the label "Muslim" can conceal these differences. Citizenship regimes, labor markets, housing patterns, and discrimination influenced integration, and so did the presence or absence of state systems for recognizing religious communities: Austria, Belgium, and Germany had arrangements for religious education and recognition that allowed some accommodation, while France's laïcité and the British multicultural approach framed matters differently.

Public controversies have been frequent and have varied by country. In France, disputes over headscarves in schools from 1989 led to the law of 2004 prohibiting conspicuous religious symbols in public schools and to the 2010 law banning face coverings in public. The Rushdie affair in Britain in 1989, the Danish cartoon controversy of 2005–2006, the murders of Theo van Gogh in 2004 and the staff of Charlie Hebdo in 2015, and terrorist attacks in several cities made Islam a subject of security and identity debates. A Swiss referendum in 2009 banned the construction of minarets. The European Court of Human Rights has addressed several such cases: in Lautsi v. Italy (2011), the Grand Chamber upheld the presence of crucifixes in Italian public school classrooms, and in S.A.S. v. France (2014), it accepted the French ban on full-face veils. These decisions show how the court has granted states a margin of appreciation in religious matters, reflecting differing national traditions. Observers disagree on whether these controversies reflect hostility to Muslims specifically, tensions over secular norms, or legitimate security and integration concerns, and historians should note that contemporary political rhetoric is not mature scholarship.

Regional differences matter. Western Europe has the largest and most established populations of post-war migrants, southern Europe, historically a region of emigration, became a destination only in the late twentieth century, and many eastern European states have had fewer recent migrants but have experienced religion-linked nationalism and heated debates, particularly after the arrival of refugees in 2015. Pluralism in contemporary Europe therefore consists of ancient religious minorities, communities formed by recent migration, long-established Christian denominations, and a large nonreligious population, and legal systems distribute rights and recognition in different ways.

Multiple Perspectives

Religious Change as Experienced by Different Groups

From the perspective of an established church and its clergy: Establishment brought legal privilege, property, and public duties, and clergy often regarded religious unity as essential for social order. Reforms, revolutions, and secularizing laws were experienced as loss of status and as threats, though some churches adapted and found new forms of influence through education, charity, and political parties.
From the perspective of a religious minority, such as a Jewish community or a Muslim community: Legal status determined security, taxation, occupation, and mobility. Toleration was valuable but conditional and revocable, and emancipation brought civil rights but also pressure to assimilate and new forms of hostility. For many minorities, the secular state could mean either protection from a dominant church or restrictions on public religious expression.
From the perspective of a dissenter, such as a Huguenot, Anabaptist, or Old Believer: Dissent meant conviction at the cost of exclusion, fines, imprisonment, or exile. Dissenters often generated the arguments for liberty of conscience, though some, once in power, did not extend the same liberty to others.
From the perspective of a nonreligious or doubting person: For most of the period before the late eighteenth century, open unbelief was dangerous and rarely recorded. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, freethinkers, socialists, and others formed associations, but unbelief could still carry social costs, and under communist regimes the situation was reversed, with believers disadvantaged. In many modern societies, indifference rather than conscious atheism is the most common nonreligious position.
Section 140.20

Secularization, Religious Persistence, and Europe's Changing Identity

The term secularization names several processes that historians and sociologists distinguish. The sociologist José Casanova, in his study Public Religions in the Modern World (1994), separated the differentiation of religious from political, economic, and scientific institutions; the decline of religious belief and practice; and the privatization of religion. These can occur independently. A society may separate church and state while retaining high levels of belief, as the United States has often been described, or it may retain a state church while most of its population rarely attends services, as in several northern European countries. The older "secularization thesis," associated with early sociologists and often connected with ideas of modernization, held that industrialization, science, and urbanization would inevitably reduce religion, and it has been substantially revised by those who proposed it and by historians who emphasize that the pattern is contingent.

Evidence of change in Europe is substantial. Attendance at services, reception of sacraments, church membership, and the numbers of clergy and members of religious orders declined in much of western, northern, and central Europe after the 1960s, with variation by country and denomination. Surveys such as the European Values Study and Eurobarometer show widely differing levels of religious identification, belief in God, and practice, with relatively higher levels in Poland, Romania, Greece, Cyprus, and Malta and lower levels in Estonia, the Czech Republic, Sweden, and the Netherlands. The proportion of people who describe themselves as having no religion has grown in many states. Yet these measures capture only parts of religion. The sociologist Grace Davie described a pattern of "believing without belonging" and, in a later formulation, "vicarious religion," in which many people who do not attend regularly expect churches to function on behalf of society at moments of national mourning, baptism, marriage, and death. Baptism, church weddings, and funerals continue in many places, even while participation in weekly worship has fallen. Religious practice has also changed form: pilgrimage, particularly to Santiago de Compostela, has seen renewed interest, Pentecostal and charismatic churches have grown, and spiritual practices outside organized religion have expanded.

The legal frameworks for religion vary widely. France has a constitutional principle of laïcité; Germany cooperates with recognized religious bodies and collects church tax on their behalf; the United Kingdom has an established Church of England and a plural legal landscape; Greece grants a prominent constitutional position to the Orthodox Church; Sweden disestablished its Lutheran church in 2000; Norway changed its constitutional arrangement in 2012; and Denmark retains a national church. At the supranational level, Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights, in force since 1953, protects freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, including the freedom to change religion, subject to limitations necessary in a democratic society. The Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, since the Treaty of Lisbon, commits the Union to respect the status that churches have under national law and to maintain dialogue with them. The debate over a reference to Europe's Christian heritage in the preamble of the proposed constitutional treaty of 2003 to 2004, which did not include one, illustrated disagreement among states about religion's role in European identity.

Contemporary debates involve both freedom of religion and freedom from religious coercion. They include questions about religious symbols in public institutions, the funding of religious schools, exemptions from general law on grounds of conscience, the regulation of religious dress, blasphemy laws in some states, the treatment of minorities, and the relationship between religious and national identity. Political actors use religious language in different ways: some speak of a "Christian Europe" as a cultural identity, others stress secular and Enlightenment values, and others emphasize pluralism. Historians note that all three rhetorics invoke selected pieces of a much more complicated past. Treating Europe as "essentially Christian" ignores Jewish, Muslim, and nonreligious histories, while treating it as "essentially secular" ignores the persistence of religion and the late and contested character of secular institutions.

European religious history cannot be reduced to a single movement from belief to unbelief, or from Christianity to pluralism. The record shows recurring interactions among belief, political authority, community membership, cultural identity, legal status, and individual conscience, with different outcomes in different places and times. Contemporary Europe's religious situation is a historical result, not a final stage, and its future is not predictable from this history. The chapter's concluding point is therefore methodological as well as substantive: religion in Europe must be studied as a changing set of relationships, among institutions, communities, and individuals, and not as a fixed essence of the continent. Chapter 141 turns to a related comparative theme, the long relationship among war, states, law, and sovereignty.

Historical Vocabulary
Secularization

Secularization refers to several related but separable processes: the differentiation of religious institutions from politics, law, education, and economy; the decline of religious belief and practice; the privatization of religion; and, in an older sense, the transfer of church property or functions to secular authorities (as in the revolutionary confiscations of church land). Because these processes can occur independently, claims that a society is "secularized" require specifying which meaning is intended. The term also carries the risk of teleology, implying that decline is inevitable, a view that this chapter rejects.

Historical Debate

Does Secularization Necessarily Mean the Decline of Religious Belief?

The question: Have industrialization, urbanization, education, science, and the modern state caused religion in Europe to decline, or have they changed the form, location, and public role of religion without eliminating it?

Interpretation A, secularization as long-term decline: Some sociologists and historians argue that the evidence of falling church attendance, declining membership, weakening of religious authority in public institutions, and increasing proportions of people with no religion indicates a real and lasting weakening of religion in much of Europe, linked to modern conditions such as pluralism, prosperity, and education. Scholars such as Steve Bruce have defended versions of this view, and some historians of Britain, such as Callum Brown, have argued for a particularly rapid decline from the 1960s associated with cultural change.
Interpretation B, transformation, persistence, and revival: Other scholars argue that the picture is more complex. Grace Davie has emphasized continued belief without institutional belonging and the vicarious role of churches; José Casanova has argued that religion remains publicly significant even where institutional differentiation has occurred; and historians point to persistent popular religion, new movements, and religious dimensions of national identity. They also argue that Europe's pattern, rather than being the global norm, is an exceptional case among world regions, and that the older assumption that modernization inevitably reduces religion has been undermined by developments elsewhere.

Evidence: Both positions rely on survey data, church statistics, legal changes, and qualitative studies. Interpretation A emphasizes long-term series of attendance and membership figures, while B emphasizes data on belief, ritual participation, and public religion. Historians also debate the chronology of decline, since some find evidence of falling practice in industrial cities in the nineteenth century, while others locate the main shift in the 1960s, and they note that survey questions and census categories vary and change over time, which complicates comparison.

Why it matters: How one understands the debate affects interpretations of contemporary European politics, including questions about religion in public life and about the integration of minority religious communities, and shapes whether secular modernity is seen as a universal destination or a historically specific outcome.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has synthesized the religious history of Europe across the six volumes of the textbook, asking how Europe moved from diverse ancient religious worlds, through a long era of Christian institutional dominance, toward modern societies with varied combinations of religious continuity, pluralism, and secularization. Its central argument is that this movement was neither uniform nor inevitable, and that European religious history consists of recurring negotiations among belief, political authority, community, identity, law, and conscience.

Division I showed that pre-Christian Europe had no single religious identity. Greek and Roman religion was civic and ritual, Celtic, Germanic, Slavic, and other societies had varied practices known mainly through archaeology and outsiders' accounts, and Jewish communities were part of the Mediterranean world. Christianity emerged within Second Temple Judaism, spread through Roman urban networks, developed internal diversity, and became an imperial religion in the fourth century through a sequence of political decisions. Councils defined doctrine, bishops and monasteries became central institutions, and divisions after Chalcedon established that Christendom was never a single church. The Christianization of post-Roman, northern, central, and eastern Europe, from the baptism of Clovis around 500 to the conversion of Lithuania in the 1380s, was a prolonged and uneven social process, not a single event.

Division II examined medieval Christendom as a plural world. Latin Christianity developed the papacy, episcopal and parish networks, monastic and mendicant orders, canon law, and universities, and shaped law, learning, art, and daily routines, though not every person was uniformly devout. Byzantine Orthodoxy developed a different relationship between church and empire, extended through Slavic conversion to Bulgaria, Serbia, and Rus, and became estranged from the Latin church through a gradual process in which 1054 is a symbolic and not a decisive date. Jewish communities were scholars, merchants, physicians, and neighbors, and also targets of restrictions, massacres, and expulsions. Muslim societies in Iberia and Sicily and, later, Ottoman rule in southeastern Europe placed Islam inside the European story and produced varied arrangements of coexistence, hierarchy, conversion, and conflict.

Division III treated the Reformation as the culmination of long-standing patterns of reform and dissent, not their beginning. Monastic and mendicant reform, Waldensian, Lollard, and Hussite dissent, and conciliar movements preceded Luther. The Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, and radical Reformations, and the Catholic reform associated with Trent and the Jesuits, produced competing confessional systems tied in different ways to rulers, cities, and communities. Confessionalization offers a valuable comparative model of the link between religious discipline and state formation, but it must be tested against regional evidence and does not fit all cases. Religious conflict in France, the Netherlands, the British Isles, and the Holy Roman Empire involved religion together with politics and dynastic rivalry.

The settlements that followed, in 1555, 1598, 1648, and 1689, were instruments of selective toleration or corporate privilege and not of universal religious liberty. Distinguishing toleration, corporate religious privilege, established churches, and individual freedom of religion is one of the chapter's principal analytical tasks. Individual freedom was argued by a minority of thinkers and sects and became law slowly and unevenly.

Division IV broadened the frame. Early modern religious diversity in the Ottoman Empire, Poland-Lithuania, the Habsburg lands, Transylvania, and Russia shows that the western model of one state, one confession was not typical of the continent. The Enlightenment produced criticism, deism, and some atheism, but also religious revival and conciliatory thought, and it contributed both to ideas of universal rights and to prejudice against Jews. The French Revolution and Napoleonic settlement redefined the relation of church and state, and the nineteenth century saw the unequal extension of civil rights to dissenters, Catholics, and Jews, together with Catholic revival, Protestant activism, national Orthodox churches, social Christianity, and the emergence of modern antisemitism.

Division V examined the twentieth century. Churches were often associated with national causes in the world wars, took varied positions toward authoritarian regimes, and responded to Nazi persecution of Jews and others with accommodation, complicity, protest, rescue, and resistance, in a context where Christian anti-Judaism, modern antisemitism, and Nazi racial ideology were connected but distinct. After 1945, communist regimes attempted to reduce religion's role with varying results, from the church's autonomy in Poland to deep secularization in East Germany and Czechoslovakia, while western churches worked within democracies, Christian democracy, and ecumenism, and many experienced declining participation. Migration and long-established minority communities produced a pluralism that includes Muslims, Jews, Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, new Christian communities, and large nonreligious populations, with distinct national debates over symbols, education, and belonging.

Secularization, as the final section argued, names several processes that do not always coincide: institutional differentiation, decline of belief and practice, and privatization of religion. Europe shows significant decline in institutional participation in many countries, alongside persistent belief, vicarious and cultural religion, new movements, and public religion, and legal arrangements ranging from laïcité to cooperative systems to established churches. The older assumption that modernization inevitably eliminates religion has been substantially revised.

Regional differences were large throughout. Iberia, the Balkans, Poland-Lithuania, Scandinavia, the Low Countries, the German lands, and Russia each had distinctive histories, and treating any single region as the European norm distorts the pattern. Continuity and change coexisted: the tension between religious and political authority, the gap between doctrine and practice, and the connection between religion and communal identity recurred across periods, while the legal status of religious communities, the functions of churches, and the range of religious options changed profoundly.

The chapter's conclusion is that European religious history cannot be represented by a single movement from Christianity to secularism. It is a history of interactions among institutions, communities, and individuals, in which Christianity was central but not exclusive, Judaism and Islam were integral, dissent and unbelief were persistent, and contemporary arrangements are historically contingent outcomes. These themes lead directly to Chapter 141, which examines the comparative history of war, states, law, and sovereignty.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Christendom
The community of Christian peoples, and in the medieval Latin west an ideal of a society unified under Christian faith; distinct from the geography of Europe and divided among Latin, Orthodox, and other churches.
Latin Christianity
The Christian tradition of the western Roman Empire and its successors, using Latin liturgy and law and recognizing papal primacy in the medieval period.
Orthodoxy
The Christian tradition associated with Constantinople and the Greek and Slavic churches; also used generally for correct doctrine as defined by an authority, a label that depended on who held power to define it.
Heresy
A doctrine or movement condemned by church authorities as contrary to accepted teaching; the label expressed institutional judgment and often hid considerable diversity among those so labeled.
Ecumenical council
An assembly of bishops from across the church that defined doctrine and discipline, beginning with Nicaea in 325; later councils such as Chalcedon produced lasting divisions.
Non-Chalcedonian churches
Eastern Christian traditions, including the Coptic, Syriac, and Armenian churches, that did not accept the Council of Chalcedon's definition of 451.
Schism
A formal division within a church; the Great Schism between Latin and Greek churches developed gradually, with 1054 as a symbolic date, and the Western Schism of 1378 to 1417 divided the Latin church between rival popes.
Conciliarism
The late medieval theory that a general council held authority within the church above the pope, influential at Constance.
Anticlericalism
Criticism of clergy for wealth, privilege, or misconduct; present in medieval and modern societies and not identical with rejection of religion.
Interpretatio Romana
The Roman practice of identifying foreign gods with Roman deities, a feature of the religious pluralism of the empire.
Anti-Judaism
Religious and cultural hostility to Jews and Judaism rooted in Christian theology and practice, in which conversion was in principle an exit.
Antisemitism
A modern term, popularized in the late 1870s, for political and racial hostility to Jews that treats them as a biologically defined group.
Converso
A Jew who converted to Christianity in medieval Iberia, or the descendants of such converts, who often faced suspicion about the sincerity of conversion.
Mudéjar and Morisco
Mudéjars were Muslims living under Christian rule in Iberia; Moriscos were converts from Islam and their descendants, expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614.
Millet system
A modern term for Ottoman arrangements granting recognized non-Muslim communities, such as Orthodox Christians and Jews, internal religious and legal administration; historians stress its gradual and uneven development.
Devshirme
The Ottoman practice of recruiting Christian boys, mainly from the Balkans, for military and administrative service.
Lollards and Hussites
Followers of John Wycliffe in England and of Jan Hus in Bohemia, whose movements anticipated aspects of Protestant criticism.
Justification by faith
Luther's doctrine that sinners are made right with God through faith and divine grace rather than through their own works.
Magisterial Reformation
Reform movements, including Lutheran, Reformed, and Anglican, that worked through rulers and magistrates, as distinct from the radical Reformation.
Radical Reformation
Movements such as the Anabaptists that rejected infant baptism and often advocated a degree of separation from established church-and-state authority; they were persecuted by Catholics and Protestants.
Confession
A formally defined religious body and statement of faith, such as Lutheran, Reformed, or Catholic, in early modern usage.
Confessionalization
A historical interpretation describing how churches and states together shaped confessional identities and discipline in early modern Europe.
Cuius regio, eius religio
A later formula summarizing the Peace of Augsburg's principle that a territorial ruler determined the religion of the territory, with the right of dissenting subjects to emigrate.
Toleration
Forbearance of a religion an authority regards as mistaken; before the eighteenth century usually conditional and revocable, and distinct from a right to religious freedom.
Corporate religious privilege
Legal rights held by recognized religious groups as collectives, as in the Holy Roman Empire or Ottoman Empire.
Established church
A church officially recognized and supported by the state, such as the Church of England or, formerly, the Lutheran churches of the Nordic states.
Concordat
An agreement between the papacy and a state regulating the church's legal position, such as Napoleon's of 1801.
Emancipation
The removal of legal disabilities based on religion, as with Catholic and Jewish emancipation in the nineteenth century.
Disestablishment
The ending of a church's official status, as with the Church of Ireland under the Irish Church Act of 1869 (effective 1871) and the Church of Sweden in 2000.
Laïcité
The French principle of state neutrality toward religion, formalized in the 1905 law; related to, but distinct from, secularism in other countries.
Deism
The belief in a creator God known through reason and nature, without special revelation or church authority, common among Enlightenment writers.
Secularization
A set of processes including institutional differentiation of religion from other spheres, declining belief or practice, and privatization of religion; not an inevitable disappearance of religion.
Pluralism
The coexistence of several religions or worldviews within a society, which can be legally recognized or only informal.
Ecumenism
The movement for cooperation and unity among Christian churches, institutionalized in the World Council of Churches in 1948.
Spanish Inquisition
A tribunal established in 1478 under the Spanish crowns that investigated the beliefs of converts and later others.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because religion touched nearly every institution and experience the earlier chapters describe, from imperial legitimacy and monastic land to the family, the school, the courts, and the borders of states. Bringing those threads together shows that the history of European religion is a history of relationships. Its recurring problems were how rulers and religious institutions would share or contest authority, how dominant and minority communities would live together, how belief and practice would diverge, and how individuals would claim a conscience of their own.

The chapter also reveals how much later developments depended on contingent decisions. The imperial adoption of Christianity, the failure to restore a single western church after 1517, the compromises of 1555, 1598, 1648, and 1689, the revolutionary confrontation with the church, and the destruction of European Jewry were not predetermined. Neither was the emergence of the legal protections of religious freedom after 1945. Present arrangements, whether in the French Republic, the Nordic state churches, post-communist Poland, or multi-faith cities, are historical products and may continue to change.

For Volume VI, the chapter provides a long-term background to the Cold War, integration, and contemporary pluralism, and shows that the apparent contrast between a religious east and a secular west conceals many local differences. For the whole textbook, it demonstrates that the continent's history cannot be told through Christianity alone, nor without it. Jewish, Muslim, Orthodox, Protestant, Catholic, dissenting, and nonreligious communities each form part of the record, and each experienced the same events differently.

Chapter 141 will take up a related comparative problem: the long relationship among war, states, law, and sovereignty. Many of the questions raised here, including who may command obedience, how conscience relates to law, and what rights belong to individuals and communities, will reappear there in political and institutional form.
