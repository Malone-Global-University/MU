Industrial Workers, Mines, Unions, and Social Conflict

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Japan's industrial revolution is often told through machines, ministries, and great firms. This chapter tells it through the people who tended spindles, cut coal, smelted copper, laid track, and farmed the fields downstream of the mines, and through the arguments, petitions, strikes, and riots by which they made their experience a political question.

Consider three places in the Japan of the early 1900s. In the Suwa basin of Nagano, in steam-heated sheds beside a mountain lake, thousands of young women reeled raw silk that would be shipped from Yokohama to buyers abroad. In the Chikuhō coalfield of northern Kyushu, miners and their families, often working in pairs underground, cut the coal that fueled steamships, railways, and the furnaces of a growing industrial economy. Along the Watarase River in Tochigi and its neighboring prefectures, farmers watched fish die, floods carry poisoned silt onto their fields, and crops fail, while upstream at Ashio a copper mine earned export income for its owners and for the state. These were not separate Japans. Silk and copper earned foreign exchange that paid for machinery and warships; coal powered the ships that carried the exports; and the workers and farmers in each place were drawn into the same national project, though on very different terms.

The chapter examines industrialization from the standpoint of the people who experienced it. Its central argument is that Japan's industrial expansion created concentrations of wage labor and generated great productive capacity and export earnings, but that workers met this growth in the form of long and demanding schedules, workplace discipline, migration, insecure contracts, occupational hazards, and sharply unequal bargaining power. Their responses, including quitting and bargaining, strikes, mutual-aid societies, unions, environmental protest, reform campaigns, and socialist organizing, made labor conflict a central political question in modern Japan. The chapter does not treat workers simply as victims, nor does it treat employers as a uniform group. Wages sometimes rose, young women gained earnings and experiences unavailable in village life, skilled men moved between employers on favorable terms, and some companies provided real welfare. These gains coexisted with coercion, danger, and repression, and the chapter keeps both in view.

A second argument concerns what "the labor movement" means for this period. Early labor organizing was not a single, continuous rise of modern trade unions. Craft unions of the late 1890s, the spontaneous mining riots of 1907, employer-sponsored welfare associations, farmers' petition marches against mine pollution, and the Friendly Society (Yūaikai) founded in 1912 represented different forms of industrial politics, with different participants, aims, and relationships to the state. Some were crushed or faded; others survived by moderating their goals. Treating them as one linear story would hide the contingency of the period and the genuine differences among skilled men in railway shops, women in textile mills, contract miners in the mountains, and downstream farmers.

The chapter relies on a wide range of evidence, each kind with its own strengths and biases. Government investigations such as the 1903 Shokkō Jijō (Conditions of Factory Workers), company records, police and court documents, newspapers and labor journals, petitions, parliamentary debates, and journalistic exposés allow historians to reconstruct the outlines of industrial life. Direct testimony from workers themselves is scarcer, and much of it was recorded later, sometimes decades later, by memoirists, novelists, and painters. Students should therefore ask of every source who produced it, for what purpose, and whose voice is missing. The chapter points out these limitations as they arise rather than treating any single source as transparent.

Regional and minority histories remain visible throughout. Industrial Japan was not simply Tokyo and Osaka: it was also the silk districts of the central mountains, the coalfields of Kyushu and Hokkaido, the copper mines of Tochigi and Shikoku, and the shipyards and arsenals of port cities. Hokkaido, colonized and developed in these decades, relied on migrant and convict labor in its mines and railways, and its Ainu communities faced displacement and exclusion from the new economy. Ryukyuan migrants had only begun to move to the mainland and overseas. Descendants of the former outcaste groups faced discrimination in hiring even after the emancipation of 1871. Where evidence permits, the chapter attends to these communities rather than allowing a generic "worker" to stand for all.

The chapter's chronological focus runs from the 1870s through the late 1910s, with its greatest depth between the 1880s and 1916. It builds directly on Chapter 56, which examined industrial capitalism and the corporate groups, and on Chapter 57's account of the political movements of the 1880s. It sets aside the colonial labor system in Korea (Chapter 62), the broader history of gender and family (Chapter 64), the wartime economy after 1914 (Chapter 66), the Rice Riots (Chapter 68), and the mature labor and suffrage politics of the Taishō period (Chapter 69). What it supplies is the foundation: how a wage-working population took shape, how its first generation of protests unfolded, how the state answered with both repression and reform, and why the "labor question" had become unavoidable by 1916.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how industrial wage labor developed out of Tokugawa-era household and workshop production rather than replacing it instantaneously.
  2. Compare silk reeling, cotton spinning, coal mining, and metal mining as workplaces, identifying differences in recruitment, work organization, gender, and bargaining power.
  3. Analyze the role of rural households, migration, recruiters, contracts, and dormitories in supplying and controlling the industrial workforce.
  4. Evaluate evidence for factory and mine conditions, including government investigations, journalism, and later memory, with attention to the limits of each.
  5. Trace the Ashio pollution crisis from mine expansion to farmers' petitions, Tanaka Shōzō's campaigns, and the displacement of Yanaka Village.
  6. Distinguish the agrarian anti-pollution movement from the miners' disturbances of 1907 while assessing how historians relate them.
  7. Describe the forms of early labor organization from craft unions and the Kiseikai (1897) through the railway strike of 1898 to the Yūaikai (1912), and explain why they differed.
  8. Assess the purposes, provisions, and effects of the Public Peace Police Law of 1900 on labor organizing.
  9. Distinguish the enactment of the Factory Act of 1911 from its implementation in 1916, and explain the gaps in protection.
  10. Interpret corporate paternalism as both welfare provision and a system of labor discipline.
  11. Connect industrial labor conflict to wider questions of constitutional government, state authority, gender, and social movements in late Meiji and early Taishō Japan.
  12. Include marginalized and regional communities, among them women, children, Ainu, Burakumin, and migrants, in an account of industrial society.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who became Japan's industrial workers, why did they enter industrial employment, and how did their experiences differ by gender, region, age, and occupation?
  2. How much choice did young women recruited into textile factories actually have, and how should historians weigh employer control against worker agency?
  3. Why did mining labor develop such different employment practices from factory labor, and what were the consequences for safety, debt, and protest?
  4. What does the Ashio crisis reveal about the relationship among industrial growth, environmental damage, private enterprise, and the Meiji state?
  5. Why did early unions in the 1890s fail to become durable mass organizations, and what did later organizers learn from them?
  6. How did the Public Peace Police Law of 1900 shape the possibilities of labor organizing without outlawing unions entirely?
  7. Why did it take until 1911 to pass a Factory Act and until 1916 to enforce it, and who benefited from the delay?
  8. How should historians evaluate sources that describe factory and mine life, including official surveys, journalism, employer records, and retrospective memoirs?
  9. To what extent did industrial workers participate in, rather than merely endure, the making of modern Japan's political order?

Historical Context

Chapter 56 traced the emergence of industrial capitalism in Meiji Japan: government-built model enterprises, the sale of many of them to private buyers in the 1880s, the growth of banks and railways, and the rise of the great business groups. Here it is enough to recall three points. First, the state actively sponsored industrial development and was deeply entangled with private firms, so that conflicts between workers and employers were never purely private matters. Second, Japan's strongest export industries before the First World War were not heavy industries but raw silk and cotton yarn, along with copper and coal. Third, industrial growth was financed in part by a heavy land tax and by agricultural surpluses, so rural households were bound to the industrial economy from the beginning.

Tokugawa society had not lacked wage labor, specialized craftsmen, or commercial manufacturing. Sake brewers, textile putting-out merchants, mine operators, and urban craft shops employed hired and bound workers, and rural households routinely combined farming with by-employment in spinning, weaving, and sericulture. Mining had its own institutions, including fraternities of miners. What changed after 1868 was the scale and the technology: steam power, imported machinery, large-capital firms, new transport, and the pressure of competition in a world market. The legal framework changed too. The abolition of the status order (Chapter 55) removed formal restrictions on occupation and movement, while the Meiji state's gradual construction of civil, commercial, and criminal law defined the contract, the wage, and the permissible forms of collective action.

The constitutional order of 1889–1890 (Chapter 59) mattered as well. The Imperial Diet gave landholders and some businessmen a voice, and the franchise was restricted by a tax qualification that excluded virtually all workers. Workers could not vote, women could not take part in political associations, and the police retained wide powers. Labor grievances therefore had to find expression through other channels: petition, newspaper campaigns, social reform movements, religious philanthropy, strikes, and riots. The Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 (Chapter 61) intensified these pressures through taxation, inflation, and the expansion of heavy industry.

This chapter treats the metropole, meaning the Japanese islands including Hokkaido. Industrial labor in the colonies, such as Taiwan and Korea, followed different legal and ethnic hierarchies and belongs to other chapters. The wartime boom after 1914 transformed industrial employment again, and its consequences, including the Rice Riots and the maturing of unions, are the subject of Chapters 66, 68, and 69.

Section 63.1 · Division I — Industrialization and the Formation of a Wage-Working Population

From Household Production to Industrial Wage Labor

The industrial workers of Meiji Japan did not appear from nowhere. Late Tokugawa villages and towns already contained commercial manufacturing: rural households spun and wove cotton and reeled silk for merchants who supplied materials and collected the product; brewers hired seasonal skilled workers; urban craft shops trained apprentices; and mines employed specialized crews. Many farming families depended on by-employment, meaning income from sericulture, handicrafts, or seasonal labor, to supplement what small plots of land could provide. The new industries of the Meiji era built on these habits. Hand-reeling silk at home, for example, gave rural families both the skills and the expectation that women's labor would produce marketable goods. What changed was that production moved increasingly out of the household and into establishments owned by others, where hours, pace, and quality were dictated by managers and machines.

The transition was gradual and uneven. The government's own model enterprises of the 1870s, such as the Tomioka Silk Mill (1872), naval and army arsenals, and mines, offered early examples of concentrated wage employment. In the 1880s the sale of many state enterprises to private buyers and the founding of large private firms enlarged the factory sector. Yet for most of the period covered here, a majority of Japanese still worked in agriculture, and a great many "factories" were small workshops with a few workers. Official statistics generally counted only establishments above a minimum size, which means that the numbers used by historians describe a rapidly growing but still minority segment of employment. The factory sector coexisted with a large world of household production, small workshops, day labor, and domestic service.

Industrial wage labor in this sense meant something different from earlier hired labor. Workers sold working time to an employer and were paid wages, either by the day or by the quantity produced, in a setting where machines or a rationalized division of tasks set the rhythm of work. They were often housed away from their families, supervised by foremen, and subject to written or customary rules. Because rural households continued to hold land and to provide a safety net, many workers moved between farm and factory rather than becoming permanent proletarians. Historians have debated how far this circulation reduced workers' dependence on wages and how far it strengthened employers' ability to treat workers as replaceable.

Section 63.2

The Geography of Industrial Employment: Cities, Rural Districts, and Mining Regions

Industrial employment in Japan was concentrated in distinct regions, each shaped by its own resources, transport links, and labor supply. Osaka, with its port, commercial capital, and access to the Inland Sea, became the center of cotton spinning and was widely called the "Manchester of the East." Large mills there and in neighboring Hyōgo and Mie drew workers from the surrounding Kinki region and from western Japan, and housed many of them in company dormitories. Tokyo and Yokohama developed machinery, shipbuilding, printing, and light manufacturing, and the Kobe–Osaka corridor added shipyards, ironworks, and machine shops. Naval arsenals at Yokosuka and Kure, army arsenals, and the state-owned Yawata Steel Works (opened in 1901 in northern Kyushu) offered concentrated employment for skilled and unskilled men.

Silk reeling followed a different geography. The raw material came from the mulberry-growing, cocoon-producing districts of central and eastern Japan, and reeling establishments clustered in basins such as Suwa and Okaya in Nagano, in Gunma, and in Yamanashi, close to cocoon supply but not necessarily close to large cities. Workers came from nearby villages and from more distant mountain districts, so that a Nagano silk town was a seasonal destination for migrant women. The export route ran through Yokohama, where foreign merchants graded and bought the silk, and prices there transmitted world demand directly to workers' wages and employers' decisions.

Mining regions were more isolated still. The Chikuhō field in Fukuoka Prefecture became Japan's greatest coal-producing area, joined by the Miike mine near Ōmuta, the island mine of Takashima off Nagasaki, and, increasingly, mines in Hokkaido, where the Horonai and Yūbari fields were developed with the aid of migrant workers and, for a time, convicts. Copper and other metals came from Ashio in Tochigi, Besshi in Ehime on Shikoku, and other mountain mines, whose communities lived beside the works. Mining settlements often had their own housing, shops, schools, and rules, and workers were dependent on the company for nearly everything. Railway shops, shipyards, and printing houses formed a fourth, more urban, and generally more skilled sector. Understanding these differences is the first step toward understanding why industrial conflict took such varied forms.

Section 63.3

Rural Households, Migration, and the Supply of Industrial Workers

Industrial employers did not draw workers from an undifferentiated mass. They recruited mostly from farm households whose circumstances made outside earnings valuable. The land-tax reform of the 1870s fixed tax payments in cash, and the severe deflation of the early 1880s (Chapter 56) pushed many smallholders into debt and tenancy. By the early twentieth century, roughly forty percent of cultivated land was farmed by tenants, who paid substantial rents in kind. Households with small plots, large families, or heavy obligations therefore saw wage work, and particularly the wages of daughters, as a means of paying taxes, rent, and debts, of meeting ceremonial costs, and of surviving poor harvests.

The characteristic pattern was dekasegi, temporary work away from home. Young women might spend a few years in a mill before returning to marry; men might work seasonally in mines, construction, or fishing and return for planting. Some did not return, and a growing number settled in industrial towns and mining communities. Decisions were typically made at the household level. Parents, particularly fathers, often negotiated with recruiters and accepted cash advances against a daughter's future wages, which meant the household received income before the work began and the worker owed service afterward. The young worker's own preferences are harder to recover. Some evidently sought wages and independence from the village; others were sent reluctantly.

Regional differences mattered. Households in silk-producing districts could combine cocoon production with daughters' reeling; households in poorer mountain districts, with little arable land, depended more heavily on sending members away. Hokkaido drew settlers and temporary laborers from northern Honshu, and its mines and railway construction camps relied on men recruited by contractors, often in coercive conditions. Ryukyuan migration to the mainland factories was still small in this period, though emigration overseas had begun at the turn of the century and the pattern of labor outmigration from Okinawa would expand later. The result was a workforce linked to the countryside by family obligations, remittances, and seasonal return, and employers who depended on those links while also treating rural labor as plentiful and replaceable.

Section 63.4

Factories, Workshops, Mines, and the Changing Organization of Production

Not all industrial workplaces were organized alike, and the differences shaped workers' bargaining power. Large cotton-spinning mills were centralized, machine-paced establishments with hundreds or thousands of employees, hierarchically supervised, and dependent on continuous operation. Silk filatures were smaller, often employing dozens or a few hundred reelers working at basins, with output measured and graded by quality. In weaving and many other trades, small workshops and putting-out arrangements persisted, with household members working in dispersed units for merchants. Heavy industry, shipyards, machine shops, and railway workshops required skilled men whose training took years.

Skilled trades were frequently organized through the oyakata system, in which a master craftsman or labor boss contracted with the company to produce a task and hired, trained, and paid his own subordinates. The arrangement drew on Tokugawa-era craft relations and left considerable authority with the oyakata, who could discipline workers and shape wages. Skilled workers also moved readily among employers, sometimes called wandering craftsmen, partly because their skills were scarce and partly because they resisted the loss of autonomy. Managers in turn sought to reduce the oyakata's independence and to employ workers directly. This long struggle over who controlled the worker, the boss or the company, is a recurring theme in the history of Japanese industrial relations.

Mines used a different form of subcontracting, examined in Section 63.14, in which labor bosses housed and supervised miners and took a share of their earnings. In mines workers were often physically isolated and dependent on a single employer for housing and food, which weakened their ability to bargain. In textile mills, the employer could hire cheaply from a large pool of rural women but depended on retaining trained workers, which gave workers some leverage when competition for labor was fierce. Skilled machinists and railway engineers possessed the strongest bargaining positions, because their skills were scarce and their employers' operations depended on them. These contrasts help explain why the first unions arose among skilled men in railways and metalworking rather than among the much larger numbers of women in textiles or men in mines.

Section 63.5

Wages, Working Hours, Contracts, and the Evidence of Industrial Life

Wages were paid in several forms. Daily wages were common in construction, ports, and many mines and workshops; piece rates were widespread in textiles, mining, and some skilled trades; and many mills combined a base wage with bonuses, grading, and fines. Women's wages were generally well below men's, reflecting both assumptions about women's work and the fact that women were concentrated in lower-paid industries. Deductions for food, lodging, tools, and penalties for spoiled work could reduce net pay considerably, and employers often withheld part of wages as forced savings, released only at the end of a contract, which discouraged workers from leaving. Real wages are difficult to calculate because prices, regional costs of living, and deductions varied. Most historians conclude that wages rose slowly over the period for many groups, with notable fluctuations, and that the gains for unskilled workers were modest compared with the growth in output and profits.

Working hours were long. Twelve hours was common in many factories, and some workplaces, particularly silk filatures during busy seasons, required longer days. Night work in cotton spinning ran on a two-shift system. Rest days were irregular and holidays few. Mining shifts depended on the work to be done and on the contractor's arrangements. Contracts varied from daily hire to multi-year terms, and many were negotiated not with workers themselves but with parents or intermediaries. The law gave workers little protection; contract law treated the worker's obligations strictly, and until 1916 there was no national statute limiting hours or regulating conditions in factories.

Reconstructing this world requires caution. Government statistics of factories and workers were incomplete and defined by size thresholds. Employers' records emphasized efficiency and discipline and omitted worker complaints; official investigators depended on employers' cooperation; journalists and reformers wrote to persuade; and memoirs and later novels, such as the retrospective accounts that appeared in the 1920s, shaped public memory but were written long after the events. Direct testimony by factory women is scarce, and the voices of miners and their families survive mainly in legal records, newspapers, and later reminiscences and paintings. A careful historian compares sources, asks what each leaves out, and avoids generalizing from the most dramatic examples.

Historical Vocabulary
Wage Labor, Piece Rates, and Dekasegi

Wage labor refers to work performed in exchange for pay under an employer's direction. In Meiji Japan it took the form of daily wages, piece rates (payment by the quantity or quality produced), and contract wages negotiated for a term of months or years. Dekasegi (literally "going out to earn") describes temporary migration for paid work, typically by members of farm households who intended to return.

These terms matter because they correct two modern assumptions. Industrial workers were not simply permanent city proletarians cut off from the countryside, and wages did not necessarily mean the freedom of an individual bargain. Much of the labor was embedded in family decisions, and many contracts were arranged by others. The modern English phrase "wage labor" should not hide that mixture of market relations and household obligation.

Section 63.6 · Division II — Textile Factories, Women Workers, and Industrial Discipline

Silk-Reeling Workers in Nagano and the Export Economy

Raw silk was Japan's most important export for much of the late nineteenth century, and by about 1909 Japan had overtaken China as the world's leading exporter of raw silk. The industry rested on a chain of producers: farm households raised silkworms and sold cocoons; filatures, in which cocoons were steamed and the filaments reeled into skeins, produced raw silk; merchants and exporters, concentrated at Yokohama, sold to foreign buyers, especially in the United States. The government's Tomioka Silk Mill of 1872 introduced French-style mechanized reeling, and its trainees, many from former samurai families, carried technical knowledge to new regions. Private filatures in Nagano's Suwa and Okaya, in Gunma, and in Yamanashi later developed more modest, cheaper methods, using steam power and hand-operated or gradually mechanized reeling frames.

The workforce in the filatures was overwhelmingly female. Reeling required dexterity and attention: reelers kept filaments from several cocoons spinning at an even thickness and tied broken threads quickly. Wages often depended on the quantity and quality of silk reeled, and filatures used ranking systems that publicly graded workers and paid higher wages to those with better results. This system gave skilled workers real incentives and a measure of pride, but it also drove competition and intensified work. Because the industry's profits depended on the price of silk in Yokohama, which fluctuated, owners sought to hold labor costs down and to retain the best reelers.

Competition for skilled reelers was intense. Filature owners recruited from distant districts and also attempted to lure workers from rivals, and they responded with contract rules, penalty clauses, and agreements among employers to limit poaching. The research of E. Patricia Tsurumi and others has shown how these disputes over contracts and movement among establishments shaped the Suwa labor market. At the same time, the silk industry was highly seasonal in its demand for labor and sensitive to price swings, and when markets fell, wages were cut or workers dismissed. Women in silk reeling were therefore both valued for their skill and exposed to the volatility of a global commodity market.

Section 63.7

Osaka's Cotton-Spinning Industry and the Factory Shift System

Cotton spinning developed along different lines. The industry arose in response to competition from imported British and Indian yarn, and the founding of the Osaka Spinning Company in 1882–1883, with the support of Shibusawa Eiichi and capital from wealthy investors, showed that large mills using imported machinery and steam power could succeed. Other companies followed, and by the 1890s the sector had expanded rapidly; in 1897 Japan's yarn exports exceeded its imports. Raw cotton was imported from India, China, and later the United States, and yarn was sold at home and, increasingly, abroad. Large capital investment in machinery made it profitable to run the mills continuously, and the characteristic cotton-mill innovation was the two-shift system, in which separate groups of workers, usually working twelve-hour shifts, operated the machines day and night.

The work in cotton mills was machine-paced. Women and girls tended spinning frames, mended broken threads, and carried bobbins; men filled supervisory, mechanical, and heavy-handling positions. Wages were generally lower and the tasks less dependent on individual skill than in silk reeling, which meant that mill owners could replace workers more easily and relied less on competitive wage grading. Cotton lint, noise, heat, humidity, and night work imposed physical burdens. Large firms ran dormitories, enforced strict rules, and housed workers close to the mill, so that the company controlled much of the worker's life outside the workplace as well as within it.

The contrast between silk and cotton is an important one for students. Silk reeling was smaller in scale, dependent on skilled and graded labor, and sensitive to the world silk market; cotton spinning was larger in scale, more capital-intensive, and based on continuous machine operation, with more uniform tasks and a more systematic dormitory regime. Both depended on young women from rural households, and both used contracts and recruiters to obtain them, but conditions and the room for worker bargaining differed. Section 63.8

Recruitment Agents, Employment Contracts, Dormitories, and Labor Mobility

Factories obtained young women through recruiters (boshūnin), who traveled to villages, paid commissions or advanced money to families, and arranged transport. Recruiters were often local men or women familiar with the district, sometimes former workers. They persuaded parents by promising wages, board, and, in some cases, education or skills training. Contracts typically ran for one year in silk reeling or for terms of one to three years in cotton mills, and they specified wages, rules, and penalties for leaving early. Parents commonly received an advance, which the worker repaid through her labor. Because these arrangements tied families to employers and gave recruiters a financial stake, historians have described a system that mixed free negotiation, family strategy, and constraints that limited workers' freedom.

Dormitories were the second pillar of control. A company that housed its workers could organize shifts, regulate meals, enforce curfews, and supervise conduct. Employers argued that dormitories protected young women away from home and offered cheap, safe lodging; critics noted that gates, watchmen, and rules restricted movement and made it difficult to leave. Reports of locked or guarded dormitories, with fire danger and restrictions on visitors, appear in investigations, though practice varied from firm to firm and the evidence for particular claims has to be weighed carefully. Forced savings, fines, and withheld wages added financial reasons to stay. Workers nevertheless found ways to leave, and desertion and early departure were a persistent problem for employers, which is why they invested so heavily in surveillance and in agreements to return runaways.

Labor mobility thus presents a paradox. Workers had few formal rights, yet high turnover shows that many exercised the one option they had, which was to leave. Employers responded with inducements and with collective measures such as agreements not to hire workers from rival firms. Scholars influenced by Elyssa Faison's work on factory management have emphasized how company paternalism, with its language of family and moral protection, served both as genuine concern and as a strategy for managing labor, while others, such as Janet Hunter, have emphasized the labor-market dimension and the economic choices women made. The two emphases are compatible: women acted within strong constraints, and those constraints were shaped by gender, family, and contracts.

Section 63.9

Women at Work: Wages, Discipline, Health, and Everyday Experience

By the early twentieth century women made up a majority of Japan's factory workers, around six in ten in official counts of the period, concentrated in textiles. Their days were structured by bells and shifts. Work began early; meals were brief and usually provided by the employer; supervisors, often men, inspected work, imposed fines, and graded output. Fatigue was constant, particularly in the cotton mills' night shifts and the silk filatures' busy seasons. Machines caused injuries; steam, damp air, and dust damaged health. Living conditions in dormitories, with crowding, poor ventilation, and shared bedding, aided the spread of infectious disease. Tuberculosis was the most serious. A 1913 study by the official Ishihara Osamu of the Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce, based on investigation of workers who left factories because of illness, drew attention to the high incidence of tuberculosis among returning workers and to the way that returning home to die concealed the toll from factory records.

The experience was not simply misery. Many women earned wages that their families valued, learned skills, formed friendships, and sometimes used earnings or savings to support dowries, family needs, or education. Some enjoyed the novelty of towns, festivals, and shared life with other young women. Women also negotiated with employers, evaded rules, and left. Historians such as Tsurumi have documented both the severity of working conditions and the active forms of response, while Hunter and others have argued that studying women as participants in a labor market, rather than only as victims, reveals the economic logic of their choices. Memory complicates this further. The most famous accounts of factory life, among them the journalist Yokoyama Gennosuke's 1899 Nihon no Kasō Shakai (Japan's Lower Classes) and Hosoi Wakizō's Jokō Aishi (The Pitiful History of Female Factory Workers), published in 1925, were written to expose abuses and are powerful but selective; Hosoi's book, moreover, was written well after the period covered here.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Working Day in a Silk-Reeling or Cotton-Spinning Factory

The following is a composite reconstruction drawn from investigations and scholarship. Details varied by firm, region, and season, and it is not the record of any individual worker.

The day began before dawn with a bell or whistle. In a silk filature, reelers took their places at basins of hot water in which cocoons floated, and spent the long working day keeping the silk thread even, repairing breaks, and handing finished skeins to inspectors. The steamy atmosphere, the smell of cooked cocoons, and the sound of machinery filled the shed. In a cotton mill, a worker on the day shift or night shift attended rows of spinning frames, running among them to restore broken threads, with the whirr of the machinery, cotton dust, and heat filling the air. Meals were taken quickly, in a dining room or at the workplace. When the shift ended, workers returned to a dormitory room shared with others, where bedding was shared by shift in some mills and where rules governed lights, visitors, and outings.

Rest days were few and often tied to festivals. Letters home, remittances, and occasional visits maintained the link to the village. Friendships formed in the dormitory, and informal solidarity, such as sharing food, covering for a sick friend, or warning of an inspector, could be as important to daily life as the rules. For many workers, the strongest memory was fatigue; for some, it was also pride in skill or the independence of earning.

Section 63.10

Child Labor, Apprenticeship, and the Boundaries between School and Work

Children worked in many Meiji industries. In spinning, reeling, matchmaking, glassmaking, and other trades, girls and boys in their early teens, and sometimes younger, performed tasks suited to small hands, such as tying broken threads, carrying bobbins, or tending simple machines. Their wages were low, and their presence reduced employers' costs. In small workshops, children entered as apprentices (deshi) who exchanged labor for training, board, and eventual skill. The older craft tradition of apprenticeship gave these children a defined path, but it could also be a cover for unpaid service and harsh treatment, and the boundary between instruction and exploitation was often thin.

Compulsory elementary schooling had been established in 1886 and was extended to six years in 1907, and attendance grew steadily, with rates around ninety percent overall by the early twentieth century, though lower for girls and for poor households. Yet school fees and the need for children's wages kept many families from sending their children regularly. In industrial districts, factories and mines competed with schools for children's time, and some mills ran company schools to meet requirements while preserving the labor supply. Thus the age at which a child began full-time work was shaped by the household economy, school availability, and employers' willingness to hire.

Reformers, officials, and some employers became concerned, in part for humanitarian reasons and in part because of the long-term effect on the health of the population and its military and economic potential. The 1903 factory survey drew attention to the number of young children in workplaces, and the debates that led to the Factory Act (Sections 63.26–63.27) addressed minimum age and hours for children. The Act's provisions, as will be seen, set a minimum age with exceptions and phased in limits, so that for many children the practical change came slowly.

Section 63.11

Resistance and Negotiation in the Textile Workplace

Textile workers resisted in many ways, most of them not formal strikes. Absenteeism, slowdowns, leaving without notice, returning home, and moving to rival firms were individual forms of response that signaled dissatisfaction and imposed costs on employers. Workers also submitted petitions to managers, protested particular supervisors, objected to fines, and organized informal groups in dormitories. These forms of negotiation reveal both the limits of workers' power and the fact that they were not passive. Employers' repeated efforts to prevent runaways and to share information about deserters show how real the threat was.

Collective action also occurred. In June 1886, women workers at the Amamiya silk-reeling establishment in Kōfu, Yamanashi, stopped work to protest longer hours and changes in wage conditions. The strike, often described as among the first modern strikes in Japan, ended with partial concessions, and it indicated that women could act together in the workplace even in the 1880s. Later disputes in silk and cotton factories, including walkouts over wage cuts, fines, and treatment, occurred sporadically through the 1890s and 1900s, though they rarely produced lasting organizations. The Public Peace Police Law of 1900, discussed in Section 63.25, discouraged efforts to organize them. It is therefore important to distinguish everyday workplace negotiation, which was common, from formal unionization, which among women textile workers was rare before the 1910s.

Section 63.12 · Division III — Mines, Industrial Hazards, Ashio, and Environmental Conflict

Coal Mining and the Energy Foundations of Industrial Japan

Coal was the energy base of Meiji industrialization. It fired the boilers of factories, powered railways and steamships, supplied the gas and smelting industries, and, through exports to Shanghai, Hong Kong, and other Asian ports, earned foreign exchange. Japan's coal output rose more than twentyfold between the late 1870s and the First World War, from under a million tons annually to well over twenty million. The Chikuhō field in northern Kyushu supplied close to half of national production, supplemented by Miike, Takashima, and the growing Hokkaido mines, which were developed after the colonization of the island and served Otaru and Muroran. Demand from the navy, the railways, and the merchant marine anchored the market.

Ownership shifted over the period. The state sold Miike to Mitsui in 1889 (after an auction in 1888); Mitsubishi had bought Takashima in 1881 and later acquired other mines; and independent operators, many of them local entrepreneurs, developed much of Chikuhō. By the early twentieth century the big firms, Mitsui and Mitsubishi, together with a few large independents, controlled much of the output, and coal was sold through trading arms such as Mitsui Bussan. Mining employment grew from a few thousand to well over a hundred thousand workers, including men, women, and children underground and on the surface. The scale of employment, combined with the isolated locations of many mines, created distinctive communities of mining families and distinctive systems of labor management, described in Section 63.14.

The state's own use of convict labor deserves mention. In the 1870s and after, prisoners were put to work in mines, and Miike employed convicts from several prefectures until 1931. In Hokkaido, convict labor built roads and worked mines in the 1880s, and the deaths of prisoners in this labor were a source of controversy. Convict labor kept costs low, set a harsh standard for free labor, and illustrates how coercion and industrial development were linked. The treatment of Ainu and other Hokkaido populations in the colonization of the island, discussed in Section 63.29, formed part of the wider setting of Hokkaido's industrial development.

Section 63.13

Copper Mining: Ashio, Besshi, and the Expansion of Metal Production

Copper was among Japan's chief exports in the nineteenth century and a key input for electrical and industrial development. Two mines dominated its production. Ashio, in Tochigi, had been worked since the early seventeenth century and had declined by the end of the Tokugawa period. Furukawa Ichibei, who took over operations in 1877, modernized the mine with new equipment, the discovery of rich ore bodies in the 1880s, and later the introduction of electric power and improved smelting. Within a few years Ashio produced a large share of Japan's copper, roughly a third or more of national output by the mid-1880s and the largest single source into the 1890s. Furukawa's firm also benefited from political connections; Mutsu Munemitsu, a prominent minister, saw his son adopted into the Furukawa family, which strengthened ties between the enterprise and the government.

Besshi, in Ehime on Shikoku, had been operated by the Sumitomo family since 1691. Under Sumitomo's managers it modernized from the 1870s and 1880s, applying Western mining engineering and smelting methods and retaining the family's long investment in the mine. Sumitomo's later development of banking, metals, and other businesses grew out of this base. Besshi and Ashio thus illustrate two paths: a relatively new entrepreneur, Furukawa, rising on a rapidly expanded mine, and an old merchant house, Sumitomo, using an inherited enterprise as the foundation of an industrial group. Both firms linked mining with smelting and financing, and both exported copper through trading companies.

Both mines also faced environmental conflict, but their outcomes differed. At Besshi, smelting at Niihama produced sulfurous smoke that damaged crops and prompted protests from neighboring farmers in the 1890s. Sumitomo moved smelting operations to Shisakajima in the Seto Inland Sea, where the new plant began operating in January 1905. Contrary to expectations, winds carried sulfurous emissions back toward Shikoku, expanding damage in some areas; relocation shifted rather than solved the pollution problem. At Ashio, the environmental conflict was much more severe and politically explosive, as Sections 63.16 and 63.17 explain.

Section 63.14

Life and Labor Underground: Contractors, Mining Families, and Company Settlements

Mining employment was organized through intermediaries. In many coal fields, particularly in Kyushu and Hokkaido, the naya system placed labor in the hands of bosses, nayagashira, who housed miners in barracks or huts (naya), supplied food and goods on credit, and took a share of the miners' earnings. The company paid the boss according to the quantity of coal produced, and the boss paid the miners after deductions. This arrangement allowed companies to avoid the burden of recruiting and supervising workers, while giving the boss strong leverage over men who were in debt for their housing, food, and travel. In some Hokkaido mines, railway construction, and other remote sites, the harshest versions of this system, sometimes called tako-beya (octopus-pot rooms) labor, confined workers in barracks, enforced discipline by violence, and made escape difficult. Large companies in the 1900s and 1910s began to replace these arrangements with direct employment and company housing, though practices persisted in many places.

Metal miners at Ashio and other mountain mines also drew on an older institution, the tomoko, a fraternity of miners with oaths, ceremonies, and mutual aid, which regulated the training of apprentices, assisted sick or traveling members, and arranged funerals. Tomoko networks gave miners an organizational tradition that could support collective action, a point emphasized by historians of the 1907 Ashio disturbances. Family labor was characteristic of Kyushu coal mining, where a man and a woman often worked as a team underground, the man cutting coal and the woman hauling it. Women's mine labor was widespread, and the legal prohibition of women's underground work came only in the late 1920s and 1930s. The later paintings and records of Yamamoto Sakubei, a Chikuhō miner who began work as a child and recorded mining life in drawings made decades afterward, preserve a detailed memory of this world, though as a retrospective source it must be read as such.

Company settlements concentrated miners in housing owned by the firm, with company stores, schools, and sometimes hospitals. These arrangements offered certain benefits but also gave the company power over every part of life. Workers depended on the company for employment, housing, credit, and medical care, and loss of the job meant loss of home. Efforts to change coercive employment practices came from several sources: journalists exposing abuses, reformers, state officials, and workers themselves. A well-known 1888 exposé of the Takashima mine by Matsuoka Kōichi, published in the magazine Nihonjin, described conditions there in harsh terms and provoked public debate, leading to some official and company responses, though it did not end the system.

Historical Vocabulary
Naya System and Tomoko

The naya system was a form of labor contracting in mining, especially in coal, in which a labor boss recruited and housed miners and supplied them on credit, receiving payment from the mine and distributing wages after deductions. Debt and dependency were built into the arrangement. The tomoko was a traditional fraternity of metal miners that provided mutual aid, ritual structure, and regulation of training.

The two institutions point in different directions. The naya system tended to bind workers to a boss and to employers; the tomoko offered workers a source of solidarity independent of management. Both mattered in explaining how mining labor was organized and why labor conflicts at the mines took the forms they did. Neither should be mistaken for a modern company personnel system or a modern trade union.

Section 63.15

Mine Accidents, Disease, Workplace Safety, and Industrial Health

Mining was dangerous. Roof collapses, flooding, fires, and gas and coal-dust explosions killed workers in large numbers, and everyday hazards such as rock falls, haulage accidents, and heat caused many injuries. The gas and coal-dust explosion at the Mitsubishi Hōjō coal mine in northern Kyushu on December 15, 1914 killed hundreds of miners, among the deadliest disasters in Japanese mining history. Published totals differ: local commemorative accounts report 671 deaths, while other mining histories give 687. Ventilation and gas-detection technologies were limited, and companies' incentives to maintain output often outweighed investment in safety. Dust diseases of the lungs, parasitic infections such as hookworm, heat illness, and chronic injuries affected miners' health over longer periods. Medical services were provided, when at all, by company doctors and hospitals, whose independence from management was limited.

Reliable statistics are hard to reconstruct. Companies and officials recorded deaths and injuries unevenly. Long-term diseases were often not attributed to work, and workers who left sick, like women returning from factories, disappeared from employment records. Contractors might not report accidents involving their own workers, and the families of the dead received little compensation. Until the early twentieth century, the state offered few legal guarantees of relief. The Mining Act of 1905 and later regulations, especially measures adopted around 1916, began to require some assistance for injured and bereaved mining families, but protection remained limited and mining was outside the scope of the Factory Act.

For historians, mining accidents are important evidence of the human cost of industrial growth and of the political choices behind it. They also show why mining conflicts differed from factory conflicts: danger, family participation, and company control of daily life made grievances over safety, compensation, and the treatment of injured workers central to miners' demands. Students should be cautious with numbers, which vary by source and are often incomplete, and should avoid treating a single disaster as typical while recognizing that accidents were an ongoing feature of mining life.

Section 63.16

The Ashio Copper Pollution Crisis and the Watarase River Basin

As Ashio's output grew in the 1880s, its effects spread downstream. Smelting released sulfur dioxide and other emissions that damaged the surrounding mountain forests; the cutting of timber for mine supports and fuel, together with this smoke damage, stripped the hills of vegetation, and the bare slopes accelerated erosion and flooding. Mine waste, including tailings and copper-bearing wastewater, entered the Watarase River, a tributary system of the Tone basin, killing fish and contaminating irrigation water. Farmers along the river in Tochigi and neighboring prefectures such as Gunma, Ibaraki, and Saitama reported damage to rice and other crops, to their fisheries, and to their health. Floods, especially the major flood of 1890 and later disasters, deposited contaminated silt across farmland, intensifying the damage. The pollution was neither imagined nor hidden; it was visible, widely reported, and contested.

The company and its supporters disputed the cause and extent of the damage and argued that the mine was essential to the national economy and the export trade. Government officials were divided, and the state's own interest in copper exports, along with the political connections of the mine's owners, shaped its response. Local farmers petitioned authorities, formed associations, and demanded the suspension of mining or the installation of pollution-control measures. In the 1890s the company made payments to some farmers in private settlements that often required recipients to forgo further claims, an arrangement critics saw as a way to buy silence. In 1897, after the farmers' large petition marches to Tokyo (oshidashi) and after official investigation, the government issued a pollution-prevention order requiring the mine to install settling ponds, filters, and smoke-treatment equipment within a set period. The measures were incomplete and their effectiveness was contested, and the damage continued.

Farmers' marches continued, and the confrontation escalated. In February 1900, a march of villagers toward Tokyo was stopped by police at Kawamata, on the Tone River, in a violent clash in which many protesters were injured and arrested. The authorities prosecuted the leaders for incitement and unlawful assembly. The Kawamata Incident, as it came to be known, showed how the state would treat peasant protest that threatened public order, even as it recognized, through official commissions, that the pollution was real. Later investigative commissions, including one in 1902, shifted the government's emphasis toward flood control and water management rather than suspension of the mine.

Cause and Effect

The Ashio Pollution Crisis

Long-term causes

Rising demand for copper in world and domestic markets; state support for export industries; weak legal protection for farmers' property against industrial harm; the mine's political and financial connections; and a low-cost, high-output approach to mining and smelting.

Short-term causes

The discovery of rich ore in the 1880s and rapid expansion of production; large-scale smelting and timber cutting; floods in the early 1890s and later; and the failure of early measures to treat emissions and wastewater.

Immediate effects

Damaged fisheries and crops, contaminated farmland, farmers' petitions and marches, Diet interpellations by Tanaka Shōzō, official commissions, and a government order in 1897 requiring pollution-prevention equipment.

Long-term consequences

A national debate about the limits of private enterprise and the state's responsibilities; the displacement of Yanaka Village; an enduring example in Japanese environmental memory; and a model of how local communities could appeal to the public and the Diet.

Historical complexity: The pollution cannot be explained by corporate greed alone, nor by state indifference alone. It arose from a combination of commercial incentives, technological limits, political connections, a legal order that favored enterprise, and a state that treated copper exports as vital. Equally, the response was not simply suppression: the government commissioned investigations and ordered measures, but within limits that preserved production.

Section 63.17

Tanaka Shōzō, Farmers' Petitions, and the Struggle over Yanaka Village

Tanaka Shōzō, born in 1841 into a village-headman family in Tochigi, had served in local administration and in the People's Rights movement before his election to the first Imperial Diet in 1890. In December 1891 he introduced questions in the Diet about the damage caused by the Ashio mine, demanding that the government enforce the mining laws and protect the people's rights. Over the following decade he returned to the issue repeatedly, using parliamentary questions, speeches, and publicity to press the government. He argued that a state that allowed rivers and villages to be destroyed for private profit was violating its duty to protect the people and the constitutional order. His campaign drew on the farmers' own mobilization, and he also made the case to newspapers and to reformers and students, so that Ashio became a national cause.

Frustrated by the government's failure to act decisively, Tanaka resigned his Diet seat in October 1901. On December 10, 1901, as the emperor's carriage returned from the opening of the Diet session, Tanaka rushed toward it to hand over a petition, a direct appeal (jikiso) that had been drafted with the help of the journalist Kōtoku Shūsui. Police seized him; he was detained briefly and released. The act was widely reported and created a public sensation, even though it did not change policy. It dramatized a point about the political system: ordinary farmers could not reach the government through ordinary channels, and a former legislator was reduced to a symbolic gesture toward the throne.

The government's eventual response was to reshape the river system. After investigation, officials decided to build a flood-control basin on low-lying land in Tochigi, a project that required the removal of Yanaka Village. Residents who had suffered from the pollution and floods now faced the loss of their homes and land, and many resisted compensation offers and relocation. Tanaka moved to Yanaka in 1904 and lived among the villagers. The village was merged administratively into a neighboring town in 1906, and in 1907 authorities forcibly demolished the houses of residents who still refused to leave. The affair showed how the burden of both pollution and its remedy fell on the same community. Tanaka died in 1913, continuing to campaign. The reservoir was built in the following years, and the site was later recognized for its wetlands, a reminder that the landscape carried layers of meaning for subsequent generations.

Section 63.18

The Ashio and Besshi Labor Disturbances of 1907

In February 1907, miners at Ashio rose in a multi-day disturbance that became one of the largest mining riots of the period. The grievances were rooted in the internal organization of the mine: wages and piece rates that had not kept pace with rising prices after the Russo-Japanese War, deductions, and the power of supervisors and contractors who controlled hiring, housing, and pay. Organizing by Nagaoka Tsuruzō and Minami Sukematsu, including the Ashio branch of the Dai-Nippon Rōdō Shiseikai (Great Japan Labor Sincerity Society), established in 1906 after earlier miners' organizing, built on existing tomoko networks. The riot began on February 4 in a confrontation between miners and worksite supervisors; historians have also examined possible provocation by labor contractors. The violence spread into attacks on company buildings and confrontations with company staff and police. The authorities sent troops to restore order. Many participants were arrested and tried, and the company reorganized its labor management in the aftermath, moving toward more direct control of employment.

In June 1907, a separate riot occurred at the Besshi mine of Sumitomo in Ehime, where miners attacked company offices and facilities over wages and treatment. Troops were again called out. These events occurred during a financial downturn that followed the postwar boom, in a year when official statistics recorded a notable number of labor disputes. The government and employers responded with arrests and prosecutions, and they interpreted the disturbances partly as a threat to public order and partly as evidence that mine labor management needed reform. They did not recognize the miners' organizations as legitimate bargaining parties.

It is essential to distinguish the 1907 miners' disturbances from the earlier agricultural anti-pollution protest, even though both concerned Ashio. The farmers' movement involved landholding households and a campaign for compensation, remedy, and legal protection, mobilized through petitions and marches and championed by a politician. The 1907 disturbance involved wage workers in a dispute with their employer over pay, authority, and conditions inside the mine. Their participants, grievances, organization, and targets differed. At the same time, both occurred in the same industrial enterprise, both confronted the company's power and the state's reliance on its output, and both ended in suppression by the police or army. Historians continue to debate how closely they should be connected.

Historical Debate

Were the Ashio Pollution Protest and the Miners' Riot Expressions of One Social Conflict or Distinct Struggles?

The question: Ashio is remembered both as an environmental scandal and as the site of a major labor riot. Should historians treat these as parts of a single story about industrial power and popular resistance, or as separate conflicts that happened to occur at the same mine?

Interpretation A — Distinct struggles: Scholars who emphasize the internal history of mining labor argue that the 1907 disturbance grew out of the mine's labor system, including piece rates, contractors, tomoko traditions, and postwar inflation. Nimura Kazuo's study of the Ashio riot is a leading example of work that explains the event primarily through the structure of mining work and the miners' own organizations rather than as a continuation of the farmers' campaign. On this view, treating the two as one obscures the miners' specific grievances and agency.
Interpretation B — Linked by a common structure of power: Others stress that both conflicts exposed the same configuration of corporate power, official indulgence, and a legal order that favored enterprise. Public attention to Ashio in the 1890s shaped how journalists, socialists, and reformers viewed the mine, and the same state that suppressed the farmers in 1900 sent troops in 1907. On this view, Ashio illustrates a broader pattern of industrial conflict in which different groups confronted the costs of growth.

Evidence: Both views draw on company and police records, trial documents, newspaper reports, Diet debates, and the later testimony of participants. They differ in emphasis: the first on the mine's internal documents and labor practices, the second on the political and public context. Many historians combine the approaches, treating the conflicts as distinct in participants and aims but connected by setting and by state response.

Why it matters: The answer affects how Ashio is taught. If the conflicts are treated as one, Ashio becomes an early example of environmental and labor protest together; if separate, the history of Japanese labor movements and of environmental movements can be seen as having distinct origins with points of contact.

Section 63.19 · Division IV — Early Labor Organization, Strikes, and Socialism

Skilled Workers, Railways, Shipyards, and the Beginnings of Collective Organization

The first durable forms of labor organization arose among skilled men whose bargaining positions were strong. Printers, ironworkers, machinists, railroad employees, shipbuilders, and artisans in urban trades worked in shops where skills were scarce, employers depended on them, and workplace networks already existed. They had a tradition of mutual aid, fraternal gatherings, and occasional collective action rooted in the craft and apprenticeship systems. In shipyards and railway shops, workers with technical training and relatively high wages could move among employers, and they had reasons to defend their status against both employers and unskilled competition.

These workers also faced new pressures. Companies sought to reduce the authority of the oyakata and to make workers directly dependent on the firm, to introduce rules and fines, and to lower wages in downturns. Skilled workers responded with mutual-aid societies, lobbying, and, occasionally, strikes. The nationalization of major railways under the Railway Nationalization Law of 1906, which brought most trunk lines under state ownership, shifted railway workers' relationships to a government employer and added questions about their status as state employees. Craft solidarity among skilled men did not necessarily extend to unskilled workers or to women, and some associations were exclusive.

The organizational tradition of skilled workers provided the base on which the first modern union efforts were built. The leaders who sought to create unions in the 1890s found their most receptive audience among machinists, ironworkers, and railway engineers, and not among the much larger populations of textile women or miners, who were harder to organize for the reasons set out in earlier sections.

Section 63.20

Takano Fusatarō, Katayama Sen, and the Labor Union Preparatory Association of 1897

Takano Fusatarō, born in Nagasaki in 1868, spent years abroad in the United States, where he encountered American labor organizations, and returned to Japan in the mid-1890s convinced that Japanese workers needed unions of the American craft-union type. In July 1897, with Katayama Sen, Jō Tsunetarō, Sawada Hanshirō, and others, he founded the Rōdō Kumiai Kiseikai (Association for the Formation of Labor Unions) in Tokyo. Katayama, who had studied in the United States and in 1897 opened Kingsley Hall, a settlement house in Tokyo, brought Christian social-reform networks and connections to American organizations. The group launched the newspaper Rōdō Sekai (Labor World), which promoted unionism, mutual aid, and education.

The Kiseikai's most successful offshoot was the Ironworkers' Union (Tekkō Kumiai), established in December 1897, which recruited machinists and ironworkers in Tokyo and nearby districts and eventually claimed membership in the thousands. Its program emphasized mutual benefit, such as sick and death payments, along with improvement of wages and conditions and the dignity of the trade. Other unions followed in related trades, and the association helped to publicize unionism. The movement was moderate in tone, avoiding revolutionary rhetoric and stressing practical benefits and cooperation. Even so, the organization struggled with finances, employer opposition, high turnover, and the difficulty of keeping members. By 1899–1900 the Kiseikai had lost momentum, and Takano withdrew from leadership; he died in China in 1904.

The scale and fate of these organizations should not be overstated. They were small compared with the whole industrial workforce, concentrated in particular skilled trades, and short-lived. Their significance lies in establishing the idea of a permanent union organization, in training a first generation of organizers, and in showing the problems later unions would face. Stephen Marsland's study of Takano and the Kiseikai provides the fullest account of the institutions and their American inspirations.

Section 63.21

The Railway Strike of 1898 and the Practical Meaning of Collective Bargaining

In February 1898, locomotive engineers and firemen of the privately owned Nippon Railway Company, which ran lines in eastern and northern Japan, organized a campaign over pay and treatment. The engineers were skilled, their position in the railway system gave them leverage, and their network circulated demands before a formal union existed. Their grievances included low wages relative to their responsibility and the poor status they held compared with other railway employees. Approximately 400 workers stopped work beginning February 24, and the brief strike disrupted train service. The company then entered negotiations, but the settlement process continued into early April.

The strike illustrates what workers could achieve and what they could not. A short, disciplined work stoppage by indispensable skilled workers could win concessions. By April 5, the workers had largely secured improvements in pay and occupational status, and they established the Nippon Railway Reform Society (Nittetsu Kyōseikai) following the settlement. This was a genuine union-building result, although subsequent employer and government pressure weakened the organization. The distinction between a successful dispute and a durable union is central. The 1898 strike showed that collective action could secure concessions and create a union, but it did not guarantee lasting legal protection or sufficient financial resources.

The railway workers continued to organize mutual-aid and association movements through the 1900s, and the nationalization of railways in 1906–1907 changed the context by making most railway workers state employees. Subsequent rail disputes would belong to a different legal and political setting. The 1898 strike remains an important early example of skilled workers using their position to bargain, a model that later unions would seek to generalize.

Section 63.22

Socialism, Christianity, Labor Newspapers, and the Politics of Industrial Reform

Japanese socialism and labor reform grew from several roots. Christian social reformers, many educated in or influenced by American Protestantism, took up poverty, labor conditions, and public morality. Abe Isoo, Katayama Sen, Kinoshita Naoe, and others combined religious conviction with social criticism. The Social Democratic Party (Shakai Minshutō), founded in May 1901 by Abe, Katayama, Kinoshita, Kōtoku Shūsui, Nishikawa Kōjirō, and Kawakami Kiyoshi, was the first socialist party in Japan, with a platform that included labor protections, universal suffrage, and arms reduction, but it was banned by the Home Ministry within hours of its formation. The episode showed the limits of tolerance for organized socialism under the constitutional order.

The movement's newspapers carried its ideas. In 1903 Kōtoku Shūsui and Sakai Toshihiko, who had left a mainstream newspaper over its stance on war with Russia, founded the weekly Heimin Shimbun (Commoners' Newspaper), which combined socialist analysis with antimilitarism and criticism of the government. In late 1904 it published a Japanese translation of a portion of The Communist Manifesto, and the authorities moved against it. The paper closed in early 1905. Socialists disagreed among themselves. Some, like Katayama, favored parliamentary methods and labor organization; others, including Kōtoku after his time abroad, turned toward direct action; and Christian reformers often differed over violence and revolution. After the Russo-Japanese War, the Japan Socialist Party was permitted briefly in 1906 and banned in early 1907, and the state's repression escalated, culminating in the High Treason Incident of 1910–1911, in which Kōtoku and eleven others were executed.

For labor history, the significance of the socialists lies in their role as publicists, organizers, and critics of the industrial order, as well as in the state's response. They supplied ideas, journals, and a vocabulary for labor conflict. But they were a small group of intellectuals with limited connections to workers' daily lives, and their divisions and repression weakened their influence. By the end of the 1900s, the police had created a special political police (the Special Higher Police, Tokkō) in 1911 to monitor radical movements. This helped set the conditions in which a more moderate organization, the Yūaikai, could emerge in 1912.

Section 63.23

Industrial Conflict in the Years after the Russo-Japanese War

The years after 1905 brought new tensions. The war had increased taxes and prices, and its victory raised expectations that were not matched in workers' wages. Heavy industry expanded, and the number of workers in shipyards, arsenals, steelworks, and machinery factories grew. After a brief boom, the economy turned down in 1907, and employers cut wages or staff. The disturbances at Ashio and Besshi in that year, together with disputes in other mines, shipyards, and factories, made the period one of notable unrest. Disputes arose over wages, discipline, supervision, and the actions of managers.

Not all unrest took the same form. Organized strikes, prepared by workers' groups and presented with demands, differed from spontaneous riots, in which workers attacked company property after an accumulation of grievances or a particular incident. The Ashio and Besshi events were closer to riots, although Ashio had an organizational background. In other cases, petitions, work stoppages, and negotiations went on without violence. The police monitored and intervened in many disputes, and the Public Peace Police Law's restrictions on "incitement" could be used to prosecute organizers. Employers often responded by dismissing leaders and offering limited concessions.

By the early 1910s, many large employers recognized that rigid repression alone did not produce stability, and they combined it with welfare programs and efforts to cultivate worker loyalty (Section 63.28). Officials, reformers, and some businessmen began to speak of "social policy" (shakai seisaku) as a way to avoid class conflict, influenced by German models, and the Association for Social Policy founded in 1896 provided a forum for scholars and bureaucrats. This mixture of repression and conciliation shaped the environment in which the Yūaikai appeared.

Section 63.24

Suzuki Bunji, the Yūaikai, and the Reorganization of Labor Activism after 1912

On August 1, 1912, Suzuki Bunji, a young Tokyo Imperial University graduate and a Christian associated with the Unitarian movement, and about fifteen colleagues founded the Yūaikai (Friendly Society) in Tokyo. The society's early goals were moderate: mutual aid, moral and educational improvement of workers, and cooperation between labor and capital, without direct challenge to employers or the state. Its leaders cultivated support from sympathetic businessmen, including Shibusawa Eiichi, and from officials, and it presented itself as a means to defuse conflict. The organization published a journal, offered insurance and education, and recruited members among workers in Tokyo and other cities, especially skilled men in machinery, shipbuilding, and printing.

Over the following years the Yūaikai grew, adding branches and members, a women's section, and a more activist stance as workers brought it grievances. Suzuki's travel to the United States in 1915, where he met leaders of American labor, strengthened his commitment to trade unionism. By the later 1910s, as wartime inflation and industrial expansion changed the labor market, the Yūaikai began to function more like a union federation, negotiating on behalf of members and supporting disputes. In 1919 it was renamed the Greater Japan Federation of Labor, Yūaikai, and it became the main national labor organization of the early Taishō period. These developments belong to Chapter 69.

The Yūaikai was a bridge between the 1890s and the 1920s. It took from the Kiseikai the idea of a permanent union with mutual aid and organizers; it took from the post-1900 experience the need to avoid appearing revolutionary; and it benefited from a labor market and political climate more open to conciliation than in the 1890s. It also reflected the continued influence of Christian and reform networks and the careful positioning of its leaders within the constraints of the Public Peace Police Law.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Unions of the 1890s to the Yūaikai

What Changed

  • The Kiseikai and Ironworkers' Union were craft-oriented and short-lived; the Yūaikai built a broader membership base and lasting organizational structure
  • The legal environment hardened with the Public Peace Police Law of 1900, which pushed organizers toward cautious, conciliatory language
  • Organizers moved from American craft-union models toward a blend of mutual aid, moral reform, and cooperation with sympathetic elites
  • Heavy industry, state enterprises, and larger firms enlarged the base of workers for whom organization was possible

What Stayed the Same

  • Skilled male workers in cities supplied the core membership, while textile women and miners remained mostly outside formal unions
  • Christian and reform-minded intellectuals remained prominent leaders
  • Unions had no secure legal recognition and operated at the sufferance of police and employers
  • Mutual aid remained a central attraction and a source of stability

Who experienced change most directly? Skilled male workers in metalworking, shipbuilding, and printing saw the most new organizational opportunities, while women in textiles and miners saw little formal change before 1916. Why the pattern matters: It shows that labor organization developed unevenly and through adaptation to a restrictive political environment, not as a straightforward ascent toward the mass unions of the 1920s.

Section 63.25 · Division V — State Repression, Factory Legislation, and Employer Control

The Public Peace Police Law of 1900 and the Restriction of Labor Organization

The Public Peace Police Law (Chian Keisatsu Hō) was enacted in March 1900 under the second Yamagata Aritomo cabinet, replacing the earlier Law on Assembly and Political Association of 1890. It required political associations to register with the police, authorized the police to prohibit or disperse meetings, and barred certain categories of people, including soldiers, police, teachers, and students, from joining political associations. It also excluded women and minors from political associations and from attending political meetings, restrictions that affected women's participation in public life (Chapter 64). Officials presented the law as a means of preserving public order amid the rise of socialism and labor activism, and the Diet passed it with little resistance.

The provision most important for labor was Article 17. It did not forbid unions or strikes by name, but it criminalized the use of violence, threats, or "incitement" (sendō) and "seduction" (yūwaku) to induce workers to join or leave an association for the purpose of collective bargaining, to stop work together, or to press employers to accept demands. Because the language was broad and the words incitement and seduction were open to interpretation, police and prosecutors could treat organizing itself as an offense. Union leaders became vulnerable to arrest for ordinary recruitment and strike preparation. The article therefore did not need to ban unions to constrain them; it made their central activities legally risky. Article 17 remained in force until 1926, and the law as a whole until 1945.

The law reveals the structure of the relationship between state and employers. The police were not neutral arbiters but guardians of public order whose definition of order favored established enterprise. At the same time, the state was not simply the employers' instrument: officials pursued social policy and factory legislation (Sections 63.26–63.27) to reduce conflict and to protect labor power, and sometimes disagreed with employers. The result was a combination of control and conciliation that characterized official labor policy through the Taishō period. Sheldon Garon's work on the state and labor in modern Japan emphasizes this dual approach.

Section 63.26

Government Factory Investigations and the Recognition of the Labor Question

By the late 1890s, bureaucrats, journalists, and scholars were treating the "labor question" (rōdō mondai) as a national problem. Yokoyama Gennosuke's 1899 account of the poor, including factory workers, had reached a broad readership. The Association for Social Policy, formed in 1896 by economists influenced by German social reform, argued for state intervention to protect workers and avoid class conflict. Officials at the Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce, who regarded the labor question as an administrative challenge, undertook systematic investigations. The resulting Shokkō Jijō (1903), described in Section 63.9, provided evidence about hours, wages, health, and housing, and about the number of women and children employed.

Debate over what to do divided opinion. Reformers and some officials argued that protective legislation was needed for humanitarian reasons and to safeguard the health of the nation's future workers and soldiers. Many employers, particularly in textiles, opposed regulation as a threat to their competitiveness and to the system of recruitment and night work on which their profits depended. They invoked the value of "warm" relations between employers and employees and argued that Japan's industries, still developing, could not bear Western-style regulation. The government hesitated, drafting bills and withdrawing them as opposition arose.

The investigations thus served a double purpose. They gave officials the knowledge needed for policy, and they signaled that the state considered conditions in factories a legitimate subject of inquiry and, potentially, of regulation. They also created a documentary record on which later historians depend. But recognition of the labor question did not equal action. Years passed between the survey and legislation, and the political balance among officials, employers, and reformers determined the shape of the law that finally emerged.

Section 63.27

The Factory Act of 1911 and Its Implementation in 1916

The Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce prepared factory legislation from the 1890s, with officials such as Oka Minoru playing a leading role. Drafts circulated, were revised, and were opposed by employers' organizations, notably the cotton spinners, who argued that restrictions on night work and on the employment of young workers would harm exports. The bill was passed by the Diet and promulgated in March 1911, but it was not to take effect until September 1, 1916, a delay that gave industry time to adjust and reflected the strength of employers' objections.

The Act's main provisions covered factories with fifteen or more workers, or those in dangerous or unhealthy trades. It set a minimum age, generally twelve, with transitional exceptions allowing children aged ten or older who were already employed to continue to work. It limited the working day of women and of children under fifteen to twelve hours, restricted night work for them, required rest days and breaks, and obliged employers to provide relief to workers injured, ill, or killed through work, with details left to imperial ordinance. But the law allowed exemptions and transitional rules that left important gaps: certain industries, notably silk reeling, were allowed longer hours, and a transitional period permitted night work in two-shift factories for many years. Small workshops, mines, and household production fell outside its scope. Enforcement depended on a small staff of factory inspectors, and the law did not guarantee workers the right to organize or bargain. Further revision came in 1923, with new rules taking effect in 1926 and later, a subject for Chapter 69.

The Act therefore marked a genuine break, in that the state recognized a responsibility for workers' protection in law, and a limited one, in that it left most workers outside and softened its own rules. Its protective provisions focused on women and children, reflecting both humanitarian concern and assumptions about women's frailty and role in reproduction, assumptions that could justify restrictions and also reinforce a view of women as dependents. Historians therefore read the Act as both a gain for the workers it covered and an expression of the gendered and paternalistic assumptions of the officials who drafted it.

Section 63.28

Corporate Paternalism, Welfare, Surveillance, and Labor Discipline

Many Japanese employers, especially in textiles and large industrial firms, developed welfare programs: dormitories with dining halls and bathhouses, company schools and training courses, hospitals and clinics, mutual-aid societies, savings plans, recreation facilities, and festivals. The language of paternalism (onjō-shugi, a policy of warm benevolence) and of the firm as a family presented the employer as a protector and the worker as a loyal member. Figures such as Shibusawa Eiichi promoted these ideas, and managers like Mutō Sanji at Kanegafuchi Spinning built welfare systems that became models. Welfare was real: dormitory meals, clinics, and schooling provided benefits that workers often valued and that were scarce outside the firm.

Welfare also served control. Providing housing, medical care, and schooling tied workers to the company and gave managers insight into their lives. Supervisors enforced rules governing conduct, thrift, and morality; company dormitories restricted visitors and movement; and loyal workers were rewarded with promotion, bonuses, or long-service recognition. For women, the language of protection and moral guidance reflected assumptions about female frailty and the need for guardianship, as Faison's research has shown. Welfare was also a response to competition for labor and to the fear of strikes: firms that could offer better conditions retained workers and reduced turnover. Scholars such as Hazama Hiroshi have traced the roots of later Japanese corporate management in these early paternalist practices, while critics warn against reading later "Japanese-style management" back into the Meiji period.

Thus welfare and discipline coexisted. A worker could benefit from the clinic and still resent the surveillance; could value the school and still be unable to leave; could appreciate a paternal manager and still lack a voice in decisions. The state, for its part, encouraged paternalism as a means of social peace, even as the Factory Act imposed limited obligations. Understanding this combination requires avoiding two simple readings: that welfare was only a mask for exploitation, and that it demonstrates employers' benevolence.

Section 63.29 · Division VI — Social Consequences and Historical Synthesis

Industrial Communities, Class Identities, and the Relationship between Town and Countryside

By the 1910s, industrial work had created recognizable communities: mill dormitories and company towns, mining settlements, working-class neighborhoods around shipyards and arsenals, and urban districts of small workshops. Family economies connected these places to the countryside through remittances, return migration, and marriage. Class identities formed unevenly. Skilled male workers in cities increasingly identified as "workers" (rōdōsha), a term that gained currency in the 1890s and 1900s, while women in textile mills often saw themselves in terms of family and village ties. Miners identified with mining communities and fraternities. Regional differences remained strong. Gender relations inside industrial communities were unequal: men held supervisory positions and higher wages, and women's labor was valued less, even as their earnings sustained families.

Marginalized communities experienced industrial society in distinct ways, though evidence permits only partial treatment. Descendants of the former outcaste groups, legally freed in 1871, remained concentrated in leather work, butchery, and some other trades, and many faced discrimination in hiring and housing that limited access to factory and mine employment; some Burakumin entrepreneurs prospered in leather and footwear production, which supplied military needs, but discrimination persisted. In Hokkaido, colonization and development displaced Ainu from fishing grounds, hunting territories, and land, and many were pushed into low-paid wage labor in fisheries, forestry, and survey and construction work, while the colonial development of mines and railways relied mainly on migrant settlers and convicts. Ryukyuan communities, annexed as Okinawa Prefecture in 1879, saw the beginning of labor emigration and later migration to the mainland. Korean workers in the Japanese metropole were few before the annexation of 1910 and became more numerous afterward, a story told in later chapters. The government also restricted foreign laborers from living and working outside the former foreign settlements under an 1899 ordinance, limiting the presence of Chinese workers in particular.

The relation between town and countryside remained central. Industry depended on rural labor and rural savings, and rural communities depended on remittances; the movement between them shaped households and politics. Landlords, tenants, and factory owners overlapped in local elites. The tensions of the period, including tenant disputes, mining riots, and farmers' protests, belonged to a single, uneven transition, not to separate worlds of "agrarian" and "industrial" Japan.

Section 63.30

Labor Conflict and the Political Meaning of Meiji Industrialization

The first generation of industrial disputes revealed several features of Meiji capitalism and the constitutional state. They showed that industrial growth depended on cheap, flexible labor, often drawn from rural households and managed through contracts, dormitories, and contractors. They revealed the close link between state and enterprise, visible in the response to Ashio, in the use of troops in 1907, and in the police regulation of labor organizing. They exposed the limits of representation: workers and most farmers had no vote, and petitions, newspapers, and social movements were their main means of political expression. And they led to the emergence of reform as well as repression, in the form of investigation, factory legislation, and company welfare.

Workers should not be regarded merely as by-products of Japan's rise to great-power status. They were participants in, and increasingly critics of, the economic and political order that emerged. Their actions pressed the state to acknowledge the "labor question," and their organizations, however fragile, provided precedents. The scholarship of Andrew Gordon on labor and "imperial democracy" has argued that workers in this era drew on ideas of citizenship, rights, and the nation to make claims within the existing order, rather than rejecting it. By 1916 the pace was quickening. Wartime industrial expansion would swell the workforce, raise prices, and transform labor relations, leading to the Rice Riots of 1918 (Chapter 68) and the more extensive labor politics of the 1920s (Chapter 69).

Historical Debate

Did Industrial Growth Expand Workers' Opportunities Faster than Their Political and Economic Protections?

The question: Industrialization offered new jobs, wages, skills, and mobility, but workers lacked legal rights, political representation, and safety guarantees. How should historians weigh these gains against the costs?

Interpretation A — Opportunities were real and often undervalued: Some scholars, especially those studying the labor market and women's employment, emphasize that wage work gave workers, particularly young women, incomes, skills, and choices that village life did not provide, and that mobility and the competition among employers gave workers some leverage. On this view, portraying workers only as victims misrepresents their agency and the economic logic of their decisions.
Interpretation B — Protections lagged far behind: Others emphasize that hours were long, hazards severe, and legal remedies minimal; that contracts and dormitories constrained mobility; that unions were restricted; and that the state delayed even modest protection until 1916. On this view, whatever gains workers achieved came despite, and not because of, the institutions that governed industrial work.

Evidence: Both positions draw on wage and price series, factory investigations, company records, memoirs, and statutes. The first stresses turnover and evidence of choice; the second stresses disease, accident, and legal constraint. Many historians hold that both statements are true for different workers at different times.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how the era is remembered: as a story of national achievement, of exploitation, or of contested, uneven change.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the industrial workers of Meiji and early Taishō Japan: who they were, how they worked, how they organized and protested, and how the state and employers responded. Before proceeding to Chapter 64 and the chapters on the First World War and the Taishō labor movement, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these foundational points.

The core insights are these. Industrial wage labor grew out of Tokugawa household and workshop production and coexisted with agriculture; workers came largely from farm households and often moved between farm and factory. Women were central to the early workforce, particularly in silk reeling and cotton spinning, where recruiters, contracts, dormitories, and long hours constrained them but did not eliminate their choices. Mining labor was organized differently, through contractors, debt, and company settlements, and carried severe hazards. Ashio showed how industrial growth could produce pollution, rural protest, and later miners' revolt, which should be kept distinct while recognizing their shared setting. Early unions arose among skilled men and were fragile; the Public Peace Police Law of 1900 criminalized the practices on which organizing depended; the Factory Act of 1911 was enacted but not enforced until 1916, and then with major exemptions. Welfare and paternalism offered real benefits and served as instruments of control. Evidence for all of this comes from sources with strong perspectives and notable silences, and honest history requires comparing them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Japan's industrial expansion between the 1870s and 1916 created a large wage-working population whose experience of growth was shaped by long hours, discipline, migration, insecure contracts, hazards, and unequal bargaining power, and that workers' responses made labor conflict a central political question. Industrial labor did not replace earlier work systems overnight. It grew out of Tokugawa household production, workshops, and mining traditions, coexisted with a still-agrarian majority, and remained tied to farm households through dekasegi, remittances, and return migration.

Industrial employment was regionally and occupationally diverse. Osaka's cotton mills, Nagano's silk filatures, the coalfields of Chikuhō and Hokkaido, the copper mines of Ashio and Besshi, and the shipyards, arsenals, and railway shops of port cities each had their own labor markets, workplace organization, and conflicts. Skilled men in metalworking and railways held the strongest bargaining positions; women in textiles and miners in isolated settlements held the weakest, though both groups found ways to bargain, evade, and resist.

Women were central to the early industrial workforce. Recruiters, advances, term contracts, forced savings, and dormitories connected rural households to factories and limited workers' ability to leave, while competition for labor, high turnover, and wage-grading gave workers some leverage. Conditions imposed fatigue, injury, and disease, notably tuberculosis, yet women also earned wages, learned skills, and formed networks of solidarity. Silk and cotton differed in scale, organization, and market exposure, and the historiography, from Tsurumi's emphasis on exploitation to Faison's attention to management and Hunter's attention to labor markets, reflects different facets of a complex reality.

Mining labor followed a different logic. Naya bosses, debt, company settlements, and family labor structured work in the coalfields; tomoko fraternities gave metal miners a tradition of mutual aid. Accidents and diseases imposed heavy costs, and the evidence for them is incomplete. Convict labor at Miike and in Hokkaido illustrated how coercion and industrial development were linked, and the colonization of Hokkaido affected Ainu communities in ways that largely excluded them from the new industrial economy.

Ashio revealed how industrialization could generate several kinds of conflict. Pollution damaged the Watarase basin and farmland across several prefectures; farmers petitioned and marched; Tanaka Shōzō carried their cause into the Diet and, in 1901, toward the emperor's carriage; the government ordered measures in 1897 but ultimately preserved production, suppressed the Kawamata march in 1900, and resolved the flood problem through a basin that cost Yanaka Village its existence. In 1907, miners at Ashio and Besshi rose in separate disturbances against their employers' labor systems and were suppressed with troops. Historians distinguish these struggles while recognizing their shared setting and the state's consistent defense of order.

Formal labor organization advanced unevenly. The Kiseikai and Ironworkers' Union of 1897, the railway strike of 1898, and socialist and Christian reform movements established ideas and leaders but did not create durable mass unions. The Public Peace Police Law of 1900, especially Article 17, made organizing and strike preparation legally dangerous without banning unions outright. The Yūaikai, founded in 1912 on a moderate basis of mutual aid and cooperation, became the bridge to the larger labor movement of the later 1910s and 1920s.

The state's response combined control and conciliation. It policed organizing and sent troops to mines, while the Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce investigated conditions, culminating in the 1903 Shokkō Jijō, and drafted legislation. The Factory Act of 1911, in force from 1916, was the first national protective statute, yet its thresholds, exemptions, and limited inspectorate left many workers uncovered, and its protections for women and children rested on gendered assumptions. Employers' welfare programs offered real benefits and served as tools of loyalty and control.

Industrial communities connected town and countryside and included groups whose experience differed: Burakumin facing discrimination in hiring, Ainu displaced in Hokkaido, early Ryukyuan migrants, and the few foreign workers restricted by law. Class identity formed unevenly across gender, region, and occupation.

The chapter's larger point is that workers were participants in and increasingly critics of the economic and political order that produced modern Japan, not merely its by-products. Their strikes, petitions, riots, and organizations forced the state to recognize a labor question. The evidence for their lives requires careful, critical reading, for it comes from sources with strong perspectives and notable silences.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has moved Japanese industrialization from the boardroom and the ministry to the workshop floor, the mine shaft, the dormitory, and the riverside village. Seen from there, the Meiji transformation was neither a simple success story nor a simple tale of exploitation. It was a process in which millions of people took on new kinds of work, gained and lost in ways that differed by gender, region, and occupation, and pressed claims on a state that was both their regulator and, often, their employer's ally.

The chapter also shows how the era's conflicts were tied to the wider history of the archipelago. Silk and copper earned the foreign exchange that financed imperial ambitions treated in the surrounding chapters; coal powered the ships and railways of an expanding empire; and Hokkaido's development and the position of its Ainu communities illustrate the colonial dimension of industrial growth within the islands. Ashio, in particular, shows how the costs of growth could fall on rural communities far from the centers of power, while the Factory Act and the Yūaikai show how the state and reformers sought to contain conflict.

What survived beyond 1916 was considerable: the habit of treating labor as a question of public order and social policy together; the combination of enterprise welfare and discipline; the fragility of independent unions; and the memory of Ashio as a touchstone for later environmental and labor movements. These developments carry forward into Chapters 66, 68, and 69, where wartime expansion, popular protest, and a far larger labor movement transform the setting. Understanding the first generation of industrial workers is the necessary foundation for those later transformations, and a reminder that the making of modern Japan was the work of many hands whose experience historians can recover only by reading many kinds of evidence together.
