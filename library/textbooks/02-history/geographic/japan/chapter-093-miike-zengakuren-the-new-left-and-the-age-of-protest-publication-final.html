Miike, Zengakuren, the New Left, and the Age of Protest

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Three dates frame this chapter. On January 25, 1960, Mitsui locked out the miners of Miike and the union began an indefinite strike. On January 18–19, 1969, thousands of riot police cleared student barricades at the University of Tokyo. In late February 1972, much of the country watched, live, a siege at a mountain lodge in Nagano. Each event belongs to a different movement, and each ended differently.

The first date belongs to organized labor. The Mitsui Miike coal mines of northern Kyushu were the site of one of the longest and most closely watched industrial disputes of the postwar era. A conflict that began as a dispute over dismissals became a contest over who would control the workplace in an economy that was turning from coal to oil. The strike lasted until November 1960, as the country was beginning to speak of "income doubling," and it ended in a settlement whose meaning historians still debate. The second date belongs to the students. By January 1969, a protest that began as a quarrel over medical training and university administration had closed one of the country's most prestigious universities, and the government's decision to cancel that year's entrance examinations made the confrontation a national event. The third date belongs to a small armed faction. The siege at Asama-Sansō exposed, in the same weeks, the killing of members of that faction by its own comrades, and it altered how many Japanese viewed revolutionary politics.

The central problem of the chapter is a paradox. Between roughly 1957 and 1972, Japan's economy grew at a rate that made it, by the end of the 1960s, one of the largest in the world. Its parliamentary system, established under the Occupation, functioned continuously, and living standards rose for most households. Yet the same years produced a coal strike lasting most of a year, student rebellions that shut down hundreds of campuses, a citizens' antiwar movement, a prolonged farmers' struggle against an airport, mass demonstrations over Okinawa and the security treaty, and, finally, political violence and terrorism. The chapter asks why growth and stability did not eliminate social conflict, and why the movements that expressed that conflict followed such different paths.

The answer begins with a refusal to treat "the protest era" as a single movement. Unions, student organizations, citizens' groups, farmers, Okinawan residents, and revolutionary factions had different grievances, different ideologies, and very different methods. Some operated within the institutions of postwar democracy: strikes under labor law, demonstrations, petitions, and elections. Others rejected those institutions. A small number used weapons against police, against rival factions, against their own members, and eventually against civilians. The chapter keeps these categories apart. Nonviolent civic activism, militant labor action, campus occupation, inter-factional fighting, and terrorism are not interchangeable terms, and the history of the last should not be allowed to swallow the history of the others. Neither should the history of the larger movements be used to minimize the violence that did occur.

The chapter belongs to Part XIII and rests on its neighbors. Chapter 91 explained the corporate institutions, employment practices, and growth that formed the economic setting, and Chapter 92 examined the parliamentary politics of the 1955 System and the 1960 security-treaty crisis. This chapter assumes both and concentrates on militant labor disputes, student organizations and university struggles, antiwar mobilization, regional resistance, and revolutionary factions. The movements for environmental justice, women's liberation, and minority rights that grew from the same years are treated in Chapter 94, the artistic currents in Chapter 95, and the economic transformation after 1973 in Chapter 96. Okinawan history, treated here in Section 93.16, was not an extension of mainland protest and should be read as a distinct political history that intersected with it.

The evidence for this period is abundant and unreliable in characteristic ways. It includes union newspapers and strike records, company statements, Central Labour Relations Commission documents, Ministry of Education and National Police Agency publications, court records, newspaper and television coverage, leaflets, wall posters, memoirs, oral histories, and the later reflections of participants. Each type shapes the story. Police and organizers gave different attendance figures for the same demonstration. Memoirs written decades later, by participants who had become scholars, novelists, or politicians, tend to organize memory around what the movement came to mean. Television made some events, notably the 1972 siege, unusually visible while leaving others, particularly in rural and regional settings, nearly invisible. Where this chapter gives a figure, readers should treat it as an estimate, and where it describes a contested event, it attributes the claim.

Three conclusions run through the chapter. First, economic success and political stability did not remove conflict: industrial restructuring, institutional hierarchy, Cold War alignment, and disputes over political representation continued to generate organized resistance. Second, the movements overlapped without merging, and their cooperation should not be mistaken for shared aims. Third, the decline of mass revolutionary politics after 1972 cannot be explained by police repression or by extremist violence alone; fragmentation, failed expectations, changing public opinion, and broader economic and political conditions all contributed. The aim is a historical explanation of the possibilities, limits, achievements, and tragedies of collective political action in postwar Japan, not a chronology of strikes and clashes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why serious industrial conflict persisted during the high-growth era, distinguishing militant unions from enterprise-based unionism.
  2. Trace the origins, course, and settlement of the 1959–1960 Mitsui Miike strike and assess competing interpretations of its significance.
  3. Distinguish Zengakuren, Zenkyōtō, and the New Left factions as organizations with different structures and purposes.
  4. Analyze the ideological and organizational break between the Japan Communist Party and the New Left, without treating Marxism, Leninism, Trotskyism, and Maoism as interchangeable.
  5. Explain how university expansion, institutional grievances, and corporate-employment expectations contributed to campus protest.
  6. Reconstruct the Nihon University and University of Tokyo struggles, including the Yasuda Auditorium confrontation, and the government's response.
  7. Compare Beheiren, the Sanrizuka farmers' campaign, and Okinawan opposition to the bases as forms of mobilization with different social bases and aims.
  8. Distinguish the Red Army Faction, the Yodogō group, the United Red Army, and the Japanese Red Army, and explain how armed violence developed.
  9. Evaluate why the movements of 1970 and after did not reproduce the impact of the 1960 Anpo Crisis and why mass revolutionary mobilization declined.
  10. Assess primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits, and keep nonviolent mobilization distinct from terrorism when judging the era's legacy.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the high-growth economy and the postwar parliamentary system coexist with militant strikes, campus rebellions, and revolutionary politics?
  2. What was at stake at Miike beyond the dismissals themselves, and why do historians disagree about its outcome?
  3. Why did the student movement divide from the Japan Communist Party, and how did that division shape its later forms?
  4. To what extent did campus protest arise from practical institutional grievances, and to what extent from revolutionary ideology?
  5. How did Beheiren's decentralized civic model differ from party-led and factional politics, and what did it achieve?
  6. How did Sanrizuka and Okinawa show regional communities pursuing their own aims in relation to, and apart from, national movements?
  7. How did factional competition, repression, and failed expectations contribute to political violence, and why was that outcome not inevitable?
  8. What changed in Japanese political life because of the age of protest, and what historical judgments are most defensible about its legacy?

Historical Context

The legal framework for postwar protest was created during the Occupation. The Trade Union Law of 1945 and subsequent labor legislation gave workers rights that prewar law had denied, and union membership expanded explosively in the late 1940s. Occupation authorities also set limits: in January 1947, General MacArthur prohibited a planned general strike, and during the Reverse Course (Chapter 89) the Red Purge removed communists from many workplaces. The postwar labor movement was therefore born with both legal protection and a history of intervention. By the 1950s it was divided among rival national federations, with the General Council of Trade Unions of Japan (Sōhyō) the largest and most politically oriented, supported by public-sector unions and allied with the Japan Socialist Party.

The students' political inheritance was similarly mixed. Universities in the late 1940s were centers of democratic reform and left-wing activism, and the Japan Communist Party (JCP) had strong influence among students. The party's shifts of line, including a brief period of armed-struggle rhetoric in the early 1950s and its renunciation in 1955, left many young activists disillusioned. The Anpo Crisis of 1960, discussed in Chapter 92, mobilized hundreds of thousands of people, including students, against revision of the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty. It failed to stop the revision, and it left a generation of activists asking why mass protest had not succeeded and who had failed it.

The setting after 1960 was one of rapid change. Prime Minister Ikeda Hayato's Income Doubling Plan inaugurated a decade of growth. The baby-boom generation born after the war entered universities in the mid-1960s, and enrollments expanded much faster than the institutions that housed them. The United States escalated the war in Vietnam in 1965 while Japan served as a rear base, and Okinawa remained under American administration, a fact that made the islands both a military hub and a symbol of unfinished postwar settlement. Prime Minister Satō Eisaku, in office from 1964 to 1972, pursued Okinawa's reversion while renewing the security alliance.

Students should keep in mind that this chapter covers a particular slice of a larger period. Industrial conflict did not end in 1960, and student, civic, and labor protest continued after 1972. The chapter's boundaries (approximately 1957 to 1972) are those of the movements that most visibly challenged the political order during the high-growth years. They are analytical boundaries, not sharp breaks, and the people who lived through the period did not experience them as chapters in a book.

Section 93.1 · Division I — Labor Militancy and the Crisis of Industrial Relations

Labor Conflict in the Age of Economic Growth

Japan's high-growth economy is often described as a story of cooperation between firms, workers, and the state. Chapter 91 examined the institutions behind that description: enterprise unions, long-term employment ideals, and the annual spring wage offensive (shuntō). This section addresses what that account leaves out. The cooperative model was not simply a natural outgrowth of Japanese culture. It was built, in part, through years of conflict in the 1940s and 1950s over who would control the workplace, and in the late 1950s that conflict was still unresolved.

The legal basis for union activity was laid during the Occupation. The Trade Union Law of 1945 and the labor legislation that followed protected the right to organize, bargain, and strike, and union membership rose rapidly in the late 1940s. But the Occupation also curtailed those rights when they appeared to threaten its priorities. MacArthur forbade the general strike planned for February 1, 1947, and, as the Cold War deepened, the Red Purge of 1949–1950 removed communists from many workplaces. Out of this setting emerged Sōhyō, founded in 1950 with the encouragement of American officials who hoped for a labor federation independent of communist influence. Within a few years it had moved sharply to the left, supporting the Socialist Party, opposing rearmament and the security treaty, and mobilizing against revisions of labor law. A well-known quip, usually cited in studies of the period, said that the "chicken" the Occupation had hatched had turned into a "duck."

Sōhyō's strength lay in public-sector workers, including teachers, railway workers, and postal employees, and in the large industrial and mining unions of the private sector. It was not the only federation. Rivals and splinters, among them Zenrō (1954), the Federation of Independent Unions (Chūritsu Rōren), and, after 1964, Dōmei, favored more cooperative relations with employers and often opposed Sōhyō's political line. Differences among them were as much about strategy as ideology. Sōhyō leaders in the later 1950s promoted what was called "community-wide struggle" (machigurumi tōsō), in which unions sought the support of whole towns, including shopkeepers, families, and local organizations, to sustain disputes.

Within this landscape, historians distinguish two broad orientations. Enterprise unions in growing firms generally sought negotiated improvements, such as wages and bonuses, in return for acceptance of managerial authority over production and personnel. Militant unions, concentrated in coal, steel, shipbuilding, and public services, challenged management directly over layoffs, work assignments, and the right of the union to shape how work was organized. The latter were more common in industries under pressure from technological change and international competition. The tension between the two was driven by industrial restructuring, which firms called "rationalization" (gōrika), meaning reorganization to cut costs through mechanization, redeployment, and reduction of employment. Where rationalization threatened jobs and union power, unions fought. Where it came with growth, unions bargained. Miike was the first.

Historical Vocabulary
Sōhyō, Enterprise Unionism, and Industrial Rationalization

Sōhyō (Nihon Rōdō Kumiai Sōhyōgikai, General Council of Trade Unions of Japan) was a national federation of unions founded in 1950. Its membership numbered in the millions, concentrated in public-sector and heavy-industrial workers, and it worked closely with the Japan Socialist Party. It is best understood as a federation of unions with different traditions, not as a single disciplined organization.

Enterprise unionism refers to unions organized within a single firm or workplace and including both blue-collar and many white-collar employees, in contrast to unions organized by craft or industry across employers. It facilitated negotiated cooperation with management but could limit solidarity across firms. Rationalization (gōrika) was the contemporary Japanese term for restructuring of production to cut costs. The English word "rationalization" can mislead modern readers, because in this context it almost always meant mechanization, staff reduction, and tighter managerial control. Chapter 91 treats the institutions in detail; this chapter concerns where they were contested.

Section 93.2 · Division I

Coal Mining, Energy Transition, and the Origins of the Miike Dispute

The Miike coalfield lay in the area of Ōmuta (Fukuoka Prefecture) and Arao (Kumamoto Prefecture) on the Ariake Sea in northern Kyushu. Its mines had been developed in the Meiji era, passed from the state to Mitsui in 1889, and made Mitsui one of the great industrial combines of prewar Japan. The history of Miike includes much that later chapters of this textbook confront: convict labor in the nineteenth century, and, during the Pacific War, the use of conscripted Korean and Chinese workers and Allied prisoners of war in its mines (Chapter 79). In 2015 the Miike mine and port were among the sites inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List as part of the Meiji industrial heritage, an inscription accompanied by controversy over how this wartime forced-labor history should be presented. After the war, the mines operated under Mitsui Mining Company, which had been separated from the dissolved Mitsui combine (Chapter 89).

Coal mattered because it was the energy base of postwar reconstruction, and Miike was one of the country's largest producers. It was also dangerous work. Seams ran deep beneath the sea floor, and miners faced cave-ins, explosions, gas, and dust that produced chronic lung disease. Ōmuta was a company town, in which Mitsui and its affiliates dominated employment, housing, shops, and local politics, and miners lived in company housing in neighborhoods organized around the pits. Work underground was often arranged through a hierarchy of regular employees and subcontracted laborers, and the divisions between them mattered when disputes arose.

By the late 1950s, coal's position was weakening. Imported petroleum, cheaper and cleaner to handle, was displacing coal as the principal energy source, a transition that took place in the early 1960s, and Japanese coal, mined at great depth, was costly by international standards. The government encouraged coal-industry rationalization, and the mining companies responded with plans to close high-cost pits and reduce employment. For Mitsui, which held its position through the more efficient Miike mines, the aim was to raise productivity and shed workers, and to do so on management's terms.

The Miike union was among the strongest in the country. In 1953, Mitsui proposed a companywide personnel-reduction and restructuring program that the Ōhara Institute describes as involving 6,739 proposed voluntary retirements and related measures, not 6,739 people already dismissed. Its retrospective chronology identifies 3,464 designated dismissals within the conflict and 1,815 of those workers restored to their previous posts. The Mitsui miners’ federation and its member unions conducted a 113-day campaign, using partial stoppages and other industrial tactics rather than a single uninterrupted general strike. The settlement was widely regarded as a union success. This experience strengthened the confidence of miners who believed collective organization could resist management. Marxist study circles, associated in scholarship with the Socialist Association and economist Sakisaka Itsurō, influenced activists who saw the workplace as a site of class conflict. Mitsui and employers’ organizations drew the opposite conclusion: they sought to restore managerial control over personnel and production, helping prepare the later confrontation.

What was at stake, then, was not merely a number of dismissals. It was whether managers or the union would decide how work was organized, who would be employed, and who would be dismissed. Both sides saw Miike as a test case for the entire coal industry and, in the eyes of both, for Japanese industrial relations generally. That is why the dispute drew the attention of federations, business organizations, and national politicians, and why the language of "general capital versus general labor" appeared in the press.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Mining Household during the Miike Dispute

The following is a composite reconstruction of patterns described in studies of coal-mining communities. It describes no particular family and quotes no actual person.

A mining household in Ōmuta in 1960 typically lived in company-owned or company-arranged housing near the pit, in a community where neighbors worked for the same employer and where the union, the company store, and the local school were part of one social world. The husband's work underground was dangerous and dirty, and wages, which included allowances, were the household's principal income. Wives managed the household budget and often carried out additional work, and children grew up in a community where the pit determined the rhythm of the day. Mine work was not only a job but a social identity, and for many families it was multigenerational.

When the lockout began, wages stopped. The union paid strike benefits drawn from funds supported by Sōhyō and other unions, but these were smaller than wages, and households relied on savings, credit from local shops, relief supplies from other communities, and part-time work. Wives organized into a miners' wives' association that supported the strike through meetings, fund-raising, and demonstrations, and some women took part in picket lines. Some households were also divided, because the formation of a second union meant that members of the same community, sometimes of the same family, ended up on opposite sides. Historians of the dispute emphasize that these household and neighborhood relationships, as well as the formal confrontation between the union and the company, explain the strike's intensity. Painter and former miner Yamamoto Sakubei of the Chikuhō coalfield, whose records of mining life were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2011, offers an example of the kind of testimony that survives from coal-mining communities of the period.

Section 93.3 · Division I

The Mitsui Miike Strike of 1959–1960

In January 1959 Mitsui Mining proposed major layoffs across six mines as the coal industry faced competition from oil. Its companywide plan was followed by voluntary-redundancy campaigns and a particular target of 2,210 reductions at Miike. According to Michio Nitta’s detailed account, Mitsui issued 1,278 compulsory-redundancy notices on December 10, 1959, after earlier approaches to voluntary separation; on December 16 it dismissed 1,210 workers who had refused the requests. These figures mark different stages, not contradictory estimates of one event. Approximately 300 targeted employees were workplace union activists, making the action a direct test of union influence over production as well as staffing costs. Collective bargaining had broken down in November. After further stoppages and failed negotiations, Mitsui began a lockout on January 25, 1960, and the union commenced an indefinite strike. The confrontation that had been building since the 1953 dispute had begun.

The dispute was national almost at once. Sōhyō treated it as a central struggle, sending money, organizers, and delegations, while the business community supported Mitsui through the employers' federation and fellow firms. The press framed it as a showdown between organized labor and organized capital. Support also came from beyond unions: students, intellectuals, and residents of other towns visited the mining district, collected funds, and joined demonstrations. The miners' wives, mentioned above, took an active role, sometimes more militant than that of the union leadership, and their participation made the strike a community mobilization, as the strategy of community-wide struggle intended.

Management sought to restore production and weaken the first union. A second union formed on March 17, 1960, comprised of employees more willing to resume work under an agreement with the company. Its formation produced tensions among coworkers and across mining families. On March 29, a first-union member was stabbed to death. An International Labour Organization freedom-of-association complaint identifies the victim as Mr. K. Kubo and records allegations that the assailant was associated with company-aligned intimidation; those allegations should not be confused with findings independently established by the ILO. The threatened mass confrontation over coal shipments and the hopper intensified during June and July, when first-union pickets, second-union members, company efforts to resume production, police and court action converged. Accounts differ on crowds, alleged intimidation and responsibility for episodes of violence. The dispute was serious enough to attract national attention, but the distinct March killing and later hopper standoff should not be merged into one incident.

The strike overlapped with the Anpo Crisis of May and June 1960 (Chapter 92). Many activists, including Sōhyō's leaders, moved between the two, and some contemporaries saw them as linked parts of a single contest between the working population and the establishment. Yet the connection was not simple. The treaty revision was a question of foreign policy and constitutional politics, while Miike was a question of workplace power, and the mass mobilization that surged in June did not translate into victory in the mines. After Kishi Nobusuke's resignation in July, the new Ikeda government turned to economic themes, announcing the income-doubling plan in the fall, which had the effect of shifting public attention from confrontation to growth.

Mediation began earlier than the dispute’s final months. After negotiations failed, Central Labour Relations Commission (Chūrōi) chairman Nakayama Ichirō presented an initial proposal on November 21, 1959, which both sides rejected. His successor, Fujibayashi Keizō, offered another proposal on April 6, 1960: formal rescission of dismissal notices paired with voluntary retirement for the affected workers. Management accepted; the first union rejected it. When confrontation at coal-shipping facilities threatened to escalate, the commission issued a third proposal on August 10 along similar lines. After fierce debate, the union side accepted it at an emergency coal-miners’ congress on September 6. Settlement implementation and termination of the strike followed by early November; the confrontation is commonly described as a 282-day strike. The settlement preserved a union organization but did not return most discharged miners to their former jobs. Management gained substantial freedom to reduce employment, but at great economic and social cost. Calling Miike simply a labor victory or a management victory obscures what each side gained and lost.

Section 93.4 · Division I

Miike's Aftermath: Union Divisions, Industrial Restructuring, and Labor Strategy

The immediate consequences for Ōmuta and the mining community were severe. The union that emerged from the strike was weakened and divided, with the second union a presence in the pits and in the neighborhoods. Relations between the two groups remained bitter for years, and families that had fought on opposite sides lived in the same communities. The company, for its part, secured greater control over work assignments and personnel and continued to cut employment through the 1960s as coal declined. The strike's cost to the participants, in lost wages, debt, injury, and dismissal, was very large.

The industrial setting did not improve. On November 9, 1963, an explosion at Mitsui's Miike-Mikawa mine killed 458 miners and left hundreds more with carbon monoxide poisoning (839 cases in the usual count), one of the worst industrial disasters in Japanese history. It exposed the hazards of mine work and the human cost of the productivity drive, and it became the subject of litigation and of long-term medical and welfare issues for survivors and their families. Coal production continued to contract under government rationalization programs, and the Miike mine was finally closed in 1997.

For the wider labor movement, the effects were mixed. Miike showed that a determined union with national backing could sustain a strike for most of a year, and that it could be defeated when the employer was well prepared, the labor force was divided, and the state offered mediation on terms that favored restructuring. Union leaders drew different conclusions. Some concluded that community-wide mobilization had been admirable but insufficient. Others concluded that Sōhyō's confrontational methods had reached their limit and that unions would do better to concentrate on wage bargaining across industries, as the annual shuntō was beginning to do (Chapter 91). The moderate federations took Miike as a justification for their emphasis on cooperation with management.

It is important to distinguish what Miike changed from what was already underway. The decline of coal and the shift to oil, the consolidation of enterprise-based labor relations in the growing manufacturing sector, and the movement of the labor force into new industries were long-term developments that would have proceeded without Miike. The dispute accelerated some outcomes and shaped their form: it helped legitimize managerial prerogatives in restructuring and demonstrated the costs of confrontation. Whether it was the decisive event that ended a militant era or one episode among many in a gradual change is the subject of a genuine scholarly debate. This section closes the Miike narrative; the broader story of enterprise unionism and the growth economy belongs to Chapter 91.

Historical Debate

Did Miike Mark the Defeat of Militant Unionism or a Broader Transition in Industrial Relations?

The question: Historians of Japanese labor agree that Miike was among the most important disputes of the postwar period. They differ about what it decided.

Interpretation A — A decisive defeat: Some scholars see Miike as the moment when management, with state support, broke the strongest militant union in a key industry and showed that confrontational unionism could not succeed. On this reading, the creation of the second union, the dismissal of activists, and the mediated settlement signaled that the future belonged to cooperative, enterprise-based relations, and the dispute helped to discipline unions elsewhere.
Interpretation B — One turning point in a gradual transition: Other scholars emphasize that the shift toward enterprise-based, cooperative labor relations was driven by structural change: the growth of new industries, the decline of coal, and the strategies of employers and union leaders across the economy. On this reading, Miike exemplified and accelerated a transformation that was already well advanced, and militant unionism persisted in the public sector and in other disputes, so the strike was not a single decisive defeat.

Evidence: Both views draw on union membership and strike statistics, the fortunes of the Miike union and its successor, comparisons with other disputes and industries, and the writings of participants and observers. Work by historians such as Andrew Gordon on postwar labor relations underlies much of the discussion of how employer strategy and union choices interacted, though interpretations of Miike specifically vary.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians explain the character of Japanese industrial relations in later decades, whether it arose through consensus or through the defeat of alternatives, and how they judge the choices made by union leaders and workers.

Section 93.5 · Division II — Zengakuren, Student Radicalism, and the Origins of the New Left

The Origins of Zengakuren and Postwar Student Activism

Zengakuren, the All-Japan Federation of Student Self-Governing Associations (Zen Nihon Gakusei Jichikai Sōrengō), was founded in September 1948. It brought together student self-governing associations from many universities, reportedly well over a hundred institutions, in an effort to defend student interests and promote democratic politics. The immediate impetus was a proposed tuition increase at national universities, and the federation's early activity concerned fees, living conditions, and university autonomy. It rapidly acquired a wider political agenda, in a country where students, and especially students at elite universities, saw themselves as heirs to a democratic mission and as an educated group distinct from the discredited prewar establishment.

The Japan Communist Party had considerable influence in the early federation. Many student leaders were party members or sympathizers, and the party saw students as recruits and as a source of support in campaigns against the Red Purge and rearmament. The relationship carried risks. In the early 1950s the party adopted a militant line, and student members took part in actions that led to arrests and, after the party reversed its line in 1955 (at the meeting known as the Sixth National Conference), to disillusionment. Many young activists concluded that the party had used them and then disowned them, a sentiment that fed the later break.

Between the Occupation and 1960, students took part in campaigns against American military installations. The best known concerned Sunagawa, a district at the edge of Tachikawa Air Base near Tokyo, where plans to extend the runway for American jets provoked resistance by local farmers, supported by labor unions and students. In October 1956, confrontations with police left many injured. Later, protesters who entered the base were prosecuted, and in March 1959 the Tokyo District Court ruled that the stationing of American forces violated the constitution, a decision overturned by the Supreme Court later that year. The Sunagawa campaign served as a model of alliance between local residents, unions, and students, and its example was recalled in later struggles at Sanrizuka.

The Anpo Crisis of 1960, treated in Chapter 92, brought students into confrontation with the state at unprecedented scale. Zengakuren's mainstream faction, drawn from those who had broken with the party, took more radical action than the Socialist and Communist parties wanted, including the forcing of the Diet compound in November 1959 and the sit-in at Haneda airport in January 1960. On June 15, 1960, a Tokyo University student, Kanba Michiko, died during a clash at the Diet; her death became a symbol of the protest movement. Treaty revision proceeded regardless. For this chapter the point is organizational: the failure of 1960 left the student movement bitterly divided and convinced many activists that the established left, Socialist and Communist alike, was incapable of effective action. That conviction is the starting point for the New Left.

Section 93.6 · Division II

The Split with the Communist Party and the Emergence of the New Left

The break between the JCP and a new generation of radicals began before 1960. In 1956, Khrushchev's denunciation of Stalin and the Soviet suppression of the Hungarian uprising shook faith in Soviet-style communism, and many Japanese leftists asked how the JCP, which had followed Moscow and Beijing in shifting lines, could claim leadership. Dissidents formed groups outside the party. The Revolutionary Communist League (Kakumeiteki Kyōsanshugisha Dōmei, abbreviated Kakukyōdō) was established in 1957, with the theorist Kuroda Kan'ichi among its leading figures, and the Communist League (Kyōsanshugisha Dōmei), known as the Bund after the German word, was founded in 1958 by students and young activists who had been expelled from or had left the party.

At the center of the dispute was strategy. The JCP, after 1955, pursued a path that combined parliamentary participation, mass organization, and the aim of a revolution in stages. The new groups rejected the party's authority and its strategy. They criticized it as bureaucratic and conservative, and they argued that revolution required confrontation with the state and independent action by the working class and students, not electoral competition. The label that came to be used was the "New Left" (shin-sayoku), meaning a left that stood outside the traditional parties, and, in a related phrase, "anti-Yoyogi," after the district in Tokyo where the JCP had its headquarters.

The ideas that influenced these groups were diverse, and it is a serious error to treat them as one doctrine. Marxism was the general framework. Leninism supplied the idea of a vanguard party, but the groups disagreed about what kind of party and about whether the Soviet Union, which they associated with Stalinism, was a socialist state at all. Trotskyism, with its critique of Stalin and emphasis on permanent revolution, influenced Kakukyōdō, particularly in its early period. Maoism had some influence later in the decade, although its Japanese adherents were fewer than outsiders sometimes assume, and different factions took different positions on China, Vietnam, and Cuba. The New Left was united by what it opposed, the JCP's strategy, Soviet orthodoxy, and the established order, much more than by what it proposed.

After 1960 the movement proliferated. The Bund, which had been prominent in Anpo, collapsed in the aftermath, and its members scattered into new groups. Kakukyōdō grew, but divided. The result was a landscape of small organizations, each with its own newspaper, theory, and student wing, competing for influence in the self-governing associations and in the leadership of Zengakuren. Students and historians use the word "sect" (seppa) for these groups, a term that implies both organized factions and, often, a critical view of their exclusivity.

Historical Vocabulary
Zengakuren, Zenkyōtō, and the New Left

Zengakuren is the federation of student self-governing associations founded in 1948. It was an organization of student governments, which were bodies within universities, and was later contested by rival groups, each claiming to represent it. Zenkyōtō (Zengaku Kyōtō Kaigi, All-Campus Joint Struggle Committee) refers to campus-based coordinating committees that formed at individual universities in 1968–1969, joining students from different factions and unaffiliated students. They were connected historically to Zengakuren but were not the same institution. A Zenkyōtō was organized on one campus; Zengakuren was a national federation.

The New Left (shin-sayoku) is the umbrella term for the revolutionary groups that emerged outside the JCP and the Socialist Party. It names a broad current, not a single organization, and included groups with sharply different theories. Students should be careful with all three terms: reading "Zengakuren" as a synonym for "student protesters" or "New Left" as a synonym for "terrorists" is a frequent error that this chapter works to avoid.

Section 93.7 · Division II

Chūkaku-ha, Kakumaru-ha, Bund, and the Fragmentation of Student Politics

Among the many New Left organizations, two grew from Kakukyōdō and became the most important. In 1963 Kakukyōdō split in a dispute over strategy and organization. One side became the Chūkaku-ha, "Central Core Faction," led by Honda Nobuyoshi. The other became the Kakumaru-ha, "Revolutionary Marxist Faction," in which the theorist Kuroda Kan'ichi was a dominant figure. Both claimed the heritage of the original organization and criticized the Soviet Union and the JCP, but they disagreed about the role of the party, the relationship between the movement and the working class, and the proper tactics. Their quarrel was theoretical and also organizational, and it was destined to become violent.

The remnants of the Bund and other groups formed further alignments. In the mid-1960s a "three-faction" coalition (sanpa), including the Chūkaku-ha and groups descended from the Bund, took control of the Zengakuren name for a time and became known for militant street tactics, which included helmets, staves, and organized marches against police lines. A rival Zengakuren was dominated by Minsei, the JCP's youth organization, which favored a more disciplined and parliamentary line and was bitterly opposed by the New Left. The existence of several organizations using the same name is one of the reasons that a statement such as "Zengakuren did X" is unreliable without a specification of which Zengakuren is meant.

The factions disagreed over many issues: whether the Soviet Union was a degenerate workers' state or a form of state capitalism; how to regard China; whether students could be a revolutionary force or only helpers of the working class; and whether to cooperate with the Socialist and Communist parties on specific campaigns. These were not abstract quarrels. They determined who led student governments and who controlled the money and the printing presses, and they led to fights at meetings and on campuses. Over the years the confrontations moved from arguments to beatings, and then to fatal attacks. The term used for violence between factions, uchigeba, entered Japanese usage, and police statistics later attributed well over a hundred deaths to it across the following decades, with Chūkaku-ha and Kakumaru-ha the principal antagonists.

The confrontations with the state also grew harsher. On October 8, 1967, when students of the three-faction coalition tried to block Prime Minister Satō's departure from Haneda for a trip to Southeast Asia that included South Vietnam, a Kyoto University student, Yamazaki Hiroaki, died during the clash, and accounts of the cause of his death were contested at the time. The next month a second Haneda demonstration followed. In January 1968, protests accompanied the arrival at Sasebo of the American nuclear-powered aircraft carrier Enterprise. These events established the repertoire that later campus struggles would draw on and created the impression, in the press and among the public, that students were a violent presence on the streets. The fragmentation described here is essential to the later narrative: there was no unified student movement, and Zenkyōtō in 1968 would be in part a reaction against the factions.

Section 93.8 · Division II

University Expansion, Student Life, and the Sources of Campus Discontent

Behind the organizations lay a social transformation. Japan's higher-education system expanded greatly in the 1960s. The enrollment rate, roughly one in ten young people around 1960 by commonly cited estimates, approached one in four by 1970, when junior colleges are included, and the number of students roughly doubled. The "baby boom" generation, born in 1947–1949, reached university age in the mid-1960s. Most of the growth was absorbed by private universities, some of which became very large, and Nihon University, with a student body of about one hundred thousand, was the largest. The institutions grew far faster than their capacity: lecture halls were overcrowded, faculty-student ratios were poor, and many students saw their education as an assembly line.

Administrative structures reinforced the discontent. Universities were run by professors and trustees with little student participation, and the authority of senior professors within the chair system (kōza-sei) was nearly absolute within their fields. Tuition at private universities rose, and campaigns against fee increases, such as those at Keio (1965) and Waseda (1965–1966), preceded the later wave. Medical education was an additional site of grievance: graduates were required to serve in an unpaid or poorly paid internship under conditions that students regarded as exploitative, a system that became the immediate cause of the Tokyo University crisis. Dormitories and student halls were another focus, since students demanded the right to manage them while administrators sought to control them.

The students' expectations about their future also mattered. The corporate employment system described in Chapter 91 promised secure employment to graduates of reputable universities, but it required conformity and long hours. Some students saw the prospect as a bargain they did not want, becoming a cog in the machinery of a growth economy that also supported American war-making in Vietnam. Many others simply wanted good jobs and did not take part in the protests, and nonparticipants far outnumbered participants even at the most disrupted universities. The mass character of the university, with large cohorts competing for place in a few elite companies, made students anxious about their prospects, and the combination of privilege and anxiety produced a distinctive political temper.

Women were a growing but minority share of the student body and were concentrated in particular faculties and in junior colleges, and their experience of protest, discussed in Section 93.12, was shaped by expectations about gender. The practical, institutional character of many early grievances is important: tuition, internships, dormitories, and disciplinary procedures were concrete matters that affected students directly, and many participants mobilized first around these issues before the protests acquired national and revolutionary significance. It would be a mistake to read the movement as the product only of abstract ideology, as it would be a mistake to read it as only a revolt over fees.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Early Postwar Organizations to the Movements of the Late 1960s

What Changed

  • The student left moved from JCP-influenced federation politics to a plurality of independent New Left factions and, in 1968, to campus-based committees.
  • The labor movement's militant wing lost its central position after Miike, while the student movement grew into a mass phenomenon.
  • The number of students more than doubled, and universities became mass institutions.
  • Tactics moved from demonstrations and strikes to barricades, occupations, helmets and staves, and, among a few, firearms and bombs.

What Remained Continuous

  • Opposition to the security treaty and American bases, from Sunagawa to Okinawa
  • The importance of alliance with farmers and workers as an ideal, even where it was hard to achieve
  • Reliance on organizations that claimed the Zengakuren name
  • State response through police intervention and legislation

Who experienced change most directly? Students at large private universities, where growth and administrative authority were most acute. Who experienced greater continuity? Industrial workers and farmers, whose grievances and organizations followed longer patterns. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the late-1960s movements were both a break with the older left and a continuation of its themes.

Section 93.9 · Division III — University Struggles and the Protest Explosion of 1968–1969

Nihon University, Financial Scandal, and the Rise of Zenkyōtō

The most sustained student confrontation of 1968 began at Nihon University, one of the country’s largest private universities. In April 1968, tax authorities identified about ¥2 billion in unaccounted-for funds in the university’s financial affairs. Contemporary reporting and later scholarship describe a scandal over opaque accounting and university governance; the amount should not, by itself, be taken as proof of an adjudicated tax offense against every administrator. Students at an institution marked by high fees, large classes and restrictions on independent political activity demanded financial disclosure and a greater voice in university affairs. Attempts by the administration to suppress protests, including clashes involving athletic-club members, deepened the crisis. In May and June students demonstrated on a large scale and confronted university and police authorities.

What made Nihon University distinctive was the form of organization. Students formed the Nihon University All-Campus Joint Struggle Committee (Nichidai Zenkyōtō), which brought together students from different faculties, including many who belonged to no political group, and whose leader, Akita Meidai, became a prominent public figure. The model spread. Zenkyōtō committees worked through mass meetings and collective decision-making and tried to include factional and nonfactional students alike. Their demands concerned financial disclosure, democratic management of the university, and the end of restrictions on student activity. They used barricades to seal off buildings and held large-scale negotiations, and they presented themselves as an alternative to the sects that had dominated student politics.

The high point of the Nihon University struggle came on September 30, 1968, when tens of thousands of students gathered at the Ryōgoku Kokugikan, the sumo arena in Tokyo, for a mass bargaining session with the university's president and trustees. The administration agreed to demands including its own resignation and public disclosure of finances. Within days, however, the government and the university leadership repudiated the agreement. Prime Minister Satō and the education authorities denounced it, and the administration refused to implement it. The reversal did more than any speech to convince students that the university and the state were bound together, and that agreements reached in open meetings could be revoked by authorities who held the legal power. The struggle continued, the barricades went up again, and police intervened.

Zenkyōtō must be distinguished from Zengakuren. Zengakuren was a national federation of self-governing associations that, by 1968, was divided among factions. A Zenkyōtō was a campus-based body organized around the grievances and decisions of students at one institution, with participants who might or might not belong to a sect. In many places factions participated in Zenkyōtō and tried to influence it, so the distinction was one of structure and orientation, not a clean separation. Some students saw in Zenkyōtō an alternative to the older organizations, with their rivalries and discipline, and the sentiment was powerful in 1968. It did not eliminate the factions, which stayed active inside and around the committees and later contested control of them.

Section 93.10 · Division III

The University of Tokyo Struggle and the Battle of Yasuda Auditorium

The conflict at the University of Tokyo began with a specific complaint. In early 1968, students in the medical faculty went on strike against the internship system and the conditions of training. In March the university disciplined seventeen students after a protest, and the discovery that at least one of those punished had not been present at the incident made the administration's authority harder to defend. The strike spread to other faculties. On June 15, 1968, students occupied Yasuda Auditorium, the university's central assembly hall, and the university president called riot police onto campus on June 17, an act that violated the traditional understanding that police should not enter the grounds of the university without the consent of its members. The intervention enlarged the protest and led to a general strike, and by the summer, students had formed the Tokyo University Zenkyōtō, with the physics graduate student Yamamoto Yoshitaka as its chair.

For months the university was in turmoil. Buildings were barricaded, classes disrupted, and negotiations repeatedly broke down. Students were divided. Zenkyōtō and the factions inside it demanded the dismantling of the university's authority structure and a rethinking of the purpose of the university itself, using slogans of "negation of the self" that questioned students' own position as future elite members. Other groups, led by the Communist Party's youth organization, Minsei, opposed the occupation and sought a negotiated reform. Violent clashes between the two camps occurred. Professors and administrators, for their part, were divided between those who sought reconciliation and those who demanded restoration of order. In January 1969, representatives of the university signed an agreement with student groups that did not include Zenkyōtō, which rejected it.

On December 29, 1968, amid continuing occupations and uncertainty about reopening, the authorities decided not to conduct the University of Tokyo’s 1969 entrance examinations under the normal schedule; thus the decision preceded, rather than followed, the final police assault. University representatives and delegates from seven faculties concluded a confirmation agreement at a January 10, 1969 assembly, without the participation or acceptance of Zenkyōtō, which rejected that settlement. On January 18–19, about 8,500 riot police entered the Hongō campus and cleared occupied buildings. Students defended Yasuda Auditorium with barricades, stones and improvised incendiaries, while police used water cannon and other equipment. Patricia Steinhoff’s account gives 819 student arrests over the two days. The auditorium’s fall was televised nationwide. The cancelled examinations, protracted disruption and subsequent police intervention were interconnected consequences of the unresolved crisis, but they must be placed in their proper chronological order.

The meaning of Yasuda Auditorium was contested at the time and remains so. To the government and to many in the public, it was the end of an intolerable disruption and the restoration of order, though the cost was that the police had entered a university in force. To the students, it was proof of the state's willingness to use violence against a university community that had tried to govern itself. To many of the professors it was a tragic failure of the academic community. The Ministry of Education's own account confirms the main events and the nationwide scale of campus disruption. The Tokyo conflict also drew public intellectuals into the confrontation, among them the political scientist Maruyama Masao, who was criticized by students, and in May 1969 the novelist Mishima Yukio debated Zenkyōtō students at Komaba, an episode more relevant to the cultural history of Chapter 95. The struggle did not end with the clearing of the buildings, but its character as an occupation was over, and thereafter much of the movement's energy went elsewhere.

Section 93.11 · Division III

The Spread of University Occupations and the Government's Response

Nihon University and Tokyo University were the best-known cases, but disruption in 1968–1969 was nationwide. Ministry of Education accounts record that a large share of the country's universities experienced some form of dispute, strike, or occupation, with the numbers peaking in 1969. Kyoto University, Waseda, Chūō, Hōsei, Dōshisha, and many other institutions, public and private, were affected. The causes differed from campus to campus: at one university, a tuition increase; at another, the control of student halls; at another, a disciplinary decision or a scandal. National themes, such as Vietnam, the security treaty, and the character of the university in a corporate society, were laid over local grievances, and the local character of each dispute is a reminder that there was no central command.

The methods were similar across campuses: strikes, mass meetings, barricades made of desks and chairs, the occupation of buildings, and the use of helmets, staves, and, sometimes, improvised incendiary devices. University administrations responded with negotiation, discipline, requests for police assistance, and, in some cases, the suspension of classes. The police, in turn, used riot units (kidōtai), water cannon, and tear gas. The October 21, 1968 International Antiwar Day demonstrations in Shinjuku were an example of the street dimension: after crowds occupied Shinjuku Station and set fire to vehicles, the authorities invoked the Penal Code's provisions on riot (sōjō) and hundreds were arrested.

The consequences for universities were substantial. Classes were suspended or reduced for months, examinations were disrupted, and some graduates left without completing the full course of study. Students who opposed the occupations, a majority in many places, were caught between the activists and the administration, and some organized groups to demand the resumption of classes. Non-teaching staff, whose work and workplaces were affected, and junior faculty, who were sometimes more sympathetic to the protests than senior professors, had their own positions. The legitimacy of university autonomy itself came under question, since the occupations asserted the community's right to self-government against administrations, and the administrations, in turn, called on the state.

The government responded through the Law concerning Emergency Measures on the Operation of Universities (Daigaku no Un’ei ni Kansuru Rinji Sochi Hō), promulgated August 7, 1969, and effective August 17. The time-limited law set procedures and conditions for intervention in university operations facing prolonged disruption; it did not make every protest unlawful or provide a blanket, unlimited warrant for police action. Government and university officials argued that its powers were needed to restore education for students who wanted to attend classes. Critics—including academics who did not endorse campus occupations—feared a dangerous lever for central government over university autonomy. The university crises receded unevenly through 1969–1970, shaped by police action, internal negotiations, students’ changing positions and the law together. For the legislation’s official retrospective treatment, see the Ministry of Education’s Japan’s Modern Educational System (1980).

Section 93.12 · Division III

Student Democracy, Revolutionary Commitment, and the Contradictions of Protest

The student movements of 1968–1969 held ideals that they struggled to realize. The Zenkyōtō model aimed at participatory democracy: decisions made in open assemblies, leaders accountable to the group, no permanent hierarchy. It valued the individual's autonomy and criticized the conformity of the corporate-university system. Slogans such as the "negation of the self" at Tokyo University expressed an effort to take responsibility for one's own privileged position, and the barricades became spaces of intense collective experience, with cooking, sleeping, debate, and mutual aid. Historians such as William Marotti have analyzed the performance of protest as an element of its meaning, which helps to explain its appeal beyond formal demands.

The ideals coexisted with tensions that undermined them. Factions that participated in Zenkyōtō sought to steer it, and the open assembly gave way, in some places, to the leadership of organized groups with a clear line. The practice of self-criticism (sōkatsu), originally a method of collective reflection, could become a means of enforcing conformity. The debate over violence was constant. Helmets and staves, introduced as defensive and then offensive tools, escalated confrontations with police and rivals, and the question of what kinds of force were legitimate divided participants. Nonviolent and violent students sometimes stood on the same barricade, with different understandings of what they were doing, and the violence that did occur, injuring police, students, and bystanders, damaged the movement's public standing.

Gender is the clearest instance of the gap between rhetoric and practice. Women took part in the occupations and demonstrations, and some, such as Kanba Michiko in 1960, became symbols of the movement. But many accounts, including those of women participants, describe a division of labor in which women were assigned support work, such as cooking, communication, and nursing, while men led and spoke. Some women found the experience of the barricades liberating, and others found it a repetition of the hierarchy they were protesting. The criticism of this division, expressed in the early 1970s in documents such as Tanaka Mitsu's 1970 leaflet on liberation, contributed to the women's liberation movement discussed in Chapter 94.

By the winter of 1969–1970 the campus movement was in decline. The clearing of occupations by police, the passage of the university law, the exhaustion of participants, and the sense that the barricades had not produced the expected change were all factors. Participants responded in different ways. Many left activism, resumed their studies, and entered the corporate workforce that they had criticized, some with lasting ambivalence about their youth. Others turned to civic and community movements, including the environmental and local campaigns that Chapter 94 discusses. A minority concluded that the failure of the barricades showed the need for more extreme methods, and this group, along with the factions that had remained organized, fed the radicalization discussed in Division V.

Miike and the Campuses: Four Positions

A Miike miner: For a union member in 1960, the dispute was about employment, the union's survival, and respect. He faced lost wages, the strain of a long strike, and the choice of staying with the union or joining the second one. The settlement's meaning for him depended on whether he or a colleague kept his job.
A miner's family member: Wives and other family members managed the household through months without wages and, in many cases, became activists in their own right, joining the wives' association and the pickets. Their experience combined economic hardship with political awakening, and the split of the community between the two unions affected them directly.
A university activist: A participant in a Zenkyōtō in 1968–1969 might have joined over a specific grievance, such as fees or the internship system, and found in the barricades an experience of collective decision-making and moral seriousness. Over time some became disillusioned with factional control, others with the movement's violence, and some with the failure to change the system.
A nonparticipating student: Many students did not take part and wanted to attend class, take examinations, and graduate. They were affected by closures and sometimes pressured by activists to join strikes, and some organized to resume classes. Their experience is poorly documented, because most sources record the activists and the authorities, which is a reminder that the silent majority was also part of the story.
Section 93.13 · Division IV — Antiwar Activism, Citizens' Movements, and Regional Struggles

The Vietnam War and Japan's Role in American Military Operations

The American escalation of the war in Vietnam in 1965, which began with the bombing campaign in March and the landing of Marines at Da Nang, transformed antiwar politics in Japan. The older peace movement had focused on nuclear weapons, rearmament, and the security treaty. Vietnam added a concrete, daily war in a country with which Japan had no formal conflict but to which it was tied by the American alliance. The question became not whether Japan should fight, but what Japan was doing to help a war that the Japanese constitution, in Article 9, was supposed to prevent the country from waging.

Japan's role was indirect but substantial. American forces used bases in Japan and Okinawa for training, supply, maintenance, and medical care. The Seventh Fleet operated from Yokosuka and Sasebo, and the carrier Enterprise's visit to Sasebo in January 1968 triggered large protests. Wounded soldiers were treated at the Army hospital in Tokyo's Ōji district, where protests occurred in 1968. Vehicles damaged in combat were repaired at depots near Sagamihara. In Okinawa, which remained under American administration, B-52 bombers flew missions to Vietnam, and a B-52 crashed and exploded at Kadena in November 1968, heightening the fear that the islands were exposed to the war's consequences. Japanese industry also benefited from American special procurement orders, which contributed to the growth discussed in Chapter 91.

Japanese policy under Prime Minister Satō supported the American position while avoiding direct participation. The government maintained that the bases were for the defense of Japan and the Far East under the security treaty and that prior-consultation arrangements applied to major changes in American deployments, a position critics found evasive. Satō's visits to Southeast Asia, including South Vietnam in October 1967, were opposed by protesters. The government's position, the American military's operations, and the lives of residents near the bases were distinct, and it is important to keep them apart: residents of towns near bases experienced noise, accidents, crime, and the economic dependence that the bases created, and their views varied, with some depending on the bases for their income.

For antiwar activists the argument was moral and political. Japan, they said, was complicit, because it provided the facilities that made the war possible, and the country that had suffered the atomic bombings and renounced war was helping another Asian nation be bombed. The theme of responsibility, linking the memory of Japan's own wartime conduct in Asia with the present, was central, and it shaped the citizens' movement discussed in the next section.

Section 93.14 · Division IV

Beheiren, Oda Makoto, and the Expansion of Citizen-Led Antiwar Protest

On April 24, 1965, a group of writers, scholars, and citizens organized a demonstration in Tokyo under the slogan "Peace for Vietnam!" The group became known as Beheiren, an abbreviation of Betonamu ni Heiwa o! Shimin Rengō, the Citizens' League for Peace in Vietnam. Its best-known figures were the novelist Oda Makoto, the philosopher Tsurumi Shunsuke, and the activist Yoshikawa Yūichi, and its supporters included writers such as Kaikō Takeshi. The first march drew, by reports, some fifteen hundred people. Over the following years Beheiren grew into a network of local groups across the country, whose number and membership are difficult to specify because it did not keep formal membership rolls.

Beheiren differed from the other organizations in this chapter. It was not a party or union, and it was not a student sect. It had no formal membership, no central doctrine, and no disciplined leadership; participants acted, in the movement's own spirit, as individuals and in small groups. Its leaders emphasized that anyone could take part, in whatever form they chose. This decentralized model made it open to people who disliked parties and factions, including housewives, office workers, young people, and intellectuals, and it matched the citizens' movements that would multiply later in the 1960s. Its tactics were largely nonviolent: marches, teach-ins, newspaper advertisements, leafleting, and street theater. It also held regular "citizens' demonstrations" in which participants were expected to behave as individuals and avoid helmet-and-stave confrontations.

The movement's most distinctive action was assistance to American servicemen who deserted. In October 1967, four sailors from the carrier Intrepid left their ship in Japan, and Beheiren members, working with a committee established for the purpose, hid them and arranged their departure, which took them by way of the Soviet Union to Sweden. Beheiren thereafter helped other deserters. The action challenged the premise that the war was an American affair and made Japanese citizens participants in resisting it. Beheiren also developed international contacts, including exchanges with American antiwar activists, and in the process became part of a transnational network. It dissolved in 1974, after American involvement in the war was winding down.

The movement was not only the work of prominent male intellectuals. Local groups, some led by women, carried out the day-to-day organizing, and research on the Nagasaki branch of Beheiren, for example, shows how gender and local experience shaped participation. In Nagasaki, memories of the atomic bombing influenced the character of antiwar activism, and women's roles in local organizing were substantial. Historians such as Simon Avenell, in his work on citizenship and civic activism in postwar Japan, have emphasized that Beheiren was part of a broader emergence of citizens' politics in these years. That line of civic activism connects to Chapter 94.

Section 93.15 · Division IV

Sanrizuka, Narita Airport, and the Alliance between Farmers and Student Radicals

By the mid-1960s, Haneda airport in Tokyo was approaching its capacity, and the government decided to build a new international airport. In June 1966 the cabinet chose the Sanrizuka area in Chiba Prefecture, near the town of Narita, a rural district of farms and woodland that also included land associated with an imperial household ranch. The decision was made without consulting local residents, who learned of it from the press. Many of the farmers in the area were postwar settlers, including demobilized soldiers and repatriates, who had cleared marginal land in the years after 1945, and they saw the airport as the taking of what they had built from nothing.

The opposition took form quickly. Farmers organized the Sanrizuka–Shibayama Union Opposition League (the Hantai Dōmei), with Tomura Issaku, a Christian farmer and former teacher, as its best-known chair. The league's youth and women's groups played a major role, and the opposition employed sit-ins, tree occupations, and, increasingly, physical resistance to government surveys and land acquisition. Their grievances were concrete: loss of land and livelihood, disruption of village life, and the way in which the government had acted without consent. The government's practice of negotiating with individual households, offering compensation, and, where that failed, resorting to compulsory acquisition, divided families and neighbors.

New Left groups, notably the Chūkaku-ha and the Sanpa coalition, joined the struggle and brought organized militancy, helmets and staves, and, later, Molotov cocktails. The alliance of farmers and radical students recalled the earlier example of Sunagawa and satisfied the New Left's aspiration to unite students and the rural poor. It had real benefits for the farmers, who gained numbers and publicity, and real costs, because the presence of radicals gave the authorities grounds to portray the struggle as extremist and made local residents liable to criminal prosecution. The farmers and the students were not pursuing the same objective. For the farmers, the aim was to keep their land and block the airport. For the revolutionary groups, Sanrizuka was an arena for confrontation with the state, and they continued to see it that way regardless of local priorities.

The conflict intensified in the early 1970s. In February and March 1971 the authorities carried out the first compulsory acquisitions (gyōsei daishikkō) with thousands of riot police, and in September 1971 a second round of forced execution ended in violence in which three police officers were killed by militants. The deaths, the largest loss of police life in the era's confrontations, turned public sympathy against the protesters and strengthened the government's hand, while also hardening the lines inside the movement. The airport was ultimately opened in May 1978, after further confrontations, and conflicts over the remaining land continued long afterward. The history of Sanrizuka illustrates both the legitimacy of local grievances and the complications of external radical involvement, and it shows that protest in this era was not confined to the universities.

Section 93.16 · Division IV

Okinawa, the 1970 Security Treaty Anniversary, and the Limits of Mass Mobilization

The security treaty revised in 1960 was set to reach its tenth anniversary in June 1970. Contrary to a common impression, it did not expire and require a new ratification. Under its terms, it continued in force after ten years unless either party gave one year's notice of termination, so the 1970 question was whether to let it continue or to demand its termination. The Socialist Party, the Communist Party, Sōhyō, and the New Left each sought to mobilize opposition, and demonstrations, including very large ones in June, took place across the country. Yet the government did not need to act, and the treaty continued automatically. The movements faced the awkward situation of protesting an event that required no decision.

The mobilizations of 1970 did not reproduce the political impact of 1960. There were several reasons. In 1960 there had been a specific legislative act to block, and the public's alarm at Prime Minister Kishi's methods and his wartime past had given the movement broad support. In 1970 there was no such act. Income growth, as the Osaka Expo of 1970, which drew tens of millions of visitors, symbolized, had raised living standards and weakened the sense of crisis. The opposition parties were divided, public opinion had been affected by the violent images of the late 1960s, and the New Left's confrontational methods alienated many who might otherwise have joined. The students who did take to the streets in 1970 did so in a political climate that had moved against them.

Okinawa had a different history and different priorities. The islands had been under American administration since the war's end, and for the residents the issues were the prospect of reversion to Japan, the burden of the bases, and the daily relations with American personnel. The Okinawa Prefectural Council for Reversion to the Fatherland, formed in 1960 by the teachers' association, unions, and political parties, led the reversion movement, and Yara Chōbyō, who became chief executive in 1968 through the first popular election to that office, was a reversion advocate. The Satō–Nixon communiqué of November 1969 set reversion for 1972, but Okinawans disagreed about its terms. The slogan "nuclear-free, same as the mainland" (kaku-nuki, hondo-nami) expressed the demand that the bases be reduced to the mainland level. A planned general strike in February 1969 was called off under pressure after a B-52 crash at Kadena, and the reversion agreement of June 1971 was opposed by many in the islands who felt it left the bases in place.

The Koza riot of December 20, 1970, shows how local grievances could produce an outburst that mainland activism did not direct. After a traffic accident involving an American serviceman, and in the context of anger over a military court's acquittal of a serviceman in another fatal accident, a crowd in Koza (now part of Okinawa City) set fire to dozens of American-owned vehicles. The riot was not led by mainland students or by any party, and its participants were ordinary residents expressing frustration accumulated over years. Mainland activists often saw Okinawa as a front in their own struggle, while many Okinawans regarded mainland movements as at best partial allies. Okinawa reverted to Japan on May 15, 1972, with American bases still occupying a significant part of the main island, a legacy discussed in later chapters.

Section 93.17 · Division V — Radicalization, Political Violence, and the Legacy of the Protest Era

The Fragmentation of the New Left and the Escalation of Political Violence

By 1970 the New Left faced a crisis. The campus occupations had been cleared or had faded, the security treaty remained in force, the Satō government had survived, and the revolution that some activists had expected had not arrived. The legal and political climate had also changed: the university law, the arrests of thousands of students, and the criminal prosecutions that followed increased the cost of activism. The response was not uniform. Some activists left politics; others moved into community and civic campaigns; some remained in the factions; and a few concluded that only armed struggle remained.

It is important to separate the forms of conflict that are often lumped together as "violence." Peaceful demonstrations, such as Beheiren's marches or the great treaty protests, involved no deliberate attack on people. Militant civil disobedience, including strikes, sit-ins, and the occupation of buildings, broke rules and disrupted institutions but did not necessarily aim at injury. Clashes in which participants used helmets, staves, stones, and improvised incendiary devices against riot police were a further stage of escalation, and they produced many injuries on both sides. Organized armed violence, meaning the planned use of firearms, explosives, and hijackings, was something else again, and it was undertaken by a very small number of people. The police for their part used tear gas, water cannon, and truncheons, and, in the cases of mass arrest, the Penal Code's riot provisions.

The most corrosive form of violence for the movement was internal. The rivalry of Chūkaku-ha and Kakumaru-ha, and of smaller factions, produced beatings and killings of rivals, often on campuses, that the public found frightening and meaningless. In November 1972, for example, Waseda University student Kawaguchi Daisaburō was killed by members of a rival faction who suspected him of belonging to the other, a case that shocked many and contributed to a backlash against the sects. Such deaths, collectively known as uchigeba, convinced many that the factions had lost touch with any political purpose beyond their own struggle, and they damaged the legitimacy of left-wing activism more generally.

It would be a mistake to suppose that this outcome was inevitable. The great majority of student activists never used lethal violence, and many who had been radicalized in 1968 came to reject it. Nor was the path from protest to terrorism a straight one: it ran through particular decisions by particular groups, shaped by factional competition, isolation from public support, state pressure, and ideologies that justified armed action. The discussion below attends to those decisions and the conditions that made them thinkable, without suggesting that they were a necessary consequence of student protest.

Cause and Effect

Factors Contributing to New Left Fragmentation and Political Violence

Long-term causes

The JCP's strategic reversals and the disillusion of 1956–1960; the failure of the Anpo protests; the sectarian tradition of theoretical disputes; Cold War polarization; a security alliance that tied Japan to the Vietnam War; and a mass university system with weak channels for student participation.

Short-term causes

The clearing of occupations in 1969 and the end of the campus mass movement; the university law; the failure of the 1970 treaty protests to change policy; factional competition for diminishing followings; police arrests of leaders; and the isolation of small groups from public support.

Event or transformation

Some factions turned from mass mobilization to clandestine organization and armed action, and inter-factional violence intensified, while most participants left the movement or shifted to civic activism.

Immediate effects

Hijacking, bank robberies, bombings, internal killings, and an international network; the loss of public sympathy; and stronger policing of the left.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The violence of a few discredited the left's larger project and gave the state a justification for surveillance and legislation. Yet no single cause explains it. Repression did not by itself produce armed groups, since many repressed activists chose other paths, and ideology did not by itself produce terror, since similar ideas inspired nonviolent politics. The outcome depended on the interaction of ideology, organization, group dynamics, state action, and the contingent decisions of individuals.
Section 93.18 · Division V

The Red Army Faction, the Yodogō Hijacking, and Revolutionary Internationalism

The Red Army Faction (Sekigun-ha) was formed in August 1969 by activists who split from the Bund, with Shiomi Takaya as a leading figure. It advocated armed revolution, arguing that the movement had to move beyond demonstrations to organized military struggle, first in Japan and then as part of a world revolution. It was a small group, with members numbering in the low hundreds at most, and most of the student movement viewed it with suspicion. Its plans were, nonetheless, serious enough to attract police attention. In November 1969, police raided a mountain hut at Daibosatsu Pass in Yamanashi Prefecture, where dozens of members, reported as fifty-three, were training, and arrested them. Police stated that the group was preparing attacks, including one on the prime minister's residence, a claim the faction's members later discussed in their own terms. The arrests removed much of the faction's leadership, including Shiomi.

The remaining members chose a different course. On March 31, 1970, nine Red Army Faction members hijacked Japan Airlines Flight 351, a Boeing 727 flying from Tokyo’s Haneda Airport to Fukuoka, armed with blades and a pipe-bomb-type device. Japan’s transport ministry lists 131 passengers and seven crew members (138 total); these are the ministry’s manifest categories, and the figure should not be restated as 138 uninvolved hostages. The hijackers demanded passage to North Korea. The aircraft first landed in Fukuoka and later at Seoul’s Gimpo airport, where deputy transport minister Yamamura Shinjirō offered to exchange himself for remaining passengers. The plane reached Pyongyang in early April, and the incident became known as the Yodogō hijacking. The hijackers remained a distinct North Korea-based group rather than becoming the overseas Japanese Red Army.

The Yodogō group is not the Japanese Red Army, although the two are often confused. The Red Army Faction was a domestic organization. The hijackers were a subgroup of it who left for North Korea and thereafter had little connection to developments in Japan or the Middle East. A separate network began to form in 1971 around Shigenobu Fusako, who had been a member of the Red Army Faction milieu and who left Japan for Lebanon in February 1971 with Okudaira Tsuyoshi. In Lebanon, they allied with the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine (PFLP), and the group, at first calling itself by an Arabic-language name that referred to an "Arab Red Army," trained and organized with Palestinian militants. The name Japanese Red Army (Nihon Sekigun) was formalized later, in the mid-1970s, so that the origins of the overseas network in 1971 and its later consolidation under that name should be kept distinct.

The ideology of these groups combined Marxism-Leninism with a vision of "international revolutionary base areas" and solidarity with anti-imperialist movements, particularly the Palestinian cause, as the world's revolutionary front. It reflected the experience of a generation that had watched Vietnam and had come to see Japan's own working class as incapable or unwilling to act. In practice, the ambitions of revolutionary internationalism translated into criminal violence: hijacking, hostage-taking, and attacks on civilians. Scholars such as Patricia Steinhoff, who studied the Japanese Red Army through interviews and trial records, have shown how group dynamics, ideological justification, and isolation shaped the groups' actions. The assumption that ideals could excuse the harm done to ordinary people was the fundamental moral failure of these organizations.

Historical Vocabulary
Sekigun-ha, Rengō Sekigun, and Nihon Sekigun

Sekigun-ha (Red Army Faction) was the domestic faction founded in 1969 under Shiomi Takaya. Its members included those who carried out the 1970 Yodogō hijacking. Rengō Sekigun (United Red Army) was a separate organization formed in 1971 by the merger of a remnant of the domestic Red Army Faction, led by Mori Tsuneo, with another group, the Revolutionary Left, led by Nagata Hiroko; it is the group responsible for the mountain-camp purges and the Asama-Sansō incident. Nihon Sekigun (Japanese Red Army) was the overseas network, associated with Shigenobu Fusako, that began to form in Lebanon in 1971 and was later so named.

The names are similar, and each includes "Red Army" (Sekigun), but these were different organizations with different members, locations, and histories. Writing that the "Japanese Red Army" hijacked the Yodogō airplane, or that it killed its own members in the mountains, is a common and serious error.

Section 93.19 · Division V

The United Red Army, Internal Purges, and the Asama-Sansō Incident

After the Daibosatsu arrests and the Yodogō hijacking, remnants of the domestic Red Army Faction regrouped under Mori Tsuneo. A separate Revolutionary Left organization led in part by Nagata Hiroko had pursued armed preparations. The two joined forces in 1971 in what became the United Red Army (Rengō Sekigun). The merger was preceded by violence: the Revolutionary Left had already killed two members near Inbanuma in Chiba Prefecture in August 1971. The new organization, pursued by police, moved among mountain hideouts in Gunma Prefecture and subjected members to increasingly extreme military training and ideological discipline.

In the mountain camps of late 1971 and early 1972, leaders demanded coercive sessions of self-criticism (sōkatsu) that escalated into assault, binding, deprivation and exposure. Twelve members were killed or died through abuse in the mountain-camp purge. Added to the two earlier Revolutionary Left victims, the total commonly cited for these related internal-killing episodes is fourteen. Those are different chronological categories, not merely competing estimates of the same twelve deaths. A pregnant member was among the victims. The isolated leadership equated perceived personal failings with betrayal of revolution. Historians have examined the murders through organizational coercion, ideology, group pressure and criminal responsibility, but explanations must not diminish the agency and accountability of the perpetrators.

Police began uncovering the mountain network in February 1972. Five fleeing United Red Army members seized the Asama-Sansō lodge at Karuizawa on February 19 and held the caretaker’s wife hostage. A ten-day standoff culminated on February 28, when police breached the building and rescued her; two police officers were killed during that day’s assault. A civilian who approached the lodge had been shot February 22 and died March 1, so he must not be listed as having died in the final assault. Television coverage attracted an extraordinary audience. The often-repeated figure of approximately 89.7% concerns a combined across-broadcaster audience measure reported in later accounts, not one station’s ordinary program rating; if that precise metric cannot be reproduced from the underlying ratings series, “exceptional national viewership” is the safer description.

As police investigated after the siege, the bodies of victims were discovered in the mountains and the internal killings became known more widely. The conjunction of live images of the hostage crisis and subsequent revelations was devastating for the reputation of revolutionary armed groups, although campus radicalism had already been losing strength. Mori Tsuneo died by suicide while in custody in January 1973. Nagata Hiroko received a death sentence later upheld in 1993 and died in custody in 2011. The United Red Army and the overseas network later known as the Japanese Red Army were different organizations, despite common roots in part of the New Left milieu. Treating them as identical erases their separate memberships, locations and actions.

Section 93.20 · Division V

The Japanese Red Army, the Decline of Militant Politics, and the Legacy of the Age of Protest

On May 30, 1972, three Japanese men, Okamoto Kōzō, Yasuda Yasuyuki, and Okudaira Tsuyoshi, carried out an attack at Lod Airport (now Ben Gurion Airport) near Tel Aviv, shooting and throwing grenades at passengers in the arrivals hall. Twenty-six people were killed, by the usual count, and dozens were wounded. Many of the dead were Puerto Rican Christian pilgrims visiting the Holy Land. Two of the attackers died, and Okamoto was captured. The attack was carried out for the PFLP by members of the overseas network that Shigenobu Fusako had helped to organize, and it was a stark example of how revolutionary internationalism became violence against civilians who had no connection to Japanese politics. In Japan, the news came three months after Asama-Sansō and reinforced the public's revulsion.

The overseas network continued for years. In the 1970s, groups associated with the name Japanese Red Army took part in the seizure of the French embassy in The Hague in 1974, an attack in Kuala Lumpur in 1975, and the hijacking of an airliner at Dhaka in 1977, in the last of which the Japanese government paid a ransom and released imprisoned militants. Shigenobu was arrested in Japan in 2000, sentenced to twenty years in prison in 2006, and released in 2022, and the group declared its dissolution in 2001. Okamoto was released from Israeli custody in a prisoner exchange in 1985. These events are beyond the chapter's period and are mentioned only to show the network's continuity and eventual end. Its history was small in scale and large in notoriety, and it was never representative of the movements from which it originated.

By 1972 and 1973, mass revolutionary student activism had largely subsided. Several factors converged. The campus occupations had been defeated or had burned out, and the government's legal and policing measures had taken effect. The Asama-Sansō incident and the purges had damaged the left's credibility. Okinawa's reversion in May 1972 removed one major issue, and the American withdrawal from Vietnam, following the Paris accords of January 1973, removed another; Beheiren dissolved in 1974. The oil shock of 1973 (Chapter 96) changed the economic climate. Students graduating into the corporate world, many of them in their twenties, were leaving the movement, and new generations did not rejoin it. The decline cannot be attributed to any single cause, whether police repression, extremist violence, or the changing public mood. Each mattered, along with the movement's fragmentation and the failure of its revolutionary expectations.

The legacy of the age of protest is contested and must be assessed with care. It expanded the repertoire of public participation: citizens' movements, the sit-in, the mass bargaining session, and the principle that institutions should answer to those they affect entered Japanese political life, and many of the civic, environmental, and women's movements of the 1970s drew on the experience (Chapter 94). It challenged the authority of universities and workplaces, and universities changed in the following years, though not as radically as protesters had demanded. It also provoked state responses, including expanded police capacity, new legislation, and a more cautious attitude toward student politics, and it left a durable association in public memory between protest and disorder. Historians differ over its meaning. Some, following Oguma Eiji's study of 1968, see it as a response to the anxieties of a society undergoing rapid change; others emphasize its democratic achievements; others its failures. The conflict over how to remember the era remains alive.

Finally, the history of extremist violence must not be allowed to erase the larger history. The labor campaigns of Sōhyō and other federations, the campus movements that were mostly nonviolent in aim if not always in practice, Beheiren and the citizens' antiwar activism, the farmers of Sanrizuka, and the Okinawans who demanded an end to the bases, were lawful or largely lawful movements involving many thousands of ordinary people. The Red Army groups involved a few hundred at most. To give the second the status of the essence of the age would misrepresent it, and to give the first a pass for the violence of the second would be equally wrong. Understanding both, and the relationship between them, is the chapter's purpose.

The sequence shows two patterns that overlap. The first is the rise and decline of mass movements, from Miike through Zenkyōtō, with the climax in 1968–1969. The second is fragmentation and radicalization, from the New Left's founding split through the armed groups of 1970–1972. They were not the same process, and the second did not follow automatically from the first. Rather, the mass movements declined at the point when a small number of activists took up armed methods, and the contrast between the two is what the timeline most clearly shows.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined why Japan's era of rapid economic growth and parliamentary stability produced powerful labor struggles, student rebellions, antiwar campaigns, regional resistance, and revolutionary movements, and why these movements followed such different trajectories. Its central argument is that the age of protest was not one movement but a changing political landscape in which workers, students, citizens, farmers, Okinawan residents, and revolutionary factions challenged different aspects of the social and political order, using methods that ranged from strikes and petitions to occupations, street fighting, and terrorism.

The setting was a postwar order built on legal rights granted during the Occupation and limited by the Cold War. The labor movement, divided among federations, included Sōhyō, whose militant unions fought over layoffs and workplace authority while enterprise unions in growing industries bargained within a cooperative framework. Industrial restructuring, called rationalization, was the immediate cause of conflict where it threatened jobs, as in the declining coal industry. The student movement inherited the disillusion of the 1950s, when the Communist Party's shifts of line and the failure of the 1960 Anpo protests persuaded many young activists that the established left could not lead.

The Mitsui Miike dispute of 1959–1960 was the labor movement's great confrontation. Mitsui's dismissal plan, the lockout of January 25, 1960, and the 282-day strike involved the whole community, including the miners' wives, and drew national support and national opposition. Management split the union by encouraging a second union, violence marked the dispute, and the Central Labour Relations Commission mediated a settlement in November 1960 that neither side could call a simple victory. Historians debate whether Miike was the decisive defeat of militant unionism or one turning point in a gradual transformation that structural change, including the decline of coal, would have produced in any case. The Mikawa mine disaster of 1963 and the eventual closure of the mines in 1997 are reminders of the human cost.

Zengakuren, founded in 1948, grew from a federation of student governments into the main vehicle of student politics, but its history was one of repeated division. The break with the Communist Party produced the New Left, a broad current including the Revolutionary Communist League, the Bund, and later Chūkaku-ha and Kakumaru-ha, groups with different theories drawn from Marxism, Leninism, Trotskyism, and other sources, and with a growing tendency to fight one another. The rapid expansion of higher education, the authoritarian management of universities, and anxiety over the corporate future created a mass of students with practical grievances that preceded any revolutionary ideology.

The protests of 1968–1969 centered on Nihon University, where a financial scandal produced a mass movement and the model of the Zenkyōtō committees, and on the University of Tokyo, where a dispute over medical internships led to months of occupation and, on January 18–19, 1969, the preceding December 1968 decision to cancel that year's entrance examinations and the January 1969 police clearing of Yasuda Auditorium. Disruption spread nationwide, and the government answered with police and, in August 1969, an emergency university law. The movement's ideals of participatory democracy coexisted with factional control, coercive self-criticism, disputes over violence, and gender hierarchies, and by 1970 the occupations were in decline.

Other movements followed their own logic. Beheiren, founded in 1965, built a decentralized network of citizens who opposed the Vietnam War, accused Japan of complicity through the bases, and helped American deserters. The farmers of Sanrizuka resisted the airport on grounds of land and livelihood, were joined by radical groups whose aims differed from theirs, and were confronted by compulsory acquisition and, in 1971, deadly violence. Okinawans pursued reversion and the reduction of the bases on their own terms, as the general strike of 1969 and the Koza riot of 1970 showed, and the mainland mobilizations around the treaty's tenth anniversary in 1970 failed to repeat the impact of 1960.

Radicalization came through a series of decisions by small groups. The Red Army Faction of 1969 advocated armed revolution, and nine of its members carried out the Yodogō hijacking to North Korea in March 1970. A separate overseas network around Shigenobu Fusako formed in Lebanon from 1971 and later took the name Japanese Red Army. The United Red Army, formed in 1971 from other remnants, was associated with twelve deaths in mountain-camp purges, in addition to two earlier killings by its Revolutionary Left precursor, and its remaining members held a hostage at Asama-Sansō in a siege broadcast live in February 1972. In May 1972 members of the overseas network attacked civilians at Lod Airport. These organizations were distinct, and none was representative of the broader movements.

The decline of revolutionary mobilization after 1972 had many causes: the defeat of the occupations, state policing and legislation, the discrediting effects of the United Red Army and Lod, the resolution of Okinawa's reversion and the end of American involvement in Vietnam, and the changed economic climate. The legacy was real: new forms of civic participation, challenges to institutional authority, and, in the other direction, stronger state responses and a durable association of protest with disorder. The history of violence by a few should not erase the much larger history of lawful and nonviolent mobilization, nor should the latter excuse the former.

Taken together, the chapter's three conclusions are these. Economic success and political stability did not eliminate conflict, because industrial restructuring, institutional hierarchy, Cold War alignment, and disputes over representation continued to generate organized resistance. The movements overlapped but pursued different objectives with different methods, and their cooperation should not be mistaken for unity. And the movements' decline cannot be explained by repression or extremism alone. It reflected fragmentation, failed expectations, changing opinion, and economic and political change together.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Sōhyō
The General Council of Trade Unions of Japan, founded in 1950; the largest postwar federation, strong in the public sector and heavy industry and allied with the Socialist Party.
Enterprise union
A union organized within a single firm, a form that facilitated cooperation with management but limited solidarity across employers.
Gōrika (rationalization)
Restructuring to cut costs through mechanization, redeployment, and employment reduction; the cause of conflict where jobs were threatened.
Machigurumi tōsō
"Community-wide struggle," the strategy of drawing whole towns into a labor dispute, as at Miike.
Lockout
An employer's closing of the workplace to employees during a dispute; Mitsui's lockout began on January 25, 1960.
Second union
A rival union formed at Miike in March 1960 that cooperated with the company and split the workforce.
Chūrōi
The Central Labour Relations Commission, the tripartite body that mediated the Miike dispute.
Mikawa disaster
The November 9, 1963 explosion at Mitsui's Miike-Mikawa mine that killed 458 and poisoned hundreds with carbon monoxide.
Zengakuren
The national federation of student self-governing associations founded in 1948; later contested by rival organizations.
Anpo
Short for the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty (Anzen Hoshō Jōyaku); the object of mass protest in 1960 and 1970.
New Left (Shin-sayoku)
The revolutionary groups outside the JCP and the Socialist Party that emerged after 1956.
Kakukyōdō
The Revolutionary Communist League, founded in 1957, from which Chūkaku-ha and Kakumaru-ha split in 1963.
Bund
The Communist League founded in 1958 by young dissidents; prominent in Anpo and later fragmented.
Chūkaku-ha and Kakumaru-ha
The two leading New Left factions that descended from Kakukyōdō and fought each other for decades.
Seppa (sect)
A term for a New Left faction; often implies exclusivity and doctrinal rivalry.
Zenkyōtō
All-Campus Joint Struggle Committees of 1968–1969, campus-based bodies distinct from Zengakuren.
Yasuda Auditorium
The University of Tokyo hall whose occupation was ended by police on January 18–19, 1969.
University emergency law (1969)
The Law on Temporary Measures concerning University Management, a time-limited statute enlarging the education minister's authority over disrupted universities.
Kidōtai
Riot police units used in confrontations with demonstrators.
Beheiren
The Citizens' League for Peace in Vietnam, a decentralized antiwar network founded in 1965 and dissolved in 1974.
Hantai Dōmei
The Sanrizuka–Shibayama Union Opposition League, the farmers' organization opposing the airport.
Gyōsei daishikkō
Administrative compulsory execution of land acquisition, used at Sanrizuka in 1971.
Fukki
Okinawan "reversion" to Japan, achieved on May 15, 1972, with American bases remaining.
Koza riot
The December 20, 1970 outburst in Okinawa, in which a crowd burned American-owned vehicles.
Uchigeba
Violence between rival New Left factions, which killed many people over the following decades.
Sōkatsu
A "summing-up" or collective self-criticism session, which in the United Red Army became a means of coercion and killing.
Sekigun-ha (Red Army Faction)
The domestic armed faction formed in 1969; its members carried out the Yodogō hijacking.
Rengō Sekigun (United Red Army)
The group formed in 1971 whose purges and the Asama-Sansō siege shocked the public in 1972.
Nihon Sekigun (Japanese Red Army)
The overseas network associated with Shigenobu Fusako, formed in Lebanon from 1971 and later so named.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it complicates the story of the postwar decades as one of unbroken consensus and growth. The same society that raised living standards and sustained a parliamentary system also produced a nearly yearlong coal strike, student uprisings that closed universities, and, at the margins, terrorism. Seeing both sides of that history is necessary for understanding why Japan's postwar order was stable and also how its stability was contested, negotiated, and in some respects enforced.

The chapter also shows how different movements understood democracy. Unions sought a voice in the workplace, students sought a voice in institutions, citizens' groups sought a voice in foreign policy, farmers and Okinawans sought a voice in decisions about their land, and a few revolutionaries sought to abandon democratic means altogether. These aims sometimes converged and often conflicted, and the story of their relationships is a story about the practical difficulties of collective action across differences of class, region, generation, and gender.

The developments examined here shaped what followed. The citizens' and community movements of the 1970s, discussed in Chapter 94, drew on the methods and organizational forms of Beheiren and the campus struggles. The consolidation of cooperative labor relations affected the economy described in Chapter 96. The Okinawan issue did not end with reversion in 1972, because the bases remained. And the memory of 1968, of Miike, and of the Red Army groups continued to shape how Japanese understood political dissent for decades.

Finally, the chapter shows why careful distinctions matter. Zengakuren and Zenkyōtō, the Japanese Red Army and the United Red Army, protest and terrorism, were not interchangeable. Keeping them apart makes it possible to honor the large history of lawful and nonviolent mobilization while confronting the real harm done by armed groups, and to resist the temptation, in either direction, to let one stand for the whole.
