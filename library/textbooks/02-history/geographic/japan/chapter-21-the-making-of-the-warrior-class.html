Part IV — The Rise of the Samurai and Kamakura Japan
21

The Making of the Warrior Class

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the tenth and eleventh centuries, a courtier in Kyoto who wrote the word samurai was not usually describing a feudal knight. The word comes from the verb saburau, "to serve" or "to attend" a person of higher rank, and it could apply to guards, attendants, and lesser retainers in an aristocratic household. The armed specialists later remembered as samurai did not yet exist as a recognized order. They were still being made, out of service, horses, kinship, marriage, land, and reward.

Picture a provincial household in the Kantō plain in the middle of the eleventh century. Historians reconstruct such a place from court diaries, war tales, later picture scrolls, and limited archaeological evidence, and they must do so cautiously. It was probably a residence of some size, set among fields worked by dependents, with pasture or stables nearby for horses that were expensive to keep. Its head was likely a man with some claim to office, descent, or reputation, who lived among kinsmen, followers, servants, and relatives by marriage. Some of these people were trained to fight on horseback with bow and sword. Others tended animals, grew food, or managed the household's rights to income from land. The household could be called upon by a provincial governor, a court noble, or a neighbor, and it could also be a source of disorder. Such households, and the networks that connected them, are the subject of this chapter.

Chapter 20 explained why late-Heian society increasingly relied on armed intermediaries: estates and public lands were held through overlapping rights, provincial government was carried out through contractors and local officials, temples maintained their own armed followers, and the court no longer monopolized coercion. This chapter turns from that need to the people who met it. Its central question is how armed service became organized into durable warrior households and networks capable of becoming a political class. The answer unfolds over several centuries and involves military skill, expensive equipment, family structure, marriage, patronage, land, and reputation.

The chapter's core argument is that the warrior class did not suddenly appear as an independent order opposed to the imperial court. Military specialization grew gradually inside the world of court government. Governors hired fighters, aristocratic houses kept guards, retired emperors assembled armed retinues, and temples and estate proprietors sought protectors. Families that supplied this service accumulated followers, land interests, marriage ties, and reputations, and they passed them down through generations. The court often created and used warriors even as it feared them. The word "class" therefore has to be used carefully, since a legally defined warrior status is a much later development.

The story is also regionally diverse. The Kantō plain, with its horse pastures, migrant imperial descendants, and distance from Kyoto, became one great warrior arena. Mutsu and Dewa in the north, where the court's authority met frontier societies and the descendants of communities earlier called Emishi, produced two long wars in the eleventh century and a new regional power at Hiraizumi. Kyushu, the Inland Sea, and the Kyoto region each developed distinctive patterns of armed service. To speak of "the warriors" as one uniform body is therefore misleading. The chapter attends to these differences and to the frontier populations who were not simply extensions of the central state.

The evidence is unusually literary. Aristocratic diaries such as the Shōyūki and Chūyūki, court records, Chinese-style war narratives such as the Shōmonki and Mutsu waki, collections of tales such as the Konjaku monogatarishū, and later picture scrolls and war tales preserve much of what we know. Nearly all were produced by courtiers, monks, or later writers with their own purposes, and later works, above all the thirteenth-century tradition around the Heike monogatari, shaped how the age was remembered. Provincial documents and archaeological evidence for the warriors' own households are thin for this period. Students will see repeatedly that the evidence tells us more about how warriors appeared to others than about how they saw themselves.

Finally, this chapter has firm boundaries. It does not narrate the Hōgen and Heiji disturbances, the rise of Taira no Kiyomori, the Genpei War, or the Kamakura shogunate. Those belong to Chapters 22 through 24. It ends around the mid-twelfth century, when warrior houses had become too consequential for factions in Kyoto to ignore. Nor does it treat the fuller history of warrior religion and martial traditions, which Chapter 26 develops. Its task is narrower and more foundational: to explain what the warriors were becoming before they became the rulers of a government of their own.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the terms bushi, samurai, and tsuwamono, and explain why "warrior class" is an analytical category rather than a fixed caste in the late Heian period.
  2. Trace the movement from the conscript army of the ritsuryō state to provincial governors, court police officials, elite mounted troops, and private followers as the sources of armed force.
  3. Explain how provincial conflicts, including the rebellions of Taira no Masakado and Fujiwara no Sumitomo, the Tadatsune revolt, and the northern wars, increased the value of professional military competence.
  4. Describe mounted archery, armor, horses, training, and logistics, and analyze why the resources they required favored households rather than isolated individuals.
  5. Analyze the military household as an institution composed of kin, retainers, dependents, land rights, women, and patronage ties.
  6. Evaluate how the Minamoto and Taira functioned as multiple kin networks and why "Genji versus Heishi" is a misleading shorthand.
  7. Compare service to governors, regents, retired emperors, temples, and military leaders, and distinguish these late-Heian relationships from the Kamakura vassalage treated in Chapter 24.
  8. Assess how land holding, inheritance, and disputes over offices and revenue could become military questions.
  9. Compare the warrior worlds of the Kantō, Mutsu and Dewa, Kyushu, and the Kyoto and Inland Sea regions, and identify the frontier populations involved.
  10. Distinguish evidence for late-Heian warrior values from the later ideology of bushidō.
  11. Interpret competing historiographical explanations for the origins of the warrior class.
  12. Explain why, by the mid-twelfth century, warrior houses had become politically consequential enough that court factions could no longer ignore them.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did armed service, once an obligation of the state's conscripts, become the specialty of particular households?
  2. Why is it misleading to describe the early warriors as rebels against the court, and in what ways were they products of court government?
  3. What did a mounted archer need in order to fight effectively, and how did those requirements shape who could become a warrior?
  4. How did kinship, marriage, and patronage together create networks larger than a single household?
  5. How did service to different patrons differ from later Kamakura vassalage, and why does the difference matter?
  6. In what ways did control over land and disputes over inheritance make violence a tool of ordinary politics?
  7. How did the warrior worlds of the Kantō, the northern frontier, and Kyushu differ, and what would be lost by treating them as one?
  8. What can historians say about warrior values before bushidō, and how do later texts distort the picture?

Historical Context

The ritsuryō state described in Chapters 13 and 14 was built around an army of conscripts drawn from registered households. Provincial garrisons (gundan) mobilized ordinary cultivators for training and service under officers of the provincial administration. The system was expensive for households and unevenly effective, and it was tested by the wars against the Emishi in the northeast, treated in Chapter 16. In 792 Emperor Kanmu's government abolished general conscription in most provinces, keeping it only in frontier regions such as Mutsu, Dewa, and parts of Kyushu. It reorganized and expanded the already existing kondei as the replacement: small units recruited chiefly from the sons of district chiefs and other local elites, who could supply their own horses and equipment and who were already accustomed to riding and archery. The change reflected an existing reality: effective fighting depended on skills and resources that wealthier provincial families possessed.

In the following centuries, military authority moved further from the central army system. Governors, now responsible for delivering fixed revenues to the court, acquired wide discretion, and when banditry, piracy, or frontier trouble arose, they and the court appointed ad hoc officials to suppress it. Some of these positions, such as the tsuibushi and oryōshi, were later regularized, and in the capital the kebiishi police became a key channel for military service. Meanwhile, from the early ninth century, the court reduced the size of the imperial family by giving surnames to numerous imperial descendants, notably Minamoto and Taira, and placing them in the ranks of officials. Some found careers in the provinces, especially in the east, where they could rely on their rank and connections to attract local followers. The court thus produced, without planning it, a class of men who combined noble descent, provincial connections, and military skill.

Chapter 20 explained the institutional framework in which such families operated: the shōen and public-land systems, the role of governors and local officials, armed temples, and the court's diminishing monopoly on force. Readers should bring three conclusions forward. First, land rights were layered and contested, so disputes required enforcement. Second, no single authority could guarantee order in the provinces, so private capacity to fight was valuable. Third, the court itself relied on that capacity. This chapter assumes those conclusions and does not retell them.

Students should also keep in mind the geography of frontier and contact. The northeast, where the court had earlier fought the Emishi, remained a zone of mixed populations and interests, including groups the court called fushū, a term for communities incorporated after earlier conflict. Kyushu, watched over by the Dazaifu, faced the Korean Peninsula and continental trade and had seen raids from overseas. Hokkaido, then home to Satsumon and Okhotsk-culture communities whose relationship to later Ainu society is treated in later chapters, lay beyond the court's political reach yet was connected to northern Honshu by trade. The warrior worlds discussed here formed within this varied geography.

Section 21.1

What Made Someone a Warrior?

Any account of the origins of the samurai must begin with vocabulary, because the words available to historians carry meanings that developed long after the period in question. In the tenth and eleventh centuries, court writers used several terms for men whose principal skill was fighting. Tsuwamono was an old native word for soldiers and armed men. Bushi, a Chinese-derived compound meaning "military man," appears in court writings for those with martial reputations. Mononofu and musha are other terms met in the sources. None of them named a legally defined social order. A tsuwamono might be a provincial magnate leading followers, a court noble with a reputation for archery, a guard, or a hired fighter.

The word "samurai" requires the greatest care. Derived from saburau, it originally referred to those who attended and served a superior, and in Heian usage it could include guards, clerks, and other lower-ranking members of noble households whose duties were not necessarily military. Only gradually, and mostly in the medieval centuries, did it come to mean a warrior of a certain standing, and the sense familiar from the Tokugawa period, a member of the hereditary military elite with sword-bearing privileges, is later still. Using "samurai" for a tenth-century fighter is therefore a convenient anachronism, and this chapter mostly uses "warrior" or "bushi" for the period it covers.

Historical Vocabulary
Bushi and Samurai

Bushi (武士) is a general term for a person whose specialty was military service. Samurai (侍) derives from saburau, "to serve," and originally described attendants of noble households. In late-Heian sources neither word designated a fixed hereditary caste with defined legal privileges. Over several centuries "samurai" narrowed to signify warrior status, and only in the Tokugawa period (Chapters 42 and 43) did it become the label of a legally defined, sword-bearing elite.

Modern English "samurai" is therefore useful shorthand but can mislead if applied backward without qualification. In this chapter, "warrior class" is an analytical description of a group whose members shared military specialization, household organization, and growing social recognition. It is not a statement that they already possessed a unified legal identity.

Historians also distinguish several overlapping groups within the world of armed service. At the top were court nobles of middling rank whose families had a hereditary reputation for military service and who held governorships, commands, and guard posts. Japanese historians since the later twentieth century have called them gunji kizoku, "military nobles," a category that includes the Seiwa Genji and Kanmu Heishi lineages. Below them were provincial magnates, some of them local officials of long standing, who led followers and controlled land. Beneath both were retainers, foot fighters, grooms, and dependents whose place in the household was often unfree or semi-free and who left almost no record of their own.

Two cautions follow. First, being a warrior did not exclude other roles. The same man might be a provincial official, a landholder, a hunter, a court poet, and a devout Buddhist lay patron. Second, warrior status was not the only source of military capability. Temples kept armed followers, and governors could call out local forces. The claim of the chapter is not that only warriors could fight but that a particular kind of household, specialized in mounted fighting and organized around kin and retainers, became increasingly central and durable.

Section 21.2

Provincial Warfare and Military Specialization

The conflicts of the tenth and early eleventh centuries were not a single "rise of the samurai." They are best read as episodes showing why the court and provincial society came to value professional military competence. The earliest and most famous, the disturbances associated with Taira no Masakado, began in the Kantō around 935. Masakado, a descendant of Emperor Kanmu through the Taira line, was drawn into disputes with kinsmen, including relatives by blood and marriage. As the conflict widened, he came into confrontation with provincial officials. In 939 he attacked a provincial headquarters in Hitachi and then seized offices elsewhere in the east. In the course of these events he assumed a title reported as "new emperor" (shinnō), an extraordinary claim to independent legitimacy. The court, alarmed, authorized his suppression, and in 940 he was killed by forces led by his kinsman Taira no Sadamori and by Fujiwara no Hidesato. His head was carried to Kyoto.

Several features of this episode matter for the formation of the warrior class. Masakado was not an outsider to court society. According to the Shōmonki, he had earlier served a leading Fujiwara regent in the capital and held court ties. His initial quarrels were family and local disputes. His challenge escalated because he could mobilize followers, and because official mechanisms of the region were weak. His defeat came through other warriors who received court rewards, showing how the court used one military family against another. The resulting pattern would persist: the court fought rebels by rewarding those who could raise armed forces, thereby enlarging the standing of those very families.

Almost simultaneously, in the Inland Sea, Fujiwara no Sumitomo, a former provincial official, led sea-going followers in attacks on shipping and provincial centers before being crushed in 941. Taken together, the "Jōhei–Tengyō" disturbances (named for the reign eras) demonstrate that armed challenges arose in both eastern and western Japan and that they arose from within the ranks of officials. They did not come from an outside population of bandits. Later, in 1019, raiders known as the Toi, probably connected with Jurchen peoples of the northeast Asian mainland, attacked Tsushima, Iki, and the Kyushu coast before being driven off by forces under the Dazaifu with local commanders. The episode shows that armed service also had an external dimension, and that the court's response depended on regional forces.

In 1028 Taira no Tadatsune began a revolt in the Bōsō peninsula region, killing officials and defying suppression attempts. Initial commanders failed. In 1031 Tadatsune submitted to Minamoto no Yorinobu, reportedly with little fighting, because he had ties to Yorinobu's household and feared his reputation. The Tadatsune affair is often cited as a turning point in the Minamoto family's reputation in the east. Historians should treat the details of later claims about Yorinobu's eastern followers with caution, since many derive from later compilations. Yet the central point is secure: a leader's standing as a known fighter and patron could settle a conflict that formal commissions had not.

In all these cases, military competence created value beyond the moment of fighting. Successful commanders gained offices, rank, and gifts, and they attracted followers who expected reward. Court sources record grants of official rank and posts to men who suppressed rebellions, and later generations of their descendants would cite these achievements to claim status. The northern wars of the later eleventh century, discussed in Section 21.8, extended the pattern. What emerges is not an inevitable march toward a warrior government but a repeating cycle in which disorder, court dependence on capable fighters, and rewards to those fighters strengthened particular families.

Primary Source Window

The Shōmonki (Tale of Masakado)

Source type: Early war narrative in Sino-Japanese prose (kanbun) · Date: composed some time after Masakado's death in 940; the exact date and authorship are uncertain · Subject: the Kantō conflicts of 935–940

Context: The Shōmonki is among the earliest Japanese war tales and the main narrative source for Masakado's career. Historians believe its author was well informed about the Kantō and educated in Chinese literary forms, perhaps a member of the provincial or capital elite, although this is inferred and not documented. The manuscript tradition has issues of textual transmission, so scholars do not treat every detail as verified fact.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): The tale traces Masakado's conflicts with relatives and provincial officials as a chain of quarrels that expand into attacks on government offices. It reports that he explained his actions to a court patron in terms of grievance and legitimacy, describes his self-proclaimed elevation as sovereign in the east as supported by divine sanction, and depicts his defeat in battle. It presents warriors as a group of practiced archers on horseback whose reputations and personal ties determined loyalty.

Analysis: The text shows that provincial fighters in the tenth century were embedded in court hierarchies and understood their grievances in terms of office, kinship, and legitimacy. It also shows how a literary narrator shaped Masakado's story, whether as tragic hero, rebel, or warning. Its portrayal of individual combat and heroic action reflects narrative conventions and should not be taken as a full account of tactics. It says little about the ordinary cultivators and dependents whose fields and labor supported the armies, and it gives only the perspective of the literate.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the origins of Masakado's conflicts?
  2. Who might have written it, and for what possible purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the relationship between provincial warriors and the court?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Section 21.3

Horses, Bows, Armor, and Mounted Warfare

The characteristic skill attributed to warriors of this age was fighting on horseback with a bow. The Japanese bow, the yumi, was asymmetrical and unusually long, often well over two meters, and in this period was made of laminated wood and bamboo, bound and lacquered. A rider could shoot it from the saddle, and it was carried with a quiver of arrows fitted with distinct heads for different purposes. Training required years. Skill in archery was cultivated from childhood through hunting, exercises, and ceremonial contests, and it carried prestige at court as well as in the provinces. The famous forms of mounted archery later called yabusame, kasagake, and inuoumono were codified in the following centuries, but the underlying practices of shooting from horseback at targets, at game, and at opponents were already central.

Historical Vocabulary
Kyūba no michi (弓馬の道)

Kyūba no michi, "the way of the bow and horse," is a phrase found in war tales and later texts to describe the martial arts of the warrior. A related expression, tsuwamono no michi, refers to the "way of the warrior." Both point to a body of skill, custom, and reputation, not to a philosophical code.

Students should not confuse these phrases with bushidō, the "way of the warrior" as a nationalized ethical ideology that developed chiefly in the late nineteenth century and drew selectively on earlier traditions (see Section 21.9). The earlier expressions describe competence and professional practice, and their use in texts often comes from later compilers idealizing the past.

The horse was as important as the bow. Horses in the archipelago were small by modern standards, and the ritsuryō state had organized official pastures (maki) in regions such as Shinano, Kōzuke, Musashi, and Mutsu, from which horses were sent to the court. An annual ceremony in the capital received horses from designated provinces. Over time, horse breeding passed increasingly into the hands of local families, temples, and estates. Keeping a mount fit for war required pasturage, grooms, feed, and stables. A rider needed several horses, since animals tired and were lost. These demands help explain why the east and north, with their grasslands and pastures, became centers of military strength, and why fighting on horseback was not a skill that any cultivator could adopt.

Armor was equally costly. The large box-shaped armor known as ōyoroi was made of many small plates of iron or leather, lacquered and laced together with silk or leather cords, and designed to protect a rider who stood in the stirrups and shot to the front. It was accompanied by a helmet with neck guards and large shoulder plates. Lighter armor served foot soldiers and lower-ranking fighters. The sword of the period, the curved single-edged tachi, which replaced earlier straight blades over the tenth and eleventh centuries, was carried slung from the belt. It served as a secondary weapon and marker of rank. Polearms such as the naginata were used by foot soldiers and by fighters associated with temples. Students should note that little armor from this exact period survives, and much of what we picture derives from later objects, shrine treasures, and picture scrolls of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Reconstructions of eleventh-century equipment therefore involve inference.

The war tales describe battle as beginning with formal exchanges. Champions announced their names and lineages (nanori), and archers loosed signal arrows before combatants closed. Historians interpret such scenes carefully. The announcing of names served practical and social ends: it made a warrior's claim to reputation known to commanders and witnesses who might later attest to his deeds and support a claim for reward. But literary accounts stress single combat and heroism, and scholars such as Karl Friday have argued that actual fighting also involved ambushes, night attacks, arson, foot troops, shields, and combined tactics, and that the image of noble duels reflects narrative convention as much as tactical practice. What is clear is that expensive equipment, specialized training, and heavy dependence on horses made effective military service an activity of resource-rich households.

The economics of mounted fighting reinforced the tie between skill and status. A fighter needed leisure to train, income to provide horses and arms, and followers to help maintain them. Households with land, official positions, or patrons could sustain this investment across generations, while ordinary cultivators generally could not. That does not mean warriors were uniformly rich: many were modest local families who scraped together the necessary means. But it explains why military capacity gravitated toward the household rather than the individual, the subject of the next section.

Section 21.4

The Military Household

The central institution of the emerging warrior order was the household, the ie. In the sources, military power appears less as a matter of individual heroes than of households capable of raising and equipping fighters. A leading warrior's household could include his wife and children, brothers and other relatives, senior followers, junior retainers, servants, and dependents of lower status, some of them unfree. It usually controlled a residence, agricultural fields, rights to income from land, horses, and equipment. It also held a place in networks of patronage and marriage. To treat these households as merely private armies would be misleading. They were also economic units, religious communities that worshipped a shared protective deity, and legal actors that could pursue claims at the court.

Historical Vocabulary
Ie no ko and Rōtō (家子・郎党)

Ie no ko ("children of the house") were relatives and senior followers who stood close to the head of a household, often junior branches of the same lineage or trusted dependents. Rōtō (also read rōdō) were lower-ranking retainers, frequently unrelated to the head. A third category, shojū or genin, refers to servants and unfree dependents. The term kenin ("houseman") was used for followers in personal service.

These categories were flexible, and terminology varied by region and period. The formal vassal category of the Kamakura shogunate, the gokenin, belongs to Chapter 24, and the late-Heian vocabulary should not be read as a fully developed feudal structure.

The head of the household, sometimes called a tōryō ("leader," literally the ridgepole of a building) in the tales, directed its military and economic activities. He was normally a man, although women played important roles, as discussed below. Relatives from junior branches were expected to support him and often lived nearby, managing outlying lands and providing men in times of war. Retainers with no kin ties were bound by service, gifts, and expectations of reward. Foster relationships added further links: the family of a child's wet nurse (menoto) could form a lifelong bond with the child's household, supplying trusted followers. Marriage ties connected households to one another and to patrons.

Households of this kind were the practical answer to the demands of mounted warfare. A single man could not maintain horses, armor, and followers, but a group of related and dependent people sharing land and income could. The household also solved problems of continuity. When a warrior died, his knowledge, reputation, followers, and property passed to heirs, who inherited both material assets and the standing their father had built. Such households could endure across generations in a way that individual commissions and temporary levies could not. This durability, rather than any single act of rebellion or conquest, is what made a warrior class possible.

Women were integral to this system, even though the sources say little about them. Wives and daughters could hold and inherit land in this period, and in many households women managed estates and dependents while men were away. Marriage was a means of alliance, and the birth of sons to a warrior's wife by an influential family could connect the household to a powerful line. The evidence for warrior women's roles is stronger for the Kamakura period (Chapters 24 and 25), and students should treat generalizations about the late Heian with care. What can be said is that households were not exclusively male military units, and that women's property and kin ties contributed to their strength.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Rhythms of a Warrior Household

What did life in such a household involve? The evidence is limited, but tales, diaries, and later scrolls suggest a pattern. A large part of the work was agricultural and pastoral: supervising cultivators and dependents, managing fields, breeding and training horses, and collecting income owed from land. Boys of the household were trained in riding and archery from an early age, and hunting served as both food-gathering and preparation for war. Dried rice and other portable foods served on campaign. A coming-of-age ceremony marked a young man's entry into adulthood and often his acquisition of an adult name.

Religious life was woven into the household. Many warriors worshipped Hachiman, a deity associated with protection and, by the late Heian, closely linked with martial families, especially the Minamoto. Killing was a source of anxiety under Buddhist teaching, and tales in collections such as the Konjaku monogatarishū record concern about karmic consequences, as well as sponsorship of temples and rituals to mitigate them. Warriors also cultivated court culture, seeking rank, learning poetry, and aspiring to the refinement of the capital. The household thus sat between two worlds, the provincial world of land and horses and the capital's world of rank and ritual. Ordinary cultivators and servants who sustained it appear rarely in the record, and their experience must be inferred.

Section 21.5

Kinship, Lineage, and Warrior Networks

Households did not stand alone. They were linked by descent, marriage, patronage, neighborhood, and shared experience of service into networks that stretched across provinces. The most famous such networks are the Minamoto (Genji) and Taira (Heishi), but the labels can be deceptive. Minamoto and Taira were surnames (sei) conferred on imperial descendants when the court reduced the number of imperial family members. There were many separate Minamoto lines, named for the emperor from whom they descended, such as the Seiwa Genji, and several Taira lines, including the Kanmu Heishi and the later Ise Heishi. Two men bearing the same surname could be distant, hostile, or unaware of one another. Later stories cast the Genji and Heishi as two rival armies, a narrative largely shaped by the thirteenth-century war tradition and by the Genpei War (Chapter 23). In the eleventh century, no such unified camps existed.

What connected the warriors of a region was a combination of factors. Kinship was reckoned through both male and female lines, and ties through marriage could be as significant as those of descent. A well-known tradition holds that Taira no Naokata, a Kantō figure, gave his daughter in marriage to Minamoto no Yoriyoshi and that this connection helped establish the Minamoto in the Sagami region around Kamakura, though the account comes from later sources and should be treated cautiously. Patronage joined lower-ranking households to higher ones, as when Kantō warriors attached themselves to a Minamoto leader with court standing. Neighborhood and shared enterprise mattered as well. Groups of warriors in the same locality formed bands (tō), such as the "Seven Bands of Musashi" (Musashi shichitō) or, in Kyushu, the group later known as the Matsura band. These groupings, whose names and membership are often known from later tradition, combined kin, neighbors, and allies for defense, mediation, and campaigns.

Networks were not free of tension. Kinsmen fought each other frequently, as Masakado's early conflicts and the Kiyohara division showed. Marriage alliances could be sources of conflict as well as cooperation, and competition among cousins and brothers for offices and land was normal. Genealogies (keizu), which families kept and elaborated, served both to record relationships and to make claims, and later compilers sometimes revised descent to link a family to a prestigious ancestor. Students should read genealogical assertions as evidence of how families wished to be seen. A useful analytical formula for the period is that warrior networks arose from kinship, marriage, patronage, regional connection, and repeated military service, none of which alone was sufficient.

Historical Debate

Where Did the Warriors Come From?

The question: Did the warrior class arise from below, as rural landholders who armed themselves and eventually overthrew the ancient order, or from within the court's own system of military nobles, officials, and guards? The answer shapes how historians understand the Kamakura shogunate: as a revolution or as an evolution of existing structures.

Interpretation A — Provincial origins: Influential mid-twentieth-century scholarship, notably that of Ishimoda Shō, argued that resident local landholders and development lords (zaichi ryōshu) armed themselves to defend and extend their holdings, and that the warriors' rise reflected the emergence of a new social force in opposition to the aristocratic and ritsuryō order. On this reading, the Kamakura shogunate was the political triumph of these provincial lords.
Interpretation B — Continuity-based interpretations (court-institutional and evolutionary): Later research stresses continuity rather than sudden rupture, but its scholars differ. Karl Friday emphasizes court policy and state military institutions: on his account the court channeled provincial elites into professional military service and increasingly relied on them, so that warriors were instruments of the court before they became its rivals. Japanese scholars who developed the idea of "military nobles" likewise stress capital connections and noble descent. William Wayne Farris proposes a long evolutionary development in which regional strongmen adapted over centuries to changing local conditions, emphasizing continuity with the ancient military system without a feudal break.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same limited sources: aristocratic diaries and court records that emphasize the capital, war tales that idealize the fighters, genealogies written later, and scattered provincial documents. Court-centered accounts benefit from the abundance of capital sources but may overstate the capital's importance. Provincial accounts draw attention to local conditions but face a shortage of local documentation.

Why it matters: If warriors were primarily rural rebels, the Kamakura government appears as a break with the past. If they were court-linked specialists, it appears as a transformation from within. Many scholars now favor a synthesis combining court institutions, provincial society, and regional variation, and in which the balance differed from the Kantō to Kyushu.

Student task: Identify one type of evidence from this chapter that supports a provincial-origins interpretation and one that supports a court-centered interpretation. Then explain what additional evidence you would want in order to settle the question for a specific region.

Section 21.6

Lords, Followers, and the Changing Meaning of Service

The chapter purpose speaks of "changing patterns of warrior service," and the phrase captures an important truth: in the late Heian period, warriors served many kinds of patrons in many ways. Governors used them to police provinces and to enforce collection of revenues. Fujiwara regents and other court nobles kept them as guards and enforcers, and the Minamoto leaders Yorimitsu and Yorinobu, for example, are recorded as close to the regent Fujiwara no Michinaga's circle. Estate proprietors, both aristocratic houses and temples, employed warriors as managers and protectors of their holdings. Temples maintained their own armed followers, as discussed in Chapter 20. From the late eleventh century, retired emperors, above all Shirakawa, built up guards, including the men later called the "Northern Face" warriors (Hokumen no bushi) who protected the cloistered court. Powerful military leaders, in turn, collected followers of their own.

Service took various forms. Warriors from provinces served rotating guard duty in Kyoto, served as officers of local government, carried out arrests and police actions, joined campaigns, and managed estates. The rewards were equally varied: provincial offices, court rank, rights to income from land (shiki), gifts, protection in disputes, and the chance to gain a patron's favor. Crucially, these relationships were not yet exclusive. A warrior could serve a governor, a temple, and a noble at the same time, and could change patrons as circumstances shifted. Loyalty was conditional and often transactional, and the sources record many defections.

These relationships differed from the formal vassalage of the Kamakura period, treated in Chapter 24. Under Minamoto no Yoritomo, followers were incorporated into a structured system in which the shogunal lord confirmed their landholding rights and offices in exchange for defined military and other duties. In the late Heian, by contrast, there was no comparable central institution to guarantee reward and no single lord who commanded exclusive allegiance. Bonds of lord and follower existed, and leading Minamoto and Taira houses had loyal followers, but they were personal, multiple, and revocable. Recognizing this difference prevents students from projecting the later system backward.

The Gosannen War of the 1080s shows how reward could be problematic. According to later accounts, when the court refused to treat the conflict as an official campaign and denied Minamoto no Yoshiie the customary rewards, he compensated his followers from his own resources. Whether or not every detail is accurate, the story was retold because it dramatized a new kind of bond in which a leader's personal generosity mattered as much as court sanction. A later court chronicle, the Hyakurenshō, reports a 1091 prohibition on land commendations to Yoshiie, while a contemporary diary, the Gonijō Moromichi-ki, does not record that prohibition but does record a related 1092 suspension of estates Yoshiie had established. Scholars therefore question how literally the 1091 notice should be read, but the measures together suggest that officials were aware of the political dangers of a warrior with so much influence. The court thus both depended on and sought to restrain military leaders.

Section 21.7

Land, Inheritance, and Military Power

Chapter 20 explained the shōen and public-land systems; here the question is how land looked from the warrior's perspective. Land and its income supported horses, equipment, followers, households, and political influence. Warriors gained rights in several ways. Some were descendants of local families with long-standing claims. Some held offices in provincial government, such as district or provincial local officials, that carried income. Some served as managers or stewards of estates for aristocratic or religious proprietors and drew shares of revenue as a result. Others were rewarded by patrons with rights to income from particular parcels. Many combined several sources. Rights were usually described as shiki, shares in the produce and authority over particular lands, and one could hold several at once without owning any land outright in a modern sense.

Because rights overlapped and boundaries were often disputed, conflicts over land readily became military questions. Governors, proprietors, and local holders contested the right to collect taxes and rents, the extent of estate boundaries, and appointment to profitable offices. Courts and governors' offices existed to adjudicate such disputes, but they were slow, susceptible to influence, and reliant on enforcement by others. Households that could field armed followers could defend their claims, extend them, or resist a rival's. This capacity for self-help gave force to legal claims and encouraged families to invest in military strength. The connection ran in both directions: land provided the resources for arms, and arms protected and expanded land.

Inheritance added a further layer of tension. In this period, property was often divided among heirs, including daughters in many cases, and it could pass through multiple lines. Division could fragment holdings and create rivalries among siblings and cousins, while marriage could bring new claims. Disputes over inheritance are a recurring cause of conflict in the sources. Masakado's early quarrels, according to the Shōmonki, involved kinsmen and disputes over local rights. The Gosannen War, discussed below, arose in part from the division of authority among Kiyohara heirs, complicated by stepson and half-brother relationships. A household's survival often depended on choosing heirs, maintaining unity among branches, and finding allies. Households increasingly designated a principal heir to lead the family and coordinate its members, a practice that would develop further in the Kamakura period, when law and custom regulating warrior inheritance became a central feature of the shogunate (see Chapter 25).

Land also shaped the political relationship between warriors and patrons. Estate proprietors needed managers who could defend and collect from their holdings, and warriors needed rights to support their households. Each side had leverage over the other. Governors and proprietors could withdraw appointments, but warriors could withhold cooperation, seize income, or transfer allegiance. The result was a dense network of mutual dependence and conflict. Warriors were not simply landlords or simply soldiers but managers of resources whose control over land, people, and force reinforced each other.

Section 21.8

Regional Warrior Worlds

No single "samurai society" emerged everywhere at once. The Kantō was the great arena of warrior formation. Its plains and river systems supported agriculture and horse breeding, its distance from Kyoto gave local families room to build their own bases, and it drew imperial descendants who settled in provincial posts. Here Masakado and Tadatsune operated, and here the Bandō (eastern) warriors, including families later claimed as descendants of Taira lines and others linked to Minamoto leaders, developed bands, marriages, and rivalries. Sagami, Musashi, Shimōsa, Kazusa, Awa, Hitachi, and neighboring provinces formed a region of overlapping households. Later the Kantō would become the base of Yoritomo's government, but that outcome was not predetermined by the earlier history of these households.

Mutsu and Dewa in the northeast had a different history. Long a frontier of conquest and negotiation between the court and the peoples it called Emishi (Chapter 16), the region by the eleventh century was dominated by local magnates who combined court titles, kin networks, and control of trade and pastures. The Abe family of Mutsu held authority over districts north of the Koromo River. A 1064 Dajōkan document described the deceased Abe no Yoritoki as "chief of the fushū." Because fushū was a category used by the court, it should not be treated as a transparent statement of the Abe family's ancestry or self-identification; their origins remain debated. The Zenkunen War (1051–1062) began when the court sought to assert control, and it pitted Minamoto no Yoriyoshi and his son Yoshiie against the Abe. The long conflict ended after the Kiyohara of Dewa, under Kiyohara no Takenori, intervened on Yoriyoshi's side. The Abe were defeated, the Kiyohara were rewarded, and the region reshaped.

The Gosannen War (1083–1087) grew from divisions within the Kiyohara, whose members disputed leadership and inheritance. Yoshiie, then governor of Mutsu, intervened and, with Fujiwara no Kiyohira, whose mother had links to the Abe and who had been raised within the Kiyohara, brought the Kiyohara line to an end. The court declined to recognize the war as an official campaign; later traditions consequently portrayed Yoshiie as rewarding his followers from his own resources. Kiyohira emerged as the principal power in Mutsu and Dewa and established the Ōshū Fujiwara at Hiraizumi, a regional authority that would last for about a century and that connected northern Honshu to networks of trade extending north toward Hokkaido, where interaction among Satsumon, Okhotsk-derived, and other northern populations contributed to the later formation of Ainu culture and communities, and to the court and continental trade. The northern warriors therefore formed a regional order in their own right, not merely an extension of Kyoto's authority.

Kyushu presented a third pattern. Under the supervision of the Dazaifu, which managed relations with the Korean Peninsula and continental traders, local officials and coastal magnates held land, shipping, and shrine interests. When the Toi attacked in 1019, defense fell to the Dazaifu's officials and local commanders, and coastal bands such as those later known as the Matsura developed on the frontier of the sea. Kyushu's warriors were closely tied to shrines and to overseas trade, and they operated in an environment where Chinese merchants and Korean contacts were part of ordinary life. The Ryukyu Islands lay beyond this world but were beginning to be connected to it by trade, a story taken up in Chapter 32.

The Kinai region around Kyoto and the Inland Sea formed a fourth setting. Here military households often lived close to court and worked as guards, police, and provincial officials. The Seiwa Genji lines of Yorimitsu and his relatives, based in Settsu and elsewhere, were near the capital, while Taira lines gained influence along the Inland Sea and in Ise. The Inland Sea's maritime traffic, and the piracy that sometimes accompanied it, had produced Sumitomo, and would give later Taira leaders a base for shipping and trade. The proximity of these families to the court gave them access to patronage and rank but also entangled them in capital politics.

Section 21.9

Warrior Identity Before Bushidō

Popular images of the samurai often center on a code of loyalty, honor, and self-sacrifice called bushidō, "the way of the warrior." Historians caution that the word and the systematic doctrine it names are products of later centuries. The term appeared in some early-modern writings, but there was no single, widely accepted samurai ethical code. The broad, nationalized ideology now commonly associated with bushidō developed chiefly in the late nineteenth century and became widely popular from the 1890s onward, when writers presented it as the ethical essence of Japanese culture, drawing selectively on earlier warrior traditions. To describe eleventh-century fighters as living by bushidō is to read their world through later ideology. Chapter 26 discusses warrior spirituality and martial traditions in more detail, and later chapters will trace the invention and use of bushidō.

What can be said about warrior values in the late Heian period? The sources point to a cluster of concerns. Reputation was central: the warrior's "name" (na) was his standing among peers and patrons, and the practice of announcing one's name and lineage in battle expressed a desire to be seen and remembered. Courage and skill in arms were admired, along with archery and horsemanship. Family interest mattered enormously, since the household's survival and standing depended on each member's conduct. Service to a patron brought reward and obligation, but loyalty was often conditional, and betrayal is common in the accounts. Violence, including killing prisoners and burning settlements, was treated as part of war, and the tales sometimes portray it with admiration and sometimes with disquiet.

Religion complicated the picture. Warriors worshipped Hachiman and other deities, patronized temples, and performed rituals. Buddhist teachings condemned killing, and some tales describe warriors haunted by fear of retribution or seeking rebirth in a favorable state. Some later became monks. Warriors were also often participants in court culture, valuing poetry, music, and the prestige of rank, and the war tales preserve stories in which fighters compose verse. Historians must remember that many such stories were written down later and served to portray idealized figures. A useful approach is to treat these sources as evidence of how later generations imagined early warriors, and to look for corroboration in contemporary diaries and documents.

The contrast with the ideology of later centuries is instructive. Late-Heian warriors lived in a world without a warrior government and without the concept of a shogunal vassal bound by exclusive loyalty. They operated within the court's hierarchy, aspired to its ranks, and negotiated their loyalties among many patrons. The idea of an unbroken, timeless samurai ethic is a construction that erases this history of change. Understanding early warriors on their own terms, as members of households with practical concerns, religious anxieties, and social aspirations, is more accurate and more interesting than imposing a later code.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Conscript Army to Warrior Households

What Changed

  • Armed service moved from a mass conscript obligation to a specialty of elite, self-equipped mounted fighters
  • Military capacity shifted from state garrisons to households, kin networks, and bands
  • Patrons multiplied: governors, regents, retired emperors, temples, estate proprietors, and military leaders
  • Reputation, lineage, and reward became central to military careers

What Stayed the Same

  • The imperial court remained the source of rank, office, and legitimacy
  • Provincial officials and estates remained the framework within which military service operated
  • Horses, bows, and mounted skill continued from earlier practice, including that of the ancient state
  • Most cultivators and dependents lived under obligations to those above them

Who experienced change most directly: provincial elite families who gained rank and followers, and cultivators in regions of conflict. Who experienced greater continuity: the court aristocracy in the capital, at least until the later twelfth century, and many ordinary villagers who lived their lives under the same routines of cultivation and tax. Why the pattern matters: it shows that the warrior class emerged through accumulation within existing structures, not through a sudden overthrow.

Section 21.10

From Provincial Service to Political Power

By the early twelfth century, leading warrior houses possessed a combination of assets that earlier generations had lacked. They held inherited reputations built on successive campaigns and court services. They commanded trained followers organized in households and bands. They had regional connections in the Kantō, the north, western Japan, and the Inland Sea. They enjoyed patrons at court, including regents and retired emperors, and they held provincial offices that provided income and authority. And they possessed the organizational capacity, in horses, arms, and dependents, to intervene decisively in disputes. These were the ingredients of a political class, even though its members did not yet think of themselves as rulers.

The cloistered government of Shirakawa and his successors strengthened this trend. Retired emperors, exercising power outside the regental system, needed armed men to protect themselves, enforce decisions, and confront temple protests in which monastic communities marched on the capital. They recruited warriors, granted them offices, and encouraged them to compete for favor. Leaders of the Ise Heishi, for example, rose through service to the retired emperors and gained provincial governorships and access to court honors in the decades before the 1150s, a story continued in Chapter 22. Minamoto leaders continued to hold offices and commands, and their Kantō connections remained significant. Such advancement helped warriors gain wealth and connections in the capital, and it also entangled them in the rivalries of court factions.

By the 1150s, the imperial court was divided by competition among a retired sovereign, a reigning emperor, and rival Fujiwara houses, each seeking allies and each aware that warriors could tip the balance. The presence of large numbers of armed men in and near Kyoto, drawn from households loyal in complex ways to different patrons, raised the stakes of every dispute. Court factions could no longer ignore the warrior houses. They needed them, courted them, and feared them. This chapter stops here, on the threshold of the events treated in Chapter 22, where these households move from being instruments of provincial and court service to being decisive participants in the political struggle at the center.

Cause and Effect

The Emergence of Durable Warrior Households

Long-term causes

The decline of the conscript army, the delegation of coercion to governors and local officials, layered land rights that required enforcement, the settlement of imperial descendants in the provinces, and the high cost of mounted warfare.

Short-term causes

Rebellions and frontier wars that demonstrated the value of skilled fighters, court dependence on particular commanders, and patrons such as regents and retired emperors who sought armed followers.

Immediate effects

Successful commanders received offices, rank, and followers; households accumulated land rights and reputations; kin and marriage networks expanded across regions.

Long-term consequences

Warrior houses became indispensable participants in court politics, setting the stage for the events of Chapters 22 through 24, in which military power would generate a warrior government alongside the court.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the outcome. Military skill alone did not create a class, and neither did land, nor kinship, nor court patronage. Their combination, sustained across generations and varying by region, made warrior households durable. Nor was the result inevitable: the court had ways of restraining families, as the 1091–1092 measures suggest, and many households remained modest and local.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how armed service in the Japanese archipelago became organized into durable warrior households and networks capable of becoming a political class. Its central argument is that the warrior class did not appear suddenly as an independent caste opposed to the court. It formed gradually, within the framework of court government, provincial administration, estates, and patronage, over roughly three centuries.

The starting point was the ritsuryō conscript army, which the court largely dismantled in 792, keeping conscription only in frontier regions and reorganizing the kondei, units of local elite sons, as its replacement. The change acknowledged that effective military service depended on skill and resources concentrated in particular families. Governors, police officials, and ad hoc commissioners took on the tasks of suppressing disorder, and the court gradually delegated coercion to those who could raise armed followers.

Provincial conflicts, including the disturbances of Masakado and Sumitomo, the Toi raid, the Tadatsune revolt, and the northern wars, revealed both the danger of private military power and the court's dependence on it. Each episode rewarded successful commanders with offices, rank, and followers, and strengthened families whose reputations then attracted further service. These were not stages in an inevitable march to warrior rule but a repeating cycle that gradually enlarged the standing of military households.

The characteristic skills and equipment of the age, mounted archery, the long bow, large armor, and the horse, required substantial resources and years of training. They therefore favored households with land, income, and dependents over isolated individuals. The military household, composed of kin, retainers, servants, and women who managed property and alliances, became the basic unit of warrior power, and its ability to transmit reputation, followers, and property across generations made it durable.

Households were joined into networks by descent, marriage, patronage, neighborhood, and repeated service. The Minamoto and Taira were not two armies but multiple lines whose members allied and fought among themselves. Warrior bands, foster-nurse ties, and genealogies expressed and shaped these connections. Kinship networks were sources of both cooperation and conflict.

Patterns of service were diverse and changing. Warriors served governors, regents, retired emperors, temples, estate proprietors, and other military leaders in exchange for offices, income, protection, and prestige. These bonds were personal, multiple, and revocable, and they differ from the formal vassalage of the Kamakura shogunate. Land and inheritance disputes, arising from layered rights and divided property, made violence a common tool of ordinary politics and strengthened the incentive to maintain military capacity.

Regional variation was significant. The Kantō's plains and pastures nurtured a lineage-based warrior society. Mutsu and Dewa, where the court met frontier communities and the descendants of the Emishi, produced powerful local families whose wars culminated in the Ōshū Fujiwara regime at Hiraizumi and connections to the far north. Kyushu's warriors lived in a world of coastal defense and continental trade, and the Kinai and Inland Sea regions kept warriors close to court and shipping routes. Ordinary cultivators and dependents bore the costs of war and left few records.

The chapter has also warned against anachronism. The terms "samurai" and "bushidō" belong to later periods, and late-Heian warriors valued reputation, skill, family interest, and reward within a court-centered world, while wrestling with religious anxieties about killing. Historians disagree about whether warriors emerged chiefly from rural developers or from court-linked military nobles, and current scholarship favors syntheses that recognize both.

By the 1150s warrior houses had inherited reputations, trained followers, regional connections, court patrons, and the capacity to intervene in major disputes. Retired emperors, regents, and rival Fujiwara houses courted and relied on them. The chapter ends at this threshold, where the households described here were about to become decisive in the political struggles of Chapter 22.

These developments are foundational for the history that follows. The households, networks, and habits of service traced here supplied the human and institutional material from which the Genpei War and the Kamakura shogunate were built, and the debates over origins continue to shape how historians understand medieval Japan.

Kondei
Small units of local elite sons, existing earlier but reorganized and expanded in 792 to replace the abolished provincial conscript units in most provinces, expected to provide their own mounts and equipment.
Military nobles (gunji kizoku)
A modern scholarly term for middle-ranking court nobles with hereditary reputations for military service, including Seiwa Genji and Kanmu Heishi lines.
Kebiishi
The capital's police and judicial office; an important channel through which military men entered court service.
Tsuibushi
An ad hoc official appointed to capture or suppress rebels and bandits; one of several offices through which the court delegated coercion.
Zuryō
A provincial governor responsible for delivering fixed revenue to the court, who consequently exercised broad local discretion.
Shōen
An estate consisting of land and layered rights to income and administration, often carrying tax or entry privileges (treated in Chapter 20).
Shiki
A right to a share of income and authority attached to land, which could be held in overlapping layers.
Shitō
"Private war"; conflict not sanctioned by the court, such as the Gosannen War as the court viewed it.
Fushū
A court term for communities incorporated after earlier conflict in the northeast; the identity and self-understanding of those so labeled remain debated.
Yumi
The long asymmetrical bow of laminated wood and bamboo, shot from horseback.
Kyūba no michi
"The way of the bow and horse"; a phrase for warrior competence found in tales and later texts, distinct from the later ethical doctrine of bushidō.
Ōyoroi
Large box-shaped armor of small lacquered plates laced with cord, designed for a mounted archer.
Tachi
The curved, single-edged sword slung from the belt, which replaced straight blades over the tenth and eleventh centuries.
Nanori
The announcing of one's name and lineage in battle, which served to establish reputation before witnesses who might later attest to deeds.
Ie
The household: a unit of kin, dependents, land rights, and property that served as the basic institution of warrior power.
Ie no ko
Relatives and senior followers standing close to a household head.
Rōtō
Lower-ranking retainers, frequently unrelated to the household head.
Kenin
A "houseman"; a follower in personal service to a lord.
Tō (band)
A local grouping of warriors based on kinship, neighborhood, and alliance, such as the Musashi seven bands.
Menoto
A foster-nurse; her family formed a lifelong bond with the child's household.
Insei
"Cloistered government"; rule by retired emperors from outside the regental system, which recruited armed retinues.
Hokumen no bushi
"Northern Face" warriors, the guards associated with the cloistered court.
Hachiman
A deity associated with protection and martial families, especially the Minamoto.
Bushidō
The "way of the warrior"; the term occurs in some early-modern writings, but the nationalized ideology known by this name developed chiefly in the late nineteenth century and was widely popular from the 1890s. Not a contemporary concept in the late Heian.
Zenkunen and Gosannen Wars
The eleventh-century wars in Mutsu and Dewa that elevated Minamoto prestige, the Kiyohara, and finally the Ōshū Fujiwara.

MGU Library Research Project

Reconstructing a Late-Heian Warrior Household

This project asks you to build a historically careful portrait of a warrior household or band from the eleventh or early twelfth century and to evaluate the sources on which it depends.

  1. Choose a subject. Select a family or region discussed in this chapter, such as the Abe, the Kiyohara, the Seiwa Genji in the Kantō, the Musashi bands, or Kyushu coastal bands.
  2. Gather sources. Using library resources, identify at least three types of evidence, such as court diaries, war tales, genealogies, translations of documents, and archaeological reports.
  3. Reconstruct the household. Describe its likely members, resources, land rights, patrons, and connections, noting which claims are well supported and which depend on later tradition.
  4. Evaluate perspective. Explain whose viewpoint each source represents and whose is missing.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay or equivalent presentation, present your reconstruction and identify the questions the sources cannot answer.

Library resources to use: Scholarly histories of the Heian and Kamakura periods, translations of war tales and court records, regional archaeological reports, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on medieval Japan.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a social and military order came into being that would shape Japanese history for centuries. The story it tells is not one of a sudden seizure of power but of accumulation: households that acquired skills, followers, land rights, and reputations, and networks that connected them across provinces. In tracing that accumulation, the chapter shows how a state's decisions about defense, policing, and reward can generate a class that later outgrows its creators' intentions.

It also reveals the varied geography of that process. The Kantō, the northern frontier, Kyushu, and the Kinai region each contributed distinct forms of armed service, and the frontier societies of the northeast, with links reaching toward Hokkaido, were actors in their own right. The chapter thus connects different regions of the archipelago and reminds readers that the history of warriors is not the history of Kyoto alone. Connections with the Korean Peninsula and continental trade, visible in Kyushu, likewise show that these households operated within a wider world.

The chapter's consequences unfold in the chapters that follow. Court factions would soon rely on the households described here, the conflict between them would escalate into the Genpei War, and Minamoto no Yoritomo would build from these networks a government of his own. Later ideas of samurai identity, including the modern bushidō ideology, would draw on selective memories of this age. Understanding the actual historical process protects against the idea of a timeless samurai spirit and clarifies how much of what came to be seen as tradition was constructed later.

Students who understand this chapter should be prepared to read the political struggles of the twelfth century as contests among actors with deep roots in provincial society and court service, whose leaders were shaped by centuries of household building, and to approach the Kamakura period as a transformation whose foundations lay in the world described here.
