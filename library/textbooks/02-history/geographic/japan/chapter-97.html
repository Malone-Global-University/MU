The Lost Decades: Stagnation, Precarious Work, and Demographic Change

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the last week of November 1997, Yamaichi Securities, a brokerage founded in 1897 and counted among Japan's four largest, announced that it would close. Its president apologized in tears at a televised press conference, and the image became one of the most widely reproduced of the decade. Within a single month a securities house, a bank in Hokkaido, and a regional bank had failed. For many viewers, the event suggested that the institutions on which postwar prosperity had rested were no longer secure.

The failures of November 1997 were not the beginning of Japan's difficulties, and they were not the end. They came nearly eight years after the Nikkei stock average reached its peak at the end of 1989, a moment examined in Chapter 96, and they preceded a further decade of weak growth, falling prices, and gradual institutional change. This chapter asks how the collapse of an asset bubble became a prolonged economic and social transformation. The answer cannot be found in the banks alone. It involves the way companies hired and dismissed workers, the way young people entered adult life, the way families raised children and cared for the old, and the way the central government, local governments, and the Bank of Japan responded to circumstances for which postwar experience offered few precedents.

Students should begin by separating four things that are often run together. The first is the bursting of the bubble: the fall in share prices from 1990 and in land prices over the following fifteen years. The second is the financial-system crisis: the accumulation of unrecoverable loans, the failures of 1995–1998, and the reconstruction of the banking system that lasted until the mid-2000s. The third is persistent slow growth and deflation: a macroeconomic condition that lasted, with interruptions, across two decades. The fourth is a set of social changes, including the spread of nonregular employment, rising anxiety about overwork, fertility decline, and population aging, which developed during the same years. These were interconnected, but they were not one event, and they did not all have the same cause. Population aging in particular was under way before the bubble burst.

The chapter also resists a simple story of national failure. Japan in 2010 was a wealthy country with a high standard of living, advanced manufacturing, excellent infrastructure, low crime, the world's longest life expectancies, and large holdings of foreign assets. Its economy did not contract continuously for twenty years; it grew in many years, recovered strongly between 2002 and 2007, and was hit hard by an external shock in 2008–2009. The puzzle is therefore not why Japan became poor, because it did not, but why economic security and opportunity became less predictable for many people despite those continuing strengths, and why the assumptions that had organized postwar life, such as steady wage growth, stable careers for men, early marriage, and family care for the elderly, ceased to describe how many people actually lived.

The people affected were not a single "Japanese public." Regular employees of large corporations experienced the 1990s differently from graduates who entered a frozen hiring market, married women who worked part-time, small-business owners dependent on bank credit, factory workers supplied by labor-dispatch agencies, and migrants from Brazil employed in assembly plants. A family caring for a bedridden parent in a depopulating rural town faced circumstances unlike those of a dual-income couple in central Tokyo. Older households that had bought land and saved before 1990 often held substantial assets, while younger cohorts faced a different set of risks. The chapter keeps these differences in view and notes where they intersected with gender, region, age, and legal status. Okinawa, Hokkaido, former industrial cities, and migrant communities appear because their experiences complicate any national average.

The evidence is unusually quantitative, and it needs to be read with care. Financial and labor statistics come from the Bank of Japan, the Ministry of Finance, the Cabinet Office, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the Statistics Bureau, and international bodies such as the OECD, the IMF, and the ILO. Definitions changed across the period: what counted as a bad loan, a nonregular worker, or a freeter differed by survey and by year. Many figures in this chapter are therefore rounded approximations, and where different sources disagree by a point or two, the text says so. Statistics are supplemented by statutes, central-bank decisions, corporate records, newspapers, and the testimony of workers, caregivers, and families; they cannot by themselves show how people experienced a hiring freeze or a layoff.

Historians also disagree about causes. Some stress a balance-sheet crisis in which firms repaid debts rather than invested; others emphasize delayed recognition of bank losses, errors in fiscal and monetary policy, a slowdown in productivity growth, globalization, or demographic change. This chapter presents these interpretations rather than choosing one, but it argues that the prolonged stagnation arose from interacting financial, macroeconomic, and institutional problems and not from a single mistake.

Chapter 97 inaugurates Part XIV. It follows Chapter 96, which explained how the bubble formed and peaked, and it prepares the ground for Chapter 98 (the 1995 Hanshin-Awaji earthquake, Aum Shinrikyō, and public anxiety), Chapter 99 (the 2011 triple disaster and the politics of the 2010s), and Chapter 100 (identity, memory, and Japan's contemporary debates). Those subjects are acknowledged here only as context. The chapter concludes around 2010, when Japan confronted an uncertain economic future with a population that had begun to shrink and a social order whose postwar assumptions were being reconsidered.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the bursting of the asset bubble, the banking crisis, persistent stagnation and deflation, and contemporaneous social change as related but separate processes.
  2. Explain how falling asset prices, nonperforming loans, and corporate deleveraging reduced lending and investment, and how the balance-sheet-recession interpretation differs from other explanations.
  3. Trace the banking crisis from the jūsen affair through the failures of 1997 to the nationalizations of 1998 and the bank restructuring of 2002–2005.
  4. Analyze fiscal and monetary responses, including public works, the 1997 consumption-tax increase, zero interest rates, and quantitative easing, distinguishing intention, outcome, and later scholarly assessment.
  5. Describe the transformation of corporate employment, including the expansion of nonregular work and the legal changes to worker dispatch, using consistent statistical definitions.
  6. Assess the consequences of the employment ice age for young workers, and distinguish freeters, NEETs, and other categories without treating them as uniform or voluntary.
  7. Interpret the history of karōshi and work-related suicide as an issue of labor law, public health, and advocacy, without reducing it to a single cause.
  8. Explain fertility decline, population aging, and family change as long-term demographic processes that predated the bubble's collapse and interacted with economic insecurity.
  9. Evaluate Japan's shift from family-based to partly socialized elder care through the Long-Term Care Insurance system, and the distinct policy goals of the Angel Plans.
  10. Compare the economic experiences of Tokyo, older industrial regions, Hokkaido, rural prefectures, Okinawa, and migrant communities.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of the "Lost Decades" and the usefulness and limits of the phrase.
  12. Evaluate primary sources and statistical series for their creators, definitions, purposes, and limits.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a fall in share and land prices produce a prolonged reduction in lending, investment, and demand, and why did the resulting crisis take so long to resolve?
  2. Why did Japan's financial authorities delay the recognition of bank losses, and what were the economic and political costs of that delay?
  3. How should historians weigh financial, fiscal, monetary, productivity, and demographic explanations for slow growth after 1990?
  4. How could employment protections for regular workers remain influential while insecure employment expanded across society?
  5. Why did the timing of a person's entry into the workforce affect their opportunities for decades afterward?
  6. In what ways did economic insecurity interact with the long-term processes of fertility decline and population aging, and in what ways were those processes independent of it?
  7. How did the state attempt to adapt social insurance, care, and family policy to demographic change, and how did policy intentions differ from services actually available?
  8. Why is "Lost Decades" analytically useful, and whom does the phrase leave out or misrepresent?

Historical Context

By 1990 Japan's economy had been organized for decades around a distinctive set of institutions, described in Chapters 89 through 96. Banks occupied the center of corporate finance. Many large companies had a "main bank" that provided loans, held shares, monitored management, and was expected to assist in a crisis. The Ministry of Finance supervised banks through a so-called convoy system (gosō sendan hōshiki), in which no bank was permitted to fail and stronger institutions were expected to support weaker ones. This arrangement had worked during the high-growth era, when rising land values, rising profits, and tightly regulated finance made losses rare. It left Japan with few legal or administrative tools for closing a large bank in an orderly way, because the problem had seldom arisen.

The postwar social order was likewise built around assumptions that were widely shared though never universally true. Large employers offered regular male employees long-term employment, seniority-based pay, and company benefits (Chapter 91). Women were expected, in most cases, to leave full-time employment on marriage or childbirth and to return, if at all, as part-time workers. Tax and pension rules encouraged this pattern. The family bore the main burden of caring for children and the elderly, and the three-generation household was held up as an ideal even as it became less common. The state offered pensions and health insurance, but expected households to provide most care. These arrangements fitted a society that was young, expanding, and confident of rising incomes.

The demographic foundation was already shifting. The total fertility rate had been falling below the replacement level since the mid-1970s, after the second baby boom ended in 1973. Life expectancy kept rising. The large cohort born in 1947–1949 was moving toward retirement age. Chapter 96 showed that the late-1980s boom concealed these trends behind rising asset prices and optimism. When the bubble burst, the demographic transition was already in progress, and its fiscal and social consequences were about to become more visible, but the bubble did not cause it.

Politically, the early 1990s were also a period of disruption. The Liberal Democratic Party lost its majority in 1993 for the first time since 1955 (Chapter 92), and coalition governments of varying composition followed. Hashimoto Ryūtarō (1996–1998), Obuchi Keizō (1998–2000), Mori Yoshirō (2000–2001), and then Koizumi Jun'ichirō (2001–2006) led governments that confronted economic problems under very different political conditions. This chapter refers to those governments only as needed to explain economic policy. Internationally, the yen's appreciation after 1985, the opening of China's economy, the 1997 Asian financial crisis, and the rise of global supply chains changed the environment in which Japanese firms operated. The chapter uses the term "Heisei" for the period beginning in January 1989; students should remember that period labels do not mark the boundaries of economic processes.

Section 97.1 · Division I — The Collapse of the Bubble and the Banking Crisis

After the Bubble: Falling Share Prices, Land Values, and the End of Speculative Confidence

The Nikkei 225 closed the last trading day of 1989 at roughly 38,900. In 1990 it fell by about a third over the year and, at its lowest point that autumn, was more than half below the peak. The decline was not smooth, and share prices rallied at times in the following years, but they never approached the 1989 level during the period covered by this chapter. In April 2003 the index fell to around 7,600, and after a recovery it fell again to about 7,000 in March 2009. The fall in equities was fast and visible, and it was the first signal that the speculative confidence of the late 1980s was ending.

Land prices moved more slowly and mattered more. The Bank of Japan had raised its discount rate from 2.5 percent in 1989 to 6 percent by August 1990, and in 1990–1991 the Ministry of Finance restricted the growth of bank lending to the real-estate sector. Commercial land prices in the largest cities peaked around 1990–1991 and then fell, with only brief interruptions, for approximately fifteen years. Nationally, average urban land prices declined in most years from the early 1990s to the mid-2000s. Commercial land in the six largest cities lost on the order of four-fifths of its peak value; residential land fell less but still substantially. Students should compare these two timelines: the stock market's decline was concentrated in 1990 and the early 1990s, whereas land values declined gradually across a decade and a half, steadily eroding balance sheets that had been built on the assumption that land would not fall.

That assumption was embedded in the structure of Japanese finance. Banks lent heavily against land, and the value of pledged land determined how much a borrower could obtain. Property developers, construction firms, retailers, and small businesses had borrowed during the late 1980s to buy land and buildings or to finance expansion. Large companies had raised funds in securities markets and used the proceeds, in some cases, for financial speculation (zaitech). Banks themselves held large portfolios of corporate shares, as part of long-standing cross-shareholding arrangements, and under the capital-adequacy rules then in force a part of the unrealized gains on those shares counted toward regulatory capital. When share prices fell, banks' capital shrank at the same time as the value of their loans' collateral. Falling asset prices therefore struck banks from two sides.

The consequences were not confined to speculators. Developers and construction firms that could not sell property or find tenants could not repay loans. Households that had purchased homes at inflated prices, often with long-term mortgages, found their homes worth less than their debts. Smaller firms whose credit depended on land collateral found banks less willing to lend. Official business-cycle dating places the cyclical peak in early 1991 and the trough in late 1993, and the slowdown was labeled the "Heisei recession." Many officials and business leaders at first regarded the fall as a correction that would soon end, and expected that asset prices would recover. That expectation shaped policy and corporate behavior in the early 1990s: banks extended credit to troubled borrowers in the hope that conditions would improve, and regulators allowed them to do so.

The collapse also changed perceptions of risk. Before 1990, many managers and households had assumed that land prices rose over time and that firms of a certain size would not fail. Afterward, investment decisions became more cautious, construction activity declined, and banks became wary of real-estate lending. This shift in expectations, as much as any particular loss, was the legacy of the first years after the peak. Chapter 96 explained how the bubble formed; the next section examines how its collapse left the corporate and banking sectors with debts that could not easily be repaid.

Section 97.2 · Division I

Nonperforming Loans, Corporate Debt, and the Balance-Sheet Recession

A firm that borrows to buy land at a high price still owes the loan when the land loses value. Debts do not shrink when assets do. After 1990 this simple fact produced a large and growing stock of nonperforming loans (furyō saiken): loans to borrowers who could not service them, or whose collateral was worth less than the loan. Banks faced a choice between recognizing the losses, which would reduce their capital and might force them to curtail lending or seek public help, and continuing to treat the loans as sound by extending or restructuring them. Many chose the second course. Lending to troubled borrowers so that they could keep paying interest, sometimes called "evergreening," kept reported bad-loan figures low while the underlying problem grew. Official disclosure standards in the early 1990s were narrow, and the figures that became public in the middle of the decade were far larger than early estimates. By the time the cleanup ended in the mid-2000s, cumulative disposals of bad loans by the banking system have been estimated at roughly ninety to one hundred trillion yen, a sum on the order of a fifth of a year's national output, though estimates depend on definitions.

The relationships among borrowers and lenders were close. Construction companies, property developers, and wholesale and retail firms had borrowed from banks that were also their shareholders and trading partners. Large industrial companies were less affected by real-estate losses but depended on the same banking system. Small and medium-sized firms, which employed the majority of Japan's workers, relied on bank credit secured by land owned by the firm or its owner. When banks reduced lending to protect their capital, these firms felt it first. Observers sometimes spoke of a credit crunch, though scholars have disagreed about how severe it was and about whether weak lending resulted mainly from banks' reluctance to lend or from firms' reluctance to borrow.

Firms' behavior offers a clue. Interest rates fell steadily during the 1990s, and borrowing became cheap. Yet many corporations did not borrow more to invest. Instead, from the late 1990s Japan's corporate sector as a whole became a net saver, a striking reversal of the pattern of the high-growth era, in which firms had borrowed heavily from households through the banking system. Firms used cash flow to repay debt, reduce excess capacity, and rebuild balance sheets. The economist Richard Koo described this as a balance-sheet recession: when asset prices collapse and leave liabilities intact, private-sector borrowers prioritize debt repayment even at zero interest rates, and the economy suffers from deficient demand because no one is willing to borrow and spend the savings that others deposit. On this view, government borrowing and spending had a legitimate role in absorbing private-sector savings.

Koo's interpretation is influential but not universal. Other economists have emphasized the failure of banks and regulators to recognize losses promptly, which allowed "zombie" borrowers to survive and crowded out healthier firms. Some have argued that a slowdown in productivity growth, rather than financial distress, was the principal cause of weak growth. Still others have pointed to monetary policy that failed to prevent deflation, or to demographic change. The more useful lesson for students is that these explanations are not mutually exclusive. A financial shock can become a prolonged downturn when balance sheets are impaired, when policymakers delay repair, and when deflation raises the real burden of debt, and the relative weight of each factor remains debated. The Historical Debate in Section 97.5 returns to the question.

Historical Vocabulary
Nonperforming loans, balance-sheet recession, and deleveraging

Nonperforming loans (furyō saiken) are loans for which repayment is seriously doubtful. In Japan the category was defined and redefined during the 1990s: early disclosures counted only loans to bankrupt borrowers or with long-overdue interest, while later standards, especially those introduced from 1998–1999, classified loans more strictly by the borrower's condition and collateral. A rising "NPL ratio" therefore reflected both worsening conditions and stricter measurement, and students should not treat figures from different years as strictly comparable.

A balance-sheet recession, as Richard Koo uses the term, is a downturn in which firms with damaged balance sheets repay debt rather than borrow, so that low interest rates fail to stimulate investment. Deleveraging is the reduction of debt relative to assets or income. It is prudent for an individual firm, but if many firms do it at once, total spending falls. These concepts help explain why Japan's recovery from the early 1990s was slow, and they are one interpretation among several rather than settled fact.

Section 97.3 · Division I

The Banking Crisis of 1995–1998: Jūsen, Hokkaido Takushoku, and Yamaichi Securities

For several years the problem of bad loans remained hidden or contained. The first failures involved smaller institutions: credit associations in 1994 and, in 1995, Cosmo Credit Cooperative in Tokyo, Kizu Credit Association in Osaka, and Hyōgo Bank, the first failure of a commercial bank since the war. The crisis acquired a political edge through the housing-loan companies, or jūsen. These seven nonbank lenders had been founded in the 1970s by banks and other financial institutions to provide mortgages. Squeezed by competition from banks, they turned to lending for real estate in the 1980s, with funding from their parent banks and from agricultural cooperatives, and they suffered catastrophic losses when land prices fell. Official estimates put the unrecoverable portion of their loans at over six trillion yen. The government's plan, in the 1996 budget, to apply roughly 685 billion yen of public funds to the losses provoked a storm of protest. Opposition members staged a sit-in in the Diet, and the public asked why taxpayers should cover losses incurred by lenders and borrowers during the speculative boom. The episode taught politicians that bank rescues were politically dangerous, a lesson that made them reluctant to act decisively in the following years.

Meanwhile, confidence in Japanese financial institutions was eroding. In 1996 the government announced that it would guarantee all deposits until 2001, a blanket protection later extended in stages (time deposits were phased out of full protection in 2002 and demand deposits in 2005). The government also announced, in November 1996, the "Big Bang," a program of financial deregulation to be implemented from 1998. Policymakers were attempting to liberalize and protect the financial sector at once. The Ministry of Finance, which supervised the banks, also faced criticism for the cozy relations with financial firms exposed in scandals in 1998.

The crisis became systemic in November 1997. On 3 November Sanyō Securities, a middle-sized brokerage, applied for court protection from creditors and defaulted on loans in the interbank call market, the first such default in the market's history. Banks, uncertain which counterparties were safe, restricted lending to one another, and Japanese banks were charged a "Japan premium" for funds in international markets. On 17 November Hokkaido Takushoku Bank, one of the large city banks and the main bank for many Hokkaido businesses, announced that it could not continue; its sound operations were transferred to other institutions, and the Bank of Japan supplied liquidity during the transition. On 24 November Yamaichi Securities announced that it would close, in part because it had concealed large off-balance-sheet losses; the Bank of Japan extended emergency loans to allow an orderly wind-down. Tokuyō City Bank, a regional bank based in Sendai, Miyagi Prefecture, failed at the end of the month; the Bank of Japan announced arrangements on 26 November for its operations to be transferred to Sendai Bank and portions of its assets and deposits to other institutions, including 77 Bank. The failure of a major securities firm and a city bank in the same fortnight was unprecedented.

The effects extended beyond the institutions concerned. In Hokkaido, the loss of Takushoku, which had been a major lender to regional enterprises and to the prefecture's development projects, constrained credit for local businesses and contributed to the region's economic troubles (Section 97.17). Depositors withdrew funds from weaker institutions, and the Bank of Japan, the government, and the Deposit Insurance Corporation faced pressure to prevent contagion. The Asian financial crisis, which began with the collapse of the Thai baht in July 1997 and spread to Indonesia and Korea by the end of the year, was an important international context: it reduced demand in Japan's export markets, endangered Japanese banks' loans in the region, and heightened global anxiety. But the Japanese crisis had its own domestic roots in unresolved bad debts, and it should not be treated as a consequence of the Asian crisis.

Section 97.4 · Division I

Bank Rescues, Public Capital, Nationalization, and Financial-System Reconstruction

The government's response developed in stages over about eight years. In June 1998 the Financial Supervisory Agency began operations, taking supervision of banks out of the Ministry of Finance; it was reorganized as the Financial Services Agency in 2000. In February 1998 the Diet created a framework for depositor protection and public capital injections and, in October, after the first framework proved inadequate, enacted more comprehensive laws expanding the available funds to a total of about sixty trillion yen. In March 1998, the government injected about 1.8 trillion yen into twenty-one major banks, a uniform and widely criticized measure that did little to distinguish strong banks from weak ones. In March 1999, a second round of about 7.5 trillion yen went to fifteen banks.

Two large long-term credit banks, which had financed heavy industry in the high-growth era and later turned to real-estate lending, became the symbols of failure. The Long-Term Credit Bank of Japan (LTCB) was in effect insolvent by the summer of 1998 after a rescue merger was blocked, and in October 1998 it was placed under temporary public control (a form of nationalization) under the new Financial Reconstruction Law. The Nippon Credit Bank followed in December. Both were later sold, in 2000, to private groups, LTCB to a consortium led by the American firm Ripplewood and NCB to a Japanese consortium; the banks reemerged as Shinsei Bank and Aozora Bank. The sale of a major Japanese bank to foreign investors was itself controversial. In April 1999 the Resolution and Collection Corporation was formed to purchase and collect bad loans from failed institutions.

Reconstruction also required changes in how banks measured their assets. A stricter inspection manual issued in 1999 required banks to classify loans more rigorously, and the supervisory agency conducted special inspections of large banks. The banking industry consolidated: the thirteen city banks of 1990 merged into a small number of mega-groups, which by the mid-2000s were Mizuho, Sumitomo Mitsui, and Mitsubishi UFJ, with Resona as a smaller fourth group. The decisive push came in October 2002 with the Program for Financial Revival, led by Takenaka Heizō under Prime Minister Koizumi. The program set a goal of halving the major banks' nonperforming-loan ratio, then about 8 percent, within roughly two and a half years, tightened the treatment of deferred tax assets in bank capital, and signaled that the government would no longer assume that big banks could not fail. The ratio fell to under 3 percent by 2005, and the major banks returned to profit.

Students should distinguish the forms of intervention. Temporary nationalization (LTCB and NCB in 1998) placed a failed institution under government control to protect depositors and manage its sale. A public capital injection strengthened a bank's capital without removing its management, as in the 1998 and 1999 rounds. In 2003 Resona Bank, a merger of Daiwa and Asahi banks, received about two trillion yen in public capital after its auditors limited the capital value of its deferred tax assets, which pushed it below regulatory requirements; the injection was followed by changes in management. Ashikaga Bank, a large regional bank in Tochigi, was declared insolvent in November 2003 and placed under temporary public management, which is nationalization of a regional institution. The net fiscal cost of these rescues has been estimated in differing ways, and much of the public capital was eventually recovered, although the costs of delay in output and employment are harder to measure.

The historical question is why the process took so long. Several explanations are offered. Authorities and bankers expected asset prices to recover, and assumed that forbearance would give time. The convoy system and the political legacy of the jūsen made decisive action costly. The Ministry of Finance was both regulator and promoter of the industry, and it lacked legal tools and staff for resolution. Disclosure and accounting rules permitted delay. And some officials feared that recognizing losses would cause the very collapse they wished to avoid. The costs were substantial: banks that spent a decade managing bad loans were less able to lend; borrowers kept alive by evergreened loans may have held back more productive competitors; deflationary pressure continued; the budget deficit grew; and public distrust of both banks and government deepened. Contemporary officials disagreed, even in the 2000s, about how quickly losses should be recognized and who should bear them, and historians continue to debate whether faster action would have shortened the stagnation.

Section 97.5 · Division II — Stagnation, Deflation, and Economic Reform

The Meaning of Stagnation: Slow Growth, Deflation, and Weakened Economic Expectations

The phrase "Lost Decade" was applied, first by foreign commentators and then by Japanese writers, to the 1990s. As the stagnation continued into the 2000s, it became "Lost Decades" or the "lost twenty years" (ushinawareta 20-nen). The phrase compresses several distinct conditions. Weak real growth means that the volume of output increased slowly: real GDP grew by roughly 1.5 percent a year on average in the 1990s, compared with about 4 percent in the 1980s, and by about 1 percent a year in the 2000s. Stagnant nominal output means that the money value of GDP barely changed: nominal GDP was about the same in 2010 as in the mid-1990s, below its 1997 level. Deflation means falling prices: the GDP deflator began falling in the mid-1990s, and consumer prices declined mildly (generally by less than 1 percent a year) from the late 1990s, with some interruptions. Falling asset prices and weak investment are additional conditions, and declining expectations of future income, the sense that wages and careers would not rise as they once had, were perhaps the most consequential for households.

Students should avoid two errors. The first is to assume that the economy contracted continuously. Real GDP grew in most years, with recessions in 1992–1993, 1997–1999, 2001–2002, and 2008–2009; growth reached about 3 percent in 1996, and the expansion from 2002 to 2008 was by official dating the longest of the postwar period. The second error is to treat stagnation as impoverishment. Japan remained a rich country, and in per-capita terms its performance was weaker than in the 1980s but not catastrophic. Some analysts note that growth measured per working-age person compared reasonably with that of other advanced economies in the 2000s, because Japan's working-age population was shrinking while those of the United States and others were growing. Whether that observation excuses or merely redescribes the problem depends on the question being asked.

Lower prices could coexist with financial distress because of the way debts work. When prices fall generally, the real burden of fixed nominal debts rises, a mechanism economists have called debt deflation. Firms with falling revenues and unchanged loan payments were more likely to cut costs and investment. Falling wages or bonuses in many companies, particularly after the late 1990s, reduced households' incomes and their willingness to spend, and the high saving rates of earlier decades fell more slowly than income. Cash earnings per employee peaked in the late 1990s and by 2010 were roughly a tenth lower. The experience of falling prices also changed expectations: consumers could wait for lower prices, firms were reluctant to raise them, and the central bank found that expectations of inflation had disappeared. Section 97.7 returns to that problem.

Interpretations differ on why the weakness lasted so long. One emphasizes delayed financial restructuring, so that banks and borrowers were kept in zombie-like condition. A second stresses weak domestic demand and the insufficiency of fiscal and monetary stimulus. A third stresses a slowdown in productivity growth, linked to resources locked in unproductive firms, to regulatory barriers in services, and to slower technological catch-up. A fourth stresses demographic change: a shrinking and aging workforce, reduced household saving, and pessimism about future public finances. A fifth stresses globalization, including competition from China and the shift of production abroad. Each has empirical support and each has critics.

Historical Debate

Was Prolonged Stagnation Primarily a Financial Crisis, a Policy Failure, or a Structural Transformation?

The question: Why did the aftermath of the bubble last so long? Economists and historians have offered explanations that give different weights to finance, macroeconomic policy, and structural change.

Interpretation A — A financial and balance-sheet crisis: Scholars in this group, including Richard Koo and those who emphasize delayed bank restructuring, argue that the collapse of asset values left firms and banks with damaged balance sheets, and that private-sector deleveraging and zombie lending suppressed investment and growth until losses were recognized and capital restored in the early 2000s.
Interpretation B — Policy failure: Others argue that fiscal and monetary authorities responded too late or inconsistently. They point to the tightening of 1989–1990, the premature consolidation of 1997, the slow move to zero rates, and the failure to commit to credible inflation, and conclude that a more aggressive and sustained response might have prevented deflation. Critics reply that policymakers acted under real constraints, including uncertainty about the scale of losses and the political limits on public spending.
Interpretation C — Structural change: A third group emphasizes slower productivity growth, demographic change, and globalization, arguing that Japan's potential growth rate had fallen for reasons that financial repair and stimulus could not reverse. Critics respond that structural explanations fit the 2000s better than the sharp downturns of the 1990s.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: bank-loan data, firm-level balance-sheet studies, flow-of-funds accounts showing the corporate sector becoming a net saver, estimates of productivity, demographic projections, and comparisons of policy episodes in Japan and elsewhere. Most scholars now treat the explanations as complementary and disagree about emphasis and timing.

Why it matters: The answer determines what lessons are drawn for later crises, how responsibility is assigned for the human costs of stagnation, and whether the period is understood as a failure of institutions, of policy, or of an economic model that had reached its limits.

Section 97.6 · Division II

Fiscal Stimulus, Public Works, Government Debt, and the 1997 Tax Increase

The government's first response to the slowdown was fiscal. Between 1992 and 1998 successive cabinets announced packages of public investment, tax cuts, and credit measures with headline values that, in the aggregate, exceeded one hundred trillion yen over the decade. Students should be cautious with these numbers. A package's headline total often included loans, previously planned spending, and items such as land purchases by public bodies, and the "real water" (mamizu), meaning additional spending actually injected into the economy, was much smaller. Even so, Japan's public investment was unusually high as a share of national output by the standards of other advanced economies, and it was a major component of the stimulus.

Public works had a distinct political economy. Construction was a large industry: employment in construction rose to roughly 6.8 million by the late 1990s, and in many rural districts public works were the principal source of local employment. Contractors, local governments, and Liberal Democratic Party politicians were linked by networks of subsidies, bid-rigging, and election support. Scandals in 1993, including the arrest of the party kingmaker Kanemaru Shin and bribery cases involving general contractors and local officials, revealed these relationships. Public spending was therefore not simply a macroeconomic tool: it sustained communities and political constituencies, which made it difficult to cut and also meant that its allocation was often driven by local politics rather than by returns to investment. Critics argued that bridges and roads built in thinly populated areas were wasteful; defenders pointed to regional needs and to the employment effects. Prime Minister Koizumi reduced public investment sharply after 2001, which contributed to regional resentment.

Fiscal consolidation entered the picture under Prime Minister Hashimoto. Persuaded that the deficit had become dangerous, his government passed a fiscal structural reform law in late 1997 and carried out a set of measures in 1997 that raised the burden on households: the consumption tax rose from 3 to 5 percent on 1 April 1997, a temporary income-tax cut was ended, and employees' medical copayments were raised in September. The measures together increased household burdens by several trillion yen. Consumption fell after the tax increase, which had been preceded by a rush of purchases in the first quarter. The timing was unfortunate: the Asian financial crisis began in July, and the banking failures of November followed. Output fell in the second half of 1997 and through 1998, and the unemployment rate rose, reaching a postwar high at the time.

Historians and economists disagree about how much of the 1997–1998 downturn to attribute to the tax increase. One view holds that the fiscal tightening, by sharply lowering demand, turned a fragile recovery into a recession and that the policy was a serious error. Another points out that the recession deepened after the financial failures of November 1997 and the Asian crisis, and holds that the banking crisis was the principal cause. International analysts at the time, including the IMF, treated the combination of fiscal tightening, domestic financial failures, and regional instability as the relevant explanation. Hashimoto's government reversed course in 1998, and under Obuchi Keizō large stimulus packages followed, including a package of nearly 24 trillion yen in late 1998. The sequence illustrates the difficulty of distinguishing policy errors from the shocks to which policy was responding.

The consequence was a rapid rise in government borrowing. Gross general-government debt, which stood at roughly two-thirds of national output in the early 1990s, rose to about 140 percent by 2000 and above 200 percent by 2010, the highest in the advanced world on this measure, though the government's net debt was lower because it also held large financial assets. Rating agencies downgraded Japanese sovereign debt between 1998 and 2002. Yet long-term interest rates stayed very low, because most government bonds were held by domestic institutions and households whose savings flowed into them through banks, insurers, and the postal savings system. The debate over fiscal policy therefore has several sides: whether stimulus was inadequate, inefficient, poorly timed, or necessary to avoid a deeper depression, and whether rising debt was a danger or a feature of a savings-rich economy with weak private demand.

Section 97.7 · Division II

The Bank of Japan, Zero Interest Rates, Quantitative Easing, and the Deflation Problem

Monetary policy moved by stages toward unconventional measures. After raising rates in 1989–1990, the Bank of Japan lowered its discount rate from 6 percent in 1991 to 0.5 percent in September 1995, then the lowest level on record in any major economy. A new Bank of Japan Law, in force from April 1998, gave the bank formal independence from the government. Under Governor Hayami Masaru the bank adopted the zero-interest-rate policy on 12 February 1999, encouraging the overnight call rate to fall as low as possible, to nearly zero. In August 2000, believing that the economy was recovering, the bank ended the policy and raised the rate to 0.25 percent, over the objection of the government representatives attending its board meeting. The recovery faded with the global slowdown in information technology in 2001, and the decision became widely regarded as premature.

On 19 March 2001, the bank introduced quantitative easing. It changed its operating target from the call rate to the balance of current accounts that banks held at the Bank of Japan, initially about five trillion yen and later raised in steps to thirty to thirty-five trillion yen by early 2004, and it increased its purchases of long-term government bonds. It also committed to maintain the new framework until consumer prices registered stable year-on-year increases of zero percent or more. Later measures included purchases of shares held by banks and of asset-backed securities. The program ended on 9 March 2006, and the zero-rate policy ended in July 2006 with a rise to 0.25 percent; a further increase to 0.5 percent followed in February 2007.

The problem policymakers faced was unusual. When the policy interest rate cannot go below zero, central banks lose their ordinary tool for stimulating demand. Firms and households that are repaying debt or are pessimistic about future income may not borrow, even if borrowing is nearly free. Deflation makes things worse: the real interest rate, which is the nominal rate minus expected inflation, remains positive or even rises when prices fall, so that a zero nominal rate may not be low in real terms. Students should therefore distinguish several concepts: the policy interest rate, which the bank sets; bank reserves, the funds that banks hold at the central bank; central-bank asset purchases, by which the bank increases those reserves and may lower yields on the assets it buys; inflation expectations, which determine how people respond to prices; and the real interest rate.

What policymakers intended, what occurred, and what scholars later concluded are distinct questions. The bank intended to stabilize financial markets, ease liquidity pressures, and convince the public that interest rates would remain low until deflation ended. Its supporters argue that the measures prevented a worse financial crisis and reduced anxiety about liquidity. Critics, including prominent foreign economists such as Paul Krugman and Ben Bernanke, argued before the 2001 decision that the bank should have used stronger commitments to raise inflation expectations. Subsequent research generally finds that quantitative easing had a stabilizing effect on the financial system and on long-term interest rates, but little demonstrable effect on bank lending or on inflation, in part because banks and firms were still repairing balance sheets. Consumer prices stopped falling for a time in 2004 and 2005 but turned down again after 2008. Another context was the yen: it rose to about 80 to the dollar in April 1995 and, after weakening in the early 2000s, rose again during the global crisis to levels near 80 or lower, which pressed on exporters and on prices.

Section 97.8 · Division II

Corporate Restructuring, Koizumi Reform, and the Uneven Recovery of the 2000s

While the banking system was being repaired, Japanese corporations reorganized themselves. A 1999 government council on industrial competitiveness summarized the problem as three "excesses": of employment, debt, and capacity. Legislation facilitated change: the ban on pure holding companies was lifted in 1997, enabling groups to reorganize; the Industrial Revitalization Law of 1999 encouraged restructuring; and in 2003 the government created the Industrial Revitalization Corporation of Japan to assist viable but overindebted firms. Mergers consolidated steel, chemicals, electrical machinery, and finance. Cross-shareholdings between banks and industrial firms were gradually unwound, partly because banks needed to reduce exposure to stock-price risk, and the role of the main bank diminished as firms turned to capital markets or built up retained earnings. Foreign ownership of Japanese shares rose. Some changes were emblematic: Nissan, deeply in debt, accepted a capital alliance with Renault in 1999 and a foreign chief executive who closed plants and reduced its supplier network.

Production networks also changed. Japanese firms expanded production in China and Southeast Asia, where costs were lower, and China's entry into the World Trade Organization in 2001 accelerated this shift. Many manufacturers kept high-value work and parts supply in Japan while moving assembly abroad. The recovery of 2002–2007 depended heavily on exports to the United States and China, as well as on a weak yen. Exports rose, corporate profits reached record levels, and the stock market recovered, though not to its 1989 level. Official business-cycle dating places the expansion from early 2002 to early 2008, which at about six years was the longest of the postwar period, though the average pace of growth was modest.

Prime Minister Koizumi Jun'ichirō, in office from April 2001, made "structural reform without sanctuaries" his slogan. His government pursued banking reform through the Program for Financial Revival, reduced public works spending and the issue of government bonds, raised medical copayments for employees to 30 percent in 2003, and cut transfers to local governments as part of a "trinity reform" of taxes, subsidies, and local allocations. Its most famous project was the privatization of the postal services, which combined the world's largest savings institution with a national delivery network and had been a pillar of the political machinery of the Liberal Democratic Party. After the upper house defeated the bill in 2005, Koizumi dissolved the lower house and won a large majority; privatization began in 2007. Koizumi's reforms were popular with many urban voters and criticized in rural areas that depended on public spending. The politics of his government belong to the general political history of Part XIV; here the relevant point is that reform changed the institutional setting of banks, firms, and local governments.

The recovery's most important feature for this chapter is its distribution. Corporate profits and the balance sheets of banks improved far more than household incomes. Firms kept labor costs down by restraining wages and expanding nonregular employment, and the share of national income going to employee compensation fell. Average wages stagnated or declined, and real household consumption grew slowly. In the 2000s, public discussion turned to "disparity society" (kakusa shakai), the sense that inequalities between regular and nonregular workers, between generations, and between Tokyo and the regions were widening, though economists disagreed about how far inequality had increased, since part of the apparent change reflected population aging. The recovery of the 2000s showed that the financial system could be repaired and that firms could be profitable without much wage growth. How that profitability was achieved is the subject of Division III.

Cause and Effect

From Bubble Collapse to Household Insecurity

Long-term causes

A bank-centered financial system with weak resolution tools; the convoy system; land-collateral lending; cross-shareholdings; a corporate employment system built for sustained growth; a demographic transition already under way.

Short-term causes

The bubble of the late 1980s; monetary tightening and lending restrictions in 1989–1991; the fall in share and land prices; forbearance in recognizing losses; the 1997 tax increase and Asian crisis.

Event or transformation

Bad debts and deleveraging reduce lending and investment; the financial system fails in 1997–1998; deflation and slow growth persist.

Immediate effects

Reduced credit for small firms; stricter cost control; hiring freezes; wage restraint; growing public debt; interest rates at zero.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

Firms protected core regular employees while reducing hiring and expanding nonregular work; younger workers and women bore a disproportionate share of insecurity; household saving, marriage, and childbearing decisions were affected; public debt and social-insurance costs rose. No single link in this chain explains the outcome. Demographic change, globalization, and technology also shaped employment and family life, and the same financial collapse had different effects on a retiree with assets, a graduate without a job, and a small-business owner with a bank loan.
Section 97.9 · Division III — Precarious Employment and the Changing Workplace

The Salaryman Order under Pressure: Corporate Loyalty, Downsizing, and Employment Security

Chapter 91 described the employment system of the high-growth era: a large company hired graduates together each spring, trained them internally, paid them by age and tenure, and expected them to remain until retirement, in exchange for loyalty and long hours. This "salaryman order" was never universal. It applied mainly to male regular employees of large firms and government, perhaps a third of the workforce at its height. Women were mostly excluded from career tracks, employees of small firms and of subcontractors enjoyed less security, and part-time workers, predominantly married women, were outside the system. The ideal of lifetime employment was powerful partly because it described an aspiration, a standard of respectability, rather than the experience of most workers.

After 1990 firms faced strong pressure to reduce labor costs. Debt had to be repaid, profits had fallen, global competitors had lower costs, and the yen was strong. Yet dismissing regular employees remained difficult, both because of social norms and because Japanese courts, applying the doctrine against "abuse of the right of dismissal," set a high bar for economic layoffs. Firms therefore adjusted in ways that preserved the formal principle of long-term employment. They reduced overtime and bonuses, transferred employees to subsidiaries and affiliates, offered early-retirement programs, and, above all, cut the hiring of new graduates. In the late 1990s a wave of corporate "restructuring" (risutora) became a common word for dismissal in all but name, and in some firms middle-aged managers were pressed to leave. Average employment tenure for core male workers changed relatively little in many studies, which shows that the system continued to function for those inside it.

The system was also modified. In 1995 Nikkeiren, the employers' federation, published a report proposing that firms divide their workforce into three groups: a core of long-term employees whose skills would be built within the firm, a group of specialists employed on contract for particular expertise, and a flexible group employed as needed. Whether or not firms adopted this model explicitly, the pattern it described spread. Companies also introduced performance-based pay: Fujitsu's 1993 system became a widely discussed example, and during the following decade many firms reduced the weight of seniority in wages and bonuses and introduced annual-salary and target-setting systems. In practice, these changes often meant that employees' pay was more variable and their workloads heavier. They also meant that the promise of automatic promotion with age no longer held, especially in the late 1990s and 2000s, when many firms had large numbers of middle-aged employees and few managerial posts.

The governing question for this division is how employment protections could remain influential while insecurity increased across society. The answer is that the burden of adjustment shifted. Regular employees were protected relative to others, but the number of entry-level regular positions fell, and an increasing share of work was assigned to nonregular employees whom firms could hire and release more easily. The system thus preserved its core while narrowing its coverage. Women faced their own version of this. After the Equal Employment Opportunity Law of 1985, a small number of women entered career-track positions, but many firms assigned most women to a clerical "general" track and, after the 1990s, replaced many of those positions with temporary staff. For the broad range of workers outside the core, including those in small firms that depended on large firms' orders and were pressed to cut prices, insecurity had always been normal. The 1990s extended it into groups that had expected something else.

Section 97.10 · Division III

The Expansion of Nonregular Employment: Part-Time, Temporary, Contract, and Dispatched Work

The share of employees in nonregular employment rose from about 20 percent in 1990 to about 34 percent in 2010 according to OECD estimates drawn from the Japanese Labour Force Survey, an increase of roughly fourteen percentage points. The category requires careful definition. Part-time workers (pāto or arubaito) are, in Japanese usage, defined partly by title, so that many "pāto" worked hours close to full-time. Fixed-term contract workers (keiyaku shain) are directly employed for a defined period. Dispatched workers (haken) are employed by a staffing agency and sent to work under a client firm's direction. Contract-for-service (ukeoi) workers are supplied by a contractor responsible for the work. Other categories include rehired retirees and "entrusted" workers. The broad nonregular category is much larger than the dispatched category, which accounted for only a small share of all employees even at its peak, though it received disproportionate public attention.

Legislation shaped the growth of dispatch work. The Worker Dispatch Law was enacted in 1985 and took effect in 1986, legalizing dispatch for a list of specialized occupations. A major revision in 1999 changed the principle: dispatch became permitted in most occupations unless specifically prohibited, and the prohibited fields included port work, construction, security, medical work, and manufacturing. In 2004 the ban on dispatch in manufacturing was lifted, and the general limit on the duration of an assignment was extended, with a limit of one year for manufacturing later extended to three. Employers' incentives were clear: nonregular workers cost less in wages and benefits, could be hired for a short period, and could be released when demand fell. Dispatch also allowed firms to avoid the direct employment relationship and its obligations. Workers' reasons varied. Some preferred flexible hours or lacked options; many accepted nonregular jobs because regular positions were unavailable, a distinction reflected in the survey category of "involuntary" nonregular workers.

The differences between regular and nonregular work were large. Nonregular workers typically earned lower hourly wages than regular employees of similar age, and the gap widened with age because seniority-based pay applied mainly to regular staff. They received fewer bonuses and retirement benefits, less training, and few opportunities for promotion. They were more likely to lack coverage by employment insurance and by employees' pension and health insurance, which meant that some depended on the national pension and national health insurance schemes, designed for the self-employed, with lower benefits. Job protection was weaker, since fixed-term contracts could simply be left unrenewed. Statutes in the 2000s began to address some of these differences: the Part-Time Work Law was revised in 2007 and the Labor Contract Law took effect in 2008, but their provisions on equal treatment and conversion were limited in scope.

Gender and age shaped the pattern. The expansion first reflected the growth of part-time work among married women, partly encouraged by tax and social-insurance rules that rewarded wives who kept earnings below thresholds (such as about 1.03 million yen for income-tax deductions and 1.3 million yen for dependency in social insurance), a pattern called the "wall" in public debate. In 2010 well over half of women employees were nonregular, compared with about a fifth of men. Over time, however, the share of men and of young people in nonregular work also increased, and this change is what made the issue a matter of generational as well as gender inequality.

Students should be careful to distinguish nonregular employment from unemployment. Unemployment in Japan was low by international standards, rising from about 2 percent in 1990 to a peak of about 5.4 percent in 2002 and again to about 5 percent in 2009. The expansion of nonregular work meant that many people had jobs but with lower pay, less security, and less access to social insurance. The difference matters for statistics, which counted such workers as employed, and for experience: a worker on a series of short contracts might never be officially unemployed but could be unable to plan a household budget or a family.

Historical Vocabulary
Haken, freeter, NEET, and karōshi

Haken (dispatch work) is a three-party arrangement: a staffing agency employs the worker and sends him or her to a client firm. It is distinct from direct fixed-term employment and from ukeoi (subcontracting). Freeter (furītā), a coinage of the late 1980s from "free" and "arbeiter," originally described young people who chose casual work to pursue other goals; government surveys later defined freeters as people aged 15 to 34 in nonregular employment or seeking such work, a category that included many who had been unable to find regular jobs. NEET, imported from British policy language and popularized in Japan around 2004, refers to young people not in employment, education, or training. The categories are statistical and conceal wide variation in circumstances and motives.

Karōshi means death from overwork (typically from brain or heart disease linked to long working hours), and karōjisatsu means suicide associated with work-related psychological pressure. The terms entered public debate in the 1980s and 1990s, and they describe medical and legal claims, not a single diagnosis.

Section 97.11 · Division III

The Employment Ice Age, Freeters, NEETs, and Japan's Lost Generation

Japan's system of recruiting graduates concentrated opportunity at a single moment. Large firms hired new graduates (shinsotsu) in a coordinated cycle that began during the final year of university; those who missed the cycle or graduated into a recession found it hard to enter career-track positions later, because firms preferred fresh graduates to workers with job histories in temporary employment. When firms cut hiring after the bubble, the effect was therefore concentrated on those who happened to leave school in the 1990s and early 2000s. This period of weak graduate hiring, roughly 1993 to 2005 and worst around 2000–2003, is called the "employment ice age" (shūshoku hyōgaki). In 2003 only a little more than half of university graduates were recorded as entering employment in the spring after graduation, and the ratio of job offers to applicants fell far below the peaks of the bubble years, especially for the large firms that students most wanted to enter.

The consequences did not end with the first job. Workers who began in nonregular positions often found it difficult to move into regular ones, because training and promotion were tied to regular employment and employers were wary of candidates who lacked firm-specific experience. Researchers have described a "scarring" effect: workers who entered the labor market in bad years earned less and had less secure careers than those who entered in better years, and the disadvantage persisted. Those born in the 1970s and early 1980s were sometimes called a "lost generation," though the phrase can imply that all members shared the same fate, which they did not. Young people with elite degrees were less affected than graduates of less selective schools, and women and those from poorer families faced additional obstacles.

Public debate used categories that deserve scrutiny. Freeters, as defined by government surveys, numbered roughly two million at their peak in the early 2000s. NEETs, a smaller category defined as young people neither working, studying, nor seeking work, were estimated in the hundreds of thousands. Commentators sometimes treated these groups as evidence of changing attitudes among the young; the sociologist Yamada Masahiro's term "parasite singles" for unmarried adults living with parents was widely used in this way. Researchers have shown that many freeters and NEETs would have preferred regular jobs, that parental coresidence often reflected the cost of housing and low incomes, and that blaming "attitudes" ignored the structure of hiring. A related and separate phenomenon, extreme social withdrawal (hikikomori), attracted attention in the late 1990s; it has multiple causes and should not be confused with unemployment. The government responded with youth-support measures in the 2000s, including a 2003 plan on youth independence and consultation centers, but the programs were small relative to the scale of the problem.

The disadvantages were cumulative. Lower and less stable incomes limited the ability to save, to rent or buy housing, and to marry: surveys found that men in nonregular employment married at markedly lower rates than regular employees, and women's decisions about marriage and children were also influenced by the economic prospects of potential partners. Delayed access to regular employment also meant reduced entitlements to company pensions and benefits, which created problems that would appear only decades later. Social participation was affected, as irregular schedules and low incomes limited ties to colleagues, neighbors, and associations. The experience of the ice age thus illustrates how a difficult start to working life could have enduring consequences even when general conditions later improved. The recovery of 2002–2007 raised hiring, but it did not erase the position of the cohorts who had missed the earlier cycle.

Others became dispatched workers or part-time employees, intending to move later into regular work. Families were often involved: parents who had experienced a different labor market might not understand the new conditions, and living at home could cushion financial strain but also prolong dependence. Recruitment practices tied the transition from school to work to a single narrow window, and a period of stress at that moment could shape careers and, as Section 97.13 suggests, family formation for years afterward.

Section 97.12 · Division III

Karōshi, Workplace Stress, and the Human Costs of Economic Insecurity

Insecurity affected not only those outside the core of employment but also those within it. Karōshi, which can be translated as death from overwork, became part of public vocabulary after physicians and lawyers drew attention in the late 1970s and 1980s to sudden deaths from heart attacks and strokes among men in their thirties, forties, and fifties who had worked extremely long hours. Medical researchers such as Uehata Tetsunojō studied the link between long hours and cardiovascular disease; in 1988 a group of lawyers established a telephone hotline for karōshi cases, and in 1991 families of victims formed a national network. The Ministry of Labour had standards for recognizing brain and heart disease as work-related, which it revised in 1987, 1995, and 2001. The 2001 revision gave weight to overtime hours over a period of months, with a benchmark of about 80 hours a month as an indicator of a strong link to illness. Claims accepted for compensation numbered in the low hundreds each year in the 2000s, a figure that is small relative to the number of claims and, advocates argued, relative to the true incidence of overwork-related disease.

Work-related psychological illness and suicide became an issue in the 1990s. The case that received most attention involved a young employee of the advertising company Dentsu who died by suicide in 1991 after working extremely long hours; his parents sued the company, and in March 2000 the Supreme Court held that the employer bore responsibility for failing to prevent the harm. The decision established that employers have a duty to attend to workers' health and that overwork could give rise to liability. In 1999 the labor authorities issued guidelines for recognizing mental disorders caused by work as compensable, and claims for mental illness and suicide grew rapidly during the following decade. Cases such as that of a Toyota quality-control worker who died in 2002, which a court in 2007 recognized as work-related, extended discussion to activities that firms had treated as voluntary, such as quality-control circles.

The relationship between economic conditions and workplace stress was complicated. Downsizing could intensify demands on the employees who remained, who faced heavier workloads and unpaid "service overtime" in a climate of fear for their jobs. Average annual hours worked per employee fell from about 2,100 in 1990 to about 1,800 by 2010, but most of the decline reflected the growth of part-time work; full-time regular employees' hours changed little, and a minority worked very long hours. Nonregular workers experienced other forms of stress: income uncertainty, weaker health coverage, and exclusion from the community of the workplace. The unemployed and those leaving jobs under pressure faced further strains.

The statistics on suicide suggest the scale of distress but cannot be attributed to any single cause. The annual number of suicides rose from about 24,000 in 1997 to nearly 33,000 in 1998 and remained above 30,000 each year until 2011, a majority of them men, with a concentration among middle-aged men. The police recorded motives, including health problems, debts and economic hardship, family problems, and work problems, but the categories are imperfect and suicides usually involve several factors. Historians should link the rise in 1998 to the economic and financial crisis of that year with caution: the timing is suggestive, and studies have found associations between unemployment, bankruptcies, and suicide, but individual cases are not causally identical, and Japan's suicide rate was already high by international standards and had other determinants.

Note for students: this section discusses suicide in a historical and statistical context. If the subject affects you personally, please speak with a teacher, counselor, or health professional.

Advocacy changed practice slowly. Families, defense lawyers, unions, and researchers pressed for stronger recognition, and the International Labour Organization has documented how the term karōshi and the associated medical and legal debates developed from the 1970s. Reforms in the 2000s, including requirements for medical interviews with employees working long overtime, were limited, and legislation specifically aimed at preventing karōshi came later and belongs to subsequent chapters. The conclusion is that the 1990s and 2000s intensified a concern with the human costs of the employment system. Overwork and insecurity were different problems, but both shaped how households judged whether a stable career was still possible. That judgment influenced decisions about marriage and children, which is the subject of Division IV.

Section 97.13 · Division IV — Fertility Decline, Aging, and Family Transformation

The 1.57 Shock: Fertility Decline, Marriage, and the Changing Expectations of Family Life

In June 1990 the Ministry of Health and Welfare reported that Japan's total fertility rate in 1989 was 1.57, lower than the record low of 1.58 in 1966. That year, the "year of the fire horse" (hinoe-uma), a superstition about women born in such years had led many couples to avoid having children, so that the earlier low was understood as an aberration. The 1989 figure had no such explanation, and it startled officials and the media: it was called the "1.57 Shock." The government began to treat declining births, a trend described from 1992 as shōshika ("fewer children"), as a national problem. The rate continued to fall, to about 1.42 in 1995 and 1.36 in 2000, reaching a low of 1.26 in 2005, before rising modestly to about 1.39 in 2010.

Students need to distinguish several measures. The total fertility rate is a synthetic estimate of the number of children a woman would have if current age-specific birth rates continued over her lifetime. The number of births is an absolute count: about 2.09 million in 1973, about 1.2 million around 1990, and just over 1.06 million in 2005. The crude birth rate expresses births per thousand people. Population growth also depends on deaths and migration. A further complication is that the period fertility rate can understate lifetime fertility when women postpone childbearing, because births are delayed rather than abandoned; the average number of children of women born in the 1960s was still close to two, and cohort measures fell more slowly than the period rate. But as postponement turned into foregone births for successive cohorts, completed fertility declined as well.

The decline preceded the bubble's collapse. The fertility rate had been below the replacement level of about 2.1 since the mid-1970s. The principal proximate cause was changing patterns of marriage: women and men married later, and a growing share did not marry at all. The average age at first marriage for women rose from about 26 in 1990 to about 29 in 2010, and for men from about 28 to about 30.5. By 2010, about one in five men and one in ten women aged 50 had never married, compared with roughly 6 percent and 4 percent in 1990. Because fewer than 3 percent of births in Japan occurred outside marriage, changes in marriage had an immediate effect on fertility. Within marriage, couples generally continued to have about two children until the 1990s, after which average family size slipped.

Why marriage and childbearing declined is a subject of debate, and single-cause explanations are inadequate. Women's higher education and employment expanded their options; many who married found that long working hours for husbands and unequal domestic labor made combining employment and children difficult, and approximately six in ten women left work around the birth of their first child. Housing costs were high in the large cities, and the expense of education, including cram schools and private tuition, raised the cost of raising children. Employment insecurity mattered: men with unstable or low incomes were less likely to marry, and couples were reluctant to have children without financial security, while the long economic uncertainty after 1990 reinforced such caution. Childcare services were insufficient in many places. Changing norms about marriage and individual autonomy also played a role. Historians should avoid reducing the trend to changes in "values," because the choices were made under constraints that employers, legislators, and families had helped create, and because surveys consistently found that many people wished to have more children than they did.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's history of the issue shows the shift in concern from a statistical anomaly in 1990 to a central subject of social policy by the 2000s. The decline would have consequences for the labor force, pensions, health care, and local communities, which the following sections consider.

Historical Vocabulary
Total fertility rate, dependency ratio, and population aging

The total fertility rate (TFR) is the average number of children a woman would bear in her lifetime if she experienced the age-specific birth rates of a given year. A TFR of about 2.1 is roughly the level at which a population replaces itself in a low-mortality society. The dependency ratio compares the number of people in age groups conventionally regarded as economically dependent with the number of working-age people (usually 15–64). The old-age dependency ratio counts people aged 65 and older per 100 working-age persons. Because many people over 65 work and many people of working age do not, the ratio is a rough indicator of demographic structure and not a measurement of actual economic dependence.

Population aging means a rising share of older people in the population, caused by increases in life expectancy and decreases in fertility. It is distinct from population decline, which occurs when deaths exceed births (and net migration cannot compensate), and which in Japan began later than aging. A society may therefore be aging while its population is still growing.

Section 97.14 · Division IV

Aging Japan: Population Structure, Pensions, and Intergenerational Responsibility

The share of people aged 65 and older in Japan's population rose from about 12 percent in 1990 to about 15 percent in 1995, 17 percent in 2000, 20 percent in 2005, and 23 percent in 2010. Two forces drove this: longer lives, with life expectancy at birth reaching about 86 years for women and 80 for men by 2010, and low fertility, which narrowed the base of the age pyramid. A further factor was the size of particular cohorts. The large cohort born in 1947–1949, the first postwar baby-boom generation, began to reach the age of 60 in 2007, and the prospect of their mass retirement was widely discussed as the "2007 problem." The ratio of people 65 and older to those aged 15–64 roughly doubled between 1990 and 2010, from about 17 to about 36 per 100.

The working-age population, ages 15–64, peaked at 87,164,721 in the 1995 census (about 87.16 million) and declined to 84,092,414 in 2005 and 81,031,800 in 2010 (about 84.09 and 81.03 million). These are October census counts of residents reporting an age, not estimates of employed people. Total population continued to increase for years after the working-age peak, despite the first small annual natural decrease (more deaths than births) in 2005. In the Statistics Bureau's retrospectively revised October population-estimate series, total population reached approximately 128.084 million in 2008; the October 2010 census recorded 128,057,352 (approximately 128.057 million). The revised 2008 estimate and 2010 census are thus close, but they should not be confused with older statistical vintages that reported roughly 127.7 million around 2008. Students should distinguish 1995 (working-age census peak), 2005 (first annual natural decrease), and 2008 (peak of the revised October total-population estimate series).

Aging raised questions about pensions, medical care, and retirement. Japan's public pension system consisted of a basic pension for all residents, established in 1986, to which employees added earnings-related coverage. It was financed largely on a pay-as-you-go basis, so that current contributors paid for current retirees. Reforms in 1994 and 2000 raised the age of eligibility for the earnings-related portion toward 65, and in 2004 a major reform fixed a ceiling on future contribution rates, raised the government's share of the basic pension toward one half, and introduced an automatic adjustment of benefits to demographic change. In 2007 the discovery that tens of millions of pension records could not be matched to individuals became a political scandal. Health expenditure rose with the number of older patients, and reforms increased the copayment burden and created, in 2008, a separate medical-insurance system for people aged 75 and over, which was widely criticized and was a source of political controversy. Mandatory retirement at age 60 became a legal minimum in 1998, and a 2004 amendment required employers to provide employment for those who wished to work to 65, in stages.

Public discussion often framed the issue as one of intergenerational fairness: that current workers would pay more and receive less than earlier cohorts. Economists have produced "generational accounts" showing large imbalances. Yet students should avoid assuming that everyone aged 65 or older was economically inactive or dependent. Older Japanese had a high labor-force participation rate by international standards, and many lived independently. Older households also held a large proportion of household financial assets, which accumulated before 1990, and the experience of retirees varied sharply with income, health, and housing. At the same time, older people living alone, especially women with small pensions, faced a high risk of poverty, and the proportion of older people among recipients of public assistance rose. In 2010 a television documentary on the "relationless society" (muen shakai) drew attention to isolated deaths and to the weakening of family and community ties.

Demographic aging created institutional challenges, but outcomes depended on employment, productivity, public policy, health, and household resources. A declining ratio of workers to older residents makes it harder to finance social insurance from payroll contributions, but increases in the employment of women and older workers, in productivity, and in immigration could change the balance. The policy discussions of the 2000s turned on which of these adjustments to emphasize, and the institutional choices that followed are part of the history of the following chapters.

Section 97.15 · Division IV

Elder Care, Long-Term Care Insurance, and the Changing Responsibilities of Families

Through the 1980s, caring for older relatives was regarded largely as a family responsibility. Government policy since the late 1970s had promoted a "Japanese-style welfare society" in which families, and especially daughters-in-law and wives, provided care at home. Many frail older people were instead kept in hospitals for long periods, a practice called "social hospitalization," because hospital care was covered by health insurance while social services were scarce. As the older population grew, this arrangement became costly and strained families, and the problem of "care hell," in which a family member exhausted by caring for a bedridden relative, entered public discussion.

The government responded in stages. In December 1989 it launched the Gold Plan, a ten-year strategy to increase home-help services, day services, and nursing-home places; a revised New Gold Plan followed in 1994 and Gold Plan 21 in 1999. These plans expanded services but depended on municipal budgets and on means-tested assignment of care by local officials. The principal innovation was the Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) system. The Diet passed the enabling law in December 1997, and benefits began in April 2000. Under the system, everyone aged 40 and older paid premiums. People aged 65 and older could receive services if they were certified by their municipality as needing care, and people aged 40–64 could receive them if they had age-related conditions such as dementia. About half of the cost was financed by premiums and the rest by national, prefectural, and municipal taxes, and users paid a copayment of 10 percent. Municipalities served as the insurers, certifying need on a graded scale, and care managers drew up service plans. The system separated long-term personal assistance from medical care, and it allowed users to choose among providers, including private companies and nonprofit organizations, which created a market for care services.

The effects were substantial. The number of recipients roughly doubled within a few years, from about 2 million at the start to about 5 million in 2010. Costs rose from about 3.6 trillion yen in the first year to roughly 8 trillion yen by 2010, and average monthly premiums for those aged 65 and older rose from about 2,900 yen to about 4,100 yen, differing considerably by municipality. Employment in care expanded greatly, though care workers earned lower wages than workers in comparable fields, turnover was high, and shortages were widespread. In 2008 and 2009 Japan began admitting nurse and care-worker candidates from Indonesia and the Philippines under economic partnership agreements, an arrangement that met few of its targets because of language and licensing requirements. Revisions in 2005 emphasized preventive services and facilities' charges for room and board.

Family caregivers did not disappear. The majority of principal caregivers remained women, mostly wives, daughters, and daughters-in-law, although the share of sons and husbands increased, and the age of caregivers rose, so that older people increasingly cared for older people. Surveys indicated that about a hundred thousand people left jobs each year to care for relatives, and a law against elder abuse was passed in 2005. Access to care also varied by region: urban areas had more private providers, while rural districts faced shortages of staff and of facilities, and municipalities with older populations had higher premiums. Waiting lists for public nursing homes included more than 400,000 people around 2009. The result was a mixed system: LTCI socialized part of the cost of care and legitimized services outside the home, but the family continued to bear significant responsibility, and inequalities in access reflected income, location, and the availability of kin.

Section 97.16 · Division IV

Changing Households: Marriage, Childcare, Women's Employment, and Family Policy

Household composition changed substantially. The average household size fell from about 3.0 persons in 1990 to about 2.5 in 2010, and one-person households rose from about 23 percent of all households to about 32 percent, comprising both older people living alone and younger unmarried adults. Dual-income couples became more common than households with a full-time homemaker wife in the 1990s, though many wives worked part-time. Divorces nearly doubled between 1990 and 2002 from a low base. Single-parent households, mostly headed by mothers, faced a high risk of poverty: international comparisons in the 2000s found that the poverty rate for single-parent families in Japan was among the highest in the OECD, in part because the mothers worked at high rates but in low-paid, often nonregular jobs.

The difficulty of combining paid work with childrearing was central. Long hours were common for men, and surveys found that fathers of young children spent about an hour a day on housework and childcare, among the lowest figures in advanced economies. Many workplaces expected employees to prioritize work. The Equal Employment Opportunity Law of 1985 and its revision (in force in 1999), the Childcare Leave Law of 1991 (in force 1992), and the Child Care and Family Care Leave Law of 1995 gave workers rights, but their uptake was limited, particularly among men and among nonregular workers who often did not qualify. In 1999 the Basic Law for a Gender-Equal Society set a national framework. Childcare facilities, though extensive by international standards, did not meet demand in urban areas, and by 2010 about 25,000 children were officially on waiting lists, a figure that likely understated unmet demand.

The government's response to low fertility began with the Angel Plan of December 1994, a ten-year basic policy on childrearing support with a five-year program of action, followed by the New Angel Plan in December 1999. The plans increased childcare places, extended hours, and supported community childrearing facilities. In the 2000s the government passed the Basic Act on Measures for a Society with Declining Births and the Next Generation Act of 2003, which asked large employers to set action plans for supporting employees' childrearing, and it created further plans, increased childcare leave benefits, and extended child allowances. The Democratic Party government that took office in 2009 introduced a child allowance in 2010. Critics argued that policies concentrated on childcare services without addressing long working hours, insecure employment, and costs of education, and that policy stressed the declining birthrate as a demographic problem rather than treating family arrangements as matters of individual circumstance.

The state attempted to address low fertility while preserving significant expectations of family responsibility, a tension apparent in tax and pension rules that favored dependent spouses and in the expectation that families would care for children and elderly relatives. The experiences of different groups varied: working mothers with stable positions in large firms, single parents in low-wage jobs, unmarried women and men in cities, and sandwiched families caring simultaneously for children and aging parents faced different pressures. This section connects Division III and Division IV: insecure employment, gendered work expectations, and long hours made marriage and parenthood more difficult, and the demographic changes that resulted in turn affected labor supply and social insurance. Neither employment nor family change can be understood without the other.

Section 97.17 · Division V — Uneven Adjustment and the Meaning of the Lost Decades

Regional Inequality, Rural Depopulation, and the Concentration of Opportunity in Tokyo

Japan's stagnation was geographically differentiated. The Tokyo metropolitan region (Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, and Chiba) held roughly a quarter of the national population in 1990 and a larger share, well over a quarter, by 2010. It concentrated corporate headquarters, finance, major universities, media, and professional employment, and young people from other regions continued to move there for education and work. Land-price declines in the 1990s made central Tokyo housing more affordable, and population returned to inner wards, a "return to the city center." The Urban Renaissance program of 2002 eased building regulations and encouraged large redevelopment projects, such as Roppongi Hills (2003) and the Marunouchi district around Tokyo Station. The capital was not immune to the downturn, but its diversified economy and its role as the national center cushioned it.

Elsewhere, the picture was more mixed. Older manufacturing centers, such as parts of the Kitakyūshū region and electronics and machinery towns that depended on single large employers, were hit by plant closures and relocation abroad. The Nagoya region, anchored by Toyota and its suppliers, boomed in 2002–2007 and then fell sharply in 2008–2009, showing how export-oriented regions swung with global demand. Local shopping streets in many towns declined as roadside superstores and large malls, encouraged by deregulation of large-store rules in the 1990s, drew customers and as young residents left. Agriculture, partly opened to imports in the 1990s after the Uruguay Round, was increasingly worked by older farmers: the average age of core farm workers exceeded sixty-five by 2010.

Public policy sustained many regions. Public works, local-allocation tax grants from the central government, agricultural subsidies, and regional development programs supported local economies and employment. When Koizumi's government cut public works and transfers in the early 2000s, and when national policy encouraged municipal mergers (the number of municipalities fell from about 3,200 in 1999 to about 1,700 in 2010), many localities lost income and administrative capacity. Some pursued tourism, specialty products, or the attraction of factories and call centers. Efforts to rely on resorts and amusement facilities sometimes failed: Yūbari, a Hokkaido coal city whose population fell from over 100,000 in 1960 to about 10,000, built tourist attractions after its mines closed and in 2006 revealed debts that led it to effectively declare bankruptcy and to come under national supervision in 2007.

Population aging was strongest in rural areas and old industrial towns, where young people left and older residents remained. Sociologists used the term "marginal settlements" (genkai shūraku) for villages in which more than half the residents were over sixty-five and community functions such as maintaining paths, festivals, and irrigation were endangered. Aging and depopulation affected services: schools closed or merged, rural railway lines and bus routes were abandoned after deregulation (rail exit rules were relaxed in 2000 and bus rules in 2002), clinics and hospitals struggled to recruit doctors, and local governments faced rising care costs with shrinking tax bases. For residents without cars, especially older people, these changes could mean isolation.

Hokkaido and Okinawa illustrate different forms of regional difficulty. Hokkaido's economy depended heavily on public works and primary industries, and it suffered from the failure of Hokkaido Takushoku Bank and the closure of its coal mines, with population decline and aging in many districts, while Sapporo functioned as a regional center. Ainu communities in Hokkaido, whose history is treated in other chapters, experienced these conditions alongside the long-term effects of earlier dispossession, and surveys of Ainu living conditions in Hokkaido found disparities in education and income relative to neighboring residents. Okinawa had among the highest unemployment rates and lowest average incomes of the prefectures, a large share of nonregular employment, and an economy that combined public spending, tourism, and, to a limited extent, income related to the American bases, which made up only a few percent of the prefectural economy. Okinawa also had Japan's highest fertility rate and a younger population than the national average, a reminder that demographic change was not uniform. Inbound tourism, promoted by the national "Visit Japan" campaign from 2003, grew to about 8.6 million visitors in 2010 and benefited Okinawa, Hokkaido, and several other regions. Detailed discussion of base politics belongs to Chapter 99.

Section 97.18 · Division V

Foreign Workers, Nikkeijin Migration, and the Changing Labor Market

Japan responded to labor demand in part through international migration, though in ways that avoided a formal immigration policy. A revision of immigration law, passed in 1989 and effective in June 1990, created a "long-term resident" status for descendants of Japanese emigrants up to the third generation and their spouses, with no restrictions on the kind of work they could do. The measure was intended to allow those of Japanese ancestry to visit relatives, but it also answered the demands of manufacturers facing labor shortages in the late 1980s. Large numbers of Japanese Brazilians, and many Japanese Peruvians, moved to Japan: Brazilians numbered about 56,000 in 1990 and about 300,000 by 2007. They worked in automobile, electronics, and food factories, concentrated in places such as Hamamatsu and Toyota City, Gunma's Ōizumi, and other manufacturing towns. The OECD identifies the 1990 change as a turning point for migrants of Japanese ancestry.

Labor contractors, who recruited workers, arranged housing, and supplied them to factories, played a central role. Many Nikkeijin were employed through dispatch and contract arrangements, which meant that they were among the first to lose jobs when production fell. Their position was complicated by language barriers, uncertainty about residence plans, difficulties with schooling for their children, and gaps in health and pension coverage; many children of migrants did not attend Japanese schools regularly. The experience was not uniform: some families settled permanently, while others moved back and forth, and Japanese Brazilians' relations with their Japanese neighbors involved both reliance on their ancestry and distinctions of language and culture.

The technical intern training program, established in 1993 from earlier "trainee" arrangements, brought workers, mainly from China and later Vietnam, Indonesia, and other countries, to Japanese small and medium-sized firms in manufacturing, agriculture, and construction for fixed periods, officially for the transfer of skills to developing countries. In practice many interns filled labor shortages in industries with low wages. Reports by lawyers, unions, and international bodies documented problems such as unpaid overtime, confiscation of passports, and restrictions on changing employers, and a revision effective in July 2010 extended labor-law protection to interns from their first year. Other migrants entered in different categories: foreign "entertainers," many from the Philippines, whose numbers peaked in the early 2000s until visa rules were tightened in 2005 after criticism over trafficking; foreign students who worked part-time; and undocumented overstayers, who numbered nearly 300,000 in 1993 and fell to under 100,000 by 2010 as enforcement increased. The number of registered foreign residents rose from about 1.1 million in 1990 to just above 2 million in 2008, and in 2010 roughly 650,000 foreign workers were recorded, under 1.5 percent of employed people.

Long-established communities were in a different position. Zainichi Koreans, descendants of those who came to Japan during the colonial period and their families, numbered about 690,000 holders of Korean nationality in 1990 and fell to around 400,000 by 2010, as many acquired Japanese citizenship and as intermarriage and aging reduced the registered population. They faced a history of discrimination in employment; many had worked in small enterprises, self-employment, and sectors outside large-firm career tracks, though by the 1990s more were employed in large companies and professions. Compulsory fingerprinting of permanent residents was abolished in 1993 and for other foreigners in 2000, and issues such as exclusion from pension coverage affected older members of the community. Their experience differed from that of newcomers, whose status depended on visa categories, and from that of Nikkeijin, who had privileged legal access but precarious work. In general, different segments of the labor market relied on, regulated, and sometimes excluded workers from abroad, and the legal categories shaped wages, mobility, and social protection as much as skill did.

This section remains focused on economic history. Debates about immigration policy and national identity in the 2010s belong chiefly to Chapter 100.

Section 97.19 · Division V

The Global Financial Crisis of 2008–2009 and Japan's Renewed Economic Insecurity

When the U.S. investment bank Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, Japanese banks were less exposed to American mortgage securities than European banks, partly because their own crisis had left them cautious. The shock reached Japan through trade. Demand for cars, machinery, and electronics collapsed, exports fell by roughly a third or more year on year in early 2009, and industrial production dropped by more than 30 percent from its peak, a decline sharper than in most other advanced economies. Real GDP shrank by more than 5 percent in 2009, and the yen rose in the flight to safe assets, further pressing exporters. Toyota, the symbol of postwar industry, forecast its first operating loss in decades. The crisis showed how dependent the 2002–2007 expansion had been on external demand and on supply chains linking Japanese factories to markets in the United States and Asia.

Employment adjustment fell first on nonregular workers. Manufacturers cancelled contracts with staffing agencies and chose not to renew fixed-term contracts, a practice called "dispatch cutting" (haken-giri). Many dispatched workers lived in company dormitories or housing tied to their jobs, so that dismissal also meant loss of accommodation. Official estimates of the numbers of nonregular workers losing their jobs rose rapidly from tens of thousands in late 2008 to several hundred thousand in 2009. Regular employees were largely protected through reduced overtime and hours, supported by government employment-adjustment subsidies, which helped keep the unemployment rate (about 5.1 percent in 2009) lower than in the United States but illustrated the dual structure of the labor market. Nikkeijin workers were hard hit: in 2009 the government offered unemployed Japanese Brazilians and Peruvians payments to return home, on condition that they not return on the same visa, and several thousand accepted. In 2007 a government survey had already drawn attention to "net café refugees," people without stable housing who used all-night internet cafés, and to a "working poor" whose employment did not provide a living wage.

The New Year's Temporary Workers Village (Toshikoshi haken mura), organized in Hibiya Park in central Tokyo from 31 December 2008 through 4 January 2009, became a symbol of the crisis. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare opened emergency indoor accommodation on 2 January, continuing into the morning of 5 January; that lodging arrangement should not be confused with the dates of the principal Hibiya Park village. Organized by a coalition of labor unions, nonprofit organizations, lawyers, and activists, it set up tents with food and shelter for laid-off workers during the holiday period, when public offices were closed. Several hundred people sought shelter, and large numbers of volunteers participated. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare opened an auditorium as temporary accommodation, and the event received extensive media attention. Observers saw it as a challenge to the assumption that Japan's economic difficulties affected mainly the old and to the idea that unemployment and homelessness were individual failings. Organizers pressed workers to apply for public assistance, and the episode contributed to political debate over regulation of dispatch work.

The government introduced emergency employment measures in December 2008 and 2009, expanded subsidies for employers who kept workers on payroll, offered housing allowances for the unemployed, and in 2009 created a program to support job seekers who did not qualify for insurance, sometimes called a "second safety net." The Democratic Party of Japan, which won the general election of August 2009 and ended the Liberal Democratic Party's long period of government, promised to restrict dispatch work and introduce a child allowance. The economy recovered partially in 2010, but prices continued to fall and the yen was strong. The crisis thus exposed domestic vulnerabilities that had developed over two decades: the dual labor market, the dependence on exports, and the thinness of social protection for nonregular workers.

Section 97.20 · Division V

The Historical Meaning of the Lost Decades: Economic Security, Social Change, and the Future of Japan

The chapter's narratives can now be connected. Falling asset prices left banks and firms with debts they could not easily repay; delayed recognition of losses prolonged the damage and culminated in the crisis of 1997–1998; weak demand, deflation, and cautious investment held growth below the pace of the 1980s; and firms, under pressure to cut costs, protected a core of regular employees while hiring fewer young workers and relying on nonregular labor. These changes interacted with long-standing demographic processes: the fertility decline and aging that were already under way were reinforced by delayed marriage, insecure employment, gendered work expectations, and shortages of childcare, and they raised the costs of pensions, medical care, and elder care. Regional inequality, the use of migrant labor, and the vulnerability exposed in 2008–2009 were further consequences. The pieces do not reduce to a single cause, and not every social change was caused by the economic collapse.

Interpretations differ. Those emphasizing policy mistakes point to the delayed response of regulators, the premature tax increase of 1997, and the cautious monetary policy; those emphasizing financial institutions focus on bad loans and zombie lending; those emphasizing demographics stress the shrinking workforce and changing saving; and those emphasizing globalization stress the shift of production and competition from China. A historical account can hold several of these together. It can also recognize that some explanations fit particular periods better: the early 1990s and 1997–1998 are best understood as financial crises, the early 2000s as a period of deflation and repair, and the late 2000s as one in which demographic and structural factors loomed larger.

It would be a mistake to ignore what remained strong. In 2010 Japan had one of the highest life expectancies in the world, low crime rates, extensive infrastructure, a skilled workforce, and companies at the technological frontier in components, machinery, materials, and automobiles. It was the world's largest creditor nation, with large net foreign assets, and unemployment was low by international standards. Public institutions, from universal health insurance to national pensions and long-term care insurance, continued to function. Yet by 2010 relative poverty had reached roughly 15 to 16 percent of the population, among the higher rates in the OECD, and China's economy surpassed Japan's in size in 2010, ending Japan's long status as the world's second-largest economy. Prosperity was real, but its predictability had diminished.

The phrase "Lost Decades" is analytically useful because it names a real divergence between the expectations of the 1980s and the performance of the following twenty years, and because it calls attention to the long interval between the collapse and repair. It can be misleading when it obscures the experiences of groups whose conditions changed in different directions. Older households with assets and secure pensions, regular employees in large firms, and residents of central Tokyo often experienced stability or improvement. Graduates of the ice age, women in nonregular work, single mothers, residents of depopulating towns, and migrant workers experienced greater insecurity. The phrase also invites comparison with an idealized past; the high-growth order was itself built on exclusions.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the collapse of Japan's asset bubble became a prolonged economic and social transformation between about 1990 and 2010. Its central argument is that the aftermath of the bubble involved four related but distinct processes: the fall in share and land prices, a banking and financial-system crisis, persistent slow growth and deflation, and a set of social changes in employment, family, and demography. These processes interacted, but they did not have a single cause, and some, such as population aging, were under way before the bubble burst.

Share prices fell sharply from the end of 1989, and land prices declined over about fifteen years. Because banks lent against land and held shares, falling asset values eroded both the collateral behind loans and the capital of lenders. Borrowers were left with debts that did not shrink, producing a large stock of nonperforming loans. Banks and regulators, expecting recovery and constrained by the convoy system and political fear of bailouts, delayed recognition of losses. Firms prioritized debt repayment over investment, a pattern that Richard Koo called a balance-sheet recession, though other economists emphasize zombie lending, policy errors, productivity, or demographics.

The delay ended in crisis. The jūsen affair of 1995–1996 showed the political cost of using public funds, and the failures of Sanyō Securities, Hokkaido Takushoku Bank, Yamaichi Securities, and Tokuyō City Bank in November 1997 revealed the fragility of the financial system, in the context of the Asian financial crisis. In 1998 the government created a new supervisory agency, injected public capital, and temporarily nationalized the Long-Term Credit Bank and the Nippon Credit Bank. The Program for Financial Revival of 2002 and the rescues of Resona and Ashikaga in 2003 completed the cleanup, and by about 2005 the major banks had recovered. The cost of delay, in lost lending, deflation, and public distrust, was substantial.

Macroeconomic policy tried several instruments. Repeated fiscal stimulus packages raised public investment and government debt, and sustained regional and political networks centered on public works. The 1997 consumption-tax increase coincided with financial and Asian shocks and remains controversial. The Bank of Japan cut rates to near zero, adopted a zero-interest-rate policy in 1999, ended it prematurely in 2000, and introduced quantitative easing in 2001, a program that stabilized financial markets but did little to end deflation. Growth was not continuous contraction: the economy grew in most years and expanded from 2002 to 2008, but at modest rates, and prices and wages stagnated.

Corporate restructuring and Koizumi's reforms changed the institutional landscape, but the recovery of the 2000s depended on exports and benefited corporate profits more than household incomes. Firms protected a core of regular employees while reducing the hiring of new graduates and expanding nonregular employment. The share of nonregular employees rose from about 20 percent in 1990 to about 34 percent in 2010, and dispatch work was liberalized in 1999 and 2004. Nonregular workers earned less, received fewer benefits, and had little security, and women made up a majority of them.

The employment ice age made the timing of graduation consequential for decades. Young people who entered the labor market in the 1990s and early 2000s often could not move from nonregular to regular positions, with consequences for income, housing, and marriage. Categories such as freeters and NEETs described diverse circumstances. Overwork produced karōshi and work-related suicide, which advocacy by families and lawyers brought into law and public debate, as the Dentsu case of 2000 illustrates. Insecurity and overwork were different problems but shaped how households judged the possibility of stable careers and families.

Fertility fell from 1.57 in 1989 to 1.26 in 2005, mainly through delayed and foregone marriage, with childcare shortages, long working hours, education costs, and employment insecurity as contributing factors. The population aged 65 and over rose from about 12 percent to about 23 percent, the working-age population peaked in 1995, and natural decrease began in 2005. Pension, medical, and care systems were adjusted, and Long-Term Care Insurance, introduced in 2000, partly socialized elder care, although families, especially women, remained heavily responsible. The Angel Plans and later measures aimed to support childrearing, with limited effect on fertility.

The adjustment was uneven. Tokyo concentrated opportunity while rural areas, old industrial towns, and places such as Yūbari experienced depopulation, aging, and fiscal strain, and Okinawa and Hokkaido faced distinctive conditions. Migrants from Brazil and Peru, technical interns, and long-resident Zainichi Koreans occupied different positions in the labor market. The global financial crisis of 2008–2009 exposed the dependence on exports and the vulnerability of nonregular workers, culminating in the Hibiya "dispatch village" and in political debate over labor regulation.

The concluding interpretation is that the Lost Decades were not simply a prolonged financial disappointment but a restructuring of the assumptions that had governed postwar employment, economic advancement, household security, and the relationship between individuals and the state. Japan remained wealthy and technologically capable, yet security and opportunity became less predictable and more unequally distributed. The phrase "Lost Decades" is useful for naming this divergence and misleading if it hides the groups for whom conditions changed in different directions. These problems frame the questions taken up in Chapters 98 through 100.

Deflation
A sustained decline in the general price level, which increases the real burden of debt.
Mamizu ("real water")
The portion of a stimulus package that represents genuinely new spending.
Zero-interest-rate policy (ZIRP)
The Bank of Japan's policy from February 1999 of holding the overnight rate near zero.
Quantitative easing (QEP)
The 2001–2006 policy of targeting bank reserves at the Bank of Japan and buying government bonds.
Real interest rate
The nominal interest rate minus expected inflation; deflation raises it.
Three excesses
The 1999 diagnosis of excess employment, debt, and capacity in Japanese firms.
Seishain (regular employee)
A directly employed, open-ended employee with career prospects and benefits.
Nonregular employment (hiseiki koyō)
Part-time, fixed-term, dispatched, and similar work with less security and lower benefits.
Worker Dispatch Law
The 1985 law, liberalized in 1999 and 2003 (effective 2004), regulating staffing-agency employment (haken).
Shūshoku hyōgaki (employment ice age)
The period of weak graduate hiring, roughly 1993–2005.
Freeter
A young person in nonregular work or seeking it, as defined by government surveys.
NEET
A young person not in employment, education, or training.
Karōshi / karōjisatsu
Death, or suicide, associated with overwork or work-related stress.
1.57 Shock
The public reaction in 1990 to the 1989 total fertility rate of 1.57.
Total fertility rate
The average number of children a woman would bear given a year's age-specific birth rates.
Shōshika
"Fewer children"; the term for low fertility as a social problem.
Dependency ratio
The ratio of people in conventionally dependent age groups to the working-age population.
Gold Plan
The 1989 ten-year strategy (revised 1994 and 1999) to expand elder-care services.
Long-Term Care Insurance (kaigo hoken)
The social-insurance system for long-term care, in effect since April 2000.
Angel Plan
The December 1994 basic policy for childrearing support, followed by the New Angel Plan (1999).
Genkai shūraku
"Marginal settlement," a village where aging and depopulation threaten community functions.
Nikkeijin
Descendants of Japanese emigrants; after 1990 many migrated from Brazil and Peru to work in Japan.
Technical intern training
The program (since 1993) placing foreign trainees in Japanese firms; revised in 2010 to extend labor-law protection.
Haken-giri
"Dispatch cutting," the termination of dispatched workers' contracts in 2008–2009.
Toshikoshi haken mura
The New Year's Temporary Workers Village in Hibiya Park, 31 December 2008–4 January 2009; ministry accommodation continued to 5 January.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an economic shock can change a society's assumptions without destroying its wealth. Japan after the bubble did not collapse; it became a country in which the expectations of rising incomes, stable careers, early marriage, and family care for elders no longer held for everyone. The history of the Lost Decades is therefore a history of how security was redistributed as much as of how growth slowed.

The chapter also reveals how institutions shape the consequences of shocks. A bank-centered financial system without tools for resolving failures prolonged the crisis; a hiring system tied to graduation dates made timing a determinant of fortunes; tax and pension rules that favored dependent spouses shaped the growth of part-time work; and a family-centered model of care collided with an aging population. In each case, historical choices made in the high-growth era influenced what followed, and the response of governments and firms was constrained by those inheritances.

The developments examined here shaped later history. The financial reforms created the banking system in place in the 2010s. The expansion of nonregular employment framed later debates about labor regulation, inequality, and generational fairness. Demographic change set the terms for discussions of immigration, social insurance, and regional revitalization that Chapter 100 takes up, while the disasters and security debates of Chapters 98 and 99 occurred in a society already uncertain about its economic future.

The chapter also connects regions and neighbors. Tokyo's concentration depended on depopulation elsewhere; Hokkaido and Okinawa experienced the crisis in distinctive ways; Nikkeijin and technical interns tied Japanese factories to Brazil, Peru, China, and Southeast Asia; and exports to the United States and China linked the 2002–2007 recovery and the 2008–2009 collapse to the world economy. Students who understand the Lost Decades as uneven, contested, and open-ended are prepared to approach the later chapters without assuming that either decline or recovery was inevitable.
