Part XIII — The Economic Miracle and Late Shōwa Japan
96

From Oil Shock to Bubble Japan

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the winter of 1973–1974, the neon of Tokyo's Ginza was dimmed, television stations shortened their broadcast days, and households were urged to turn down their heating. Sixteen years later, in the last days of 1989, the Tokyo Stock Exchange's main index closed at its highest level in history, and Japanese buyers were purchasing skyscrapers, film studios, and paintings abroad. The same economy lay behind both scenes.

Contemporary reports and later economic histories agree on the general character of the first winter of the oil crisis: officials asked businesses and households to economize on electricity and petroleum products, and the visible symbols of the high-growth years, from bright commercial districts to late-night broadcasting, were curtailed. The request itself reflected a vulnerability that few Japanese had thought much about during the long postwar boom. Japan's steel mills, petrochemical complexes, power stations, and highways ran on petroleum that was almost entirely imported, and the great majority of it came from the Persian Gulf. When the price of that petroleum rose sharply and the possibility of physical shortage appeared, an economy that had grown at extraordinary speed for nearly two decades suddenly confronted the limits of its resource base.

The central question of this chapter is how Japan moved from that moment of exposure to the confidence of the late 1980s, and what that confidence concealed. The chapter's answer has three parts. First, Japan responded to the end of extraordinary high growth through adaptation rather than stagnation: it improved energy efficiency, diversified fuels, reorganized declining industries, and shifted toward higher-value manufacturing in automobiles, electronics, machinery, and precision equipment. Second, this adaptation transformed far more than production. It reshaped employment, household consumption, gender relations in the workplace, urban space, fashion, leisure, and the way heritage was preserved and marketed. Third, the success itself, combined with financial liberalization, a sharp appreciation of the yen after 1985, a prolonged period of low interest rates, and expectations that land and share prices would keep rising, produced the speculative asset bubble whose peak and first stages of unwinding conclude this chapter.

The chapter is organized in five divisions and twenty sections. Division I (Sections 96.1–96.4) examines the two oil shocks and the reconstruction of energy policy. Division II (96.5–96.8) follows industrial restructuring and the global expansion of Japanese firms. Division III (96.9–96.12) turns to consumer technology, affluence, employment, and the commercial culture of the 1980s. Division IV (96.13–96.16) considers administrative reform, financial liberalization, urban change, and cultural preservation. Division V (96.17–96.20) reconstructs the formation and peak of the bubble. A closing block of synthesis features draws these threads together. Chapter 91 explained the institutions of the high-growth economy, and Chapters 92 through 95 treated the political order, protest, accountability, and cultural change of the same decades; this chapter assumes that background and asks how those institutions adapted to a changed world.

The people who lived through these transformations did not experience them uniformly. A regular employee of a large automobile manufacturer, a subcontract machinist whose plant supplied it, a shipyard worker made redundant in a declining port city, a young woman hired as a clerical employee in a trading company, a farm household in a depopulating district, an Okinawan resident of an island whose economy remained tied to American bases and mainland capital, and a migrant laborer arriving at the end of the decade met the same macroeconomic conditions in very different ways. Ainu communities in Hokkaido, Zainichi Koreans, and other minority populations also appear in the period's political and legal history, and although the economic narrative often passes them by, this chapter keeps them in view where the evidence connects them to the developments under study. The phrase "affluent Japan" must therefore be handled carefully: it describes a real and substantial rise in material living standards, but not an equal distribution of security or opportunity.

Historians reconstruct this period from an unusually rich body of evidence. Government statutes and ministry white papers, Bank of Japan policy records and research papers, corporate histories and product documentation, trade-association statistics, household surveys, newspapers and magazines, advertising, and the memoirs of officials and executives all survive in abundance. Much of this material, however, was produced by institutions with interests in how their own decisions would be remembered, and many of the most influential accounts of the bubble were written after the collapse, when the outcome shaped how earlier choices were judged. This chapter therefore distinguishes contemporary documents from retrospective analysis, treats statistics as products of particular definitions, and avoids assuming that policymakers in 1986 knew what later observers know.

Two cautions govern the chapter. The first is against treating the oil shocks as the sole explanation for industrial change: exchange-rate movements, competition from newly industrializing economies, technological innovation, and longer-term shifts in demand also mattered. The second is against reading the bubble as the inevitable consequence of Japanese economic success. The late 1980s were shaped by specific decisions, institutional incentives, and international pressures, and contemporaries differed sharply in how they interpreted them. The boundary with Chapter 97 is deliberate. This chapter explains how the bubble formed and reached its peak and ends in 1991 at the point of rising financial vulnerability; the banking crisis, prolonged stagnation, precarious employment, and demographic consequences belong to the chapter that follows.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Japan's dependence on imported petroleum made its economy unusually vulnerable to the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979.
  2. Distinguish the growth slowdown already developing before 1973 from the specific effects of the first oil crisis.
  3. Trace the construction of Japan's energy policy, including conservation law, stockpiling, fuel diversification, and research programs.
  4. Compare the economic effects of the first and second oil shocks and explain why adjustment was smoother in 1979–1980.
  5. Analyze industrial restructuring after 1973, including the unequal burdens it placed on workers, subcontractors, and regions.
  6. Assess the rise of Japanese automobile and electronics firms without treating Japanese management practices as universally transferable.
  7. Evaluate trade frictions between Japan and its trading partners and the globalization of Japanese corporate investment.
  8. Interpret the affluent consumer society of the 1980s, including the limits of the idea of a nearly universal middle class.
  9. Examine employment institutions and the position of women, nonregular workers, and small firms in late-Shōwa corporate society.
  10. Explain how administrative reform, privatization, and financial liberalization altered the relationship between state, banks, and firms.
  11. Reconstruct the sequence from the Plaza Accord and monetary easing to land and share speculation, distinguishing contributing conditions from sufficient causes.
  12. Interpret primary documents and statistical series for their creators, definitions, strengths, and limits.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why was the world's emerging industrial powerhouse unusually exposed to disruptions in international energy markets?
  2. How much of the end of high growth was caused by the oil shocks, and how much by other domestic and international developments?
  3. What distinguished adaptation in the 1970s from decline, and who bore the costs of adjustment?
  4. Why did Japanese firms become so competitive in automobiles and electronics in some segments but not in others?
  5. How did success in export markets generate political conflict with trading partners, and how did Japanese policymakers respond?
  6. In what ways did affluence change everyday life, and in what ways did it leave inequalities and insecurities in place?
  7. How did administrative reform and financial liberalization change the institutions through which the postwar economy had operated?
  8. Why did a long period of financial and monetary decisions that each seemed defensible produce an asset bubble, and how should historians weigh competing explanations?

Historical Context

Between the mid-1950s and the early 1970s, the Japanese economy had grown at rates that astonished observers at home and abroad, averaging roughly nine to ten percent a year in real terms. Chapter 91 described the institutions of that expansion: close relations among banks, industrial groups, and ministries; enterprise unions and employment practices that bound regular employees to their firms; high rates of household saving channeled through banks and the postal system; and an export sector that took advantage of a stable exchange rate of 360 yen to the dollar. By 1968 Japan's economy had become the second largest in the non-communist world, and the Osaka Expo of 1970 was widely read as a celebration of that achievement.

The costs of the high-growth model were visible by then. Chapter 94 treated the pollution crises, the lawsuits at Minamata and elsewhere, and the rise of residents' movements that forced the passage of stricter environmental legislation in 1970 and the creation of the Environment Agency in 1971. Chapter 93 examined labor militancy and student protest, and Chapter 92 the political order of the 1955 System, under which the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) governed continuously. Labor markets were already tightening, wages were rising, and Japanese manufacturers faced growing competition in labor-intensive goods from other East Asian economies. The Japanese economy of 1972 was thus entering a period of necessary adjustment even before external shocks arrived.

The international setting changed abruptly in the early 1970s. On August 15, 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold and imposed a temporary import surcharge, shocks that Japanese commentators called the "Nixon shocks" (the label also covered the July 1971 announcement of Nixon's planned visit to China, which Tokyo learned of with almost no warning). The fixed-rate system built at Bretton Woods collapsed, the yen was revalued in December 1971 to about 308 to the dollar, and in early 1973 Japan moved to a floating rate. Meanwhile oil-exporting states were gaining control over production and pricing from the international oil companies that had dominated the market. These changes meant that Japan, which imported nearly all its petroleum and depended on exports for growth, would confront a more volatile international economy than the one in which its postwar success had been achieved.

Finally, readers should keep in mind the regional and imperial legacies that shaped the geography of the story. Okinawa had reverted to Japanese administration in 1972, but its economy remained structurally tied to the presence of American bases and to transfers from Tokyo, and the prefecture's incomes lagged behind those of the mainland. Hokkaido's coal and fishing economies, the heavy industrial belts of northern Kyushu and the Inland Sea, and the depopulating mountain and island communities of the interior all entered the period with very different prospects from those of the Tokyo–Nagoya–Osaka corridor. The chapter returns to these differences repeatedly, because a national account of the economy can disguise the fact that adjustment and prosperity arrived in different forms in different places.

Section 96.1 · Division I — Oil Shocks and the End of High Growth

The End of the High-Growth Era: Japan before the First Oil Shock

Why was the world's emerging industrial powerhouse unusually exposed to disruptions in international energy markets? The answer begins with the structure of the economy that high growth had built. Japan had few domestic fossil-fuel reserves of economic significance. Its coal industry, once central, had been run down in the 1960s in favor of cheap imported oil, and by the early 1970s petroleum supplied roughly three-quarters of the country's primary energy, almost all of it imported and the large majority of it from the Persian Gulf. Crude oil was inexpensive, at a little over two dollars a barrel for much of the early 1970s, and Japan's heavy and chemical industries had been designed around that assumption. Integrated steel mills, petrochemical complexes built around naphtha, oil-fired power stations, aluminum smelters that consumed enormous quantities of electricity, shipping, and the rapidly growing automobile fleet all rested on a fuel whose price and supply were outside Japanese control.

The exchange-rate environment was changing at the same time. The Nixon shocks of 1971 ended the fixed rate of 360 yen to the dollar that had given exporters a stable and, by the late 1960s, undervalued currency. The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 revalued the yen to about 308, and by early 1973 the major currencies had moved to floating rates. Appreciation raised the dollar price of Japanese goods and signaled that the era of automatic export advantage was ending. Domestic policy responded in ways that later proved controversial. Prime Minister Tanaka Kakuei's government, which took office in July 1972, pursued expansionary budgets and promoted a program for "remodeling the Japanese archipelago" through highways, high-speed rail, and regional industrial development. Easy money and the anticipation of public works helped fuel a surge in land speculation in 1972–1973, and the newspapers spoke of "crazy prices" (kyōran bukka) even before oil entered the picture.

It is therefore a mistake to treat the 1973 crisis as the single event that ended high growth. Several limits were already visible. Labor shortages had pushed wages upward. Pollution and urban congestion had produced political pressure for costly environmental regulation, the subject of Chapter 94. Competition from South Korea, Taiwan, and other economies was beginning to erode Japanese advantages in labor-intensive goods, and the proportion of the population moving from farms to factories was reaching its natural limit. Historians generally describe a growth model whose preconditions were eroding, with the oil shock acting as a violent accelerant rather than an independent cause. The distinction matters for explanation: Japan's growth fell from the nine or ten percent of the miracle years to a pace closer to four or five percent in the later 1970s and 1980s, and part of that deceleration reflected maturity rather than crisis.

Section 96.2 · Division I

The Oil Shock of 1973–1974: Inflation, Shortages, and Public Anxiety

The war that began on October 6, 1973, between Israel and Egypt and Syria triggered the oil crisis. In mid-October the Gulf members of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries raised posted prices, and the Arab members of the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC) announced production cuts and a selective embargo directed at states regarded as supporting Israel. Prices rose roughly fourfold by early 1974. Japan was not at first among the targeted countries, but it was warned that its supplies might be reduced, and officials feared that its dependence left it with little leverage. In late November the cabinet issued a statement calling for Israeli withdrawal from occupied territories, a significant departure from Tokyo's usual caution in Middle Eastern diplomacy, and in December Deputy Prime Minister Miki Takeo traveled to the region as a special envoy. Japan's classification as a friendly state followed, but the episode reinforced the conviction that energy dependence constrained foreign policy.

At home, the crisis arrived through prices and anxiety more than through sustained physical shortage. Wholesale prices rose steeply, and consumer prices increased by more than twenty percent in 1974, the highest inflation of the postwar period. Companies passed on higher fuel and material costs, and some used the crisis as cover for additional price increases. In November and December 1973, reports spread of panic purchasing of toilet paper, detergent, and other household goods, reported to have begun in the Osaka region and to have spread nationally through the media. Scholars who have examined the episode emphasize that the supply of such goods was not seriously constrained; rumors of shortage, together with the visible price increases and the government's own appeals for conservation, led households to hoard, and hoarding created temporary empty shelves that appeared to confirm the rumors. The episode is a case study of how distribution problems and expectations can amplify a genuine but limited supply disruption, and it left a durable cultural memory of the "oil shock" as a time of anxiety.

The government's response combined voluntary appeals with new legal powers. In November 1973 the cabinet adopted measures for oil and electricity conservation, including requests for a reduction in consumption by large users, limits on neon lighting and late-night broadcasting, and restrictions on Sunday gasoline sales. In December the Diet enacted two laws, an Emergency Law for the Stabilization of Living and a Petroleum Supply and Demand Optimization Law, which authorized the government to set priority allocations of petroleum, control prices of designated goods, and require reductions in consumption. Nakasone Yasuhiro, then Minister of International Trade and Industry, was a principal executor of the oil policy. The economy contracted in fiscal 1974, with real output falling for the first time in the postwar era. Unions, which had won wage increases of nearly one-third in the 1974 spring offensive (shuntō), accepted much smaller increases the following year, a restraint that helped bring inflation down and is often cited as an element of Japan's relatively successful adjustment.

Section 96.3 · Division I

Energy Conservation, Alternative Fuels, and the Reconstruction of Energy Policy

Japan's response to the first oil crisis built on an institutional foundation that predated it. The Agency for Natural Resources and Energy had been created within MITI in July 1973, a few months before the embargo, in recognition of the strategic importance of energy, and the crisis expanded its role. The government's program rested on four ideas: reduce dependence on petroleum by improving efficiency; diversify the sources of supply and of fuel; build stockpiles to cushion future disruptions; and invest in research that might eventually yield new technologies. In 1974 MITI launched the Sunshine Program for the development of solar, geothermal, coal-conversion, and hydrogen technologies, and in 1978 it added the Moonlight Program, which concentrated on energy-saving technologies such as high-efficiency gas turbines, heat pumps, and fuel cells. The Petroleum Stockpiling Law of 1975 obliged oil companies to hold reserves equivalent to a set number of days of consumption, and the government subsequently began building national reserves. Japan also joined the International Energy Agency, established in 1974 by the major consuming countries, which coordinated emergency sharing and stockpiling.

Diversification changed the physical basis of the energy system. Imports of liquefied natural gas, which had begun in 1969, expanded, and gas-fired electricity generation grew. Steam coal imports rose as electric utilities and industry returned to coal in new, cleaner forms. Nuclear power, whose first commercial reactor at Tōkai had entered service in 1966, became the centerpiece of the government's long-term strategy; in 1974 the Diet passed the "three power-source development laws," which provided subsidies and public works to communities that accepted power plants. The social consequences of this arrangement, including the dependence of host communities on subsidies and the political resistance to siting, belong to the later history of nuclear energy treated in Chapter 99. Oil-substitution efforts were formalized in 1980, when the government created the New Energy Development Organization (NEDO) and passed a law to promote alternatives to petroleum.

The most consequential change was less visible: Japanese firms learned to use much less energy to produce each unit of output. The Act on the Rational Use of Energy, enacted in 1979 (the "Energy Conservation Law"), required designated large energy users to appoint managers, maintain records, and meet efficiency standards. Steel mills recovered waste heat, chemical plants redesigned processes, appliance and automobile makers improved efficiency, and electric utilities optimized generation. The energy intensity of the economy fell sharply between 1973 and the mid-1980s, and the economy grew while petroleum consumption stagnated. Students should distinguish energy security from environmental protection, however. Conservation and some renewable programs lowered pollution as well as costs, and Japan's 1970s regulations on nitrogen oxides and sulfur emissions, adopted for health reasons after the pollution crises, spurred technologies such as flue-gas desulfurization. But a shift to coal or nuclear power served the goal of diversification while carrying different environmental and safety implications, and the energy program was never designed primarily as an environmental policy.

Historical Vocabulary
Oil Shock, Energy Security, Conservation, and Industrial Restructuring

An oil shock (sekiyu shokku; also oiru shokku) refers to the sudden rise in oil prices and the threat to supply in 1973–1974 (the "first" shock) and 1979–1980 (the "second"). In Japan the term also names a period of public anxiety and policy change. Energy security means the reliability of access to fuel at tolerable cost; for resource-poor Japan it involved diplomacy, stockpiling, and diversification as well as production. Conservation (shō-enerugī, shō-ene) means reducing the energy consumed per unit of output or per household, distinct from simply reducing activity. Industrial restructuring (sangyō kōzō chōsei) refers to the reorganization of the economy as declining industries shed capacity and growth industries expand.

These terms should not be treated as synonyms. An economy could improve security by stockpiling without conserving energy, or conserve energy by closing factories, which is a different matter from improving efficiency. Japan's experience combined the processes, which is why historians treat the 1970s as a period of structural change rather than merely a recession.

Context: The law was enacted during the second oil crisis and the Tokyo Summit of June 1979, at which industrial democracies agreed on oil-import targets.

Analysis: The statute reveals a policy shift from emergency appeals to a permanent regulatory regime based on standards and corporate reporting. It cannot show how much of the subsequent decline in energy intensity resulted from the law and how much from price signals, which gave firms strong incentives to economize regardless of regulation.

Section 96.4 · Division I

The Second Oil Shock of 1979 and Japan's Transition to Moderate Growth

The second shock grew from the Iranian Revolution of early 1979, which interrupted Iranian exports, and was intensified in September 1980 by the outbreak of the Iran–Iraq War. Crude prices more than doubled between 1978 and 1980, and the effects spread through the world economy. Compared with 1973–1974, however, Japan's experience was strikingly milder. Consumer-price inflation rose to roughly eight percent at its peak in 1980, in contrast to more than twenty percent in 1974, and there was no comparable episode of panic buying. Output continued to grow, and exports remained strong. The Tokyo Summit of June 1979, hosted by Prime Minister Ōhira Masayoshi, set import-ceiling targets among the industrial democracies, symbolizing the multilateral coordination that the first crisis had lacked.

Several factors explain the difference. Japan had by then built petroleum reserves and adopted the legal framework of the 1970s. Firms had already cut energy use and costs, and many had restructured to lower their breakeven points. The share of petroleum in primary energy had begun to fall as natural gas, coal, and nuclear power gained ground. Labor relations also mattered: unions accepted wage restraint, and employers, having learned the inflationary lesson of 1974, were more cautious about passing costs on. Finally, the shock affected a different economy: the heavy industries most dependent on cheap energy had already begun to shrink, while the more efficient automobile and electronics sectors were expanding. The Ministry of the Environment's and Agency for Natural Resources and Energy's retrospective energy statistics show Japanese energy consumption rising only modestly through the early 1980s even as output grew, which is the statistical signature of structural adaptation.

The consequences were nonetheless real. Growth of roughly four percent a year in the early 1980s was far lower than the miracle years, and government finances were strained. Since 1975 the government had financed deficits through bond issuance, and by the early 1980s fiscal reconstruction had become a political priority, setting the stage for the administrative reform movement treated in Section 96.13. The oil shocks thus ended the earlier pattern of extraordinary growth without ending Japan's industrial development or rising prosperity. Instead they redirected investment toward energy-saving, higher-value, and export-oriented industries and made energy efficiency part of the country's industrial identity.

Section 96.5 · Division II — Industrial Restructuring and Global Competition

From Heavy Industry to Knowledge-Intensive Production

The energy crises and the new international environment imposed their heaviest costs on the industries that had symbolized high growth. Shipbuilding, in which Japanese yards had built a large share of the world's tonnage, collapsed with the contraction of world trade and the tanker market; completions fell from a peak in the mid-1970s to a fraction of that level within a decade. Steel faced weaker demand at home and growing competition abroad. Aluminum smelting, whose production costs depended on electricity, shrank to a remnant as the price of power rose. Textiles, already under pressure from lower-wage Asian producers, and parts of the petrochemical industry also contracted. The government responded with legislation and cartel-like arrangements: a 1978 law on designated depressed industries and a 1983 law on structural improvement of specific industries promoted scrapping of capacity, coordinated reductions, and assistance to affected firms, while the Employment Insurance Law of 1974 introduced employment-adjustment subsidies that helped firms retain redundant workers rather than dismiss them.

Adjustment was nonetheless painful and unevenly distributed. Large firms reassigned workers to other plants and affiliates, transferred them on secondment (shukkō) to subsidiaries, offered early retirement, and sharply reduced new hiring, while the commitments of long-term employment meant that dismissals of regular employees were comparatively rare. Subcontractors, which lacked these options, bore a disproportionate share of the shock as parent companies cut orders and demanded lower prices. Regional consequences were severe. Port and mill towns such as Muroran in Hokkaido, Kamaishi in Iwate, Kure in Hiroshima, Sasebo in Nagasaki, and the industrial belt of Kitakyushu lost population and municipal revenue, and the coal fields of Hokkaido and northern Kyushu continued their long decline. Communities lobbied for compensation, for alternative industries, and for public works, and the results were mixed. National aggregates of employment and output can conceal the fact that restructuring meant closed workplaces and forced relocations for specific people and places.

At the same time, new growth industries emerged. Firms invested in precision machinery, numerically controlled machine tools, electronics, advanced ceramics and materials, and consumer durables, and promoted products described by the slogan keihakutansō, "light, thin, short, and small," which captured the shift from tonnage to refinement and miniaturization. Services expanded as well, including retail, logistics, finance, and information processing. The movement toward advanced manufacturing was neither painless nor automatic. It required corporate investment in research, the cooperation of suppliers, and a labor force that could be retrained or newly recruited. Government policies assisted, but corporate strategy and market competition drove most of the change. The lesson of the period is that industrial adjustment created new opportunities while imposing unequal burdens, and that the same transformation which produced celebrated corporate success also produced shuttered yards and aging mill towns.

Section 96.6 · Division II

Toyota, Honda, and the Global Rise of Japanese Automobiles

The automobile industry became the most visible emblem of Japanese industrial success. Japanese manufacturers had grown with the domestic market in the 1960s and had begun exporting small cars to the United States in that decade. The oil shocks transformed their position. American buyers who faced sharply higher gasoline prices in 1973–1974 and again in 1979 turned in growing numbers to small, fuel-efficient, reliable cars, which Toyota, Nissan, and Honda were well placed to supply. Honda's CVCC engine of the early 1970s, which met strict American emissions standards without a catalytic converter, had already established the company's technical credibility. By 1980 Japan produced more automobiles than any other country, passing the United States, and Japanese cars had taken a substantial share of the American market.

Explanations of this success have focused on manufacturing organization. The Toyota Production System, associated with the engineer Ōno Taiichi, evolved in the 1950s and 1960s at Toyota and centered on just-in-time production, in which parts arrive when needed rather than being stockpiled; on signaling cards (kanban) to regulate flow; on the authority of workers to stop a line when defects appear; and on kaizen, continuous incremental improvement drawing on workers' suggestions. Quality-control circles, a practice adopted from the 1960s with the assistance of the Japanese Union of Scientists and Engineers, institutionalized shop-floor problem-solving. Tiered networks of suppliers, many tied to the assembler by long-term relationships and shareholding, delivered components in precisely scheduled sequences and shared design and cost targets. A critical literature complicates the celebratory story: Kamata Satoshi's 1973 account of his own work as a seasonal worker at Toyota described intense pace, overtime, and the use of temporary and subcontract labor, and later scholars noted that the system depended on a division between secure regular employees and a flexible periphery. The label "lean production," popularized by an MIT study of the early 1990s, abstracted from this particular social base.

The 1970s and 1980s thus made visible innovations that had been developed earlier; the oil shocks supplied the market in which they paid off. The section's central point is that Japanese success was historically specific. It depended on products suited to a particular moment in energy prices, on cooperation among suppliers, on a labor system built on long-term employment for core workers, and on technological exchange, including licensing and learning from foreign firms, rather than on a management formula that could simply be transplanted. When Japanese firms did transplant their methods, they adapted them: Honda began automobile production in Marysville, Ohio, in 1982, Nissan opened a plant in Smyrna, Tennessee, in 1983, and Toyota entered a joint venture with General Motors in California in 1984. In the later 1980s manufacturers also moved upmarket with luxury brands, a response to quantity restraints and rising yen costs (Section 96.8).

Historical Vocabulary
Kaizen, Just-in-Time Production, Quality-Control Circles, and the Multinational Enterprise

Kaizen ("improvement") is the practice of continuous, incremental improvement in processes, involving workers at all levels. Just-in-time production aims to supply each stage of manufacturing with the parts it needs when it needs them, reducing inventory but making the system sensitive to disruption. Quality-control (QC) circles are small groups of workers that meet to identify and solve problems. A multinational enterprise is a firm that owns and manages production or other operations in more than one country; Japanese firms became multinational on a large scale in the 1980s.

These practices should not be described as uniquely "Japanese" cultural traits. They emerged from specific historical conditions, including postwar scarcity, American statistical-quality-control teaching, labor-management settlements, and supplier hierarchies, and they varied by firm and industry. Their international influence in the 1980s led scholars and managers elsewhere to study and adapt them, with uneven results.

Section 96.7 · Division II

Electronics, Semiconductors, Robotics, and the Technology Economy

Japan's electronics industry had grown from transistor radios and televisions in the 1950s and 1960s into a diversified sector that included computers, communications equipment, components, and consumer products. The leading firms were integrated electrical manufacturers, among them NEC, Fujitsu, Hitachi, Toshiba, and Mitsubishi Electric, alongside consumer-oriented companies such as Sony, Matsushita (National and Panasonic), Sharp, and Sanyo. Electronics benefited from the shift away from energy-intensive production: its products were light and valuable, its costs depended more on research and design than on fuel, and its markets were global.

Semiconductors became the strategic center of this sector. In 1976 MITI organized a cooperative research initiative on very-large-scale integrated circuits (VLSI), which ran through 1979 and brought together NEC, Fujitsu, Hitachi, Mitsubishi Electric, and Toshiba with public research institutions. The project united firms that were normally competitors in a shared laboratory on fundamental manufacturing technologies, and scholars have debated how much of the industry's subsequent success it caused and how much resulted from the firms' own investments and the fortunes of the market. By the early 1980s Japanese firms had gained leadership in dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) chips, and by the later 1980s Japan held close to half of the world's semiconductor market. Japanese companies also became leaders in industrial robots and factory automation, and Fanuc and other firms made Japan the world's largest user of robots. In office technology, Toshiba introduced Japan's first Japanese-language word processor in 1978, and NEC's PC-9800 series, launched in 1982, dominated the domestic personal-computer market in part because Japanese-language processing required special hardware and software.

The technology economy changed employment as well as exports. Automation reduced demand for some assembly and machining jobs while increasing demand for engineers, technicians, and maintenance staff, and electronics factories relied heavily on part-time and female labor on assembly lines. The record was also uneven. Japanese firms led in memory chips and certain consumer devices but lagged in microprocessors and software, where American firms retained dominance. MITI's Fifth Generation Computer Systems project, begun in 1982 with great publicity, did not achieve its ambitious goals. The inaccurate assumption that Japanese companies dominated every segment of the global technology industry reflected the anxieties of the period more than the record, and students should avoid reading the strengths of particular fields as general proof of a superior national system.

Section 96.8 · Division II

Global Brands, Trade Frictions, and the Internationalization of Japanese Business

By the early 1980s, Japanese products had become familiar in markets around the world, and Japan was running large trade surpluses, especially with the United States. Exports of automobiles, consumer electronics, machinery, and semiconductors fueled political reactions in countries whose industries felt threatened. In the United States, the contraction of the auto industry and layoffs in Detroit generated pressure for restrictions. In 1981 the Japanese government, under American pressure, adopted "voluntary" export restraints on automobile shipments to the United States, initially at about 1.68 million vehicles a year and later raised. A similar dispute arose over semiconductors: in 1986 the United States and Japan signed a semiconductor agreement committing Japan to monitor chip prices and to encourage greater foreign access to its market, and in 1987 Washington imposed retaliatory tariffs on selected Japanese electronics after concluding that Japan had not complied. Trade friction also had a darker side. The 1982 killing of Vincent Chin, a Chinese American man in Michigan who was attacked by two men angered by the decline of the auto industry, reflected how resentment of Japanese competition could turn into racialized violence against people who were not Japanese at all.

Japanese policymakers responded in several ways. They accepted export restraints, encouraged firms to expand local production in the United States and Europe, and, in 1986, issued the Maekawa Report, which argued that Japan should shift from export-led to domestic-demand-led growth and reduce its external surplus. The Structural Impediments Initiative of 1989–1990 extended the dispute to Japanese distribution, land policy, and business practices. Japanese firms meanwhile became multinational on a large scale. Honda, Nissan, and Toyota built American plants, and Nissan opened a British factory in 1986. Electronics firms established factories in Southeast Asia, Taiwan, and elsewhere to take advantage of lower wages, and after 1985 the strong yen accelerated the movement of manufacturing, parts supply, and investment to Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, and other countries. Japanese direct investment abroad climbed to tens of billions of dollars a year by the end of the decade, and Japanese banks, trading houses, and investors purchased real estate and companies in North America and Europe.

The internationalization of Japanese business thus had two geographies. In North America and Europe it took the form of trade conflict, local assembly plants, and high-profile acquisitions. In East and Southeast Asia it created networks of suppliers and factories that tied the region's economies more closely to Japanese capital and technology, a process that some scholars described through the "flying geese" metaphor and others criticized as a hierarchy that preserved Japanese advantages. Critics at home worried about the "hollowing out" (kūdōka) of domestic industry as production moved abroad. The tension between export success and market-access complaints, and between corporate expansion and domestic employment, was never fully resolved in these years, and it contributed to the political and financial circumstances that follow in Division V.

Section 96.9 · Division III — Consumer Affluence, Technology, and Everyday Life

The Consumer Electronics Revolution: Walkman, Video Recorders, and Personal Technology

The technology that Japanese firms developed for export also changed domestic life. In the 1950s the "three sacred treasures" of television, washing machine, and refrigerator had defined the aspirations of the new middle class; in the 1960s the "three Cs" of car, cooler (air conditioner), and color television followed. In the late 1970s and 1980s the focus shifted to personal and audiovisual technology. Home video recording was an early example. Sony introduced its Betamax recorder in 1975, and JVC (a Matsushita affiliate) launched the competing VHS format in 1976. The two formats were technically incompatible, and the contest turned less on picture quality than on licensing, manufacturing partners, recording time, and the availability of prerecorded and rental tapes. VHS gained the larger market share through broad adoption by other manufacturers, and by the late 1980s it had become the dominant home format. The episode is a case study of how technological standards, alliances, and distribution shape market outcomes as much as technical merit.

Sony's Walkman, launched on July 1, 1979, took a different path. It combined a small cassette player with lightweight headphones, deliberately creating a product for private listening in public space, and it became an international symbol of Japanese design and of a new pattern of individualized consumption. Sony's co-founders Morita Akio and Ibuka Masaru and its engineers drew on existing components and miniaturization skills, and the product's success depended on advertising, pricing, and retail distribution as well as engineering. The compact disc, developed by Sony and Philips and introduced in Japan in 1982, extended the trend, and electronic calculators, word processors, and personal computers, notably NEC's PC-9800 series, entered offices and homes. Appliances such as microwave ovens and "mini-component" stereos became common among urban households. Akihabara in Tokyo and large electronics discount chains in other cities became distribution centers where consumers compared models and prices.

The convergence of electronics and entertainment appeared most clearly in Nintendo's Family Computer, or Famicom, released in July 1983. A home video-game console selling at a modest price, it brought programmable entertainment into millions of households and prepared the way for an export industry. This section's subject is the technology market and the household behavior it altered: where products were sold, how standards were set, how families budgeted for new devices, and how listening, viewing, and play moved from shared family rooms toward private and portable use. The artistic, narrative, and industrial history of manga, anime, and related cultural production was treated in Chapter 95 and is not repeated here. What the consumer-electronics revolution shows is that the same technological capacities that drove export competitiveness also redefined ordinary routines, and that Japanese consumers were both customers of the new products and a test market for their global release.

Section 96.10 · Division III

The Affluent Consumer Society: Homes, Shopping, Leisure, and Status

By the 1980s, Japan had become an affluent consumer society by international standards. Retail had been transformed by chain supermarkets such as Daiei, department stores that marketed lifestyle and status, and, from 1974, the convenience-store chains that began with Seven-Eleven Japan and Lawson and offered long hours, compact assortments, and sophisticated inventory systems. Households purchased automobiles in growing numbers, ate more often at restaurants, and traveled. Domestic tourism expanded, Tokyo Disneyland opened in 1983, and a Resort Law of 1987 promoted large leisure developments in the provinces. Overseas travel roughly doubled between 1985 and 1990, to more than ten million trips a year, helped by the strong yen. Consumer credit and credit cards spread, and spending was increasingly directed toward brands, convenience, recreation, and status display rather than basic necessities. The moneylending problems of the early 1980s, which led to legislation in 1983 regulating consumer-finance firms, were a reminder that credit was not evenly benign.

Housing remained the most conspicuous gap between income and quality of life. A majority of households owned their homes by the 1980s, but owned housing was typically small, and the ownership rate masked large differences between rural and urban families. A widely quoted 1979 European Community document that described Japanese workers as living in "rabbit hutches" offended many Japanese readers but pointed to a real feature: high land costs in the metropolitan regions forced households into small apartments and long commutes. As land prices rose in the second half of the decade (Section 96.19), the dream of a house in Tokyo receded for younger families, and the price of a standard condominium in the Tokyo region reached many times the annual income of a typical household. Households that already owned land gained wealth without effort; those that did not were excluded. The distinction between owners and renters became one of the most important axes of inequality in the late 1980s.

Public opinion surveys by the Prime Minister's Office found that around nine in ten Japanese identified themselves as middle class, an idea often rendered as a "hundred-million-strong middle class" (ichioku sōchūryū). Historians treat this as significant evidence about aspirations and self-perception, but not as proof of equality. The question offered few categories, respondents tended to place themselves in the center, and the survey did not measure wealth, job security, or regional opportunity. Differences persisted between employees of large firms and workers in small enterprises, between urban professionals and farm households, between mainland prefectures and Okinawa, whose per capita income remained the lowest in the country, and between regular and nonregular workers. The affluent society was real, and it shaped everyday choices, but it was layered by income, property ownership, region, and employment status.

A comparable household in the later 1980s might own a compact car and a videocassette recorder, buy a Walkman for a teenager and a Famicom for the children, use a credit card at a department store, take a holiday at a domestic resort or an overseas package tour, and eat at family restaurants. A farm household in a depopulating district might own the same appliances but live with the consequences of aging and school closures. The contrast between the two decades shows that consumer society grew from the very adaptations that followed the oil shock, and it shows that affluence arrived through different routes for different households.

Section 96.11 · Division III

Changing Employment, Gender Relations, and the Limits of Corporate Prosperity

The employment institutions that Chapter 91 associated with the economic miracle survived the oil shocks and became more visible in the 1980s as symbols of Japanese corporate society. Large firms hired new graduates in annual cohorts each April, offered long-term employment, paid on the basis of seniority and rank (nenkō systems), provided housing, recreation, and welfare benefits, and negotiated with enterprise unions that bargained in the annual spring offensive. Observers abroad praised the arrangement as a source of loyalty and skill formation. But it applied to a minority of workers, chiefly the regular employees of large corporations and public agencies. Employees of small businesses, subcontractors, and the self-employed had lower wages, weaker benefits, and less security, and nonregular workers, including part-timers, temporary workers, and day laborers, made up a significant and growing share of the labor force, probably around one in six workers in the mid-1980s. In 1985 the Diet enacted the Worker Dispatch Law, which for the first time provided a legal framework for temporary-agency work in specified occupations, an early sign of the labor-market flexibility that became central in later decades.

Women's employment illustrates the limits of corporate prosperity. A large proportion of women worked, but many followed a pattern in which they left regular employment at marriage or childbirth and returned later as part-time workers, a pattern often described as an M-shaped curve. In large firms, women commonly held clerical positions, serving tea and performing support tasks as "office ladies" (OL), and were excluded from career-track roles. The Equal Employment Opportunity Law, enacted in 1985 and effective in April 1986, was a response to the international Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, which Japan ratified in 1985. The law prohibited discrimination in training, fringe benefits, and retirement, but for recruitment, hiring, assignment, and promotion it imposed only a duty of effort, without penalties. Companies responded by creating two-track systems that separated a career-oriented track (sōgōshoku) from a clerical track (ippanshoku), and few women entered the former in the 1980s. Other policies reinforced the pattern: the 1985 pension reform and the tax treatment of dependent spouses created incentives for married women to keep part-time earnings below thresholds. Chapter 94 examined the history of women's activism; the focus here is the economic and organizational consequences of the structure of work.

The costs of corporate prosperity were also visible in working time and health. Japanese employees worked long hours, with annual totals well above those in Western Europe and the United States, and overtime was routine. Commutes of an hour or more were common in the Tokyo region. In 1978 the physician Uehata Tetsunojō and colleagues began to describe cases of sudden death from cardiovascular disease among overworked employees, and the term karōshi entered public discourse, culminating in the establishment of "karōshi hotlines" by lawyers in 1988. In 1987 the government revised the Labor Standards Law to move toward a 40-hour standard week with a long transition. The same period also brought the first sizable wave of foreign workers filling manual jobs that Japanese youth avoided during the late-1980s labor shortage, a development that Chapter 99 takes up. The employment system, in short, delivered security and income to its core members while relying on peripheral labor, unpaid domestic work, and long hours to sustain competitiveness.

Section 96.12 · Division III

Fashion, Nightlife, Youth Consumption, and the Commercial Culture of the 1980s

The commercial culture of the 1980s was concentrated in Tokyo's districts, each with a distinct character. Shibuya was remade by the Seibu and Tōkyū groups, whose Parco (opened in 1973) and department stores treated shopping as an experience combining fashion, exhibitions, theater, and publishing. Harajuku, home to the Laforet complex (1978) and a pedestrian-only Sunday street from the late 1970s, was associated with young people and boutiques. Ginza remained the center of luxury retailing and of expense-account entertainment in hostess clubs, bars, and restaurants, where companies paid for client hospitality. Roppongi was known for discotheques, bars, and an international clientele. Karaoke, which spread in the early 1980s and took the form of private rented "boxes" from 1985, reached a mass audience, and magazines such as Hanako (launched in 1988) targeted young urban women with guides to restaurants, shops, and leisure. Students should avoid projecting the better-known Shibuya and Harajuku subcultures of the 1990s, such as the gyaru styles, back into the 1980s.

Fashion was both a commercial and an artistic field. Designers including Miyake Issey, who showed in Paris from the 1970s, and Kawakubo Rei of Comme des Garçons and Yamamoto Yōji, who presented collections in Paris in 1981, gained international recognition for work that questioned Western conventions of fit and form; their early Paris reception was divided, with some critics hostile and others admiring. At home, the 1980s were a period of widespread popularity for "designer and character" (DC) brands, which department stores and fashion buildings marketed intensively to young consumers, while imported luxury brands attracted Japanese buyers on a massive scale. The National Art Center, Tokyo's exhibition on Japanese fashion since 1945 documents this decade's importance. Domestic retail innovation, including boutique-based brand building, specialized fashion magazines, and mall-style fashion buildings, was closely connected to the international recognition of Japanese design. Retailers and publishers marketed lifestyle, and department-store groups promoted a self-consciously cultural image.

It is important to distinguish exclusive urban consumption from the experience of most households. Expense-account entertainment, designer brands, and fashionable nightlife involved a limited portion of the population, chiefly young urban professionals, company employees with entertainment budgets, and the service workers who staffed these venues. Hostesses, bartenders, boutique employees, and part-time workers, many of them women and students, were participants in a different way: they produced the glamour that others consumed, often with low pay and little security. In provincial cities and rural areas, consumption centered on supermarkets, roadside shopping, and the new mall developments. The commercial culture of the 1980s thus reveals both the capacity of an affluent society to sustain cultural experimentation and the extent to which fashion and nightlife were sites of status competition, gendered labor, and unevenly shared prosperity.

Section 96.13 · Division IV — State Reform, Urban Transformation, and Cultural Preservation

Nakasone Yasuhiro, Administrative Reform, and Privatization

Nakasone Yasuhiro, who was prime minister from November 1982 to November 1987, presided over the most extensive restructuring of public enterprises since the war. The starting point was fiscal. After 1975 the government had financed growing deficits by issuing bonds, and by the early 1980s the burden of debt service, the cost of social programs for an aging population, and the losses of public enterprises produced pressure for retrenchment. The Second Provisional Commission for Administrative Reform, chaired by the business leader Dokō Toshio from 1981 and followed by successor councils, set a goal of fiscal reconstruction without general tax increases and recommended the privatization of several public corporations. Nakasone, who cultivated an image of strong leadership and a close relationship with President Reagan, took up the program as part of what he called the "settlement of postwar politics."

Two privatizations were especially significant. The Nippon Telegraph and Telephone Public Corporation became a private company in April 1985, opening telecommunications to competition, and the government sold shares to the public in the following years. The Japanese National Railways (JNR), burdened by chronic deficits, large accumulated debts of well over thirty trillion yen, and tense relations between management and strong unions, was divided in April 1987 into six regional passenger companies and a freight company, with the debt largely assumed by a liquidation corporation. Supporters argued that privatization improved efficiency, service, and productivity; critics noted that it weakened militant public-sector unions, led to the closure of unprofitable local lines, and left thousands of employees reassigned, retired, or excluded from the successor companies, with some of the struggles lasting years. For rural and island communities, the loss of rail service meant a loss of mobility; for urban commuters, the new companies brought service improvements. The program also changed the relationship between state and management by shifting responsibility from ministries to corporate boards while retaining government oversight.

Nakasone's government also raised the defense budget above the informal one-percent-of-GNP ceiling in fiscal 1987 and made an official visit to Yasukuni Shrine in August 1985, an act that drew protests from China and other neighbors. Its remarks on national identity prompted controversy: in 1986 Nakasone's comments about a homogeneous society and the "intelligence" of minorities elsewhere offended minority communities, and Ainu organizations protested the claim that Japan was a single-ethnic nation (Section 96.16). The consumption tax of 3 percent, enacted in December 1988 and effective in April 1989, belongs to the subsequent government of Takeshita Noboru and not to Nakasone, whose attempt to introduce a broad indirect tax had failed. It was unpopular, and together with the Recruit stock scandal that implicated politicians and officials, it damaged the LDP in the 1989 upper-house election. Administrative reform thus reshaped the state's economic role at the very moment that private finance was expanding, a conjunction that matters for understanding the bubble.

Section 96.14 · Division IV

Financial Liberalization, Banking Competition, and Corporate Finance

The postwar financial system had been built around banks. Households saved in bank and postal accounts, whose interest rates were regulated, and banks lent to firms at rates that were also managed. Major commercial banks stood at the center of industrial groups and acted as "main banks" for their clients, monitoring them and extending credit in difficulty. Financial segmentation separated city banks, long-term credit banks, trust banks, regional banks, and securities firms, and the Ministry of Finance supervised the system with an informal commitment to protect the survival of institutions, often described as a convoy system. Corporate borrowing from banks financed the high-growth industrial investment described in Chapter 91, and the system's stability depended on ongoing high growth.

The slowdown after 1973 began to erode this arrangement. As growth declined, large firms needed less new bank credit, and the government, running deficits, issued large volumes of bonds that required an active secondary market and attractive interest rates. Liberalization proceeded gradually through the late 1970s and 1980s: certificates of deposit were introduced in 1979, the Foreign Exchange Law was revised in 1980 to permit transactions in principle freely, an agreement between Japanese and American authorities in 1984 on yen-dollar issues opened further opportunities for foreign participation, interest rates on large deposits were progressively freed from 1985, and the Tokyo offshore market was created in 1986. Large corporations gained access to domestic and overseas bond markets, including issues of convertible and warrant bonds, so that firms with strong credit ratings could raise capital at lower costs and with less reliance on banks.

The consequences for banks were profound. Their best customers were moving away, and they sought new borrowers in smaller firms, in property-related businesses, in housing-loan affiliates, and in consumer lending. Land provided convenient collateral because its value had risen for decades, and lending against land seemed safe. Financial liberalization did not itself create a bubble: it expanded options, increased competition, and weakened old protective arrangements, while the regulatory framework remained designed for a more controlled system. This section establishes the long-term structural background. The immediate monetary easing of 1986–1987, which gave these structural incentives their stimulus, belongs to Section 96.18, and students should not conflate the two.

Section 96.15 · Division IV

Changing Urban Landscapes: Tokyo, Regional Cities, Housing, and Development

The physical environment of Japan's cities changed visibly in the late 1970s and 1980s. Tokyo's Shinjuku district, where skyscrapers had risen from the 1970s, continued to expand as an office center, Ikebukuro's Sunshine 60 (1978) became a landmark, and redevelopment projects such as the Ark Hills complex in Akasaka and Roppongi (1986) pioneered large private-sector urban renewal. Waterfront and new-town projects, including Yokohama's Minato Mirai 21 and the Makuhari area of Chiba, were planned as commercial and residential centers. The Tsukuba Science City, the site of the 1985 Expo, symbolized the research-oriented direction of national policy. Infrastructure also advanced: the Tōhoku and Jōetsu Shinkansen lines opened in 1982, the Seikan railway tunnel to Hokkaido and the Seto Ōhashi bridge to Shikoku were completed in 1988, and preparations for a new Kansai airport began in the later 1980s.

Tokyo's role as an international financial and corporate center grew with deregulation, foreign banks and securities firms' entry, and the establishment of the offshore market. As Japanese firms internationalized and foreign institutions expanded their presence, demand for office space in central Tokyo rose sharply, and in the later 1980s office rents and vacancy rates reflected a shortage. The spatial pattern had social costs. Housing costs and land scarcity pushed families to the periphery and into longer commutes, and in central neighborhoods, small shops, wooden houses, and long-established businesses were displaced by redevelopment, sometimes through forced land assembly by speculators called jiageya, a practice that used pressure, harassment, and intermediaries linked to organized crime. Property owners benefited from rising prices while renters and small business tenants faced eviction and higher costs. The land-use rules and tax system, which favored holding land, did little to reduce speculation.

Regional differences widened. Some provincial cities gained from new plants, highways, and tourism, while industrial towns undergoing restructuring sought alternatives, and rural districts faced continued out-migration and aging. Hokkaido's coal-mining communities, such as Yūbari, saw mine closures, and mountain and island villages lost schools and services. In 1988–1989 the government distributed funds of one hundred million yen to every municipality for "hometown creation" (furusato sōsei), a program that supported local projects of widely varying value. Okinawa, which had returned to Japanese administration in 1972, hosted the 1975 Ocean Expo and developed a tourism sector, but its land remained constrained by the continued presence of American bases, and in the bubble years mainland firms bought Okinawan and Hokkaido land for resorts and golf courses. These patterns established the urban geography that made land increasingly valuable before the price inflation described in Division V.

Section 96.16 · Division IV

Traditional Culture in a Modern Consumer Society: Heritage, Craft, Tourism, and Preservation

Economic modernization threatened historic environments, and it also created new interest in preserving them. Urban redevelopment, highway construction, and housing demand put pressure on old streets, temples, and farmhouses, while rising incomes and leisure time created a domestic market for tourism that emphasized the past. In 1970, following the Osaka Expo, Japanese National Railways launched the "Discover Japan" campaign, and magazines such as an·an and non-no encouraged young women to travel to historic towns and rural settings. In the 1970s residents of Tsumago, a former post station on the Nakasendō in Nagano Prefecture, adopted principles of not selling, renting, or demolishing the old buildings, and their efforts became a model for community-based preservation. Kyoto adopted landscape ordinances in 1972 to limit building height and protect its historic setting.

National policy followed. In 1974 the Law for the Promotion of Traditional Craft Industries provided support for designated regional crafts such as textiles, lacquerware, ceramics, and metalwork, and created a certification mark. In 1975 an amendment to the Law for the Protection of Cultural Properties introduced the system of Preservation Districts for Groups of Traditional Buildings, which the Agency for Cultural Affairs identifies as a major institutional development. The system allowed municipalities to designate historic townscapes, adopt local preservation plans, and seek national selection of the most important districts, beginning in 1976, including Tsumago-juku in Nagano and Kyoto's Sannei-zaka and Gion Shimbashi, with subsidies for repair and restoration. The framework recognized that heritage consisted not only of individual monuments but of streets, villages, and the communities that lived in them. Municipalities, craft associations, specialists, and residents negotiated what could be rebuilt, what uses were permitted, and how to balance maintenance costs against tourism income. In Okinawa the reconstruction of Shuri Castle's main buildings began in the 1980s, and the project connected preservation with Ryukyuan historical identity and the reassertion of regional culture after the war and the American administration.

Preservation was contested. Residents of designated districts faced restrictions on alterations and rebuilding and sometimes disliked the commercialization that tourism brought, while property owners weighed the value of land against conservation obligations. Heritage was also an instrument of identity politics. The 1980s saw a surge of discourse on Japanese uniqueness (Nihonjinron), and the claim of ethnic homogeneity sat uneasily with the existence of Ainu, Ryukyuan, Korean, and other communities. Ainu organizations in 1984 proposed a new law to replace the Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899, and the protests of 1986 followed Nakasone's remarks; an Ainu Cultural Promotion Act was passed only in 1997, as Chapter 94 and later chapters explain. In this chapter, the point is that heritage in the 1970s and 1980s was an economic activity, a public-policy responsibility, and a contested field in which communities, tourism enterprises, and the state defined what was to be preserved and for whom.

Section 96.17 · Division V — The Formation and Peak of the Japanese Asset Bubble

The Plaza Accord of 1985, Yen Appreciation, and Japan's Economic Adjustment

In the first half of the 1980s the American dollar rose to very high levels against other currencies, driven by high U.S. interest rates and by large federal deficits that attracted foreign capital. The strong dollar swelled American trade deficits, intensified protectionist pressure in Congress, and pushed Washington toward a policy change. On September 22, 1985, finance ministers and central bank governors of the five leading industrial economies (the G5: the United States, Japan, West Germany, France, and Britain) met at the Plaza Hotel in New York and announced that further orderly appreciation of the main non-dollar currencies against the dollar was desirable and that they were prepared to cooperate to encourage it. Japan was represented by Finance Minister Takeshita Noboru and Bank of Japan Governor Sumita Satoshi. Coordinated intervention in currency markets followed immediately.

The yen's appreciation was far faster than many Japanese officials had expected. From roughly 240 yen to the dollar in September 1985, it rose to about 200 by the end of that year, to about 160 by the end of 1986, and to roughly 120–125 by the end of 1987. Exporters faced a sharp loss of price competitiveness and, in many cases, of profit margins, and commentators warned of a strong-yen recession, endaka fukyō. The effects varied: large exporters such as automobile and electronics firms absorbed part of the loss by cutting costs, raising efficiency, and shifting production and procurement abroad, while small subcontractors, textile and ceramics producers, and regional manufacturers dependent on export orders were hit harder. A stronger yen also lowered the cost of imported fuel and raw materials, held down domestic prices, and increased the purchasing power of Japanese firms and households abroad, which supported overseas acquisitions, direct investment, and foreign travel. Real output continued to grow, but more slowly in 1986.

The agreement belongs at the center of the bubble's prehistory, but it should not be described as independently causing the bubble. The Plaza communiqué addressed exchange rates and external imbalances, but it also recorded Japan-specific policy intentions, including flexible monetary-policy management attentive to the yen, financial liberalization, and measures to stimulate consumption and investment through credit markets. Those broad statements did not prescribe the particular discount-rate cuts of 1986–1987 or a fixed domestic credit target. What followed depended on decisions by Japanese authorities in response to the strong yen and to international pressure to stimulate domestic demand, including the Maekawa Report of 1986 and the Louvre Accord of February 1987, in which the G7 agreed to stabilize exchange rates. The Plaza Accord created conditions and pressures within which policymakers chose a path; other paths were conceivable. That distinction is the subject of the next section and of the debate that closes the chapter.

Section 96.18 · Division V

Monetary Easing, Credit Expansion, and the Conditions for Speculation

The Bank of Japan's official discount rate stood at 5 percent at the beginning of 1986. Over the following thirteen months the Bank cut it in five steps, to 3 percent by November 1986 and to a then-unprecedented 2.5 percent in February 1987, and it held the rate at that level until May 1989. The reasons offered at the time were several: the strong yen threatened exporters and might cause recession; domestic inflation was very low, and indeed wholesale prices were falling, because of cheaper imports; international partners, and the Maekawa Report's call for domestic-demand-led growth, pressed Japan to stimulate its economy; and after the Louvre Accord of February 1987 and the stock-market crash of October 1987, there was concern that tightening would disturb exchange rates and global markets. A large fiscal stimulus package in 1987 reinforced the policy. Bank of Japan research written later identifies both the prolonged easing and the rapid growth of money and credit as important conditions in the development of the bubble.

Low interest rates alone did not produce speculation. They interacted with the structural changes of Section 96.14. Banks had lost large corporate borrowers and were looking for lending opportunities; land was accepted as collateral, and its rising value increased borrowing capacity; large firms with cheap access to bond and equity markets could invest in financial assets; and regulators, whose attention was fixed on consumer prices, saw little inflation. Bank lending to property-related businesses, nonbank financial institutions, and construction grew rapidly, and total bank lending expanded at around ten percent a year or more in the late 1980s. A great deal of this credit financed ordinary investment in plant, offices, and housing; a significant portion financed the purchase of land and shares, which raised their prices, which in turn increased the collateral value that supported further borrowing. The explanation must therefore avoid reducing a complex episode to the decisions of one institution: the central bank, the Ministry of Finance, commercial banks, securities firms, corporations, and households all responded to incentives that reinforced one another.

A critical question concerns what policymakers could and should have known. Retrospective studies have stressed that officials in 1987 faced conflicting objectives, that consumer prices appeared stable, and that rising asset prices were widely interpreted as reflecting real improvements in earnings and in Tokyo's international status. Some officials and Bank of Japan staff expressed concern about asset-price inflation as early as 1987–1988, but the Bank kept rates low until 1989. How to weigh these considerations remains a matter of interpretation, discussed in the Historical Debate that concludes the chapter. For now, the essential point is that the monetary conditions of 1986–1989 were permissive rather than deterministic: they made speculation cheap, but they did not by themselves compel anyone to speculate.

Historical Vocabulary
Endaka, Monetary Easing, Asset Bubble, Collateral, and Financial Liberalization

Endaka ("high yen") means the appreciation of the yen against foreign currencies; endaka fukyō is the "strong-yen recession" feared after 1985. Monetary easing (kinyū kanwa) means lowering policy interest rates or otherwise increasing the availability of money and credit. An asset bubble (baburu) is a period in which asset prices rise well above levels justified by underlying earnings or rents, sustained in part by expectations that prices will keep rising; the term was applied widely only after the collapse and then used in Japanese as baburu keiki, "bubble economy." Collateral (tanpo) is property pledged to secure a loan. Financial liberalization (kinyū jiyūka) is the relaxation of controls on interest rates, capital movements, and the activities of financial institutions.

Students should note that "bubble" is a retrospective label. Contemporaries argued about whether prices reflected fundamentals, and the word became common in Japan around 1990. It should not be used to suggest that participants consciously knew they were in one.

Section 96.19 · Division V

Land Speculation, Rising Stock Prices, and the Culture of the Bubble Economy

Asset prices accelerated from 1986 and especially from 1987. The Nikkei 225, which stood at around 13,000 at the end of 1985, closed 1986 near 18,700, 1987 near 21,600, 1988 near 30,200, and 1989 near 38,900. The initial public offering of Nippon Telegraph and Telephone shares in 1987 drew millions of individual applicants and helped bring a new class of small investors into the stock market. Corporations engaged in zaitech, financial engineering in which firms used funds raised in bond and equity markets to invest in securities through special trust accounts, and for some companies, financial profits rivaled or exceeded earnings from their main business. Securities houses such as Nomura and Daiwa, banks, and insurance companies were prominent participants, and the stock market's total capitalization exceeded that of New York by the later 1980s.

Land prices rose first in central Tokyo's commercial districts, driven by demand for office space, then spread to residential areas in the metropolitan region and to Osaka, Nagoya, and other cities. Investment in golf-club memberships, resort developments, and condominiums became widespread. The mechanism linking assets and credit was central: a landowner could borrow against rising property values to buy more land or shares; developers and speculators borrowed from banks and nonbank lenders, including housing-loan companies (jūsen) established by banks; and the expectation of continued gains made lenders willing to extend more credit. Land was lightly taxed relative to its value, and a belief in the "land myth," the idea that land prices never fell, reinforced the pattern. Japanese buyers also bought assets abroad. Sony acquired CBS Records in 1988 and Columbia Pictures in 1989, Mitsubishi Estate bought a controlling interest in New York's Rockefeller Center in 1989, and a Japanese insurance company bought Van Gogh's Sunflowers in 1987 for about forty million dollars; other paintings fetched even larger sums in 1990. Popular stories, such as the claim that the land under the Imperial Palace was worth more than all of California, circulated widely and are better read as emblems of the era's confidence than as careful valuations.

The relationship between real achievement and speculative confidence is the heart of the story. Japan's manufacturing success, strong yen, large trade surplus, and international prestige made optimism seem reasonable; books and commentators described the country as an economic leader, and the success of its firms and its high savings seemed to justify high valuations. But the beliefs that asset prices were sustainable and that growth would continue indefinitely were extrapolations. The bubble also did not affect all places and groups equally. Tokyo and the other major metropolitan markets saw the largest increases; many provincial areas experienced smaller gains or only the resort and golf-course fringe; landowners and holders of shares gained while renters and young buyers were priced out; and workers in manufacturing and services experienced the period as a labor shortage that raised demand for part-time work. The word furītā, a young person who chose casual jobs rather than regular employment, was coined in 1987, and foreign workers began to fill manual jobs. The political culture also reflected the era: the Recruit scandal of 1988, in which unlisted shares of a company in the information-services and real-estate business were given to politicians and officials, became a symbol of the intertwining of money and politics in the boom.

Section 96.20 · Division V

The Peak of the Bubble, the End of Late Shōwa, and the Approach of the Lost Decades

On January 7, 1989, Emperor Shōwa (Hirohito) died after a reign of more than sixty-two years, and the Heisei era began the next day. The national mood was one of mourning and reflection, and public observances in some respects subdued the commercial exuberance of the preceding years. The end of the Shōwa era marked a generational and symbolic boundary, the close of a reign that had included empire, war, defeat, occupation, and the economic miracle, and it coincided with the approaching climax of the asset boom. The consumption tax took effect in April, and political scandal weakened the LDP.

Monetary policy turned in 1989. The Bank of Japan raised the official discount rate from 2.5 to 3.25 percent on May 31, 1989, the first increase in nearly nine years, and to 3.75 percent in October. In December Mieno Yasushi became governor and the rate was raised to 4.25 percent, then to 5.25 percent in March 1990 and 6 percent in August 1990. The Nikkei 225 closed at a record of about 38,915 on December 29, 1989 and then fell sharply, dropping below 20,000 at times in the autumn of 1990 and closing the year near 23,800. The Gulf crisis of August 1990 raised oil prices again, reminding Japan of the vulnerability this chapter began with. In 1990 the Ministry of Finance introduced quantitative controls on bank lending to the real-estate sector, and the Land Basic Law of 1989 declared the principle that land should be used in the public interest.

Students should distinguish the December 1989 stock-market peak from the later behavior of land prices and from the prolonged financial crisis. Land prices in the major cities continued to rise or remained high into 1990 and 1991 before beginning a long decline, and the economy as a whole, according to official business-cycle dating, continued to expand until early 1991, so that the boom of the late 1980s is sometimes called the Heisei boom. Between 1991 and the later 1990s, falling collateral values weakened bank balance sheets, scandals involving loss compensation for favored clients by securities houses and illegal lending practices exposed weaknesses in financial supervision, and firms and households began adjusting to a world in which asset prices no longer rose. The sustained banking crisis, nonperforming loans, prolonged stagnation, irregular employment, and the wider social consequences of the collapse belong chiefly to Chapter 97.

The final historical argument of this chapter is that Japan's bubble developed from a combination of genuine industrial achievement, institutional transformation, monetary conditions, financial incentives, and expectations of uninterrupted prosperity. Each element was in some sense a product of success: the adaptation to the oil shocks, the competitiveness of Japanese manufacturers, the liberalization of finance, the international standing that produced capital inflows and outflows, and the confidence that accompanied them. Together, they created an economy in which asset prices, credit, and expectations reinforced one another beyond any level justified by earnings, and in which the institutions that might have restrained the process were designed for a different era. By 1991 the optimism of the late Shōwa years was giving way to mounting financial vulnerability, and the transition from adaptation to adjustment, rather than to stagnation as such, had begun.

Historical Debate

Was the 1980s Bubble Principally a Monetary-Policy Failure, a Financial-System Failure, or the Result of Interacting Forces?

The question: Historians and economists have long debated why Japan experienced such a large asset boom and whether it could have been prevented.

Interpretation A — Monetary policy: Some scholars argue that the Bank of Japan kept interest rates too low for too long in 1986–1989, driven by concern over the strong yen and by international pressure, and that earlier tightening would have restrained credit and asset prices. They emphasize the timing of the rate cuts and the delay in raising them despite rapid credit growth.
Interpretation B — Financial-system and regulatory failure: Other scholars stress that liberalization outpaced supervision, that banks lacked good lending practices and relied too much on land collateral, that tax and land-use policies encouraged speculation, and that regulators protected institutions in ways that weakened discipline. In this view, even different interest-rate policies might not have prevented excesses.

Evidence: Both views rely on discount-rate chronologies, money and credit series, bank lending data, asset-price indexes, and the records and recollections of officials. Most recent scholarship, including central-bank research, emphasizes that the episode resulted from interacting factors: monetary conditions, deregulation, credit practices, tax and land policy, international pressure, and shifting expectations.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians judge the responsibility of particular institutions and what lessons are drawn for later policy, and it affects the causal link between the bubble and the long stagnation analyzed in Chapter 97.

Adjustment and Affluence: Five Experiences

A steelworker in a restructured mill town: A regular employee might retain a job through reassignment or secondment, but a mill's contraction meant fewer hires, uncertain prospects for the local community, and pressure on local businesses. A female office worker: Often employed on a clerical track, she benefited from consumer culture and rising salaries but faced limited promotion, the expectation of leaving at marriage, and the effects of a law that encouraged equality while imposing few obligations. A small-business owner: A subcontractor or shopkeeper might suffer from price demands by large firms and from the strong yen, or benefit from a landowner's rising property value, or be pushed out by redevelopment. Experience depended on location, ownership, and clients.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From High-Growth Industrial Capitalism to the Financialized Consumer Economy

What Changed

  • Growth slowed from the miracle pace to a moderate rate, and heavy industry gave way to precision and electronics manufacturing.
  • Energy efficiency improved substantially and the energy mix diversified.
  • Firms became multinational and relied less on banks; finance was progressively liberalized.
  • Consumption shifted toward brands, technology, leisure, and travel; land and shares became sources of wealth.
  • Public enterprises were privatized, and the state's regulatory role changed.

What Remained Continuous

  • Regular-employment institutions and enterprise unionism, along with a divided labor market
  • Bank-centered finance and Ministry of Finance supervision
  • Gendered work patterns and the clerical track for women
  • Regional inequality and the dominance of metropolitan areas
  • Dependence on imported energy and on export markets

Who experienced change most directly? Workers and communities in declining industries, urban landowners and investors, and managers of privatized enterprises. Who experienced greater continuity? Women in clerical and part-time work, rural households, and residents of Okinawa and other lagging regions. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the same transformation could mean opportunity or insecurity depending on position in the economy.

Cause and Effect

From Energy Crisis to Asset Inflation

Long-term causes

A growth model dependent on imported energy and exports; a bank-centered financial system; a high national savings rate; land scarcity and a belief in rising land values; slowdown after the miracle years; international monetary changes since 1971.

Short-term causes

The oil shocks and policy response; the competitive success of manufacturers; trade friction and the Plaza Accord; the yen's appreciation; interest-rate cuts in 1986–1987; financial liberalization; and expectations of continued gains.

Event or transformation

Rapid credit growth financed investment and speculation, and land and share prices rose well beyond earnings, reaching a stock peak in December 1989 and a land peak around 1990–1991.

Immediate effects

Rising wealth for owners, a labor shortage and growth in casual and foreign labor, overseas acquisitions, higher housing costs, political scandal, and a monetary tightening beginning in 1989.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The collapse weakened bank balance sheets and began the prolonged stagnation examined in Chapter 97. But the bubble cannot be explained by one cause: energy shocks produced adaptation and competitiveness, the Plaza Accord created exchange-rate pressure, monetary easing made credit cheap, liberalization changed bank incentives, and expectations amplified each. The oil shock is not the sole cause of industrial restructuring, nor the Plaza Accord of the bubble, and the process was not predetermined.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked how Japan overcame the vulnerabilities revealed by the oil shocks, became a global center of advanced manufacturing and consumer affluence, and developed the economic and financial conditions that produced the asset bubble of the late 1980s. Its first conclusion is that Japan responded to the end of extraordinary high growth through adaptation rather than stagnation. Its second is that the transformation affected far more than production. Its third is that the achievements of late-Shōwa Japan fostered confidence in continued prosperity, while financial liberalization, easy credit, asset-market incentives, and speculative expectations created vulnerabilities that economic success alone could not resolve.

Japan entered the 1970s with an economy built on cheap imported petroleum, which supplied roughly three-quarters of its primary energy. The Nixon shocks, the floating of the yen, and domestic overheating in 1972–1973 had already signaled that the growth model was changing, and the 1973–1974 oil crisis accelerated that change. Inflation exceeded twenty percent in 1974, real output fell for the first time in the postwar period, and public anxiety, most visibly in panic buying of household goods, showed how fears of shortage could amplify a limited disruption. The distinction between the slowdown already under way and the specific impact of the oil crisis remained central to the analysis.

Policy and corporate responses established a durable adaptation. The Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, the Sunshine and Moonlight Programs, petroleum stockpiling, the 1979 Act on the Rational Use of Energy, and the diversification into natural gas, coal, and nuclear power reduced petroleum dependence and energy intensity. When the second shock arrived in 1979, Japan's inflation was far milder, its adjustment smoother, and its growth steadier, though at a moderate rate. Conservation served energy security and sometimes the environment, but the two goals were not identical.

Industrial restructuring was both opportunity and burden. Shipbuilding, steel, aluminum, textiles, and parts of petrochemicals shrank, and workers, subcontractors, and regional communities bore the costs, cushioned for regular employees by employment-adjustment subsidies, secondments, and early retirement. At the same time, precision machinery, automobiles, electronics, robotics, and semiconductors expanded. Japanese automakers combined fuel-efficient products, the Toyota Production System, supplier networks, and a labor system built on a secure core and a flexible periphery; in 1980 Japan became the world's largest automobile producer. In electronics, cooperative research such as the VLSI project and corporate investment brought leadership in memory chips and certain consumer products, while failures such as the Fifth Generation project and weaknesses in software show that success was uneven.

Success in export markets generated trade friction. Voluntary export restraints on automobiles in 1981, the 1986 semiconductor agreement, and the Maekawa Report illustrate the political limits Japan faced, while the globalization of Japanese business produced factories and acquisitions in North America and Europe and dense production networks in East and Southeast Asia. Anxiety about "hollowing out" and the racialized hostility of some foreign responses form part of the story. Corporate success could not be treated as proof of universal superiority, because it depended on particular industries, markets, and labor arrangements.

Affluence reshaped everyday life. Home video recorders, the Walkman, compact discs, personal computers, and the Famicom changed how households listened, watched, and played; convenience stores, supermarkets, credit, travel, and leisure expanded; and surveys showed nine in ten people identifying as middle class. Yet the ideal of a middle-class society concealed differences between owners and renters, regular and nonregular workers, large and small firms, men and women, Tokyo and the provinces, and mainland and Okinawa. The Equal Employment Opportunity Law of 1985–1986 coexisted with two-track systems that kept most women out of career positions, while overwork gave rise to the concept of karōshi. The commercial culture of Shibuya, Harajuku, Ginza, and Roppongi, the rise of designer brands, and the international recognition of Japanese fashion involved a limited group of consumers and a larger workforce that supplied their pleasures.

The state and the financial system were restructured. Nakasone's administrative reform privatized NTT in 1985 and divided and privatized JNR in 1987 in response to fiscal pressure, with consequences for workers, rural rail service, and the relationship between ministries and firms. Financial liberalization eroded the bank-centered system by giving large firms access to capital markets and driving banks toward property and other new lending. In cities, office towers, redevelopment, and infrastructure made land more valuable, accentuating regional disparities. Heritage preservation advanced through the 1974 craft law and the 1975 Preservation Districts, while contests over development, tourism, and identity, including the claims of Ainu and Ryukyuan communities against assertions of homogeneity, kept culture a contested field.

The bubble itself arose from the interaction of the Plaza Accord's strong yen, the Bank of Japan's reduction of the discount rate to 2.5 percent and its maintenance until May 1989, expanding bank credit, land collateral, financial deregulation, and expectations that prices would continue to rise. The Nikkei peaked at about 38,915 on December 29, 1989, while land prices peaked later and the broader financial crisis developed over years. The Plaza Accord did not independently cause the bubble, and the monetary conditions were permissive rather than deterministic. Scholars continue to weigh monetary policy, supervision, tax and land policy, and expectations, with most emphasizing interacting causes.

The chapter ends in 1991 with the death of Shōwa behind and the Heisei boom at its end, as optimism gave way to financial vulnerability. The significance of the period lies in its combination of real achievement and hidden fragility: Japan became a model of industrial adaptation and a country of unprecedented consumer wealth, yet the institutions and expectations that carried it to that point also carried the seeds of the problems that Chapter 97 examines.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Oil shock (sekiyu shokku)
The sharp increases in petroleum prices and threats to supply in 1973–1974 and 1979–1980 that ended Japan's extraordinary high growth and prompted a reconstruction of energy policy.
Nixon shocks
The 1971 American announcements of a China visit and of the suspension of dollar–gold convertibility with an import surcharge, which unsettled Japan's diplomacy and its fixed exchange rate.
Agency for Natural Resources and Energy
The energy-policy agency created within MITI in July 1973, whose role expanded during the oil crisis.
Petroleum Supply and Demand Optimization Law (1973)
Emergency statute giving the government powers to set targets and allocate petroleum, enacted in December 1973 alongside the Emergency Law for the Stabilization of Living.
Sunshine and Moonlight Programs
MITI research programs launched in 1974 and 1978 for new energy technologies and for energy-saving technologies respectively.
Act on the Rational Use of Energy (1979)
The Energy Conservation Law, which imposed management duties and efficiency standards on designated large energy users.
Energy security
Reliable access to fuel at tolerable cost, pursued through diplomacy, stockpiling, diversification, and efficiency.
Energy intensity
The amount of energy consumed per unit of economic output; its decline after 1973 measured Japan's adaptation.
Industrial restructuring (sangyō kōzō chōsei)
The reorganization of the economy as energy-intensive and labor-intensive industries shrank and technology-intensive ones grew, aided by laws on depressed industries in 1978 and 1983.
Employment adjustment subsidy
A benefit created under the 1974 Employment Insurance Law to help firms retain surplus workers instead of dismissing them.
Shukkō
Secondment of employees from a parent firm to a subsidiary or affiliate, used to manage surplus labor.
Shitauke
Subcontracting relationships in which small firms supplied larger manufacturers, often absorbing cost pressures.
Keihakutansō
"Light, thin, short, small": the slogan for miniaturized, high-value products characteristic of the post-1973 industrial shift.
Toyota Production System
A production system developed at Toyota, associated with Ōno Taiichi, centered on just-in-time delivery, kanban signaling, worker authority to stop the line, and kaizen.
Kaizen
Continuous incremental improvement involving workers in suggesting and testing changes.
Voluntary export restraint
An export ceiling accepted by the exporting government under foreign pressure, such as the 1981 automobile limits.
VLSI initiative (1976–1979)
MITI-organized cooperative research project among leading electronics firms and public institutions on very-large-scale integrated circuits.
Maekawa Report (1986)
A government-commissioned report urging a shift from export-led to domestic-demand-led growth and a reduction of external surpluses.
Walkman, Betamax, VHS, Famicom
Consumer-electronics products and formats of 1975–1983 that illustrate standards competition and the shift to personal technology.
Ichioku sōchūryū
The idea of a "hundred-million-strong middle class," supported by surveys in which about nine in ten identified as middle class, but not proof of equality.
Nenkō system
Pay and promotion based on length of service and rank, characteristic of regular employment in large firms.
Equal Employment Opportunity Law (1985)
Law effective in 1986 that prohibited some forms of workplace discrimination against women but imposed only efforts on recruitment, hiring, assignment, and promotion.
Sōgōshoku / ippanshoku
The career-track and clerical-track employment categories that many firms created after the 1985 law.
Karōshi
Death from overwork, a term that entered public discourse from the late 1970s and 1980s.
Worker Dispatch Law (1985)
Statute creating a legal framework for temporary-agency work in specified occupations.
DC brands
"Designer and character" fashion brands promoted through department stores and fashion buildings in the 1980s.
Administrative reform (gyōsei kaikaku)
The program of fiscal retrenchment and privatization pursued by commissions and by the Nakasone government in the early to mid-1980s.
Financial liberalization
The gradual relaxation of controls on interest rates, capital flows, and institutional activities from the late 1970s through the 1980s.
Main bank system
The pattern in which a firm's principal lender monitored it and supported it in difficulty, weakened as firms shifted to capital markets.
Preservation Districts for Groups of Traditional Buildings
The 1975 system allowing designation and protection of historic townscapes, with national selection beginning in 1976.
Plaza Accord (1985)
The September 22, 1985 G5 announcement encouraging orderly appreciation of non-dollar currencies, followed by rapid yen appreciation.
Endaka fukyō
The "strong-yen recession" feared in 1986 as exporters faced higher dollar prices.
Zaitech
Financial engineering by corporations investing borrowed or raised funds in securities, prominent in the late 1980s.
Asset bubble (baburu)
A period of asset prices far above levels supported by earnings, sustained by expectations of further increases; here the late-1980s boom in Japanese shares and land.
Heisei boom
The expansion that official dating places from late 1986 to early 1991, often identified with the bubble economy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a society responds to the end of a successful growth model. Japan's adaptation after 1973 was neither inevitable nor accidental: it combined state institutions, corporate strategy, labor restraint, technological innovation, and the particular character of international markets. The result was a remarkable record of efficiency and competitiveness. But the same period shows that adaptation has distributional consequences, and that institutions built for one set of conditions can generate new risks when conditions change.

The chapter also connects regions and societies often kept apart in narratives of national success. The shipyards of Kure and Sasebo, the coalfields of Hokkaido, the factories of Aichi, the offices of Tokyo, the resorts and bases of Okinawa, the plants of Ohio and Thailand, and the households that purchased Walkmans and the households that did not were parts of the same story. Ainu and Ryukyuan communities, women workers, subcontractors, and migrant laborers appear at the margins of the prosperity they helped sustain.

The developments of these years shaped what followed. The employment and financial institutions that supported the economy of the 1980s were strained by the bubble's collapse, as Chapter 97 will show; the question of nonregular labor, introduced in the 1985 dispatch law and the casual work of the late 1980s, became a defining social issue; energy policy and the siting of nuclear plants would return in Chapter 99; and debates over national identity, heritage, and regional rights continued into the twenty-first century as Chapter 100 explains.

Finally, the chapter invites caution about hindsight. Policymakers, executives, and investors in 1987 did not know that the boom would end as it did, and observers abroad were as impressed as Japanese were. Understanding the bubble requires neither excusing nor condemning but reconstructing the incentives, constraints, and beliefs that made each decision seem reasonable at the time. Students who approach the period in this way are prepared to examine the lost decades without treating them as the inevitable result of the achievements that preceded them.
