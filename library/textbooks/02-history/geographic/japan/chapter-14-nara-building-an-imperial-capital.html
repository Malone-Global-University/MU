Part III — Nara and Heian Japan
14

Nara: Building an Imperial Capital

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A law is only words until someone must carry out its instructions. Chapter 13 followed the making of the ritsuryō state: its codes, its ranks, its provinces and districts, its registers. This chapter asks what happened when the court tried to make that framework work, in a new capital built for the purpose, with officials to staff it, roads to reach beyond it, and taxes to feed it.

In 710, the court of Empress Genmei left Fujiwara-kyō and relocated to a new planned city on the northern edge of the Nara Basin. Its contemporaries called it Heijō-kyō; the name "Nara" for the city and the period is a later and more familiar label. The move placed the sovereign, the ministries, the aristocratic families, and a growing population of officials, artisans, laborers, and merchants in an urban space laid out on a rectilinear grid, with a walled palace at its northern center and a broad avenue running south from the palace gate. Everything about the layout was meant to be read. Who lived near the palace and who lived far from it, where markets stood, and where the sovereign received officials on ceremonial occasions all expressed a particular idea of political order.

Yet a city is also a machine for gathering things. Officials at Heijō consumed rice, cloth, salt, dried fish, seaweed, timber, and labor that had to be produced and delivered from provinces hundreds of kilometers away. Instructions from the capital had to reach governors and district magistrates; reports, registers, and shipments had to travel back. The archaeological remains of the palace and the capital include tens of thousands of inscribed wooden tablets (mokkan) that record precisely these mundane transactions. They show a state operating below the level of grand chronicles, through labels, requisitions, and receipts. This chapter uses them, together with the palace foundations, the city plan, the codes, and the court chronicle Shoku Nihongi, to examine how the Nara state worked.

The chapter's governing question is this: How did the Nara court turn the legal framework of the ritsuryō state into a functioning capital-centered system of government, and how centralized was that system in practice? The answer developed here is qualified. Heijō-kyō made the new imperial order physically visible, and ranks, offices, provinces, roads, registers, and taxes connected the capital to much of the archipelago more systematically than any earlier Yamato court had managed. But the Nara state was not a modern centralized bureaucracy. It depended on aristocratic families at court, hereditary local elites in the provinces, household labor, slow communications, and negotiated implementation. Even the capital itself was not fixed: during the 740s the court left Heijō for several years, and in 784 it left again.

This chapter also observes a boundary. It concerns the political and administrative organization of the Nara state. The great Buddhist temples of the capital, state-sponsored Buddhism, the Great Buddha, and the political influence of clergy belong to Chapter 15. The rural societies, local elites, frontier expansion, and Emishi communities of the provinces, and taxation as lived by rural households, belong to Chapter 16. The move away from the Nara-centered order and the founding of Heian-kyō belong to Chapter 17. Here those subjects appear only as far as needed to understand how the state functioned.

Students should also keep in mind who is, and is not, visible in the evidence. Officials, aristocrats, and the court's chroniclers wrote most of the surviving texts. Taxpayers, porters, laborers, women in palace service, and ordinary residents of the city appear mainly as names on tablets, as entries in registers, or as categories in legal provisions. The chapter attempts to read these sources against the grain where possible, and to acknowledge silences where it is not. It also resists two temptations: to describe Nara as a fully realized bureaucratic state that simply worked as its codes prescribed, and to dismiss the codes as empty formalities. The evidence supports neither extreme.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Heijō-kyō as both a place and an argument: an argument, made in earth, timber, and roof tile, about how authority should be arranged. They should also understand why historians treat the Nara state as a real and consequential attempt at centralization whose reach and effectiveness were nonetheless uneven and constantly negotiated.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the court moved to Heijō-kyō in 710 and how a planned capital differed from earlier palace-centered courts.
  2. Analyze the spatial organization of Heijō-kyō as an expression of political order, while distinguishing continental influence from simple copying.
  3. Describe the palace as residence, ritual center, and administrative headquarters.
  4. Trace the organization of central government, including the Council of State, the ministries, and the rank system.
  5. Distinguish bureaucratic organization from bureaucratic meritocracy in the Nara state.
  6. Interpret how aristocratic and princely rivalry, including the Nagaya affair of 729, operated alongside written administration.
  7. Explain how province, district, and local communities were linked, and the respective roles of kokushi and gunji.
  8. Evaluate roads, stations, and wooden tablets as evidence of how information and resources moved.
  9. Assess how taxation supplied the capital and why transport was as important as assessment.
  10. Contextualize the court's movement away from Heijō in the 740s and its abandonment in 784 within the limits of the "permanent capital."
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of how centralized the Nara state actually was.
  12. Avoid anachronistic descriptions of Nara government as a modern merit bureaucracy or of Heijō-kyō as a copy of Chang'an.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What does the spatial plan of Heijō-kyō reveal about how the court imagined political order, and where does it depart from continental models?
  3. How did the palace combine residential, ritual, political, and administrative functions?
  4. In what ways was the Nara bureaucracy organized bureaucratically but not meritocratically?
  5. How did personal and family politics continue to shape government despite written codes and formal offices?
  6. How did the court incorporate, rather than eliminate, local elites in provincial government?
  7. What did roads, stations, and wooden tablets make possible, and what limits did distance and geography impose?
  8. How could the capital sustain itself, and who bore the costs of carrying resources to it?
  9. Why did the court leave Heijō in the 740s, and what does that departure suggest about the stability of the Nara order?
  10. How centralized was the Nara state in practice, and what evidence should historians weigh in answering this question?

Historical Context

Chapter 13 traced how the Yamato court, in the decades after the Taika-era reforms, the Jinshin War of 672, and the reigns of Tenmu and Jitō, built the legal and administrative foundations of an imperial state. The Taihō codes of 701 and the Yōrō revision of 718 (put into effect later, in 757) defined offices, ranks, provinces, households, registers, and obligations in the language of a continental-style legal system. Late seventh-century Fujiwara-kyō, the first capital laid out on a grid plan and occupied from 694, tested many of these ideas at scale. This chapter takes those foundations as given and asks how they operated after 710. Definitions of the individual tax categories, in particular the distinction between yō (a corvée-exemption levy commonly paid in cloth or other goods) and zōyō (an additional compulsory labor obligation, often imposed for provincial projects), were established in Chapter 13 and are used here rather than re-derived.

Three features of the environment shaped what the Nara state could do. First, the court's home region was the Kinai, the central provinces around the Nara Basin, Yamashiro, and the Osaka Plain, where the sovereign, the great families, and the state's most reliable resources were concentrated. The Nara Basin is drained by the river system that flows toward the Osaka Plain, and Naniwa, on Osaka Bay, was the principal port through which continental embassies, coastal shipping, and Inland Sea traffic reached the capital region. Second, the archipelago's mountainous topography, its rivers, and the seasonal weather made overland movement slow and unpredictable, so that the reach of any government depended on how effectively it could maintain roads, stations, and boats. Third, the population that supported the state lived overwhelmingly in agricultural households, whose production, in rice, textiles, and local products, was the basis of all taxation. Population estimates for the eighth century vary widely and are uncertain; a few million people is a common order of magnitude, and students should treat any single figure with caution.

The evidence base for this period differs from the earlier chapters. The Nihon Shoki (completed 720) closed with the reign of Jitō, and its successor Shoku Nihongi (completed 797) covers the years from 697 to 791. The court also ordered provincial gazetteers (fudoki) in 713, of which only the Izumo Fudoki survives nearly complete, and it produced or preserved many administrative documents. Excavation of the Heijō Palace and the capital, carried out since the late nineteenth century and conducted over decades by the Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties and local authorities, has produced building foundations, roof tiles, and an enormous number of mokkan. The Shōsōin repository, associated with the Tōdaiji, preserves administrative papers such as household registers. From the Nara period onward, then, historians can compare texts of different kinds with each other and with archaeological evidence, rather than depending on one retrospective chronicle. Some Buddhist and religious dimensions of this period are treated in Chapter 15, and the provincial and frontier societies in Chapter 16.

Section 14.1

From Fujiwara-kyō to Heijō-kyō

The story of Heijō-kyō begins with the Taihō system and a practical problem. The Taihō codes, promulgated in 701, envisioned an administration of ranked officials in specialized offices, provinces and districts reporting to the center, registers of households, and taxes flowing toward a capital. This was a system of government that depended on continuous paperwork, on large numbers of officials working in shared spaces, and on the ability to store and redistribute goods. The palace-centered courts of earlier centuries, each associated with a particular sovereign and often abandoned at the sovereign's death or a change in fortune, could not readily support it. Fujiwara-kyō (694–710) went a considerable way toward solving the problem. It was a planned city with a grid of streets, and it accommodated the early operation of the codes. Why the court abandoned so recently built a capital remains debated. The 708 decision framed the new site in auspicious terms, while political, logistical, spatial, and continental capital-planning considerations have all been proposed.

Records preserved in the Shoku Nihongi show the decision-making process. An edict of 708 announced the intention to build a new capital, and the court set about surveying the site, organizing labor, and preparing the ground. The choice of the northern edge of the Nara Basin reflected several considerations at once. The site had access to the river network leading toward Naniwa and Osaka Bay; it was accessible to roads to the north and east; it lay close to the older heartland of Yamato; and, in the language of the court's edicts, it satisfied continental principles of geomancy, an important consideration in legitimizing a new capital. Historians are cautious about the relative weight of these motives, since the surviving accounts are official and stress the auspicious features of the site. The relocation itself took place in 710, under Empress Genmei, who had succeeded the young Emperor Monmu after his death.

A careful use of terms is important. Heijō-kyō was not Japan's first capital; Fujiwara-kyō came before it. It is better described as a much larger and more fully developed planned imperial capital. Nor was it the first "permanent" capital without qualification, since the court itself moved away in the 740s and abandoned the city in 784. What distinguished Heijō was that it became infrastructure for government, not merely the sovereign's residence. The palace, the ministries, the rank-linked residential allotments, the markets, and the roads that converged on the city together formed a system in which the state's functions were physically situated. In earlier courts, the sovereign's residence was the center of a network of personal and clan relationships; in Heijō, the network of offices, records, and storehouses was a permanent institution with its own space.

The move also had costs, and the costs were borne by people who left little record. Building a city required labor levies from provinces, timber and roof tile, and transport. Households were conscripted for construction; some resettlement of population from Fujiwara-kyō and from other areas occurred. The court was also constructing temples in the new city, and the shift of institutions such as Yakushiji and Daianji, along with the establishment of Kōfukuji, belongs to the story told in Chapter 15. For our purposes, the key point is that the capital's construction was itself an exercise in the state's ability to mobilize labor and materials, and the modest fact that it was accomplished within a few years indicates the reach of the Taihō system, at least in the Kinai and neighboring provinces.

Historical Vocabulary
Heijō-kyō and the jōbō grid

Heijō-kyō (平城京) means roughly "capital of the peaceful castle" or "capital of the level land," and was the name used by the court. The modern word "Nara" applies to the city and to the period (710–794 in conventional usage, though the court was based at Heijō only from 710 to 784, with interruption). The layout is called the jōbō system: the city was divided by east–west avenues numbered as jō (from Ichijō in the north to Kujō in the south) and north–south avenues defining bō, producing regular blocks, each subdivided into smaller plots called tsubo that could be allotted to households according to rank.

The term matters because it names an administrative technology. The grid allowed the state to assign, record, and locate residences and to relate physical position to rank. It is a poor guide, however, to actual urban life, which included informal settlement, marketplaces, workshops, and neighborhoods beyond the regular grid. Students should not treat the neat plan as a description of how the city looked or functioned in daily practice.

Section 14.2

Designing Heijō-kyō

The planned capital raises a question that runs through this chapter: What does a planned capital tell us about how the court imagined political order? Heijō-kyō's basic plan is well documented in archaeology and known from surviving descriptions. The main grid of the city occupied a rectangle on the order of four to five kilometers on each side, divided into east and west halves (sakyō and ukyō, the "left" and "right" capitals, by the convention of viewing from the sovereign facing south) by the great central avenue, Suzaku-ōji, about 74 meters wide. The avenue ran south from the palace's main southern gate (Suzakumon) to the city's principal southern entrance, the Rashōmon. To the east, a projection of the city (the gekyō, or outer capital) extended beyond the main grid, and this eastern district later became associated with major temples.

The Heijō Palace occupied the northern center of the city, an enclosed compound roughly a kilometer on each side with a further extension to the east. South of the palace, the avenues and blocks were allotted. High-ranking officials and members of the royal house received large plots near the palace; lower-ranking officials were assigned smaller allotments, often at greater distance. Excavations of the residence of Prince Nagaya, which occupied a very large plot, and of smaller compounds elsewhere show the correspondence between rank and space. The eastern and western markets (higashi no ichi and nishi no ichi), overseen by a government market office, stood on either side of the capital, and served both officials and the city's non-official population. Streets, canals, and drainage systems supported this urban fabric. Estimates of the city's population differ, commonly running to several tens of thousands and sometimes proposed as high as around 100,000, but the number cannot be established with confidence.

The city's plan reflects continental capital-planning traditions, especially those associated with Tang China's Chang'an, and this influence should be recognized. But historians caution against the crude formula that Japan "copied" Chang'an. Chang'an was far larger, walled, and organized around ward enclosures with gates; Heijō had no comprehensive city wall, and its layout was modified for local topography and purposes, including the eastern extension and the adaptation of the grid to the site. Fujiwara-kyō, which preceded it, followed a different arrangement with the palace near the center of the city, suggesting that the court was experimenting with different models rather than following one template. The Japanese court selected, adapted, and combined ideas from the continent in ways suited to its own political situation, and the process is better understood as selective adaptation than imitation. The debate about continental models is discussed in Section 14.10.

The plan is best read as a political statement. The sovereign's palace occupied the northern, most honored position, oriented to face south. The avenue ran from the palace gate to the world outside, a ceremonial route by which envoys and processions approached. Officials lived in a graded space, closer to the palace as their rank rose. The markets were placed under state regulation, and their location in the city expressed a view that commerce, too, should be supervised by government. Anyone entering the city from the south walked or rode up a route that placed them progressively closer to the seat of sovereignty. This was, in effect, a map of the ideal hierarchy of the ritsuryō state, and the fact that the map could be built at all reflected the court's ability to organize labor and resources.

At the same time, a plan is not a description. Some allotments were not fully built up, and the eastern and western halves of the city seem to have developed unevenly. The city also depended on infrastructure that was not always reliable: the water supply, drainage, and the transport of goods required constant maintenance. Students should keep the difference between the ideal and the realized city in mind, and the excavations, by showing what was actually built and used, provide a corrective to the planner's diagram.

Section 14.3

The Palace as the Center of Government

If the city was a map of order, the palace was its working center. Heijō Palace was simultaneously an imperial residence, a ritual center, a stage for political theater, and the administrative headquarters of the state. Its compound was enclosed by walls and entered through gates, of which the Suzakumon on the south was the principal one. Within, the palace was divided into zones. The residential quarters of the sovereign (the dairi) lay in an inner area. A large ceremonial precinct contained the great audience hall, the Daigokuden, together with the courtyard in front of it, where officials assembled by rank on formal occasions. Around these lay the halls and offices of the Council of State and the ministries, and a complex of workshops, storehouses, and service buildings. The excavated foundations and reconstructions, including a reconstructed Daigokuden at the site, allow students to appreciate the scale and arrangement.

The audience hall was the place of the throne. Major ceremonies took place there, including enthronements and New Year observances, when officials appeared in order of rank to pay homage to the sovereign. The arrangement of the courtyard and the requirement that officials stand in assigned positions turned political hierarchy into a visible form. It was, in a real sense, political theater: the sovereign was elevated and separated from the assembled officials, who in turn were separated from the population outside the palace walls. The palace's design thus expressed a symbolic separation between ruler and governed, one that would remain a durable feature of Japanese court culture, although the actual capacity of sovereigns to rule directly varied considerably across time (see Section 14.5).

But the palace was also a workplace. Everyday government took place in the ministry compounds, where officials received documents, drafted responses, and kept records. Excavated tablets and discarded scraps show the palace as a busy office complex. Nabunken excavations have also recovered evidence of the gardens of the palace's eastern section, including a landscaped pond area used for banquets, indicating that the palace was also a site of aristocratic entertainment and diplomacy. Envoys from Silla and Balhae, and Tang embassies, were received in ceremonial settings; such occasions were an occasion for the court to display its authority in a broader East Asian context. The palace, in short, joined ritual, hospitality, residence, and bureaucracy in one enclosed space.

Archaeology has also revealed that the palace was not built once and left unchanged. Excavations have distinguished phases of construction, including a substantial rebuilding associated with the court's absence in the 740s and its return, discussed in Section 14.9. Students should therefore imagine the palace as a changing complex, rebuilt, repaired, and modified over its use, rather than as a fixed, single design. The religious dimensions of the capital, including the temples that arose within and beside the city and their relationship to imperial authority, belong to Chapter 15.

Section 14.4

Governing the Capital: The Ritsuryō Administration

Chapter 13 described how the ritsuryō system was established. This section shows its machinery. At the summit of government stood the Daijōkan, the Council of State, made up of senior officials: the Chancellor (daijō-daijin, a post often left vacant), the Minister of the Left and the Minister of the Right, and the Major Counselors (dainagon), together with lower secretarial officials, who deliberated on major matters and transmitted decisions to the administration. Practice soon added important extra-code offices: the chūnagon, dropped when the Taihō code took effect, was restored in 705, and the sangi developed into a regular advisory office in the early eighth century, taking settled form in the early 730s. Even the ritsuryō bureaucracy thus evolved through institutions outside the written code. Beside it stood the Department of Deities (Jingikan), responsible for the state's ritual affairs and conceptually parallel rather than subordinate to the Council of State. Below the Council were the Eight Ministries (hasshō), each responsible for a functional area: the Ministry of Central Affairs (Nakatsukasa), handling documents and the sovereign's secretariat; Ceremonial (Shikibu), overseeing appointments, ranks, and the university; Civil Affairs (Jibu), for families and foreign relations; Popular Affairs (Minbu), responsible for registers, taxation, and public works; Military Affairs (Hyōbu); Justice (Gyōbu); the Treasury (Ōkura); and the Imperial Household (Kunai). Within each ministry were bureaus and offices, and a censorial board oversaw the conduct of officials.

Government was carried out through written documents. Orders, reports, petitions, registers, and receipts moved between offices. Positions were arranged in a standard four-tier pattern in most offices (a head, an assistant, a third-ranking official, and a scribe), so that the chain of responsibility could be traced. Officials were graded by rank (i), a system of some thirty grades, and rank determined the offices one could hold, the ceremonial position, the dress, and a range of privileges, including salary, allotments of land, and exemptions from certain obligations. Rank at the fifth level and above conferred noble status, and the small group at the third rank and above formed a core of high aristocrats (kugyō).

The central distinction of this section is that bureaucratic organization was not bureaucratic meritocracy. The Taihō and Yōrō codes borrowed continental institutions, including an examination system for the university, but the Japanese system as implemented was heavily shaped by birth. Under the on’i (蔭位, "shadow rank" or hereditary rank privilege) system, sons and grandsons (the eligible sons being called onshi) of high-ranking officials received rank automatically at a level determined by their forebears' status, and thus entered office ahead of most men who advanced by examination. High office was concentrated in a small circle of aristocratic families and members of the royal house, and family ties, marriage connections, and court patronage counted a great deal. The university and examination system produced a stream of scholar-officials, but they generally held lower positions and did not challenge the dominance of the aristocracy. One should therefore describe the Nara government as an organized administration with written procedures and a formal chain of authority, but staffed and led principally through inherited standing.

A schematic chart of the system helps students see its intended flow: sovereign → Council of State and central offices → ministries and bureaus → provincial governors → district officials → villages and households. But real authority did not always move smoothly down the chart. Orders could be delayed, misinterpreted, or resisted. Officials could be absent from posts; tasks could be delegated informally; and aristocrats could bypass formal channels through personal influence. Even the chart's apparent top, the sovereign, exercised power in varied ways. Empress Genmei, Empress Genshō, and Emperor Shōmu were not equally involved in governing, and the effective direction of policy often lay in the hands of senior ministers and their families. Historians therefore treat the codes as describing the state's ideal form and its institutional resources, while examining actual practice through documents and events.

The number of officials working in the capital was considerable, though estimates vary. Several thousand men held formal positions, and they were supported by a larger body of attendants, clerks, guards, laborers, and artisans, including many who served for defined periods under the labor obligations described in Section 14.8. Women held positions in the palace, in the inner service (naishi-no-tsukasa and related offices), and were part of the court's administration in ways that the male-centered ranking system understates. The capital was thus a workplace and residence for a far more varied population than the list of ministries suggests, a theme continued in the Everyday Life feature below.

Section 14.5

Aristocrats, Princes, and Court Politics

The formal bureaucracy operated alongside, and was often overshadowed by, competition among royal princes and aristocratic houses. The court was not an impersonal civil service. Its highest positions were held by members of the royal family and of a limited number of families, among them the Fujiwara, the Ōtomo, the Tachibana (a name conferred in the early eighth century), the Ōmi, and others of older lineage. The court's essential political question was who would occupy the senior posts, whose kin would marry into the royal house, and whose candidate would succeed to the throne. Written administration did not replace personal politics; it provided the arena in which such politics took place.

The rise of the Fujiwara is a central theme. Fujiwara no Fubito (659–720), the son of Nakatomi (Fujiwara) no Kamatari, had helped create the Taihō codes and married his daughters into the royal house. His daughter Miyako bore the future Emperor Shōmu, and later his daughter Kōmyōshi became Shōmu's consort. The family's strategy, of tying the succession to Fujiwara women, would be a recurrent pattern in later periods (Chapter 18), but in the eighth century it was still contested and the family's position was far from secure.

The affair of Prince Nagaya in 729 illustrates how office, kinship, succession, and accusation were intertwined. Nagaya, a grandson of Emperor Tenmu and holder of the high post of Minister of the Left, was accused of practicing forbidden sorcery, and he died by his own hand after his residence was surrounded by troops. The Shoku Nihongi presents the accusation as a plot uncovered by the authorities. Many modern historians, noting the context, regard the accusation as a politically motivated action connected with tensions over succession and the status of Kōmyōshi, who was raised to consort-empress soon afterward, a precedent for a non-royal woman in that position. The evidence, however, consists mainly of accounts produced by the court itself, and historians differ over how much weight to give to Fujiwara initiative and how much to other factors. The excavation of Nagaya's residence, which produced a large body of wooden tablets related to the supply of the household, shows another side: a prince's household was itself a small administrative organization, receiving and managing goods from estates and provinces.

The next decade brought dramatic reversals. A smallpox epidemic in 735–737 struck the capital and provinces, killing many people, including the four Fujiwara brothers, the sons of Fubito who had risen to lead the government. The loss reshaped the political balance; figures such as Tachibana no Moroe and, later, the scholar-official Kibi no Makibi gained prominence, and a rebellion by Fujiwara no Hirotsugu in Kyushu in 740 revealed the volatility of court politics. The epidemic's demographic effects are debated: some estimates suggest a significant fraction of the population died, but such figures are uncertain, and the effects varied by region. The important point for this chapter is that the state's functioning depended on people, and when key people died or were removed, the organization of government shifted.

This chapter does not narrate every crisis. It is enough to establish that court politics shaped how the administrative machinery was used, and that the machinery in turn provided resources and legitimacy to the competing houses. The role of Buddhism in these politics, including the influence of the monk Dōkyō in the 760s, is a subject for Chapter 15, and the reorientation of the court at the end of the century is treated in Chapter 17.

Section 14.6

Governing the Provinces

Beyond the capital, the ritsuryō system organized the territory it claimed into provinces (kuni), which were subdivided into districts (kōri, commonly read gun), which in turn were composed of local communities of roughly fifty households (ri). This lowest tier itself changed: in 715 the existing ri were renamed gō and subdivided into two or three smaller ri, and around 739–740 those subordinate ri were abolished, leaving province, district, and gō. The administrative system thus evolved within the Nara period rather than simply applying a fixed blueprint of 701. The provinces were grouped in administrative circuits: the Five Home Provinces of the Kinai and seven circuits (gokishichidō), the Tōkaidō, Tōsandō, Hokurikudō, San'indō, San'yōdō, Nankaidō, and Saikaidō, each named for the principal road that ran through it. In northern Kyushu, the Dazaifu served as a regional headquarters with responsibility for defense, diplomacy, and the government of the Kyushu provinces. By the early eighth century, the court claimed a total of around sixty provinces, with the islands of Iki and Tsushima administered separately, and it created new provinces (such as Dewa in the north in 712) as its claims expanded. The number and boundaries changed over time. The frontier and its peoples, including the Emishi of the northeast, are treated in Chapter 16.

Each province had a capital (kokufu), where a provincial office (kokuchō) stood, together with storehouses, workshops, and other facilities. The principal officials, the kokushi, were appointed by the court and sent from the capital for a term of years. They held ranks and titles corresponding to their office, and they were expected to supervise registers, taxation, public works, and justice. Below them, each district was governed by a gunji, drawn from local families with long-standing influence. The gunji was often a descendant of the older local rulers (kuni no miyatsuko and similar) discussed in Chapter 13, and the position was in practice held by families for generations, with a status that was much more stable than that of the outside governor.

Historical Vocabulary
Kokushi and gunji

Kokushi (国司, provincial governors) were officials of the central government dispatched to provinces for limited terms. Gunji (郡司, district magistrates) were officials of the district, selected from local families and holding office over long periods, sometimes across generations. The court's regulations provided that the gunji should be chosen for ability and reputation, but in practice hereditary local standing was the decisive factor.

The distinction matters because it captures the two-sided nature of ritsuryō provincial government. The kokushi represented the court and its interests; the gunji possessed local knowledge, local authority, and access to labor. Neither could rule effectively without the other. Modern words such as "governor" and "mayor" suggest a clearer separation between central and local authority than existed, and should be used with care.

The key argument is that centralization worked partly by incorporating local power rather than eliminating it. A governor sent from the capital, often serving for a few years and unfamiliar with the region, needed people who knew local households, fields, irrigation systems, disputes, and obligations. The gunji supplied this knowledge, organized labor, collected and delivered taxes, and maintained the district's records and storehouses. In return, they received rank, status, and access to state power that reinforced their local position. This arrangement had strengths: it allowed the court to reach far into the provinces without a large permanent presence. It also had limits: it made the state's reach dependent on the willingness of local families to cooperate, and it left them with opportunities to advance their own interests. Historians debate how far the gunji should be seen as agents of the state or as autonomous local leaders whose cooperation was conditional; the truth surely varied across regions and time.

The administrative apparatus in the provinces produced records. Provincial offices compiled household registers, tax registers, and reports to the capital; they sent reports on local products and conditions; and the court in 713 ordered provinces to compile gazetteers describing place-names, products, land, and local traditions. The Izumo Fudoki, completed in 733, is the best-preserved example and shows both how the court sought information and how local compilers presented their region. Such sources tell us about the reach of the state's information demands, and also about the way local identities and traditions were presented to the center. This chapter examines how provincial administration was supposed to function; the social life of provinces, the standing of local elites, taxation as experienced by rural households, and frontier relationships are the subject of Chapter 16.

Section 14.7

Roads, Stations, and the Information State

A law written in Nara mattered only if instructions, officials, records, and resources could actually move across the archipelago. The court invested considerable effort in building and regulating an overland communications system. The principal roads radiated from the Kinai toward the provinces of the seven circuits, and archaeologists have identified stretches of these roads, often remarkably straight and, in places, some six to twelve meters wide, cutting across terrain with little regard for local settlement patterns. Such roads, in contrast to informal paths, reflect a state that wanted rapid, controlled movement of officials and messages, and that could mobilize labor to build them.

Along the roads were stations (eki), spaced, according to the regulations, at intervals of about thirty ri (roughly sixteen kilometers) on main routes, each with a stock of horses and staff. Authorized travelers, whether messengers or officials on state business, used post-horses on presentation of official tokens (ekirei, bell-tokens). The codes specified how many horses each station should maintain, with larger numbers on the main road toward Kyushu and smaller numbers elsewhere. The stations were supported by local communities, whose obligation to feed and shelter travelers and care for the horses was a burden that the court sought to regulate. At the district level, a parallel system of horses served local officials. The system also had a defensive dimension: signal fires and frontier garrisons in the northeast and in Kyushu were linked to the communication network.

The sea and rivers were equally important. Goods from the Inland Sea and from coastal provinces traveled by boat, with river routes and portage roads connecting them to Naniwa and Yamashiro and the basin. Distinguishing land and water routes is essential when tracing how resources reached the capital, and students should not imagine the road system as a modern highway map. The circuits were not equally well developed or equally easy to use; mountain passes, river crossings, and weather made travel unpredictable, and the effective reach of court authority attenuated with distance and difficulty.

The evidence that best reveals the administrative operation of this system is the mokkan, wooden tablets on which officials and clerks wrote brief notes. Tens of thousands have been excavated from Heijō, from the palace, from Nagaya's residence (more than 35,000 mokkan items and fragments from the Nagaya residence assemblage alone), and from other capital sites. Provincial sites have yielded further examples. The tablets served as shipping tags attached to bundles of tax goods, labels for storehouses, notices of personnel and rations, records of official travel, and practice writing. Because they were discarded and preserved in wet soil, they survive in large numbers, and because they record routine operations, they offer a view of the state's working that grand chronicles cannot.

Historical Vocabulary
Mokkan (木簡)

Mokkan are wooden tablets or strips, usually inscribed in ink with brush and characters, used for short administrative messages, labels, and records in the seventh through ninth centuries and, in some forms, later. Paper was available but relatively costly; wood was abundant and could be shaved clean and reused, and the tablets' shavings are themselves found in excavations.

The term matters because mokkan provide contemporary evidence, produced for practical purposes rather than for posterity, and thus complement chronicles such as the Nihon Shoki and Shoku Nihongi, which were written by court scholars with particular purposes. Their limitations are also important: they are brief, fragmentary, and preserved unevenly, and they represent the perspective of the administrators who wrote them.

Section 14.8

Taxation and the Flow of Resources to the Capital

Chapter 13 defined the tax system. This section asks what taxation did: How could a capital full of officials and institutions exist if provincial households did not supply it? The answer is that a substantial part of the state's income was produced by households and physically carried to the capital or to provincial storehouses. Under the system the court set up, households were registered in household registers (koseki) compiled at regular intervals. The grain tax (so) was assessed on allotted rice fields and retained largely in the provinces. Taxable males also owed chō, a levy in textiles or local products sent toward the center; yō, a corvée-exemption levy commonly paid in cloth or other goods; and zōyō, an additional compulsory labor obligation often imposed for provincial projects, together with military obligations. The distinction between yō and zōyō defined in Chapter 13 remains essential: they were not the same obligation: yō was a levy collected in goods, whereas zōyō was actual compulsory labor, in large part directed at provincial projects. The state also issued loans of seed rice (suiko), which became a source of revenue.

What these obligations paid for is central. Goods sent to the capital supported the officials, whose stipends included rice, cloth, and other goods, as well as court households and the palace itself. Labor built and repaired the palace, roads, administrative facilities, and stations, and served in workshops. Provincial storehouses held grain for local administration, defense, and emergencies. Certain provinces supplied specialties, such as marine products from coastal areas, and the tablets record dried abalone, seaweed, fish, and salt among the goods sent to the capital, evidence of the diversity of resources the court sought. The court also introduced copper coinage in 708 and sought to encourage its use, including by attempting to reward accumulation and to regulate exchange, but coins seem to have circulated mainly in and near the capital region and did not replace the tax system in kind.

Transport was as important as assessment. A taxpayer's obligation was not fulfilled when the amount was determined; it was fulfilled when the goods reached the designated place. In the case of chō and related levies, households or their representatives were responsible for carrying goods to the capital, often on foot, over long distances and for weeks. Travelers had to bring their own food, and contemporary documents and poems record that journeys could be arduous and that some carriers fell ill or died on the way. Hardship stories, including the well-known poems of the period, suggest that the burden of transport could exceed that of the goods themselves, and they should be revisited when Chapter 16 examines taxation as lived experience. From the state's point of view, however, the routes of taxation were the arteries that kept the capital supplied, and their maintenance was among the chief tasks of provincial officials.

The system's productivity was thus a function of registers, roads, storehouses, and personnel. Where registers were accurate and local officials cooperative, the flow was regular; where households fled, registers were falsified, or officials diverted goods, the flow weakened. The state responded with inspections, penalties, and adjustments, including changes to the land allotment system that Chapter 16 and later chapters address. Students should recognize that taxation in the Nara state was not a uniform, automatic mechanism: implementation varied by region, and the ability to collect depended on negotiation with local elites and on the households' capacity to pay. The picture of a state drawing resources from the whole archipelago is real, but partial.

Everyday Life and Environment

Living and Working in Heijō-kyō

The capital's inhabitants belonged to several very different worlds. A mid-ranking official's household lived on an allotment whose size corresponded to rank, in a house whose scale and quality also depended on status; the official's stipend arrived as rice, cloth, and other goods, and the household included family members, servants, and dependents. The official's daily routine began early in the palace's ministry offices, where written orders and reports were handled. Women's palace service, in the inner offices and in the attendance of the imperial family, formed a parallel structure of positions and ranks, though evidence for women's lives is thinner than for men's.

Artisans worked in palace and ministry workshops producing tools, textiles, lacquer, metalwork, and roof tile; many were skilled workers from families who had served the court for generations, and some were of continental descent. Laborers conscripted under the labor obligations lived in crowded conditions, working on construction and maintenance and receiving rations that are recorded on tablets. Porters carrying tax goods arrived from distant provinces, delivered their loads to the storehouses, and then faced the journey home. In the markets, sellers offered rice, cloth, salt, and other goods to officials and residents, supervised by a market office. Some people lived at the edge of the plan, in informal settlements. The capital was thus a place of a few very privileged households and many whose contributions were essential but poorly recorded.

Environmental conditions shaped daily life: earthquakes and fires were hazards; wood and thatch were the main building materials for most buildings; the need for firewood and timber affected the surrounding hills. Epidemic disease, of which the smallpox outbreak of 735–737 is the best-known example, could spread rapidly in a dense population and along the roads that connected the capital to the provinces. Rural life in the provinces, which Chapter 16 addresses, is the necessary counterpart to this picture.

Section 14.9

A Capital That Did Not Stay Still: The 740s

Without this section, students might imagine that Nara was continuously and unquestioningly the capital from 710 to 784. It was not. Beginning in 740, Emperor Shōmu moved the court away from Heijō. The circumstances were turbulent: the smallpox epidemic and the deaths of the Fujiwara brothers had disrupted the government; harvests and finances were strained; and in 740 Fujiwara no Hirotsugu launched a rebellion in Kyushu, which was suppressed. Amid these events Shōmu traveled to the east and, at the end of 740, established a new capital at Kuni, in the Yamashiro region to the north. Construction there mobilized labor and materials, and the audience hall from Heijō was transferred to Kuni. In 742 construction began on a palace at Shigaraki, and in 744 the court declared Naniwa the capital. In early 745 Shigaraki itself briefly became the capital before Shōmu abandoned the site and returned the court to Heijō later that year, restoring the capital at Nara.

The analytical question is this: If Heijō-kyō represented stable centralized government, why did the court abandon it temporarily? Several explanations are proposed. Political crisis, the desire to escape the atmosphere of Heijō after the epidemic and rebellion, and the sovereign's own preferences seem to have played roles. Kuni and Naniwa offered better access to water routes and different relationships with powerful families and regions. Religious considerations also mattered, including Shōmu's turn toward Buddhist projects in this period, though that subject, with the provincial temples and the Great Buddha, belongs to Chapter 15. Historians differ on the relative weight of these factors, and the sources, again largely court chronicles, give limited insight into motives. What the episode demonstrates, whatever its causes, is that the capital's location was not fixed and that the state's infrastructure could be dismantled and reassembled at considerable cost, largely paid by laborers and taxpayers.

Archaeology at Heijō Palace records the effect. Excavators have distinguished an earlier and a later phase of the audience-hall area, with the first hall's complex removed to Kuni and a new complex associated with the restored capital; the palace was substantially rebuilt after the return. The physical evidence thus supports the textual account of relocation and adds detail on scale and sequence. It also suggests how much of the state's investment was in movable or replaceable structures and in the labor that shaped them.

Cause and Effect

The Capital-Centered State

Long-term causes

Seventh-century state-building, continental models of law and capital planning, the growing power of the court over regional rulers, and the experience of Fujiwara-kyō.

Short-term causes

The Taihō ritsuryō framework created a need for a larger administrative center; the court, under Genmei and her advisers, decided in 708 to build Heijō-kyō.

Event or transformation

The move of 710 concentrated officials and records in a planned capital, supported by provincial administration, roads, and taxation.

Immediate effects

Movement of goods, labor, and information to the capital; a larger population of officials and artisans; new archives, storehouses, and markets.

Long-term consequences

Stronger central claims and durable institutions and precedents for later courts, coupled with continuing dependence on aristocratic houses and local elites, and vulnerability to crisis.

Historical complexity

The chain from law to capital to provincial administration was not automatic. Politics, epidemic, resource limits, and local negotiation shaped each step.
Section 14.10

How Centralized Was the Nara State?

The chapter's evidence can now be gathered into an assessment. On one side, the Nara state was more centralized than earlier Yamato government. It had written law; permanent administrative offices; a planned capital; ranked officials; provinces and districts; registers; taxation collected across many regions; a communications network; and extensive record keeping. The scale of construction at Heijō, the movement of goods and labor across long distances, and the survival of large numbers of administrative documents all indicate an institutional capacity that earlier courts lacked.

On the other side, the state was not uniformly centralized. Aristocratic families dominated high office and the flow of patronage. Local elites remained essential in the provinces. Implementation varied geographically, and the reach of the state diminished with distance from the Kinai. Travel and communication were slow and difficult. Taxation depended on household and provincial compliance. Frontier areas remained outside or only partly inside effective control. Political rivalry persisted at the center, and the court itself could be disrupted, as the events of the 730s and 740s showed. The evidence therefore supports a qualified conclusion: the Nara state was a real and consequential attempt at centralization that reached a substantial part of the archipelago, but it operated through negotiation and inherited social structures rather than through the impersonal authority of a modern bureaucracy.

The chapter ends in 784, when Emperor Kanmu moved the court from Heijō-kyō to Nagaoka-kyō. The reasons for this move, the political concerns behind it, and the subsequent founding of Heian-kyō in 794 are the subject of Chapter 17. For present purposes it is enough to note that the capital that had embodied the ritsuryō order for three-quarters of a century was left behind, and that the institutions it had housed, the ranks, ministries, provincial offices, and tax practices, continued in modified form.

Historical Debate

How Centralized Was the Nara State?

The question: Historians agree that the Nara court created new institutions, but they disagree about how effectively those institutions governed. How much weight should be given to the design of the system and how much to its implementation?

Interpretation A — An institutional interpretation: Emphasizing codes, ministries, governors, roads, registers, and taxation, this view holds that the Nara state was a genuinely centralized government by the standards of its time, and that the evidence of documents and archaeological remains shows the court's capacity to organize resources and information across much of the archipelago.
Interpretation B — An implementation interpretation: Emphasizing aristocratic control of office, the persistence of local elites, regional variation, difficulties of transport and communication, and incomplete reach on the frontiers, this view holds that centralization was partial and depended on negotiation, so that the codes describe aspirations more than outcomes.

Evidence: Both draw on the same materials. The codes, the palace, and the tablets support the first view; evidence of hereditary gunji, the concentration of office in aristocratic houses, the difficulties of taxpayers' journeys, and the events of the 740s support the second. Many scholars combine the two, treating the Nara state as institutionally advanced but socially embedded.

A second debate: Was Heijō-kyō a Chinese city in Japan? Advocates of strong continental influence point to the grid, the axial avenue, and the borrowed vocabulary of rank and office. Others stress adaptation: the absence of a city wall, the different scale, and the shift from Fujiwara-kyō's arrangement. Most historians see selective adaptation rather than copying.

Why it matters: How historians judge the Nara state's centralization affects how they view later developments, including the rise of estates and provincial warriors (Chapter 20), which may be read either as the breakdown of an effective system or as the continuation of tensions present from the start.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Palace-Courts to the Capital State

What Changed?

  • Government came to be located in a planned, large capital with permanent offices and records
  • Provinces and districts were linked to the center by registers, roads, and taxes
  • Written documents became routine tools of administration

What Remained Continuous?

  • The dominance of a small aristocratic and royal elite in high office
  • The local authority of hereditary families
  • Household agriculture as the base of the economy, and personal and kin politics at court

Who experienced change most directly? Officials, artisans, and laborers in and around the capital, and households required to provide labor or goods. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural communities, whose local leaders and agricultural routines changed less visibly, and high aristocrats, whose privileges were preserved. Why does the pattern matter? It shows the state's power as layered onto existing structures rather than replacing them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has shown the ritsuryō state of Chapter 13 in operation. Its central argument is that Heijō-kyō made the new imperial order physically visible and gave it working infrastructure, and that the resulting state was substantially more centralized than earlier Yamato government but still depended on aristocratic families, local elites, household labor, and negotiated implementation.

The move to Heijō-kyō in 710 under Empress Genmei followed the Taihō codes and the experience of Fujiwara-kyō. Heijō was not Japan's first capital, and it was not simply a "permanent" one, but it was a much larger and more fully developed planned imperial capital. Its significance lay in the fact that the capital became infrastructure for government: a place where officials, records, storehouses, markets, and ceremonies were concentrated.

The city plan, with its jōbō grid, central avenue, northern palace, and rank-related allotments, expressed a view of political order. It drew on continental traditions, especially those associated with Chang'an, but it adapted them; it lacked a city wall, differed in scale, and departed from Fujiwara-kyō's layout. Historians therefore speak of selective adaptation rather than copying. The plan was also an ideal, and excavation shows a more uneven reality.

The palace joined residence, ritual, political theater, and administration. The audience hall and its courtyard displayed hierarchy in the arrangement of officials by rank, while the surrounding offices carried on the documentary work of government. Archaeology reveals successive building phases, including the rebuilding connected with the 740s.

Central government consisted of the Council of State, the Department of Deities, and eight ministries with their bureaus, staffed by ranked officials. This was bureaucratic organization but not bureaucratic meritocracy: the hereditary privileges of the on’i system, family ties, and patronage concentrated high office in a small elite. Politics remained personal, as the Nagaya affair of 729, the rise of the Fujiwara, and the upheavals of the 730s illustrate. Written administration did not replace competition among princes and aristocratic houses.

In the provinces, governors (kokushi) sent from the capital worked with hereditary district magistrates (gunji). Centralization worked partly by incorporating local power rather than eliminating it, an arrangement that extended the state's reach at the price of dependence on local cooperation. Provinces produced registers, reports, and gazetteers, giving the court information but also giving localities a channel for presenting themselves to the center.

Roads, stations, and waterways carried officials, messages, and goods. The tablets excavated from Heijō record shipments, personnel, and transactions and show the state working below the level of chronicles. A law written in Nara mattered only if resources and information could move, and the system's capacity to do so was real but limited by distance, terrain, and the burdens placed on those who carried the goods.

Taxation supplied the capital. Rice, textiles, local products, and labor supported officials, court households, construction, roads, and administration. Transport was as important as assessment, and the costs of carrying tax goods fell on taxpayers. The distinction between yō and zōyō from Chapter 13 was preserved, and the lived experience of rural taxpayers is taken up in Chapter 16.

The court's departure from Heijō in the 740s, to Kuni (740), Naniwa (744), and briefly Shigaraki (745) before returning to Heijō in 745, shows that the "permanent capital" was neither permanent nor secure. Epidemic, rebellion, political crisis, and the sovereign's choices combined to move the state, at considerable cost to laborers and taxpayers. In 784 the court left again for Nagaoka-kyō, a transition explained in Chapter 17.

Historians assess the Nara state as both innovative and limited. Interpretations emphasizing institutions and those emphasizing implementation each capture part of the evidence, and many scholars combine them. The chapter's main lesson is that a state can be real, effective in important ways, and yet dependent on the very social structures it seeks to organize. The next chapter turns to one of the most important of those structures, the Buddhist temples and clergy that became intertwined with imperial authority.

Key Terms

Heijō-kyō
The planned imperial capital, the principal seat of government from 710 to 784 with interruption in the 740s; the later "Nara."
Fujiwara-kyō
The grid-plan capital occupied from 694 to 710, predecessor of Heijō-kyō.
Jōbō system
The grid of numbered east–west avenues (jō) and north–south divisions (bō) organizing the capital and its allotments.
Tsubo
A subdivision of a city block, allotted to households according to rank.
Suzaku Avenue
The wide central avenue running south from the palace's main gate, dividing the left and right capitals.
Sakyō / ukyō
The left (east) and right (west) halves of the capital.
Gekyō
The eastern extension of Heijō-kyō beyond the main grid.
Heijō Palace
The walled palace compound combining the imperial residence, ceremonial halls, and offices.
Daigokuden
The Great Audience Hall, site of the throne and major ceremonies such as enthronements and New Year rites.
Dairi
The sovereign's inner residential quarters within the palace.
Daijōkan
The Council of State, the highest administrative body of the ritsuryō government.
Jingikan
The Department of Deities, responsible for the state's ritual affairs, parallel to the Council of State.
Hasshō
The Eight Ministries, functional departments of central government.
Rank (i)
The graded system of court ranks determining office, status, dress, and privileges.
Kugyō
The small group of highest-ranking aristocrats, generally of the third rank and above.
On’i
The system (蔭位) by which descendants of high-ranking officials received rank by inheritance, favoring aristocratic birth.

The province, the principal unit of regional administration.

The district, subdivision of a province.

Governors sent from the court to provinces for limited terms.

Hereditary local district magistrates whose cooperation made provincial government function.

Post stations on official roads, and the bell-tokens authorizing travelers to use their horses.

Koseki
Household registers compiled at intervals as the basis of taxation and labor obligations.
Chō
A tax in textiles or local products delivered to the capital.
Yō / zōyō
Yō was a corvée-exemption levy commonly paid in cloth or other goods; zōyō was an additional compulsory labor obligation, often imposed for provincial projects. The two were separate obligations.
Fudoki
Provincial gazetteers ordered by the court in 713; the Izumo Fudoki is the best-preserved.
Shoku Nihongi
The court chronicle covering 697–791, completed in 797.
Kuni-kyō
The temporary capital established by Shōmu in the Yamashiro region in 740.
Nagaoka-kyō
The capital to which Kanmu moved the court in 784.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it moves the history of the imperial state from design to practice. Chapter 13 explained the concepts; Chapter 14 shows what it took to make them work, and how much of that work was carried by wood, roads, registers, and human labor. The result was a real transformation in the scale and character of government in the archipelago, and one that left an archive from which historians can still reconstruct daily administration.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern: political authority in premodern Japan was rarely a matter of a single center commanding a passive periphery. Even at the height of the ritsuryō system, power was layered, shared, and negotiated among sovereigns, aristocratic houses, provincial governors, and hereditary local leaders. Later regimes, from the estate system to the bakufu and domain states, would build on and reshape this layered structure, and the questions raised here about the reach and limits of central authority recur throughout the book.

The chapter connects the Nara Basin to regions well beyond it. Tax goods from coastal provinces, roads to Kyushu and the northeast, and diplomatic contact with continental states all fed into and drew from the capital. At the same time, the evidence in this chapter comes mainly from the center, and the societies of the provinces and frontiers, to which Chapter 16 turns, must be understood on their own terms. Chapter 15 first takes up another force that shaped the capital and its politics: the temples and Buddhist institutions that grew alongside the palace.

Students who understand Heijō-kyō as both a place and an argument will be prepared to see how the Nara order, after decades of construction, crisis, and movement, gave way in 784 and 794 to a new capital and a different political arrangement, the story of Chapter 17 and the Heian court beyond.
