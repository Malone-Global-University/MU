The 1955 System and the Anpo Crisis

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the night of June 18, 1960, crowds that organizers put in the hundreds of thousands filled the streets around the National Diet in Tokyo. They were waiting for something they could not stop. Under the constitution, the revised U.S.–Japan Security Treaty would receive parliamentary approval automatically at midnight, because the House of Councillors had not acted on it.

The scene outside the Diet is a useful entry into this chapter because it contains its central tension. The government had a legal path to its goal and used it. The people in the streets believed that the manner in which that path had been taken had damaged the democracy the constitution was meant to protect. Both claims had substance. The treaty did take effect, and the prime minister who negotiated it resigned within weeks. The alliance survived, and the protest movement became one of the most remembered episodes of postwar civic life. This chapter asks how a political order could produce both outcomes.

The chapter has two connected subjects. The first is the political system that took shape after the occupation ended in 1952 and was fixed in place in 1955, when the divided Japan Socialist Party reunited in October and the Liberal and Democratic parties merged into the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) in November. Scholars and journalists later called the resulting arrangement the 1955 System (gojūgo-nen taisei). It was a parliamentary democracy with free elections, a lively press, active opposition parties, and a constitution that guaranteed civil liberties. It was also an order in which the LDP retained national governing power for nearly four decades, until the 1993 election produced an opposition-led coalition; the party had already fallen short of an outright seat majority in some earlier elections. Explaining how competition and dominance coexisted is a main task of the first two divisions.

The second subject is the security relationship with the United States. The 1951 Security Treaty, signed on the same day as the San Francisco Peace Treaty, allowed American forces to remain in Japan after sovereignty was restored. It created no clear American obligation to defend Japan, and it contained no expiry date or consultation mechanism. Prime Minister Kishi Nobusuke made its revision a central goal. The revised Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security, signed on January 19, 1960, gave Japan a more equal formal footing. Opponents nonetheless read it as binding Japan more tightly to the American Cold War strategy that the 1947 Constitution's Article 9 seemed to forbid. Divisions III and IV follow that dispute from the Self-Defense Forces and the base system through the parliamentary confrontation of May 19–20 and the demonstrations of May and June.

The people involved did not form two clean sides. LDP politicians disagreed over Kishi's leadership, and some stayed away from the decisive May vote. Opposition parties, trade unions, student associations, intellectuals, housewives, shopkeepers, and ordinary citizens joined the movement for different reasons. Some rejected the alliance itself. Others accepted a security arrangement but objected to Kishi's methods, to his wartime record as a cabinet minister, or to what they saw as an unnecessary risk of involvement in an American war in Asia. Okinawa, which remained under American administration, lay outside the revised treaty's mutual-defense clause and had its own distinct relationship to the bases. Chapter 92 therefore treats "the Anpo movement" as a coalition of overlapping grievances, not as a single ideology.

The evidence is unusually rich. It includes party platforms and founding declarations, the treaty text and exchanged notes published by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Diet records, election statistics, police and union estimates of crowd size, newspapers (including a joint editorial by seven major dailies on June 17), memoirs, opinion polls, and photographs, notably those of Hamaya Hiroshi. These sources often disagree. Crowd figures differ widely between organizers and police, and official minutes of the May 20 session capture very little of what happened in the chamber. Students should treat figures here as estimates unless stated otherwise.

Chapter 91 described the economic transformation that supplied the political stability and the material bargain on which LDP rule increasingly rested. This chapter does not retell that story, and it also leaves the detailed histories of militant labor, student factions, and the later New Left to Chapter 93, and environmental and minority movements to Chapter 94. Its aim is narrower: to explain how the political institutions of high-growth Japan were assembled, why the security alliance became the most divisive question of the age, and what the crisis of 1960 revealed about the relation between lawful procedure and democratic legitimacy.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the political stability of post-occupation Japan was not assured in 1952 and how the mergers of 1955 changed party competition.
  2. Describe the structure of the Diet, the cabinet, and the electoral system, including the single non-transferable vote in multi-member districts.
  3. Compare conservative, socialist, communist, and centrist positions on constitutional revision, rearmament, neutrality, and the U.S. alliance.
  4. Analyze how factions, kōenkai, business and farm interests, and the bureaucracy sustained LDP dominance.
  5. Assess the opposition's strengths and limits, including the Japan Socialist Party's reliance on unions and the diversification of the opposition after 1960.
  6. Explain how the Self-Defense Forces coexisted with Article 9 and why that coexistence remained politically contested.
  7. Compare the 1951 and 1960 security treaties, including Articles V and VI and the exchange of notes on prior consultation.
  8. Distinguish the groups that made up the anti-Anpo coalition and their differing reasons for opposing the treaty or the government's conduct.
  9. Reconstruct the sequence from the Diet confrontation of May 19–20 to the treaty's entry into force on June 23, 1960, distinguishing lawful procedure from democratic legitimacy.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of the 1955 System and of the meaning of Anpo as victory, defeat, or both.
  11. Read treaty texts, parliamentary records, and photographs critically, identifying whose perspective each represents and what it omits.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did two party mergers in 1955 produce not a two-party system but a lasting asymmetry between one governing party and a divided opposition?
  2. How did electoral rules and local organization shape the way politicians competed, and what did that competition reward?
  3. In what sense was the LDP a coalition of rivals rather than a unified ideological organization?
  4. Why did national security become the most divisive issue in postwar politics, and how did Article 9 shape the terms of that division?
  5. How did the 1960 treaty change the legal relationship between Japan and the United States, and what did it leave unchanged?
  6. Why did the government's parliamentary tactics in May 1960 become a controversy separate from the treaty itself?
  7. Who took part in the Anpo protests, and how do their varied motives complicate the idea of a single movement?
  8. Did the Anpo movement fail, succeed, or do both? What evidence supports each judgment?

Historical Context

The institutions inherited from the occupation were new in many respects. The 1947 Constitution (Chapter 84) made the Diet "the highest organ of state power," made the cabinet responsible to it, reduced the emperor to a symbol of the state, extended the vote to women, and renounced war in Article 9. Party politics had existed before 1945, as Chapter 69 showed, but the prewar cabinets answered partly to the emperor, the peers, and the military. After 1947 elected representatives controlled the choice of prime minister. The occupation also left a powerful civil service, since SCAP worked through Japanese ministries, and a political landscape reshaped by the purges, land reform, the legalization of unions, and the Reverse Course (Chapter 89).

Yoshida Shigeru, prime minister for most of the years between 1946 and 1954, was the central political figure of the occupation era. A former diplomat, he cultivated a group of ex-bureaucrats, including Ikeda Hayato and Satō Eisaku, who later led the conservative camp. He accepted the American security relationship, kept rearmament limited, and prioritized economic recovery. His rivals among the conservatives, many of whom had been purged by the occupation and returned after 1951, favored a more autonomous defense policy and constitutional revision. The conservative parties of the early 1950s were therefore divided as much by their views of Yoshida as by any difference in policy.

The international setting reinforced domestic conflict. The Korean War (Chapter 90) turned Japan into a base for American operations and a beneficiary of procurement spending. The San Francisco settlement, signed by Japan and most Allied states but not by the Soviet Union, China, or either Korea, left unresolved relations with several neighbors and left Okinawa and the Ogasawara Islands under American administration. Socialists and many intellectuals argued for a "comprehensive" peace including the communist powers and for neutrality. Conservatives replied that alignment with the United States was the realistic basis of security. Memories of the war, the atomic bombings, and the Lucky Dragon No. 5 incident of 1954, in which a Japanese fishing crew was contaminated by American hydrogen-bomb fallout, gave the debate emotional force that economic statistics alone cannot explain.

Section 92.1 · Division I — The Formation of the 1955 Political Order

Japan's Political Landscape after the Occupation

When the San Francisco Peace Treaty took effect on April 28, 1952, Japan regained formal sovereignty over the main islands. The country that did so was a parliamentary democracy by constitution, but no one could yet say whether that democracy was secure. Parties had been legalized only in 1945, many leading conservatives had spent years under purge, the unions and the left had grown rapidly and then been split by the Reverse Course, and the Communist Party had endured a turbulent period of illegality and factional violence. Public opinion was divided over the alliance and over rearmament, and the Diet itself was a place where rival conservative leaders competed fiercely.

The conservative camp had no single home. Yoshida's Liberal Party competed with the Progressive Party and its successors, and after 1952 Yoshida faced a revolt from politicians who had returned from the purge, notably Hatoyama Ichirō and Kishi Nobusuke. A scandal in 1954 over shipbuilding subsidies reached the ruling party's leadership, and the government's use of the Justice Minister's power to halt the arrest of a senior Liberal politician damaged its prestige. Yoshida resigned in December 1954, and Hatoyama formed a government. The early 1950s therefore produced not a settled system but a sequence of unstable alignments in which cabinets rose and fell with changing factional deals.

Restoration of sovereignty did not remove the constraints inherited from occupation diplomacy. U.S. installations, the unfinished settlement with several Asian neighbors, the limited resources of the new Self-Defense Forces, and the political salience of constitutional pacifism all narrowed the choices facing successive cabinets. Economic reconstruction encouraged officials to seek international stability, but the means of securing it remained contentious. In parliament, the same conservative politicians who shared an anticommunist orientation disputed how much autonomy Japan could exercise in an alliance dominated by Washington. Their rivalries did not resemble a simple government-versus-opposition contest, and they explain why a majority party had not yet emerged as the permanent center of government.

Party fragmentation also reflected social change. Land reform had weakened some old rural landlord networks; women newly enfranchised after the war remained a growing and unevenly represented electoral constituency; unions organized industrial and public employees; and the expanding press made political conduct visible beyond Tokyo. Electoral competition therefore required politicians to build constituencies rather than rely solely on prewar connections. This gave the conservatives strong local roots, but it also opened the political field to people and associations that had been marginalized under the earlier imperial order. The mergers of 1955 were responses to those uncertainties, not evidence that postwar democracy was already settled.

The opposition was also divided. In 1951 the Japan Socialist Party split into left and right factions over whether to accept the San Francisco treaty, with the left opposing both the peace settlement and the security pact. Each faction gained seats in the early 1950s. Observers, including some within business, feared that a socialist government might eventually emerge, and that fear helped motivate the conservative merger described in the next section. The stability that later seemed inevitable was in fact a product of choices made in 1955.

Section 92.2 · Division I

The Socialist Reunification and Conservative Merger of 1955

In the lower-house election of February 1955, the two Socialist parties together won around a third of the seats, and the Hatoyama government's Democrats held a plurality without a majority. Socialists had one reason to unite: unified, they could approach the one-third threshold that blocks constitutional amendment, which requires a two-thirds vote in each house followed by a national referendum. On October 13, 1955, the left and right Socialist parties reunified. Their agreed platform opposed constitutional revision and rearmament, supported peaceful coexistence, and aimed at socialist transformation through parliamentary means, though the compromise masked the left wing's more radical commitments.

The reunification alarmed conservatives and business leaders, whose organizations pressed the Liberals and Democrats to merge. After negotiations among the party leaders, the Liberal Party and Japan Democratic Party joined on November 15, 1955, to form the Liberal Democratic Party. The merger was driven partly by fear of a Socialist government and partly by the shared aim of strengthening the conservative camp for revision of the constitution and for a more independent foreign policy. Leadership of the new party was initially collective, with several senior figures sharing the role, and Hatoyama was formally elected president in April 1956.

The arithmetic of the two mergers was important, but their sequencing mattered as well. Socialist reunification preceded the conservative union, heightening the pressure on conservative leaders who feared that three-way competition would divide their vote. Business organizations favored a governing party capable of delivering predictable budgets and foreign policy, while conservatives worried about socialist opposition to defense and constitutional revision. At the same time, members of the newly reunited Socialist Party had not resolved their own differences about socialism, relations with communist states, or the alliance. Political unity could therefore bring resources and publicity while carrying unresolved internal conflicts into the next decade.

The new LDP’s foundation did not abolish its predecessor parties’ networks. Politicians retained followers, fundraising contacts, and leadership ambitions; those survived inside the new organization as factions. Thus the party’s strength included a contradiction: merger reduced competition between conservative labels in elections but preserved competition among conservatives within the organization. Voters could influence which LDP politician won a local seat without easily changing which party held the cabinet. This provides a bridge from the political realignment itself to the institutions of the 1955 System studied in the following sections.

Politicians and political scientists later spoke of the "1955 System" because the two mergers froze the party structure in a pattern that lasted many years. Yet the label should not suggest that the outcome was planned. The LDP was assembled from rival groups, and the socialists remained split in spirit. The arrangement is sometimes described as a "one-and-a-half party system," because the LDP held a majority roughly twice the size of the main opposition party's seats. The system's durability was not guaranteed by the mergers alone, and it was shaped further by the electoral rules, the economy, and the opposition's own choices.

Historical Vocabulary
Gojūgo-nen taisei (the 1955 System)

The term refers to the political arrangement that followed the party mergers of 1955, characterized by LDP control of the cabinet, a larger Socialist party in permanent opposition, and a stable pattern of parliamentary conflict. It is a retrospective analytical label, applied by journalists and scholars rather than by the politicians who made the mergers. It is most often applied to the period from 1955 to 1993.

Students should be careful not to read the label as a claim that nothing changed across these decades. The party's factions, the opposition's composition, and the economic setting all shifted considerably. The term describes a pattern of dominance, not an unchanging institution.

Section 92.3 · Division I

The National Diet, Electoral Rules, and Parliamentary Competition

The Diet has two houses. The House of Representatives, the lower house, had roughly 467 members elected for terms of up to four years and could be dissolved by the cabinet. The House of Councillors, with 250 members serving six-year terms, half of whom faced election every three years, could not be dissolved. The prime minister is chosen by the Diet, and where the houses disagree the lower house's choice prevails. The same principle applies to treaties: if the House of Councillors does not act within thirty days after the lower house has approved a treaty, the treaty is deemed approved. This provision was decisive in June 1960. In principle ministers are answerable to the Diet, but in practice day-to-day policy drafting rested with ministry officials, and a central question of the period was how far elected politicians directed the bureaucracy and how far they depended on it.

Lower-house elections used medium-sized districts that returned three to five members, with each voter casting a single vote for one candidate. This single non-transferable vote (SNTV) system had been in use since 1947 in one form or another, and its effects were large. To win a majority, a party had to run several candidates in the same district, so LDP candidates competed against each other as well as against the opposition. They could not easily distinguish themselves by party platform, because they shared one, and instead relied on personal service, local connections, and organized support. The system also meant that a Socialist or small-party candidate needed only a modest share of the vote to win a seat, which helped sustain a divided opposition and weakened incentives for it to consolidate.

The SNTV system converted votes into seats through district-level arithmetic. Because multiple seats were available but each voter cast only one vote, a major party had to distribute its own supporters across candidates. Too many candidates could divide the vote and lose otherwise attainable seats; too few could squander a large aggregate vote. Parties therefore needed local intelligence, funding, and nomination discipline. Incumbents with organized personal supporters resisted having their votes pooled with factional rivals. The electoral rule did not mechanically create the LDP’s dominance, but it rewarded the party’s ability to fund several distinctive candidates and maintain ties across municipalities.

The House of Councillors deserves separate attention. It was not a decorative chamber: it could delay measures, scrutinize ministers, and provide a political forum for opposition parties, even though constitutional provisions gave the lower house the decisive role in choosing the prime minister and, under specified conditions, in budget and treaty approval. Students should distinguish ordinary legislative bicameralism from the special thirty-day rule that became relevant in June 1960. They should also ask what a national seat total conceals. Rural overrepresentation, differing turnout, and regional party organizations all shaped the political meaning of a vote, so party support cannot be read directly from national seats without checking the underlying districts.

Election results in the late 1950s illustrate the pattern. In May 1958, with turnout above three-quarters, the LDP won a clear majority of about 287 seats while the Socialists won about 166, their best result of the period. The conservatives held about 57 percent of the vote and the left about a third. Turnout and competition were real, and alternation in government never followed. Rural districts, which were overrepresented because of unchanged apportionment as population moved to cities, also favored the party strongest in the countryside, a point revisited in Section 92.7.

Section 92.4 · Division I

Conservatism, Socialism, Pacifism, and the Ideological Divisions of Postwar Japan

Conservatives shared a commitment to economic development, social order, anticommunism, and the American alliance, but they differed on how far to go in changing the occupation settlement. Some, associated with Hatoyama and Kishi, wanted constitutional revision, a stronger defense, and a more autonomous foreign policy. Yoshida's followers placed priority on economic recovery and were wary of revision. Others, such as Ishibashi, favored a degree of engagement with the communist powers. Business leaders supported the alliance as a basis for trade and stability. In this sense the conservative camp was a coalition of positions, held together by the shared aim of keeping power from the left.

The opposition was divided as well. The Socialists opposed constitutional revision and the security treaty and sought neutrality, a "positive neutrality" in some formulations, though the party's right wing was less committed than the left. The Communists supported neutrality and an end to American bases but were smaller and had been compromised by earlier violent tactics. Union federations such as Sōhyō lent the left money and mobilization, while moderate unions and the future Democratic Socialists sought a more reformist path. Pacifism itself was not owned by either camp: ordinary citizens who had lived through the war and the bombings, many of them not political activists, held strong antiwar convictions.

Pacifism was a field of competing constitutional and ethical positions. Some opponents sought an unarmed Japan outside great-power blocs; others accepted limited self-defense but objected to U.S. bases or to prospective participation in a war on the Korean Peninsula or Taiwan Strait. Conservative politicians likewise differed over whether constitutional revision was necessary or strategically unwise. The U.S. alliance offered deterrence and economic advantages, but it also symbolized incomplete independence to its critics. Even those who agreed that Japan should avoid another aggressive war disagreed over whether alliance membership made that goal more or less likely.

The strongest institutional consequence of these divisions was a durable obstacle to constitutional change. Article 96 required supermajorities in both houses and popular ratification, so a substantial opposition minority could make revision impracticable even when conservatives commanded the cabinet. Everyday political debate often moved among moral memory, legal text, national security, and relations with the United States without sharply separating them. A historian must reconstruct those arguments as contemporaries made them, not assume that “pacifist” always implied socialist affiliation or that “conservative” meant unqualified support for a larger military.

Security was the most divisive question because it joined several issues: the interpretation of Article 9, the presence of foreign troops, the memory of the war, relations with China and the Soviet Union, and the risk of nuclear conflict. Revision of the constitution was a related matter. A Cabinet Commission on the Constitution, authorized in 1956 and organized under Kishi, studied the question and reported without consensus in 1964, and revision never occurred. The reason, in part, was that the Socialists and their allies held enough seats to block it and that public opinion remained wary.

Section 92.5 · Division II — LDP Dominance and the Institutions of Political Power

The Liberal Democratic Party: Factions, Leadership, and Internal Competition

The LDP was organized around factions (habatsu), groups of Diet members gathered around a senior figure who supplied campaign funds, posts, and support in leadership contests. In the late 1950s there were roughly eight such groups, with divisions that reflected personal followings, bureaucratic backgrounds, and policy leanings. Leaders of the party's "mainstream" and "anti-mainstream" groups competed for the presidency, which carried with it the premiership because the party held a Diet majority. The party's presidential elections were therefore the real contests for power, and they were fought through bargaining among factions.

The leaders of the 1950s had different priorities. Hatoyama, a party politician who had been purged by the occupation, aimed at normalizing relations with the Soviet Union, achieving UN membership (accomplished in 1956), and revising the constitution. Ishibashi, a liberal economist, won the presidency in December 1956 by a narrow margin over Kishi but fell ill and resigned after about two months. Kishi, a former economic bureaucrat, pursued treaty revision, a stronger police power, and a larger role in Asia. Ikeda, the Yoshida protégé, stressed economic growth. These were differences of emphasis within a conservative framework, but they shaped how the party responded to the crisis of 1960.

Factions operated as career networks as well as ideological groupings. Junior Diet members depended on senior patrons for access to campaign finance, cabinet and party committee posts, advice in a crowded legislative environment, and introductions to ministries. Senior politicians accumulated influence by demonstrating that they could deliver followers in leadership ballots. These relationships created personal obligations, but factional loyalties could change as leadership candidates lost credibility or policy interests diverged. They also gave the governing party multiple centers of bargaining at moments when parliamentary opposition had limited prospect of replacing it in office.

The institutional costs were real. Cabinet appointments could reflect factional balance rather than expertise; the need to distribute posts could reinforce short ministerial tenures; and fundraising competition intensified relationships with major donors. Yet leadership turnover inside the LDP allowed a new prime minister to change emphasis without precipitating a change of ruling party. The replacement of Kishi by Ikeda was an especially visible example. It should not be mistaken for the same degree of accountability as a general election producing an opposition government, but it helps explain why internal competition mattered to a dominant-party system.

Factionalism has been interpreted in contrasting ways. Some scholars see it as a source of weakness that made decisive policy difficult. Others see it as a functional substitute for a two-party system: because leadership changed through factional bargaining, the party could change prime ministers and policy emphases without changing governing party. The Anpo crisis tested the second view directly, since it was an intra-LDP bargain that removed Kishi and replaced him with a leader who promised a gentler style.

Section 92.6 · Division II

Kōenkai, Electoral Finance, and Local Political Networks

Because LDP candidates in the same district shared a party label, they built personal support organizations called kōenkai. These associations enrolled local notables, shopkeepers, farmers, and neighborhood leaders, and they kept in touch with members between elections through meetings, trips, gifts, and favors. They also provided what politicians called constituency service: introductions to officials, help securing permits or school admissions, and attention to local public works. Kōenkai existed in the earlier 1950s and expanded in visibility and organizational reach during the later 1950s and 1960s; their scale and costs varied by constituency and candidate.

The need for money tied politicians to donors. Business federations, notably the Federation of Economic Organizations (Keidanren), channeled corporate donations to the conservative party through a central body, the Kokumin Kyōkai (People's Association), set up in 1955. Politicians also raised funds through their factions and through personal contacts. Because election law limited what could be spent and what could be said in campaigns, a great deal of political activity happened outside the formal campaign period and could blur into gifts, patronage, and vote-buying, which scandals periodically exposed. The shipbuilding scandal of 1954, described in Section 92.1, was an early example.

Personal support associations did not operate in a single national mold. In some rural districts they grew from longstanding agricultural, commercial, and community ties; in expanding cities they might rely on occupational contacts, neighborhood events, and secretarial staff. The candidate’s personality and the reach of local brokers mattered greatly. Associations could be helpful to constituents in navigating an opaque administrative system, but the same relationships created pressure to reward loyal supporters preferentially. Because their work continued outside the brief official election campaign, they were a durable mechanism for keeping names, obligations, and requests alive between votes.

It would be a mistake to equate every service delivered by a politician with bribery. Helping a resident contact an agency could be ordinary representation; attaching a valuable private reward to a vote could cross legal and ethical boundaries. Historians therefore study account books, newsletters, memoirs, court cases, and election regulations rather than relying on stereotypes of bought rural ballots. Attention to women is especially useful: they often staffed, organized, or attended association activities while remaining underrepresented among Diet candidates. Kōenkai thus reveal the social labor of electoral politics as well as the relationship between money and office.

The kōenkai system rewarded personal reputation more than policy. A politician who could bring a bridge or a subsidy to his district might hold his seat for decades, and sons or relatives often inherited both the seat and the organization. The same intimacy limited who could compete: newcomers without local roots or money faced high barriers. This helps explain why the party's grip persisted even when urban voters moved toward the opposition, since the local networks it built were hard to match.

Historical Vocabulary
Kōenkai

A kōenkai ("support association") is a politician's personal organization of local backers. It is not a party branch but belongs to the individual candidate, which is why it mattered so much under SNTV, where candidates of one party competed with each other. Members might receive newsletters, invitations to outings, and help with administrative problems in exchange for votes and loyalty.

The term should not be translated simply as "fan club" or "machine." It combined sociability, service, and political obligation, and its character varied between rural and urban areas.

Members of a politician's local association might gather on a spring weekend for a bus tour to a hot-spring inn, with the politician or a secretary present, meals paid by the association, and small gifts exchanged. Section 92.7 · Division II

Business, Agriculture, Bureaucracy, and the Political Economy of LDP Rule

The LDP rested on a set of reciprocal relationships. Organized business, represented by Keidanren and other economic federations, supplied money and favored policies of stable growth, low taxes on enterprise, and the American alliance. Agricultural cooperatives (Nōkyō) mobilized rural votes and lobbied for price supports, notably for rice, which the state purchased and distributed under a food-control system. Local governments and construction firms looked to national subsidies for roads, dams, and public buildings. Small business owners, doctors, and other occupational groups formed their own associations. Chapter 91 explained the economic institutions behind these groups; the point here is how they translated into votes and policy.

The bureaucracy was a partner rather than a rival. Many LDP leaders, among them Yoshida's protégés and Kishi, were former officials, and they maintained close relations with ministries that drafted legislation and administered subsidies. Ministries, in turn, relied on the party to pass bills and defend budgets. Observers have debated who led whom: some emphasized the power of elite bureaucrats, others the growing influence of party specialists who mastered particular policy areas. In practice the two worked together, and the arrangement gave the government considerable administrative continuity.

These interest relationships were not uniform alliances of all farmers with all businesses. Large exporters wanted competitive finance and favorable access to foreign markets; small shopkeepers sought protection against concentrated retail competitors; some farmers depended on price supports even when urban consumers disliked the resulting costs. Party leaders balanced these competing demands through committees, budgets, and periodic compromises. The LDP’s success rested partly on being able to combine interests that could not easily be placed along a single left-right economic line. The result was a network of exchanges, not a centrally planned system controlled by one donor or ministry.

Policy also had a geography. Public expenditure on infrastructure could deliver tangible improvements to rural districts, yet it also created incentives for local politicians to compete for the same ministry funds. The expanding metropolitan regions gained commercial activity and population, while depopulating districts retained political weight because their representation adjusted more slowly. Development, electoral rules, and constituency service reinforced one another. The mechanism becomes clearer if students follow a proposed road project from its local advocates through Diet members, construction contractors, budget committees, and administrative offices, identifying who gained and who paid.

Accounts of a supposedly omnipotent bureaucracy require similar caution. Ministries could draft complex regulations and maintain expertise across cabinet changes, but politicians could block proposals, demand redistribution, and negotiate priorities. Business groups could organize donations, yet the governing party did not always deliver a common business preference. To determine whether an outcome reflected bureaucratic initiative, factional pressure, local politics, or economic conditions, historians need correspondence, cabinet records, parliamentary debate, and budget evidence. That evidentiary approach avoids reducing the political economy of LDP rule to either heroic technocrats or automatic patronage.

These relationships should be assessed carefully. Representing the interests of farmers, merchants, and industry is a normal function of parties in any democracy. Clientelism and corruption arise when benefits are exchanged for votes or donations in ways that bypass public rules, and the record of the period contains both legitimate representation and abuses. The system's electoral advantage also came from the unequal weight of rural votes, as population shifted to cities faster than districts were redrawn. This made rural overrepresentation a structural feature of the LDP's strength, and it became a target of reform movements and court cases in later decades.

Section 92.8 · Division II

The Japan Socialist Party and the Changing Opposition Party System

The Japan Socialist Party was the principal opposition throughout the period, and in some respects the most significant. It had a base in public-sector and industrial unions through Sōhyō, and it attracted intellectuals and voters in cities and in some rural areas. It ran fewer candidates than the number needed to win a majority in most elections, which meant it was structurally positioned as an opposition rather than a would-be government. Within the party the left wing, inclined toward Marxist analysis and neutrality, was frequently at odds with the right wing, which favored a more moderate, welfare-state program. These conflicts weakened its ability to appeal to wider audiences.

The Communist Party, after the violent tactics of the early 1950s, adopted a policy of legal parliamentary activity in 1955 and rebuilt slowly. It remained small in the Diet but influential in some unions, student groups, and local governments. The Socialists' own divisions produced another break in January 1960, when right-wing members led by Nishio Suehiro left to form the Democratic Socialist Party (DSP), which supported a moderate social-democratic line and, in the case of security, accepted the alliance in principle. The DSP drew support from the Zenrō-affiliated unions. In 1964 the Sōka Gakkai, a lay Buddhist movement, formed Kōmeitō as its political arm, adding another party that would later compete for urban voters.

Opposition fragmentation was a political outcome with several causes rather than merely an electoral mistake. Socialists disagreed about the route to social reform and about the practical implications of neutrality in a divided East Asia. Communist leaders had to rebuild credibility after the party’s earlier internal conflict and turn away from violence. Moderate social democrats could appeal to voters uneasy with Marxism, but by founding a separate party they also competed with the larger Socialist organization for candidates, money, and union support. Religiously based political mobilization further complicated the urban vote. Each party had a real constituency even when their separation weakened the prospect of alternation in government.

Opposition influence cannot be measured only by the number of cabinets it headed. A party holding one-third of seats could obstruct constitutional revision, contribute to committee scrutiny, develop policy alternatives, and help organize public campaigns. Local governments gave reformers additional experience in administration and opportunities to experiment with social policy. Yet the inability to unite around foreign policy and governing coalitions made a national transfer of power less plausible. The 1960 Anpo movement would demonstrate both capacities at once: opposition forces could help mobilize enormous numbers, but mobilization did not produce agreement on a successor government.

The result was an opposition that was meaningful but fragmented. Elections were genuinely contested, opposition parties governed many cities and some prefectures in later decades, and they shaped debate in the Diet. But the multiplication of parties, combined with SNTV, meant they split the anti-LDP vote and rarely threatened to form a government. This is the sense in which Japan had real competition without frequent alternation in national power.

Section 92.9 · Division III — The U.S.–Japan Alliance and the Road to Anpo

Article 9, the Self-Defense Forces, and the Political Meaning of Pacifism

Article 9 of the 1947 Constitution renounces war as a sovereign right and states that land, sea, and air forces will not be maintained. Almost immediately Cold War events pressed on that text. In 1950 the occupation authorities ordered the creation of a National Police Reserve, which grew into the Safety Forces and, in July 1954, into the Self-Defense Forces (SDF) under a new Defense Agency. The government's position, developed by successive cabinets, was that Article 9 does not deny Japan's inherent right of self-defense and that forces limited to the minimum necessary for that purpose are not the "war potential" the article prohibits.

Critics regarded this reading as a strained reinterpretation. The Socialists and many constitutional scholars argued that the SDF was a military force in all but name, and the debate was carried into the courts. In the Sunagawa case, arising from a protest at the Tachikawa base, the Tokyo District Court ruled in March 1959 that the stationing of American forces in Japan violated Article 9. The Supreme Court reversed that decision in December 1959, holding that matters of such high political character should not be judged by courts unless clearly unconstitutional. The ruling cleared the way for the government's treaty revision, and it left the constitutional question to politics.

The Sunagawa dispute demonstrates why Article 9 could not be settled as a straightforward legal proposition. The lower-court judgment of March 1959 and the Supreme Court’s reversal in December addressed American forces and the political character of the security arrangements, rather than delivering an exhaustive answer about every possible use of the Japanese Self-Defense Forces. Government officials relied on constitutional interpretations developed in cabinet practice, while scholars and opposition parties challenged their consistency. Students should distinguish a court’s holding from the broader claims later politicians drew from that holding, and should note that constitutional meaning developed through institutions as well as through formal amendment.

The SDF also had a social presence that cannot be reduced to theoretical law. Recruiting, training, budgeting, local installations, and public responses differed across communities. Some citizens accepted limited defense capability as a safeguard against foreign attack; others feared that soldiers and alliances could restore political habits associated with the war. The political significance of the SDF in 1960 lay in this unresolved relationship between the constitutional text, actual institutions, and memories of imperial militarism. Later defense-policy changes belong elsewhere, but the origins of these arguments are essential to understanding Anpo.

Pacifism had a more complicated political meaning than either camp suggested. For some it was a principled rejection of war grounded in memories of the Pacific War, Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the Lucky Dragon incident. For others it was a practical position that low military spending would help the economy. Conservatives could also invoke pacifism by pointing to restraint on foreign deployment. It is useful to separate the legal question of how Article 9 should be interpreted from the political debate over Japan's wartime record and its responsibilities in the Cold War, since participants often moved between them.

Section 92.10 · Division III

The 1951 Security Treaty, American Bases, and the Limits of Sovereignty

The first Security Treaty, signed at San Francisco on September 8, 1951, permitted the United States to station forces in and around Japan. It did not oblige the United States to defend Japan, it set no expiry date, it contained no mechanism for consultation, and it allowed American forces, at the request of the Japanese government, to help suppress large-scale internal disturbances. A separate Administrative Agreement of 1952 governed the legal status of the troops and the facilities they used. Many Japanese regarded these terms as unequal and as a limitation on the sovereignty they had just regained.

Bases were a daily presence and a source of friction. Land expropriation for base expansion provoked local opposition, most famously at Sunagawa near Tachikawa in the mid-1950s, and incidents involving American servicemen, including a fatal shooting of a Japanese woman in Gunma in January 1957 (the Girard case), inflamed public opinion. American ground combat units were largely withdrawn from the main islands in the late 1950s, and Marine units were redeployed in part to Okinawa, so the burden of the military presence shifted rather than disappeared.

American bases had different consequences depending on their location and function. Facilities could bring wages, contracts, and infrastructure to some communities, while nearby residents experienced accidents, restrictions on access to land, noise, and legal conflicts involving personnel. The Sunagawa movement had a local history rooted in land and livelihood as well as a national meaning as opposition to the alliance. The Girard case made questions of legal jurisdiction and accountability vivid far beyond the affected area. Neither economic benefit nor resentment was distributed evenly; landowners, tenants, shopkeepers, farmers, workers, and local officials could disagree over the same installation.

For Okinawans, the distinction between sovereignty and administration was not a technical footnote. American government, base construction, and land acquisition shaped political participation and daily life, while campaigns for reversion developed their own leadership and objectives. Describing Article V’s territorial boundary in 1960 therefore requires more than drawing a line on a map. The Japanese government claimed sovereignty in principle, but the islands’ residents did not have the same institutional standing as those in Japanese-administered prefectures. The 1972 reversion should be reserved for later chapters; here it serves only to prevent students from assuming that the 1952 end of occupation applied uniformly across the archipelago.

Okinawa stood in a different situation. Under Article 3 of the peace treaty it remained under American administration, with the United States holding what American officials called residual Japanese sovereignty. Authorities there governed through the U.S. Civil Administration, and the islands were the site of large American installations. In the mid-1950s the military's acquisition of farmland, sometimes through forcible clearing that Okinawans remembered as "bulldozers and bayonets," provoked a broad popular movement, and in April 1960 an Okinawa Prefecture Council for Reversion to the Fatherland was founded. Okinawa was thus not simply "Japan with more bases." It was a territory whose residents were excluded from the Diet and the 1947 Constitution's protections in practice, and its status is essential to understanding the sovereignty debates of the period.

Section 92.11 · Division III

Kishi Nobusuke and the Campaign to Revise the Security Treaty

Kishi Nobusuke became prime minister in February 1957 after Ishibashi's illness. He was a former Commerce and Industry official who had helped run the economy of Manchukuo and served as a minister in Tōjō Hideki's wartime cabinet. Arrested in 1945 as a suspect for Class A war crimes, he was held in Sugamo Prison and released in December 1948 without being tried. The purge lifted after 1951, and he rose rapidly in the conservative camp. His past gave opponents a powerful symbol: to them his return to the premiership showed that the old wartime elite had survived. The charge was politically potent, but it would be a mistake to reduce opposition to the treaty to a reaction against his biography, since many who criticized the treaty had little interest in Kishi's career and many who disliked Kishi supported the alliance.

Kishi's aim was to put the alliance on a footing he regarded as more equal and more consistent with Japanese independence. In June 1957 he met President Eisenhower, and the two governments agreed to establish a committee to study revision. He argued that Japan should assume a clear role in its own defense, that the American commitment should be explicit, and that the unequal features of the 1951 arrangement should be removed. Critics responded that revision would deepen Japan's involvement in an American strategy, not lessen it, and they pointed to the treaty's reference to the "Far East."

Negotiation over the treaty involved Japanese and American calculations that were not identical. Tokyo sought a clearer defense commitment and less humiliating legal language; Washington wanted assured access to bases in a region where military plans involved Korea, Taiwan, and the wider western Pacific. The concept of prior consultation was therefore important but contested, because a provision that sounded like an increase in Japanese control could also be interpreted narrowly by the side operating the forces. The eventual text was a compromise shaped by what each government needed to defend at home, not merely a bilateral expression of shared sentiment.

The failure of the police-powers bill in 1958 mattered because it showed that broad coalitions could make a government retreat even before the treaty was ratified. Opponents learned how to coordinate unions, parliamentary parties, professional organizations, and citizens. Kishi’s supporters, by contrast, concluded that further delay could invite escalating obstruction of a strategic goal. Both sides drew lessons that made compromise more difficult in May 1960. The relationship between earlier mobilization and later confrontation should be shown as a series of contingent political choices, not a march toward an inevitable street battle.

Kishi also pursued domestic measures that made him controversial. In autumn 1958 his government sought to expand police powers over searches and assemblies through a revision of the Police Duties Execution Law. The bill provoked broad protest among unions, intellectuals, and even moderate voters, who feared a return of prewar-style policing, and the government withdrew it. The episode shaped the political climate before Anpo and gave opponents experience in joint action. It also fixed an image of Kishi as willing to push controversial measures, which affected how his later parliamentary tactics were received.

Section 92.12 · Division III

The Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security of January 1960

The revised treaty was signed in Washington on January 19, 1960, by Kishi and Secretary of State Christian Herter, among others. It replaced the 1951 pact with a document that was more symmetrical in form. The internal-disturbance clause was dropped. The new text stated that each party recognized an armed attack on territories under Japanese administration as dangerous to its own peace and safety and would act to meet the common danger under its own constitutional provisions and processes (Article V). It provided for consultation when either party saw the security of Japan or international peace and security in the Far East under threat (Article IV), and it called for economic cooperation (Article II). Japan's obligation was limited to defending territory it administered, which was understood not to require sending forces overseas.

Article VI is the section most closely tied to controversy. It granted the United States the use of facilities and areas in Japan for the purpose of contributing to the security of Japan and to the maintenance of international peace and security in the Far East. The reference to the Far East meant that American forces based in Japan could be used in operations beyond Japan's own defense. An exchange of notes between Kishi and Herter specified that major changes in the deployment of American forces, major changes in their equipment, and the use of Japanese bases for combat operations other than the defense of Japan would be subject to prior consultation with the Japanese government. The treaty was to last ten years, after which either party could give one year's notice of termination.

The treaty must be read article by article rather than as a single pledge of mutual defense. Article V required each party to respond to armed attack in territories under Japanese administration, but it stated that action would occur in accordance with constitutional provisions and processes; it was not an unconditional promise to undertake an identical military response. Article VI permitted American military facilities for the defense of Japan and for peace and security in the Far East. Articles IV and X were also politically significant: the former provided for consultation, and the latter established the ten-year period after which either party could end the treaty with notice. The distinction between those legal functions explains why supporters could call the treaty a formal improvement while critics continued to fear regional entanglement.

The accompanying consultation arrangements require special care because the publicly visible diplomatic text and confidential interpretations were not synonymous. Materials prepared for U.S. Secretary of State Christian Herter in June 1960 treated the “introduction” of nuclear weapons differently from some kinds of naval transit; a separate issue concerned possible U.S. combat operations from Japan in a Korean emergency. Later Japanese official inquiry and archival releases illuminated the negotiations but did not erase differences in interpretation between governments. Students should compare the published treaty and notes with the declassified memoranda and identify what was agreed in public, what was treated as an interpretation, and what later observers disputed. This is a stronger historical method than using “secret agreements” as an undifferentiated accusation.

The political meaning of equality also had limits. Japan could negotiate a more reciprocal written agreement without possessing equivalent military capacity or full command over bases. Conversely, the treaty did not simply abolish Japanese sovereignty: it fixed defined commitments and procedures in an international agreement that Japan’s legislature could scrutinize. The debate therefore turned on how sovereignty should be measured—formal legal rights, practical freedom to refuse operations, security guarantees, or independence from any foreign military presence. The balance between those definitions is an essential interpretive problem of the chapter.

The provisions were contested from the outset. Supporters noted the explicit American defense undertaking, the deletion of the internal-disturbance clause, and the consultation framework. Critics worried that Article VI’s “Far East” purpose and the continued presence of bases could draw Japan into conflicts despite Article 9. Later declassified records and Japan’s March 9, 2010 Foreign Ministry investigation revealed or revisited confidential understandings, but the issues must be distinguished: one concerned U.S. combat operations from Japan in a Korean emergency; another involved how the introduction of nuclear weapons was understood relative to naval transit and port calls. American and Japanese interpretations of the latter were not uniformly acknowledged as identical. A 1960 treaty analysis cannot treat all of these as one agreed, unambiguous secret pact. Okinawa, outside Japanese administration at the time, was not protected by Article V in the same way and was governed under a separate American administration.

Section 92.13 · Division IV — The Anpo Crisis and the Mobilization of Japanese Society

The Anti-Anpo Coalition: Parties, Labor, Students, and Citizens

In March 1959 the Japan Socialist Party, Sōhyō, and a large number of other organizations formed the People's Council for Preventing Revision of the Security Treaty. The Communist Party took part as an observer. The council organized rallies, petition drives, and regional "united actions," often called waves, aimed at the Diet. Its core was the labor movement, which could supply numbers and the capacity to strike, and the Socialists, who provided parliamentary representation. Between 1959 and 1960 the movement staged repeated mass demonstrations, including one on November 27, 1959, when thousands entered the Diet grounds.

The coalition's motives varied. Some participants rejected the alliance outright and sought neutrality. Others criticized specific provisions, in particular the Far East clause and the risk of being drawn into a war in Korea or the Taiwan Strait. Many cited the government's conduct, the Police Duties bill, and Kishi's wartime record. Intellectuals, among them the political scientist Maruyama Masao and the Chinese-literature scholar Takeuchi Yoshimi, framed the issue as one of democracy as much as security. Women's groups, including housewives' associations, joined demonstrations, and neighborhood and professional groups formed in the course of the crisis. This diversity should not be reduced to a single ideology.

Participation in the coalition did not mean that organizers agreed about political ends or tactics. The Socialists sought to prevent a treaty they opposed and weaken Kishi; unions mobilized workplaces and members around security and democratic procedure; some university groups argued for direct confrontation; and citizens without party affiliations joined out of anger at the government's conduct. Differences were visible in the allocation of speaking roles, slogans, protest routes, and willingness to confront police barriers. A teacher, farmer, or newspaper reader could disapprove of a particular government action without adopting the ideological program of the most militant student faction.

Coalition infrastructure also mattered. Labor federations could organize nationwide action through local branches, student associations could assemble rapidly near universities, and civic networks circulated notices, petitions, and arguments through print culture. Once the May parliamentary confrontation expanded the issue from treaty policy to democratic legitimacy, these channels were available to people with different commitments. It is therefore more accurate to reconstruct the movement as overlapping organizations that sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed than as one actor with one collective will. The resulting diversity explains both its extraordinary breadth and its difficulty converting protest into an agreed political settlement.

Students were a particularly visible element. The Zengakuren federation, whose mainstream faction rejected the Communist Party's leadership, staged confrontational actions. In January 1960, as Kishi left for Washington, several hundred students occupied part of Haneda Airport in an attempt to stop him, and many were arrested. Chapter 93 treats the factions and their ideas in detail. For this chapter it is enough to note that the students were influential in the crisis but that the movement included far more than students, and the more militant tactics also divided the coalition between those who wished to maintain peaceful demonstrations and those who wished to confront police.

Historical Vocabulary
Anpo

Anpo is the common abbreviation of Nichibei Anzen Hoshō Jōyaku, the Japan–U.S. Security Treaty. By extension it names the protest movements of 1959–1960 and, in some usages, the later controversy of 1970. Context decides which meaning is intended.

The word should not be read as shorthand for "anti-American." Participants held a range of views, and many objected to the government's conduct more than to the alliance itself.

Section 92.14 · Division IV

The May 19–20 Diet Crisis and the Parliamentary Passage of the Treaty

By May 1960 the government faced a deadline. President Eisenhower was scheduled to visit Japan in June, and Kishi wanted the treaty approved first. The ordinary Diet session was due to end on May 26. Socialist legislators employed procedural delay and physical obstruction to prevent an extension and a treaty vote. On May 19 they crowded and barricaded the approaches to House of Representatives Speaker Kiyose Ichirō’s office, blocking his passage toward the plenary chamber. After repeated warnings, Kiyose requested police intervention to clear the obstruction and permit the parliamentary process to resume. Police removed the blockaders from the area of the Speaker’s office; it is inaccurate to describe them as entering the plenary chamber and carrying Socialist Diet members out of it. An extension of the session was approved, and in the early hours of May 20 the lower house voted for treaty approval without the Socialist opposition present. A contemporary American embassy dispatch reports that 25 LDP members boycotted the treaty vote, a distinct figure from those absent earlier in the night.

A contemporaneous dispatch from the U.S. embassy provides a rare detailed sequence of events. It reports that the Speaker was blocked near his office for more than six hours, that he issued repeated warnings before requesting police assistance, and that the subsequent treaty committee action and lower-house plenary vote occurred after the obstruction was cleared. The same account records internal hesitation among LDP leaders about proceeding immediately, as well as the boycott by 25 LDP members of the treaty vote. This is valuable evidence but not an omniscient account: it is a diplomatic report from an American government deeply invested in the treaty and should be compared with Japanese parliamentary minutes and contemporary Japanese newspaper reporting.

The sequence also matters legally. The House of Representatives approved the treaty under rules that gave it priority after a designated waiting period if the House of Councillors failed to act. The resolution of the constitutional question, however, did not resolve the political question of whether a parliamentary majority should employ these tactics for a consequential international commitment. Students should ask what each side understood by “obstruction” and “due deliberation,” and why a recorded vote could be technically valid but broadly controversial. Treating the event as simply an unlawful coup or an ordinary vote would miss the dispute that brought hundreds of thousands into the streets.

Earlier procedural crises in 1954 and 1956 make the episode more, not less, historically intelligible. They reveal a postwar parliamentary culture in which physical obstruction and police assistance had precedents, and in which politicians already debated the limits of majority authority. What changed in 1960 was the international stake, the timing of an American presidential visit, the scale of civic response, and the relationship between the parliamentary controversy and the anti-treaty coalition. The record thus supports careful comparison rather than a misleading claim of complete novelty.

The government maintained that it had acted within the legislature’s rules: a majority could extend debate and approve the treaty, while obstruction could not permanently prevent a recorded decision. Opponents argued that the forced removal, hurried committee and plenary actions, and exclusion of the opposition gravely damaged public trust. The claim that police intervention in the postwar Diet was wholly unprecedented is incorrect. National Diet Library materials describe a police-assisted parliamentary confrontation during the 1954 police-law dispute and a further deployment during the 1956 education-board confrontation. The novelty of May 1960 lies in its particular sequence, scale, political stakes, and consequences for treaty approval—not in the first-ever appearance of police in a postwar parliamentary conflict.

The effect was to transform the protest. Before May 19 the opposition had concentrated on the treaty's content. After May 20 the slogan was increasingly the defense of democracy. Intellectuals who had been moderate in their criticism spoke out, scholars resigned posts in protest, and newspapers condemned the procedure. The constitutional timetable also mattered: because the House of Councillors had thirty days to act and the lower house's approval would otherwise prevail, the treaty would be approved by the middle of June in any case, which gave the movement a fixed deadline and gave the crisis its characteristic urgency.

Section 92.15 · Division IV

Demonstrations, Strikes, Public Opinion, and Democratic Participation

After May 20 the protests grew in size and range. Nightly processions circled the Diet, and the People's Council organized a series of "united actions." Sōhyō-led work stoppages on June 4 and again on June 15 drew millions of workers, according to the unions' own figures, which police estimates placed lower. The stoppages were mostly short and symbolic rather than prolonged industrial actions, but they showed unusual breadth, including railway, public-sector, and manufacturing workers. Smaller groups of doctors, writers, teachers, shopkeepers, and company employees marched under their own banners. Newspapers carried detailed coverage, and some writers described the movement as a rare moment of political participation by ordinary people who were not party members.

Organization and scale require different kinds of evidence. A labor federation’s account of participation may count employees in brief work stoppages, while a police estimate may refer to demonstrators observed in a particular street at a particular time. Neither number can be converted automatically into a unique count of individuals who participated over several weeks. A person who attended several rallies could appear repeatedly in cumulative totals, and the movement included smaller events that never reached central Tokyo. The appropriate historical question is not simply how many people marched, but how their geographic spread, duration, institutional support, and diverse composition changed political possibilities.

Photographs show families, professional groups, workers, and students sharing urban space, but they do not tell historians whether participants favored neutrality, opposed Kishi’s tactics, or hoped for a different government. Newspapers and polls can help, yet each has a sampling frame and editorial point of view. Opposition to the government’s parliamentary conduct should not be casually equated with opposition to the alliance under all circumstances. The movement’s power came partly from that ambiguity: diverse participants could agree on protest without endorsing the same future security policy. Such political breadth should be treated as a source of historical explanation, not as a flaw to be erased.

The movement also took place beyond the symbolic space of the Diet. Rural participants traveled to Tokyo, and local experiences with military bases shaped some of their banners and demands. Women’s groups and informal citizen circles presented themselves as voices outside professional party politics. While political parties and unions supplied resources, these associations challenged the notion that ordinary residents must speak only through elected representatives. The conflict therefore involved the social practice of democracy—who could address the state, through which organizations, and with what authority—as well as the formal fate of a treaty.

One striking example was the formation of small independent groups. A housewife's invitation to join her on a demonstration grew into an informal body that called itself the Voiceless Voices Society (Koe naki koe no kai), which marched with a simple banner and welcomed anyone who wished to walk. The name played on the government's suggestion that the "silent majority" supported the treaty. Protests were also held in other cities, including Osaka, Kyoto, Nagoya, and Fukuoka, and in smaller towns, though Tokyo remained the focus.

The movement raised competing understandings of democratic authority. The government and LDP maintained that parliamentary decisions by the elected majority were the legitimate form of democracy and that demonstrations should not dictate legislation. Protesters argued that when a government used procedures they regarded as illegitimate and had not sought a mandate on the specific question, citizens had a right to resist through assembly and protest. Public opinion polls showed that many respondents disapproved of the way the treaty was passed, even as support for the alliance in the abstract was more mixed. The movement was therefore not simply a student rebellion but a wide, uneven mobilization whose participants disagreed about its goals.

Section 92.16 · Division IV

June 15, 1960: Violence at the National Diet and the Death of Kanba Michiko

On June 10 the White House press secretary, James Hagerty, arrived at Haneda to prepare Eisenhower's visit and was surrounded by demonstrators. His car was stuck, and he was removed by a U.S. Marine helicopter, an episode that embarrassed the government. On June 15 a second round of strikes and demonstrations took place. During the day right-wing militants attacked a procession of protesters. In the evening groups of students, among them members of the Zengakuren mainstream, pushed into the Diet's grounds through the south gate and clashed with police. Many demonstrators and police were injured, and a student at the University of Tokyo, Kanba Michiko, aged twenty-two, died in the grounds.

Reconstruction of the June 15 confrontation requires attention to time and geography. Groups approaching the south gate attempted to enter the Diet grounds, police used barriers and water, and repeated clashes continued into the night. Hamaya Hiroshi photographed the disorder from positions near the scene. His images are important eyewitness evidence, but their dramatic composition and later selection cannot by themselves identify each individual, determine exact causes of injury, or establish total numbers. The much-discussed image of a body being carried has been identified as probably Kanba; MIT’s commentary explicitly notes that certainty is impossible. A careful caption should preserve that qualification instead of presenting the identification as proven.

Kanba’s death altered the politics of protest because it gave the crisis an individual victim whose loss could be publicly mourned. Memorial actions drew people whose relation to militant student politics varied widely. Some interpreted the confrontation as evidence of coercive policing; others saw students’ forced entry into the compound as unacceptable and feared a collapse of public order. The reaction of newspaper editors differed from that of radical organizers. The seven major newspapers prepared their joint statement on June 16 and published it in their next editions, calling for an end to violence and the preservation of parliamentary democracy. It is historically useful to ask why their plea could be read both as criticism of government methods and as a rebuke to militant tactics.

Contemporary and retrospective accounts must not be merged without warning. Reports that senior officials contemplated Self-Defense Forces deployment, and that Defense Agency leaders opposed it, help explain the perceived gravity of the crisis, but the details rest substantially on later recollections. Present them as attributed accounts and avoid treating them as a proven cabinet order. This distinction reinforces a larger lesson: the historian’s responsibilities increase, rather than diminish, when reconstructing highly emotional, politically charged episodes.

The details of her death have been the subject of dispute. Contemporary reports described her as having been trampled or crushed in the crowd, and some witnesses and later writers emphasized police violence in the clashes while others stressed the confusion of a surge. Because evidence is contradictory, historians generally describe the cause as asphyxiation or crushing in the clash and avoid assigning precise responsibility without clear documentation. Her death became a powerful symbol. Mourners held large memorial gatherings, and she was remembered for decades as a figure of the movement, though her own politics, as a member of a radical student organization, were not those of the majority of demonstrators.

The consequences were immediate. On June 16 Kishi asked Eisenhower to postpone the visit, and the cabinet announced that it was cancelled. On June 17 the seven major Tokyo newspapers issued a joint declaration urging an end to violence and a defense of parliamentary democracy. The statement criticized the protesters' violence but also called on the government to resolve the crisis, and it was criticized by some as treating the movement's violence and the government's tactics as equivalent. Reports suggested that Kishi had considered calling out the Self-Defense Forces and that the Defense Agency's leadership declined, though the details rest on later accounts. The events showed how violence could both galvanize and divide a movement.

Section 92.17 · Division IV

The Treaty Takes Effect: Political Defeat and the Fall of Kishi

The protests did not prevent the treaty from taking effect. On the night of June 18, the largest crowds of the crisis, estimated by organizers in the hundreds of thousands, surrounded the Diet. At midnight the thirty-day period expired and the treaty was automatically approved on June 19. The formal exchange of ratifications took place on June 23 at the Foreign Minister's residence, with the American ambassador, Douglas MacArthur II, and the treaty entered into force. It is important to keep the dates distinct: June 19 completed the Diet process, and June 23 completed the international legal process.

Kishi announced on the day of the exchange that he would resign. He remained in office until mid-July, when the LDP chose his successor, and on July 14, at a celebration for the new leader, a right-wing assailant stabbed Kishi in the leg. Kishi had achieved his main policy aim, but at the cost of his political standing. The crisis had cost him the support of other party leaders, severely tested the ruling party's standing, and ended his hopes of staying in office to pursue other goals, such as constitutional revision. The party that had managed the crisis chose to replace its leader rather than to change policy.

Three different kinds of outcome must be separated. The automatic Diet approval on June 19 was a consequence of the constitutional rule governing treaties, not the same event as the international exchange of ratifications on June 23. The entry into force settled the legal status of the revised agreement; the resignation announcement did not reverse that settlement. The resignation itself reflected political judgment inside the governing party, including fears that Kishi’s continued leadership would prolong the crisis and damage the party in the next election. The LDP preserved its policy position by changing its public representative.

Accounts of the crisis sometimes use military language of victory and defeat, but such metaphors require care. The anti-treaty movement did not achieve the formal objective of preventing the agreement. It did contribute to the destruction of Kishi’s premiership, to cancellation of Eisenhower’s visit, and to the durability of Anpo as a warning against disregarding parliamentary norms. Political consequences extended beyond the moment of a vote; they included the government’s later reluctance to invite comparable confrontation and the opposition’s renewed attention to the gap between mass mobilization and electoral change. These outcomes are compatible without claiming that a protest against a treaty secretly prevailed because a leader resigned.

Judgments of the result differ. For some the protest failed because the treaty took effect and the LDP won a larger majority in the November 1960 election. For others it succeeded in forcing Kishi's resignation, in ending the Eisenhower visit, and in making later governments cautious about such confrontations. A third view holds that it was both: a defeat on the immediate question and a victory in establishing a standard by which governments could be judged. This ambiguity is part of the movement's historical importance.

Cause and Effect

Treaty Revision and the May–June Crisis

Long-term causes

The unequal terms of the 1951 treaty; Article 9 and the memory of the war; the Cold War; the division of the conservatives and the socialists; the strength of organized labor and the student federation.

Short-term causes

Kishi's determination to revise the treaty before the Eisenhower visit; the Police Duties bill of 1958; the signing of January 1960; the opposition's obstruction; the government's decision to use police in the Diet.

Event or transformation

The May 19–20 session turned a debate about security into a debate about parliamentary legitimacy, mobilizing groups that had not previously opposed the alliance.

Immediate effects

Mass strikes and demonstrations; the death of Kanba Michiko; the cancellation of the Eisenhower visit; the treaty's entry into force; Kishi's resignation.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The alliance endured and the LDP remained in power, but later governments avoided direct confrontation over security, emphasized economic growth, and treated constitutional revision with caution. The crisis cannot be reduced to one cause: the content of the treaty, the style of the government, the strength of the opposition, and the memory of the war all contributed, and participants joined for different reasons.
Section 92.18 · Division V — Political Stabilization, Democratic Contradictions, and Historical Legacy

From Kishi to Ikeda: Political Stabilization after Anpo

Ikeda Hayato took office in July 1960 and set a different tone. He promised "tolerance and patience," a low posture toward the opposition, and a shift of attention to the economy. His government adopted the National Income Doubling Plan in December 1960, whose content and effects Chapter 91 has already described. The change of leader did not alter the treaty, but it altered public debate: security issues receded from daily politics while wages, housing, and consumption occupied more space.

The general election of November 1960 gave the LDP about 296 seats and the Socialists about 145, with the new Democratic Socialist Party winning a small number. The Socialists lost ground despite the protests, a result that suggested the movement had not translated into votes. Many voters who had disliked Kishi's methods returned to the governing party once he was gone, and the economy was expanding.

Ikeda’s language of “tolerance and patience” was a political choice after a constitutional controversy, not simply an expression of a more agreeable personality. It indicated that the governing party could alter the style of leadership while preserving its parliamentary base and alliance commitments. Opposition figures, meanwhile, confronted difficult choices: whether to continue mobilization against a treaty already in force, to focus on elections, or to redirect resources to constitutional and social issues. The November results strengthened the LDP numerically even though the government had been widely criticized in May and June. That outcome helps distinguish the political meaning of a protest coalition from the electoral behavior of the national population.

The assassination of Asanuma also reminds students that formal democratic competition existed alongside ideological extremism and threats of violence. His killing occurred in public and under cameras, leaving unusually vivid evidence of how deeply contested Japanese political identity remained. It should be neither sensationalized nor used to suggest that ordinary opposition activism was inherently violent. Most anti-Anpo participants acted through marches, meetings, petitions, or temporary stoppages, and the political traditions emerging after 1960 included both parliamentary opposition and nonviolent civic organization. The chapter’s distinction between legal institutions and democratic political culture remains relevant here.

Political violence nevertheless continued. On October 12, 1960, during a televised debate among party leaders at Hibiya Hall, a seventeen-year-old right-wing activist, Yamaguchi Otoya, stabbed Socialist chairman Asanuma Inejirō, who died. The assassin took his own life in custody in November. The killing, which was broadcast and photographed, showed the intensity of ideological conflict and the danger faced by political leaders, and it also deprived the Socialists of a popular leader at a critical moment.

Section 92.19 · Division V

The Consolidation and Persistence of the 1955 System

After 1960 the LDP won many elections and retained control of national government, but it did not always win an outright majority of seats on its own. Its advantage arose from several interlocking sources: the rising living standards associated with economic growth; kōenkai and durable constituency networks; support from business and agricultural organizations; the adaptability of internal factions; and a divided opposition. Party governments also responded to changing constituencies through welfare programs, local subsidies, and, later, environmental protections. Critics called some of these accommodations co-optation; supporters described them as responsiveness. Both interpretations must be tested against policy decisions, budgets, and voting behavior rather than inferred simply from an election result.

Opposition parties continued to contest elections, serve on parliamentary committees, and govern significant municipalities and prefectures. They could influence legislation, shape public debate, and block constitutional revision by denying the governing party the necessary two-thirds threshold. The 1970 ten-year milestone of the security treaty prompted renewed protest (Chapter 93), but the treaty did not require a new ratification or affirmative “renewal.” Article X provided that after the initial ten years either country could terminate the agreement on one year’s notice; absent such notice it continued in effect. This distinction matters because constitutional debate and treaty law were not the same as the political rhetoric surrounding Anpo anniversaries.

Explaining persistence also requires distinguishing the LDP’s vote share, its number of seats, and its control of cabinet formation. Those were related but not equivalent measures. In an SNTV system with unequal district representation and several organized opposition parties, a large plurality could supply a government even without a single-party majority of the chamber. Factional negotiations and agreements with independents could provide the necessary support. The opposition retained meaningful influence through legislative work, local offices, and public campaigns, but its fragmentation reduced the likelihood that dissatisfied voters would agree on an alternative governing coalition. This is why the 1976, 1979, and 1983 election outcomes are necessary qualifications rather than exceptions to be hidden.

Long-term political history also has a changing social base. Economic growth created new urban occupations and interests; rural depopulation altered constituencies; regional public spending linked the national government to local voters; and civic movements forced politicians to respond to issues that did not map neatly onto older left-right divisions. None of these processes made continued LDP rule inevitable. The 1955 System describes the persistence of governing dominance amid adaptation, not a fixed constitution or a suspension of electoral history. It is most useful as an analytical period label when historians keep track of what changed inside it.

The later history was neither one unbroken sequence of comfortable LDP majorities nor a collapse of electoral competition. In 1976 the LDP won 249 of 511 lower-house seats; in 1979 it won 248; and in 1983 it won 250—below the 256 seats required for an outright majority in that chamber. Nevertheless it maintained national governing control through recruitment of independents, support arrangements, and, at points, cooperation with the New Liberal Club. In 1993, after splits and another election, the LDP lost national governing power for the first time since its creation in 1955. The distinction is essential: a party can fail to hold more than half of the seats by itself and still keep the premiership and cabinet through parliamentary support. This retrospective endpoint should not displace the chapter’s principal focus on 1955–1960.

Section 92.20 · Division V

Democracy, Dominant-Party Rule, and the Historical Meaning of Anpo

The central paradox of the period is that Japan combined enduring constitutional democracy and active civic participation with prolonged one-party dominance. Elections were free, the press was critical, and citizens could and did mobilize on a vast scale. Yet the same party formed every government for nearly four decades. The two facts are not contradictory if democracy is understood as the presence of competitive elections and civil liberties, and they are in tension if democracy is also understood as the regular possibility of changing governing parties.

The Anpo crisis can be read in more than one way. It demonstrated the resilience of parliamentary institutions: the crisis was resolved through constitutional procedures, a leader resigned, and a successor was chosen without coup or collapse. It demonstrated the capacity of grassroots democracy, since citizens who were not party members organized themselves in large numbers. It also showed limits on popular influence, since the treaty took effect and the ruling party kept its majority. Each reading highlights real evidence, and the most defensible account holds them together.

Historical judgments about Anpo depend partly on what counts as democratic success. An institutionally focused account emphasizes the legality of treaty approval, continued elections, leadership succession, and the absence of military takeover. A social-movement account emphasizes the capacity of ordinary citizens to challenge a government, the costs of forceful procedure, and the political education of a generation. A diplomatic account emphasizes that the alliance endured and continued to structure Japan’s security relations. Each perspective uses different evidence and asks different questions. The interpretations need not be forced into a choice between total victory and total failure.

Okinawa makes the question more complex. Japanese sovereignty after 1952 was not uniform in practice, and a security system defended as a national necessity imposed unequal territorial burdens. Anpo's significance therefore cannot be fully measured by the views of politicians and demonstrators in Tokyo. The chapter’s strongest concluding claim is that constitutional institutions, allied security, party competition, and civic activism became deeply entangled in postwar Japan. The protests did not settle these tensions; they made them visible and left a record that later generations would continue to reinterpret.

The crisis also set questions that would recur: how to interpret Article 9, what role bases should have, how Okinawa's position should be resolved, when a majority's legal authority is legitimate, and what citizens may do when they believe it is not. The later movements of the 1960s and 1970s (Chapters 93 and 94), the debates over security legislation, constitutional revision, and base politics in Okinawa (Chapter 99), and the memory of Anpo itself all drew on these questions.

  • Rural overrepresentation and the importance of local networks
  • The constitution's Article 9 and its contested interpretation

Historical Debate

Was the 1955 System Stable Democracy or Constrained Political Competition?

The question: Historians and political scientists disagree about how to characterize a democracy in which one party governed continuously for decades.

Interpretation A — A stable and responsive democracy: Some scholars emphasize that elections were free and competitive, that factional competition within the LDP functioned as a substitute for alternation, that the party adapted to voters' demands, and that opposition parties influenced policy and governed localities. In this view the system gave Japan decades of stability and growth within a working democratic framework.
Interpretation B — Constrained competition: Others emphasize that the electoral rules, rural overrepresentation, business and bureaucratic alliances, and divided opposition gave the LDP a structural advantage, that the absence of alternation reduced accountability, and that patronage and corruption were built into the system. In this view the system limited the effective choices available to voters.

Evidence: Both sides use election results, apportionment data, studies of kōenkai and finance, party records, and comparisons with other countries. Many scholars now argue the interpretations are complementary.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians evaluate the significance of Anpo, the later reforms of the 1990s, and what counts as democratic success.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Japan established a durable democratic political system dominated by one party, and how the crisis over the 1960 security treaty exposed fundamental disagreements about sovereignty, constitutional government, and political participation. Its central argument is that the 1955 System was neither a simple two-party democracy nor an authoritarian arrangement. It was a competitive but structurally unequal parliamentary order.

After the occupation ended in 1952 the future of Japanese democracy was uncertain. Conservative leaders were divided between followers of Yoshida and his rivals, the Socialists had split over the peace settlement, and public opinion was divided over rearmament and the alliance. The reunification of the Socialists in October 1955 and the merger of the Liberals and Democrats into the LDP in November created the party structure that endured until 1993.

The institutions of the system shaped political behavior. The cabinet depended on the Diet, but the bureaucracy played a large role in policy. The single non-transferable vote in multi-member districts led LDP candidates to compete against each other and to build personal kōenkai, which tied politicians to local networks and to donors. Business federations, agricultural cooperatives, and local governments exchanged support and favors with the party, and rural overrepresentation increased its advantage.

The LDP was not a unified organization. Its factions competed for the presidency, and leaders such as Hatoyama, Ishibashi, Kishi, and Ikeda had different priorities. Factional bargaining allowed leadership to change without a change of governing party. The opposition, led by the Japan Socialist Party and tied to Sōhyō, was real but divided, and it fragmented further with the Democratic Socialist Party in 1960 and Kōmeitō in 1964.

Security policy was the most divisive issue. The Self-Defense Forces, founded in 1954, were defended by the government as consistent with Article 9 and condemned by critics as remilitarization. The 1951 Security Treaty left American bases in place without a clear defense obligation, and Okinawa remained under American administration, with a status distinct from that of the main islands.

Kishi Nobusuke sought to revise the treaty, and the revised Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security was signed on January 19, 1960. It created a mutual commitment limited to Japanese-administered territory (Article V), permitted American use of bases for Japan's security and for peace in the Far East (Article VI), and provided for prior consultation through an exchange of notes. Supporters saw an advance in equality, and opponents saw deeper entanglement in American strategy.

The government's use of police to remove opposition members from the Diet on May 19 and its passage of the treaty in the early hours of May 20 turned a policy dispute into a crisis of legitimacy. Strikes and demonstrations involving unions, students, intellectuals, women's groups, and ordinary citizens followed. The death of Kanba Michiko on June 15 and the cancellation of Eisenhower's visit marked the crisis's peak.

The treaty received automatic approval on June 19 and entered into force on June 23, and Kishi resigned. Ikeda's government shifted attention to economic growth, the LDP won the November 1960 election, and the assassination of Asanuma Inejirō showed the persistence of political violence. The LDP maintained its dominance through the late Shōwa era.

Historians disagree about whether the system was stable democracy or constrained competition, and about whether Anpo was a defeat, a victory, or both. The chapter's position is that the crisis revealed both the resilience of parliamentary institutions and the limits of popular influence, and that the alliance's survival coexisted with a lasting transformation in how legitimacy was argued. These questions recur in Chapters 93, 94, and 99.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how Japan's postwar political order was built and tested. The system that emerged in 1955 was a durable democracy with an unusual feature: continuous governance by one party. The Anpo crisis exposed how fragile and how resilient that order could be, since the government passed its treaty, citizens protested in numbers not seen since, and the institutions absorbed the shock.

The chapter also shows that democracy involves more than elections. The dispute of May 1960 was about whether a legal majority could act in ways many citizens believed violated the spirit of parliamentary government. That question did not disappear after the treaty took effect, and it reappeared in later controversies over security legislation and constitutional interpretation.

Finally, the chapter connects the main islands to Okinawa and Japan to the United States and Asia. The alliance shaped Japan's economy and security, and the burden of the bases fell unevenly. The movements of the later 1960s and 1970s, treated in the next chapters, inherited both the energy of Anpo and its unresolved questions.
