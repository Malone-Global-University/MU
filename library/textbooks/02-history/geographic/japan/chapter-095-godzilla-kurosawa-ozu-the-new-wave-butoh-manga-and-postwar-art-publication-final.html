Godzilla, Kurosawa, Ozu, the New Wave, Butoh, Manga, and Postwar Art

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In September 1951 a Japanese film that its own studio had not expected to travel was screened at the Venice Film Festival and won the Golden Lion. The film was Rashomon. Its director, Kurosawa Akira, was not in Venice, and by most accounts the company that made it, Daiei, had shown little confidence in it. About six years after the firebombing of Japanese cities and Japan’s surrender, a story of a killing in a forest, told four contradictory ways, had made foreign audiences look at Japanese cinema as an art.

The Venice prize is a convenient place to begin, but it can mislead. It suggests that postwar Japanese culture was something produced for export and discovered abroad. Most of what Japanese people made between 1945 and the mid-1970s was made for Japanese audiences, in conditions that were local, commercial, and often precarious. In 1958 Japanese cinemas recorded their highest annual attendance on record, approximately 1.13 billion admissions according to the National Film Archive of Japan, a figure that says more about daily life than any festival prize does. The same decades saw comic books sold in rental shops, weekly magazines with circulations in the hundreds of thousands, television sets carried into living rooms, and artists staging events in burned-out lots, rented halls, and city streets. This chapter asks how these different kinds of cultural production were made, who paid for them, who watched and read them, and what meanings were attached to them.

The chapter's governing distinction is among four things that are easily blurred: cultural production (the works and the people who made them), the institutions that supported or constrained production (studios, publishers, broadcasters, censors, galleries, newspapers, advertisers), the audiences that consumed it, and the social meanings assigned to it afterward by critics, governments, and later generations. A film such as Tokyo Story can be understood as the work of a director, as the product of Shōchiku's studio system, as a Saturday-night experience for a family in a provincial theater, and as a canonical masterpiece in an international critical ranking. These are different historical objects, and they should not be collapsed into one.

The works discussed here responded, directly or indirectly, to conditions established in earlier chapters: the memory of war and atomic destruction (Chapters 80–81), the Allied occupation and its censorship (Chapters 82–88), the economic growth and household transformation described in Chapter 91, the 1960 security-treaty crisis (Chapter 92), the student and labor movements of Chapter 93, and the environmental and minority movements of Chapter 94. This chapter does not retell those events. It asks how Japanese filmmakers, writers, dancers, painters, photographers, architects, designers, and cartoonists represented them, argued about them, or turned away from them.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, "postwar Japanese culture" was not a single movement. The Japanese New Wave, Butoh, Gutai, Mono-ha, and underground theater overlapped in time and sometimes in personnel, but they had different institutions, audiences, and aims. Second, international recognition and domestic popularity are different measures. Kurosawa's festival success, Ozu's critical reputation, manga's enormous domestic readership, and the narrow audiences of avant-garde performance represent different patterns of influence. Third, the global media environment of the twenty-first century, including the worldwide anime market and later studio brands, must not be read backward onto the 1950s and 1960s. Where later developments are mentioned, they are foreshadowing only.

The evidence is unusually rich and unevenly distributed. Surviving films, photographs, manifestos, magazines, and exhibition records allow close analysis, and institutions such as the National Film Archive of Japan, the Guggenheim's research on Gutai, the Mori Art Museum's work on Metabolism, and the Tezuka Osamu archive provide starting points. But films were re-edited, prints deteriorated or were lost, performances were ephemeral, and the reputations of many works were fixed later by critics and exhibition organizers. Box-office figures, circulation numbers, and attendance counts are compiled by industry bodies with their own purposes and are given in this chapter as approximations. Wherever a work is described, students should distinguish what can be seen or read in the work itself from what later commentary has said about it. Nothing quoted in this chapter's primary-source windows is a verified verbatim translation; those windows paraphrase and direct students to originals.

The chapter has five divisions. Division I treats the studio system and the canonical directors of the 1950s. Division II turns to Godzilla, popular genres, television's competition, and the New Wave and independent cinema of the 1960s. Division III follows Butoh, underground theater, Gutai, Mono-ha, and photography. Division IV considers literature, architecture, and design, including the Olympics and Expo '70. Division V examines manga, television animation, popular music, and the historical meaning of the whole. Ryukyu and Okinawa, Zainichi Koreans, Buraku communities, Hibakusha, and women creators recur throughout, as the book's governing framework requires.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how occupation censorship, studio reorganization, and the mass audience shaped Japanese cinema between 1945 and the late 1950s.
  2. Analyze the work of Kurosawa, Ozu, Mizoguchi, and Naruse as responses to postwar social change rather than as isolated masterpieces.
  3. Interpret the 1954 Godzilla as simultaneously a commercial product, a technical achievement, and an expression of nuclear-age anxiety, distinguishing it from its 1956 American re-edit.
  4. Distinguish among popular studio genres, the Japanese New Wave, and independent and documentary cinema, identifying their institutions and audiences.
  5. Compare Butoh, underground theater, Gutai, and Mono-ha, and explain why they should not be treated as branches of one avant-garde.
  6. Evaluate postwar photography as both documentation and experiment, distinguishing documentary evidence from staged or interpretive images.
  7. Trace the development of postwar literature from defeat-era writing through the major novelists of the 1950s and 1960s.
  8. Assess Metabolism, the 1964 Olympic design program, and Expo '70 as visions of the modern city and as statements about national identity, separating realized projects from unbuilt schemes.
  9. Explain how publishing, rental shops, magazines, and television shaped manga and anime, and distinguish the two media.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of postwar culture as renewal of tradition, international exchange, or radical innovation.
  11. Identify the contributions of women, Zainichi Koreans, Okinawans, and other marginalized participants to cultural production and its representation.
  12. Evaluate films, manifestos, photographs, and design materials as historical sources, attending to framing, audience, distribution, and circumstances of production.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the structure of the film industry, and the end of wartime and then occupation controls, shape what Japanese filmmakers could say and to whom?
  2. In what ways did films about families, women, samurai, and monsters register social change without necessarily being about politics?
  3. Why did international recognition arrive for some works and not others, and what does that tell historians about the relation between foreign audiences and domestic ones?
  4. How did artists and performers respond to the memory of atomic destruction, the Anpo Crisis, and the rapid transformation of Japanese cities?
  5. What distinguished the aims, institutions, and audiences of Butoh, Gutai, Mono-ha, underground theater, and the New Wave?
  6. How did publishers, magazine editors, rental-shop owners, and broadcasters, not only individual artists, create modern manga and television animation?
  7. How did architects and designers use the Olympics and Expo '70 to present a Japan of the future, and what social realities did those presentations leave out?
  8. Whose creative agency has been obscured by famous-director narratives, and how does including women, Zainichi, Okinawan, and Buraku subjects alter the account?
  9. Was postwar Japanese culture a revival of tradition, an adaptation of foreign models, or something new, and what evidence bears on the question?

Historical Context

Japanese cultural life before 1945 had already been shaped by mass media. Chapter 65 described newspapers, benshi-narrated silent film, and early cinema; Chapter 70 described department stores, radio, and the modern city; Chapter 72 described the folk-craft movement and literary experimentation. By the 1930s Japan had a large film industry, a mass-circulation magazine market, a tradition of modernist art and architecture, and an established commercial theater. Wartime mobilization (Chapter 77) then placed these under state direction: film companies were consolidated, scripts were reviewed, and publishers faced paper rationing and censorship. Many postwar cultural figures had been trained, employed, or compromised within that system, and the question of how they had behaved in wartime was itself a subject of postwar argument.

Defeat and occupation changed the conditions of production without erasing continuities of personnel and technique. The Civil Censorship Detachment of the occupation reviewed films, publications, and broadcasts until 1949 and monitored them afterward in other forms; it discouraged themes of feudal loyalty and revenge, and it was especially restrictive about depicting the atomic bombings (Chapters 82 and 88). At the same time the occupation's Civil Information and Education section encouraged themes it considered democratic, such as individual choice and, famously, the screen kiss. Producers and directors learned to work within a changing framework of permissions, and when formal controls ended in 1952 a long period of suppressed subjects found new outlets.

The economic setting was the high-growth economy described in Chapter 91. Rising incomes created audiences with leisure time and disposable money. Urban migration separated young adults from rural households and created the family situations that filmmakers and novelists examined. Mass consumption also created new media: from 1953 television, and from the late 1950s weekly magazines aimed at children and adolescents. The political setting, treated in Chapters 92 and 93, included the 1960 Anpo Crisis and the late-1960s protest movements, which gave artists topics and audiences, and also posed the question of whether art should be political. Chapter 94 supplied the environmental and minority movements that documentary filmmakers and some artists accompanied.

Finally, the international setting was unusual. Japan was an occupied country until 1952 and, until 1972, Okinawa remained under American administration. Foreign films, jazz, comics, and art movements entered through the occupation, through festivals, and through international exhibitions; Japanese works moved outward through festival juries, expatriate artists, critics, and American television syndication. Students should avoid both extremes of interpretation: Japanese culture was not simply an imitation of the West, and it was not an isolated tradition. It was formed in continual, selective, and uneven exchange.

Section 95.1 · Division I — Postwar Reconstruction and the Golden Age of Japanese Cinema

Cinema after Defeat: Occupation, Reconstruction, and the Japanese Film Industry

In 1945 the Japanese film industry was damaged but not destroyed. Studios had been merged under wartime direction into a small number of firms, theaters had been bombed, and film stock was scarce, yet directors, cameramen, scenarists, actors, and technicians remained, and many of the institutional habits of the 1930s survived. Occupation authorities abolished wartime controls such as the prewar film law and the propaganda machinery, then replaced them with their own. Scripts and finished films were submitted for review. Themes regarded as feudal or militaristic, including revenge, suicide for honor, and the glorification of the sword, were discouraged, which effectively suppressed much of the sword-fighting period drama on which the industry had relied. Directors turned to contemporary stories (gendaigeki), to adaptations of literature, and to explicitly "democratic" themes. The atomic bombings were a subject on which censorship was strict until 1952; when the controls lifted, films such as Shindō Kaneto's Children of Hiroshima (1952) were among the first to address them directly.

The occupation's reforms and the industry's own conflicts reorganized production. Labor militancy in the late 1940s, including prolonged disputes at Tōhō, produced a breakaway company, Shintōhō, and left lasting marks on the industry's relations with its employees. By the early 1950s six companies dominated: Shōchiku, Tōhō, Daiei, Tōei (founded in 1951), Nikkatsu (which resumed production in 1954), and Shintōhō. Each combined production, distribution, and in most cases ownership or control of theaters. Directors, actors, and technicians were typically employed under contract and assigned to projects; a studio's identity was expressed through house styles, stars, and genres. Shōchiku was associated with domestic and sentimental stories and with Ozu; Tōhō with modern urban comedies, science fiction, and the productions of Kurosawa; Daiei with literary adaptation and period dramas; Tōei with high-volume period and action films; and Nikkatsu, after its return, with youth-oriented action. The studios coordinated through an arrangement of the early 1950s that limited the movement of directors and stars among companies, which helped stabilize production but constrained independent careers.

Block booking and double-bill programming made the system efficient. Theaters were supplied with a regular stream of paired films; audiences attended frequently and often without choosing a particular title. The audience grew explosively. Admissions rose through the early 1950s and peaked in 1958 at approximately 1.13 billion, and the industry released a very large number of feature films each year, on the order of five hundred around 1960. Cinema was inexpensive, available in towns and cities alike, and, before television, the principal visual entertainment. The postwar cultural historian must treat film as a form of shared daily life: families, couples, and groups of friends attended together, and theaters were also places of newsreels, advertising, and neighborhood sociability.

The industry also served as a laboratory for the reconstruction of public life. Films about returned soldiers, widows, youth, and family hardship addressed national circumstances without leaving the commercial format. War films of the 1950s, among them Ichikawa Kon's The Burmese Harp (1956) and Fires on the Plain (1959) and Kobayashi Masaki's The Human Condition (1959–1961), looked at soldiers' suffering and moral choices; these films were not uniform in their politics, and the question of Japanese responsibility toward victims abroad was raised more cautiously than the question of Japanese suffering. The industrial and institutional foundations described here, not the economic history of Chapter 91, are the context for the individual directors who follow.

Section 95.2 · Division I

Kurosawa Akira: Moral Conflict, Historical Drama, and International Recognition

Kurosawa Akira entered the industry in the 1930s as an assistant director and began directing in 1943. His early postwar films, such as No Regrets for Our Youth (1946), addressed political conscience under militarism. His reputation was made by a sequence of films in the 1950s. Rashomon (1950), produced by Daiei, adapted stories by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke into a narrative in which a murder and a rape in a forest are recounted in incompatible versions by different witnesses. The film's formal devices became famous: the camera tracks through dappled light in the forest, the testimony is framed by a rainstorm at a ruined gate, and the editing and performance emphasize that each account serves the speaker. The film refuses to resolve which account is true. Its argument concerns the self-serving nature of memory and the limits of human trust, a topic with obvious resonance in a society reassessing the claims made during the war.

Rashomon won the Golden Lion at Venice in 1951 and received an honorary American academy award for a foreign-language film the following year, and it is conventionally credited with opening Western audiences to Japanese cinema. Historians have qualified this account in several ways. Daiei had not been the film's strongest advocate and reportedly had little idea of its prospects abroad; the film's selection depended on intermediaries in the international festival circuit; and critics have pointed out that its reception abroad often assumed an exoticized "Japan." A fair account notes that Kurosawa's work was also addressed to Japanese audiences and rooted in Japanese literature, theater, and film history, and that Western enthusiasm for it, though consequential, was not its reason for existing.

Ikiru (1952), with Shimura Takashi as a municipal bureaucrat who learns he is dying of cancer, is a contemporary drama about institutional inertia and the search for purpose; it gives the postwar bureaucracy and the social world of ward offices a critical scrutiny that few films of the period matched. Seven Samurai (1954), made at Tōhō with an unusually large budget and a long production, recounts the defense of a village against bandits by hired masterless samurai. It is a period film that avoids the clichés of the genre, with detailed attention to the class tension between peasants and warriors, a mobile camera, multiple-camera editing in the battle sequences, and use of rain and mud. Throne of Blood (1957), an adaptation of Macbeth, borrowed from Noh staging, and Yojimbo (1961) combined the period film with the structure of American crime fiction in a comic and violent story of a wandering swordsman playing two gangs against each other. Its commercial success led to a sequel in 1962, and it was later remade abroad as a Western, which illustrates the circulation of genre across borders.

High and Low (1963) transferred a plot from an American crime novel to Yokohama, structuring it as a study of social class: a wealthy shoe-company executive faces a ransom decision, and the investigation moves from his hilltop house to the districts below. The film's first half is a chamber drama in a single room; the second is a procedural through the postwar city. It shows Kurosawa's central preoccupation with moral choice made under social pressure, and it allows the historian to see urban inequality in a high-growth society as a film's formal organizing principle. Kurosawa's interactions with Western literature (Dostoevsky, Shakespeare, Ed McBain) and with Japanese theater were integral to his method rather than imitation. They should not be read as proof that his work was primarily for foreign viewers; most of his audience was in Japan, and his career narrowed in the later 1960s as production costs and studio conditions changed.

Section 95.3 · Division I

Ozu Yasujirō: Family, Generational Change, and the Everyday Life of Postwar Japan

Ozu Yasujirō had directed films since the 1920s at Shōchiku, and his late period, beginning about 1949, is the one that secured his place in film history. His subjects were consistently domestic: parents and adult children, the arrangement of a daughter's marriage, the loneliness of a widower, relationships among office colleagues. Late Spring (1949), with Hara Setsuko and Ryū Chishū, concerns a young woman who wishes to remain with her widowed father and is gradually brought to accept marriage. In Equinox Flower (1958), his first color film, a father who thinks himself progressive is confronted by his daughter's insistence on choosing her own husband. An Autumn Afternoon (1962), his last, returns to a widower and a daughter. Together these films trace how expectations of marriage, obedience, and family duty were changing in a society of nuclear households and salaried work.

Ozu's formal style was equally distinctive and mattered to the way his subject was perceived. The camera was typically placed low and static, at roughly the height of a person seated on a tatami floor; scenes were composed with careful symmetry; conversations were often shot with characters addressing the camera lens in alternating shots; short shots of empty rooms, hallways, laundry, or railway lines punctuated the action; and cuts rarely used the dissolves or fades common in contemporary film. The effect was to give weight to small gestures and to silences. Emotions that the characters did not speak, such as a father's grief at a daughter's departure, were conveyed through composition, performance, and rhythm. This restraint was a deliberate aesthetic and not a lack of technique, and the style depended on a stable studio system that allowed him to work for years with the same writer, cinematographer, and actors.

Tokyo Story (1953) is the central teaching example. An elderly couple from the port town of Onomichi travel to Tokyo to visit their adult children, who are busy with their own lives and find the visit inconvenient; only their widowed daughter-in-law, Noriko, played by Hara Setsuko, shows them consistent kindness. The film does not condemn the children as simple villains, and it does not romanticize rural life. It suggests instead that the changes of the postwar decade, including urban work, small apartments, and the nuclear family, strain the obligations of a generation formed in a different household order. Its war legacy is present though not stated: the couple's youngest son was killed in the war, and Noriko is his widow, whose continued loyalty is itself a historical comment on how war's losses persisted in domestic life. The film avoids presenting modernization as either wholly beneficial or wholly destructive, and it is this refusal of moral simplicity that has made it so durable.

Ozu's reputation developed unevenly. In Japan he was known as a Shōchiku house director of family dramas, respected but sometimes regarded as unusually "Japanese" and therefore less suited to export; international recognition came later, and the critical elevation of Tokyo Story to the top of international polls is a phenomenon of later decades. Historians should therefore avoid reading back from that later status to the film's initial reception. It is also worth noting the limits of his world: Ozu's families are largely middle-class and metropolitan, and the film's Japan has little place for the rural poor, the colonial legacy, or the minority communities who appear elsewhere in this chapter. He died in December 1963, on his sixtieth birthday.

Analysis: Students should examine framing (what is centered, what is left out), camera height and movement, the rhythm of cuts, performance style, the use of space and weather, and the placement of silence. The Tokyo Story sequences convey disappointment and loneliness through stillness and the arrangement of figures rather than through speech; the Rashomon sequences use motion, light, and direct address to make narrative unreliability visible. As sources they reveal how studio directors addressed domestic audiences about family and moral uncertainty. They cannot by themselves show how audiences responded, what the studios intended commercially, or how representative either film was of its year's output; for that, box-office records, reviews, and studio documents are needed. The films are also the work of directors, writers, cameramen, and performers, not of one author.

  1. What do the framing and editing of each sequence show, and how do they differ?
  2. Who made these films, for what audiences, and under what industrial conditions?
  3. What can historians learn from them about attitudes to family, memory, and trust in the early 1950s?
  4. What can historians not establish from the films alone about audience reception or the experience of those absent from the screen?
Section 95.4 · Division I

Mizoguchi, Naruse, and the Representation of Women's Lives in Japanese Cinema

Women were central to the cinema of the period, both as audiences and as subjects. Mizoguchi Kenji, who had directed since the 1920s, returned in the early 1950s to a theme he had pursued for decades: the situation of women in a social order that treated them as dependent and exploitable. The Life of Oharu (1952), adapted from a seventeenth-century Ihara Saikaku story and starring Tanaka Kinuyo, follows a woman who is passed from household to household, concubine, courtesan, and servant, as a succession of men and families use her. Set in the Tokugawa period (Chapters 43 and 46), the film invites viewers to see that history's gender hierarchy and, by implication, that of their own time. Ugetsu (1953) and Sanshō the Bailiff (1954), both awarded prizes at Venice, treat war, greed, abduction, and the sufferings of families in period settings, with long takes and a measured camera. Street of Shame (1956), his last film, concerned women in a licensed Tokyo brothel district while the Diet debated legislation against prostitution; it shows how a commercial studio film could address a current public controversy.

Naruse Mikio worked at a smaller scale, usually at Tōhō, and his subject was the working lives of ordinary women: bar hostesses, widows, shopkeepers, wives disappointed by their marriages. Floating Clouds (1955), adapted from Hayashi Fumiko's novel and starring Takamine Hideko and Mori Masayuki, follows a woman's unhappy love affair with a man who had been her lover in wartime Indochina, tracing a relationship through the disintegration of postwar Japan. The film's war background is the colonial empire (Chapter 78) and its collapse; the personal story is a story about an ex-imperial society, and it was not treated as a political film in its own time. Naruse's films emphasize money, rent, work, and dependency as much as romance, which makes them valuable evidence about the economic position of women.

The performers were creators, not merely subjects. Hara Setsuko's roles with Ozu and others made her an emblem of the thoughtful postwar woman; she retired from film in 1963 and withdrew from public life. Takamine Hideko, a child star who became one of the most admired actresses of the period, worked with Naruse and Kinoshita Keisuke and later wrote about her career. Kyō Machiko, Tanaka Kinuyo, and others shaped the meaning of their roles through performance. Tanaka Kinuyo became a director, making films including Love Letter (1953) and later works in the 1950s and early 1960s; she was the only Japanese woman directing feature films in this period at a major studio, and she did so against considerable institutional resistance. That she is better known as an actress shows how the record has favored male directors, and it is a reminder that the writers, editors, and script supervisors of the studio system included women whose work is poorly documented.

These films should not be read as simple protest. Studio melodrama and period drama could contain serious criticism of marriage, prostitution, and poverty while operating within genre conventions and commercial expectations, and the same films could be received by audiences as sentimental entertainment. Their relation to the political movements of Chapter 94 is indirect: that chapter examined women's organization and legal rights; this section has examined how women's situation was represented and how women contributed to filmmaking. The two bodies of evidence are complementary. Women in the film industry were also workers within a hierarchy with few openings for advancement, and the gap between the visibility of women on screen and their opportunity behind the camera is itself historical evidence.

Section 95.5 · Division II — Godzilla, Popular Film Genres, and the Japanese New Wave

Godzilla, Nuclear Anxiety, and the Cultural Memory of Destruction

Godzilla (Gojira) was released by Tōhō on November 3, 1954. Its production team was a studio team: producer Tanaka Tomoyuki, who developed the project after a planned international production fell through; director Honda Ishirō, who had served in the army and had seen the ruins of Hiroshima after the war; special-effects director Tsuburaya Eiji, whose wartime and postwar work in miniature effects shaped the film's look; and composer Ifukube Akira, whose score and the creature's roar became part of its identity. Shimura Takashi, Kōchi Momoko, Takarada Akira, and Hirata Akihiko played the central roles, and the monster itself was realized chiefly by suit performer Nakajima Haruo, with additional suit work attributed in film histories to Katsumi Tezuka, against detailed miniature sets representing Tokyo. Exact scene-by-scene attribution between the performers is not established here. These were technical achievements of a high order: the carefully built sets, the slow movements that convey mass, and the sound design of destruction gave audiences a plausible catastrophe.

The film emerged in an unusually charged context. On March 1, 1954, an American hydrogen-bomb test at Bikini Atoll exposed the crew of the Japanese fishing boat Daigo Fukuryū Maru (Lucky Dragon No. 5) to fallout. The crew's illness was reported in the Japanese press that month, contamination of tuna and fears about rain and food spread, and the radio operator, Kuboyama Aikichi, died in September. A national petition campaign against atomic and hydrogen bombs followed (Chapter 92). Only nine years after Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and two years after the end of the occupation's restrictions on discussing the bombs, the film placed nuclear destruction before a mass audience in the form of a creature awakened by nuclear testing that attacks Tokyo. Its scenes of burned ruins, hospital wards of the injured, and a girls' chorus singing for peace refer visibly to wartime experience, and a scientist's moral dilemma over a new weapon registers anxieties about science that were not confined to Japan.

The argument of this section is that Godzilla was several things at once. It was commercial entertainment, made by a studio to earn money, and it was a hit, drawing a very large audience by contemporary standards. It was a technological achievement in the development of the special-effects tradition (tokusatsu) that became a hallmark of Tōhō. And it was a cultural expression of nuclear-age anxiety that audiences could read in different ways: as a memorial, as a warning, as a spectacle. Critics have also noted its limits. The film is primarily about Japanese victimhood and scientific responsibility; it does not engage the war's victims abroad, and its treatment of the Hibakusha is indirect. The question of how far it criticizes American nuclear policy is also debated, since the film does not mention the United States directly.

The original film must be distinguished from the American version. In 1956 Tōhō's film was substantially re-edited for American distribution as Godzilla, King of the Monsters!, with new footage featuring the actor Raymond Burr as an American journalist, shortened scenes, and the removal or de-emphasis of much of the film's explicit nuclear and wartime content. Many viewers outside Japan knew only that version for decades. Students analyzing the film as evidence must therefore ask which version they are seeing, in what language, and with what cuts, since the meanings change with the editing. The sequels that followed beginning in 1955 transformed the creature into the center of a continuing franchise, as the next section explains.

Section 95.6 · Division II

Popular Genres, Studio Competition, and the Changing Film Audience

Art cinema formed a small part of what audiences watched. Most of the industry's output was genre film. Period dramas (jidaigeki) returned to prominence after the end of occupation restrictions, and sword-fighting films (chanbara) became the specialty of Tōei, whose Kyoto studio produced them at high volume with stars such as Ichikawa Utaemon and Kataoka Chiezō. Comedies and salaryman films, many at Tōhō, depicted office life and suburban households; melodramas, often at Shōchiku and Daiei, addressed romance and family; and the youth film acquired its own identity with the taiyōzoku ("sun tribe") films of 1955–1956, derived from Ishihara Shintarō's novella Season of the Sun and starring his brother Ishihara Yūjirō, which scandalized older commentators by depicting affluent young people's sexuality and recklessness. Nikkatsu built a stable of action stars and a style of crime and adventure film that deliberately avoided identifiable national settings; the director Suzuki Seijun's exaggerated style in films such as Branded to Kill (1967) led to his dismissal in 1968 and a public dispute.

Historical Vocabulary
Jidaigeki, Chanbara, and Kaijū Eiga

Jidaigeki ("period drama") designates films set before the Meiji era, usually in the Tokugawa period; gendaigeki designates contemporary stories. Chanbara, an onomatopoeic term for the sound of swords clashing, names sword-fighting films, often a subgenre of jidaigeki. Kaijū eiga ("monster films") refers to films about giant creatures, which depended on tokusatsu (special-effects) craft such as miniature sets and suit performance.

These categories are industrial and promotional labels, not timeless cultural forms. The jidaigeki had roots in Kabuki and storytelling traditions, but its postwar versions were shaped by occupation restrictions, studio specialization, and audience tastes. Students should avoid treating the samurai film as evidence of an unchanging "warrior spirit"; as earlier chapters showed, the samurai's historical meaning changed across periods, and cinema produced yet another version.

Yakuza cinema developed in the 1960s. Tōei's "chivalrous" (ninkyō) films, beginning with series such as Japanese Chivalry (1964) and starring Tsuruta Kōji and Takakura Ken, portrayed gangsters as bound by obligation and honor in an earlier era, offering audiences a stylized morality of duty that contrasted with the commercial pressures of the present. The relationship between such films and the actual history of organized crime (Chapter 87) was loose, and later "documentary-style" gangster films of the 1970s treated the subject differently. The Godzilla series, after the first film, gradually changed. Godzilla Raids Again (1955), King Kong vs. Godzilla (1962), and later films increasingly featured the creature as a hero or protagonist and reached children and family audiences; Godzilla vs. Hedorah (1971) took industrial pollution as its subject, reflecting the concerns of Chapter 94. The kaijū film thus moved from a nuclear-age allegory toward a flexible franchise, a trajectory that students should not read as a simple decline, since later films also responded to their own contexts. Other long-running series included the Tora-san films, which began in 1969 at Shōchiku and portrayed a wandering peddler's relations with his family.

Studio competition and the arrival of television transformed the audience. Television broadcasting began in 1953, and sales of sets rose steeply after the televised wedding of the Crown Prince in April 1959, then again before the 1964 Olympics; by the mid-1960s a large majority of households owned a set. Cinema attendance, which peaked in 1958 at approximately 1.127 billion admissions, began to fall sharply: the Motion Picture Producers Association of Japan (Eiren) records about 255 million admissions in 1970 (23 percent of the 1958 peak) and about 187 million in 1972 (17 percent). Television was a principal cause, but not the only one. Rising incomes allowed households to buy other forms of leisure; urban expansion and commuting altered habits; young viewers sought different kinds of entertainment; and magazines, radio, and sports competed for time. Studios responded by cutting production, closing theaters, importing and distributing foreign films, signing television contracts, and turning to cheaper genres, and by the early 1970s the system had been severely weakened. Daiei went bankrupt in 1971, and Nikkatsu turned to low-budget erotic films. The collapse of the studio system also explains why independent and experimental film flourished, as Sections 95.7 and 95.8 show.

Everyday Life and Environment

From the Neighborhood Cinema to the Living Room Set

A composite reconstruction of patterns documented in audience surveys, memoirs, and histories of leisure. It describes no specific family.

In the mid-1950s a family in a provincial city might attend the cinema as a monthly or even weekly outing. The program was a double bill, often a studio's weekly release; children sat through period dramas or comedies, parents might prefer melodramas, and a newsreel and advertisements preceded the features. Teenagers went with friends and sometimes without permission, and the theater was a place to meet. Popular stars were known through posters, fan magazines, and the cinemas themselves; some families chose films by studio, so that a Tōei evening or a Shōchiku evening had a recognizable flavor.

Television arrived unevenly. Before the late 1950s many people watched in public: in shops, in the streets with "street television" sets, or at a neighbor's house. Wrestling and baseball were early attractions. As prices fell and household incomes rose, a set became a fixture of the main room, and evening viewing replaced outings. The new routine changed the relation among family members: programs were watched together, children's programs shaped children's play, and advertising entered the household daily. For those left behind by growth, such as rural families with little cash or small-town cinema owners whose audiences vanished, the change brought loss as well as convenience. The household's choices were part of the cause of the cultural transformations described in this chapter.

Section 95.7 · Division II

The Japanese New Wave: Ōshima, Shinoda, Yoshida, and the Challenge to Convention

Around 1959–1960 a group of young directors at Shōchiku began making films that departed from the studio's established style. Shōchiku, whose house style was associated with domestic drama and sentiment, was under commercial pressure from the youth films of Nikkatsu and from television, and its management promoted the new directors with a label borrowed from France, nūberu bāgu. Ōshima Nagisa, Shinoda Masahiro, and Yoshida Kijū were the best known. The label was a marketing device as much as a movement, and the directors themselves often disliked being grouped. It is a historian's category applied to a set of filmmakers who shared a generation, a studio, and a critical attitude, but who also differed in politics and style.

Ōshima's A Town of Love and Hope (1959), Cruel Story of Youth (1960), and Night and Fog in Japan (1960) established the tone. Cruel Story of Youth follows a young couple whose relationship is shaped by violence, sexual exploitation, and a bleak relation to the postwar adult generation; it was shot with bright color, location photography, and handheld movement. Night and Fog in Japan staged a long wedding party at which former student activists argue over the failures of the left, using extended takes, shifts between past and present, and a theatrical sense of space; it was a bitter critique of the left's factionalism and of the political disillusionment that followed the Anpo Crisis (Chapter 92). Shōchiku withdrew the film from theaters within days of its release in October 1960, shortly after the assassination of the Socialist Party leader Asanuma Inejirō. The studio's reasons, and Ōshima's protest over its decision, are well documented; he left Shōchiku and founded his own production company in 1961.

Shinoda and Yoshida pursued different paths. Shinoda's films, including Pale Flower (1964), explored crime, alienation, and the gambler's world, with composer Takemitsu Tōru contributing to the atmosphere. Yoshida's work, which included Akitsu Springs (1962) and later Eros + Massacre (1969), was more formally austere and concerned with political and sexual history. All three used experimental editing, disrupted narrative, location shooting, and a skeptical view of conventional morality. They also saw themselves in dialogue with contemporary Japanese cinema, including the work of directors such as Kinoshita Keisuke, and with critical debates in film journals.

The relation of the Japanese New Wave to the French nouvelle vague must be handled carefully. The label invited comparison, and some directors acknowledged interest in French film. But the Japanese cinema had its own traditions of experimentation, young directors' careers developed inside the studio system, and the political conflicts that shaped the films, the Anpo Crisis above all, were domestic. The Japanese New Wave was thus neither an importation nor an isolated development; it belonged to an international moment of youthful filmmaking while responding to specifically Japanese conditions. Its audiences were smaller than those of mainstream genre films, but its significance for later filmmakers was large, and its relation to the experimental and independent cinema of the later 1960s is the subject of the next section.

Section 95.8 · Division II

Independent Cinema, Radical Film, and the Art Theatre Guild

By the mid-1960s the decline of the studios opened space for filmmakers outside the system. The Art Theatre Guild (ATG), founded in 1961 as a distributor of foreign art films with a dedicated circuit of theaters, began in 1967 to co-produce Japanese films at low cost, sharing expenses with directors. This allowed filmmakers to pursue formal and political experiments that the studios would not fund. The ATG's Shinjuku theater became a center of the counterculture, linked to the district's bookstores, cafés, underground theaters, and political groups, and the Guild's films were seen by university students and intellectuals more than by the general public.

Imamura Shōhei, who began at Nikkatsu, made Pigs and Battleships (1961), a satirical film about the economy surrounding an American naval base at Yokosuka, then The Insect Woman (1963), which follows a woman's life across several decades of Japanese history, and The Profound Desire of the Gods (1968), set on a fictional southern island and shot in the southern Ryukyus, which treated ritual, incest, modernization, and the pressures of development; its setting makes it one of the film histories in which Okinawa's cultural otherness and its place in the Japanese imagination can be examined. Teshigahara Hiroshi, who came from the Sōgetsu art-school milieu, directed Woman in the Dunes (1964) from Abe Kōbō's novel and screenplay, with music by Takemitsu; the film, in which an entomologist is trapped in a sandpit village, won a special jury prize at Cannes and brought Teshigahara an Academy Award nomination for direction. Hani Susumu worked partly with non-professional actors and documentary methods in Bad Boys (1961) and later films.

The most radical works addressed sexuality, state violence, and national identity. Ōshima's Death by Hanging (1968), produced with the ATG, is based on the case of a Korean resident of Japan, who was convicted of murder and executed. It uses a Brechtian structure to examine capital punishment and the position of Zainichi Koreans in Japan (Chapter 94), and it ends by making the state's treatment of its colonial legacy the object of the film's argument. Matsumoto Toshio's Funeral Parade of Roses (1969) retold the Oedipus story in Tokyo's gay subculture, mixing documentary interviews, collage, and fiction, with performers from the city's nightlife; it is an important source for the history of sexual minorities in the late 1960s and is also a formal experiment in editing. These works challenged conventional cinematic morality as well as politics.

Documentary film developed alongside. Ogawa Shinsuke's collective, Ogawa Productions, filmed the farmers' resistance to the construction of Narita Airport in the Sanrizuka series beginning in 1968; its members lived in the community, an approach that blurred the line between filmmaker and participant. Tsuchimoto Noriaki spent years with the victims of mercury poisoning in Minamata, producing films from the early 1970s (Chapter 94). The events they filmed belong to Chapters 93 and 94, and the historian's task here is different: to analyze form, ethics, and reception. Questions include how embedded filmmaking affects representation, how the films were screened (in union halls, campuses, and community gatherings, outside commercial theaters), who controlled the footage, and how images of suffering were arranged and circulated. The independent and documentary films also show that the line between art and politics in this period was never fixed.

Section 95.9 · Division III — Butoh, Experimental Theater, and the Postwar Avant-Garde

Hijikata Tatsumi, Ōno Kazuo, and the Emergence of Butoh

Butoh, or more precisely ankoku butō ("dance of darkness"), emerged in Tokyo in the late 1950s. Its founder, Hijikata Tatsumi (1928–1986), was born in Akita in the northeast and trained in modern dance and in the expressive dance traditions that had come to Japan from Germany before the war. Ōno Kazuo (1906–2010), who was older and had performed in the prewar modern-dance world, became his long-term collaborator. Their milieu was the small world of Tokyo dance recitals, avant-garde writers, photographers, and painters, many of whom were drawn to Hijikata's work by their own dissatisfaction with both classical dance and shingeki, the modernist theater tradition associated with Western-influenced realism.

The founding event was Kinjiki (Forbidden Colors), performed at Daiichi Seimei Hall, Tokyo, on May 24, 1959, as part of the sixth New Artists’ Performance. Taking its title from Mishima Yukio's novel about homosexual love, the original duet featured Hijikata and the young Ōno Yoshito, Ōno Kazuo's son, in a short, disturbing performance in darkness that included an embrace between two male performers, a struggle, and a live chicken. Some of the audience was shocked, the organizers of the recital reportedly took offense, and Hijikata was thereafter excluded from that group's programs. A September 1959 reworking of Kinjiki involved Ōno Kazuo, who had not performed in the May premiere. The original performance's significance lay in its rejection of established ideas of beauty and grace: the body was presented as vulnerable, contorted, sexual, and unsettled. Later works such as Hijikata's 1968 performance Hijikata Tatsumi and the Japanese: Revolt of the Flesh, and his collaborations with photographers, graphic artists, and writers, extended these themes.

Historical Vocabulary
Nūberu Bāgu, Ankoku Butō, and Angura

Nūberu bāgu is the Japanese rendering of "nouvelle vague" and refers to the promotional label applied from about 1960 to young Shōchiku directors; it describes a marketing category and a loose critical tendency, not an organized group. Ankoku butō, the "dance of darkness," was the name Hijikata gave his work around 1960; it later shortened to butō and, in English, Butoh, a term that has been applied to a wide and diverse range of performers. Angura is a contraction of "underground" and refers to the experimental theater companies of the later 1960s, many of which performed in tents, small halls, and public spaces.

These terms are not interchangeable. The New Wave was a film phenomenon in the studios; Butoh was a movement in dance that began in the late 1950s; angura was a theatrical movement of the later 1960s. They overlapped in personnel, audiences, and venues but differed in chronology and institutions. Students should be wary of the tendency to blend them into a general "Japanese avant-garde."

Butoh's sources were diverse. Hijikata drew on German expressionist dance, which had influenced Japanese modern dance, on the writings of French authors including Genet and Artaud, on memories of rural Tōhoku life, and on Japanese ritual and folk movement. Ōno Kazuo's approach, shaped by his earlier training and later by his spiritual and Christian convictions, differed in emphasis from Hijikata's more confrontational and bodily experiments. This is a good illustration of why the movement should not be described as the work of a single artist or a single style. Butoh has also been linked, in popular accounts, to the atomic bombings, to the student movement, or to a purely "Japanese" aesthetic. Each claim captures a partial truth and none explains the movement. The performers' concerns were artistic and bodily: the possibility of a dance that did not depend on classical technique, the body as a site of memory, sexuality, and death. Butoh arose before the major protests of the 1960s and should not be reduced to them, although some performers moved in the same circles as political radicals and artists of the Anpo years.

Gender and sexuality were integral. Kinjiki's homoerotic subject was radical in a society where homosexuality had little public acknowledgment, and Hijikata's later work included female performers such as Ashikawa Yōko, who became a leading Butoh artist, and explored androgyny and transformation. Because performance is ephemeral, the evidence is partial: photographs, scores and notebooks, posters, a few films, and recollections. The archives of Hijikata's work, preserved by university and museum institutions in Japan, are central, and students should remember that photographs of performances are interpretive documents composed by photographers, not neutral records.

Section 95.10 · Division III

Underground Theater, Terayama Shūji, and Experimental Performance

The theater of the postwar decades had been dominated by two established traditions: the classical and commercial forms of Kabuki, Noh, and the popular stage, and shingeki ("new theater"), a modern, Western-influenced realist theater with left-leaning politics and institutional companies. In the 1960s younger artists rejected both as complacent. The result, called angura or "underground" theater, produced a wave of small companies that performed in cafés, shrine precincts, tents, and the street, with experimental scripts, physically intense acting, and an unabashedly confrontational relationship to audiences.

Terayama Shūji (1935–1983), a poet, playwright, filmmaker, and essayist, founded the company Tenjō Sajiki in 1967. His theater used surreal imagery, ritual, music, and audience involvement; he also wrote tanka poetry, essays on contemporary youth culture, and experimental films, and his 1967 book of essays and the film that followed it called on young people to abandon books and go into the streets. Kara Jūrō founded Situation no Kai in 1963 and renamed the troupe Jōkyō Gekijō (Situation Theater) in 1964. Its Red Tent production at Shinjuku’s Hanazono Shrine in 1967 turned a shrine precinct into a performance arena. The troupe’s plays blended Kabuki-inflected language, melodrama, and portrayals of people marginalized by the modern city, drawing sizeable young audiences. Suzuki Tadashi, working with the playwright Betsuyaku Minoru at the Waseda Little Theater, developed a rigorous actor-training method and a theater of the absurd that was less about spectacle than about disciplined physical presence. Other groups, such as Satō Makoto's, mixed political theater with experimental form. These companies differed in aims, and none represented the whole.

Angura theater was an urban and youth phenomenon, tied to Shinjuku's nightlife and to the universities. It interacted with cinema (Terayama and Ōshima's circles overlapped), photography, poetry, graphic design, and popular music; posters by artists such as Yokoo Tadanori and Awazu Kiyoshi became part of its identity. Its political significance is debated. Some participants saw the theater as a revolutionary act; others considered it a deliberate withdrawal from politics into the body, imagination, and community of the stage. The relation to the New Left (Chapter 93) was close at moments of overlap and sharp at others, since many theater artists rejected the organized left's doctrinal certainty. Evidence consists of scripts, posters, photographs, reviews, and recollections; the performances were ephemeral, and attendance figures are poorly documented. Critics later wrote the movement into a narrative of heroic rebellion, and the historian should separate that narrative from the evidence of what happened in the tents and basements.

Section 95.11 · Division III

Gutai, Mono-ha, and the Transformation of Japanese Visual Art

The Gutai Art Association was founded in 1954 near Osaka, in Ashiya, by Yoshihara Jirō (1905–1972), a painter and the head of a cooking-oil manufacturing firm whose wealth supported the group. Its members, mostly young, included Shiraga Kazuo, who painted with his feet while suspended above a canvas or wrestling in mud; Tanaka Atsuko, who in Electric Dress (1956) assembled a costume of colored light bulbs and tubes; Shimamoto Shōzō, who produced paintings by punching holes and by smashing bottles of paint against a surface; Murakami Saburō, who burst through paper screens; Yamazaki Tsuruko; Motonaga Sadamasa; and Kanayama Akira, among others. They staged outdoor exhibitions in Ashiya Park, stage performances in Osaka, and, from 1962, exhibitions in a gallery of their own. Yoshihara directed the group but insisted on originality, and members worked in different ways.

The group's founding principle, expressed in its 1956 manifesto, was that art should free materials from the demands of representation so that their own qualities, the matter of the work, could speak, and that the artist's body and action were part of the work. Gutai means roughly "concreteness" or "embodiment." Its experiments involved the transformation of materials, movement, bodily action, and the interaction of art with everyday experience and with the public, as in outdoor exhibits where visitors walked among installations. The group was soon known abroad. The French critic Michel Tapié, who promoted informal and gestural painting, visited in 1957, and Gutai works were exhibited in Europe and, in 1958, in New York. Western critics sometimes read the group as a Japanese version of Abstract Expressionism or of European art informel; Gutai artists and later historians have emphasized that their activities began independently and that exchange flowed both ways. The Guggenheim's exhibition research and later retrospectives helped restore the group's prominence. The association ended after Yoshihara's death in 1972, and the artistic reputation of its women members, such as Tanaka and Yamazaki, has been reassessed in recent decades.

Mono-ha ("School of Things"), which emerged in the late 1960s, was a different tendency. It did not share Gutai's expressive spontaneity. Sekine Nobuo's Phase—Mother Earth (1968), shown at an outdoor sculpture exhibition in Kobe, consisted of a cylindrical hole dug in the earth and a cylinder of the removed soil placed beside it, so that the work was the relation between absence and mass. Artists including Lee Ufan, Kishio Suga, Koshimizu Susumu, and others arranged stones, wood, steel plates, paper, and glass with minimal intervention, emphasizing the encounter between viewer, material, and space. Lee Ufan, born in Korea and educated in Japan, was the group's principal theorist, and his writings gave the tendency a philosophical vocabulary. Mono-ha has been interpreted in relation to both Zen aesthetics and Western phenomenology; it is better understood as a generation's response to industrial society and to the saturation of images, in the setting of the Tokyo Biennale of 1970 and the era's protests. Comparing the two movements, Gutai transformed materials through action, while Mono-ha arranged materials to reveal relationships; the first was exuberant, the second reserved. The presence of Lee Ufan, a Korean in Japan, also reminds students that Japanese postwar art was shaped by Zainichi and other participants and not only by ethnic Japanese artists.

Section 95.12 · Division III

Photography, International Avant-Garde Networks, and Artistic Self-Representation

Photography was both documentation and experiment in postwar Japan, and the distinction matters. Tōmatsu Shōmei, a member of the short-lived photographers' collective VIVO in the late 1950s, photographed the American military presence, the changing urban landscape, and, beginning in the early 1960s, the city of Nagasaki and its survivors. His book 11:02 Nagasaki (1966) combined images of damaged objects, scarred bodies, and ordinary streets, refusing the conventions of the sentimental memorial. He later turned sustained attention to Okinawa, where American administration and base society were part of daily life, and his work shows how photography could challenge the national story of recovery by focusing on what had not recovered. Hiroshima and Nagasaki were also subjects for other photographers and for writers, and the Hibakusha's own testimonies must be read alongside images made by others.

In 1968 a small group of photographers and critics, Nakahira Takuma, Taki Kōji, Takanashi Yutaka, and Okada Takahiko, launched the magazine Provoke, which Moriyama Daidō joined for later issues; it published only three issues. Its aesthetic, summarized as grainy, blurred, and out of focus (are, bure, boke), rejected the polished documentary photograph in favor of images shot on the move, tilted, and rough, which the photographers argued better captured the instability of experience after the political and social upheavals of the time. Moriyama's stray-dog photograph and urban nocturnal images became icons. The movement was short-lived, and some members soon moved in other directions, yet its influence was lasting. Its challenge was to the claim that a photograph could be a transparent record: the image had a maker, a vantage, and a politics.

Women artists and international networks were central. Kusama Yayoi, born in Matsumoto, left for the United States in the late 1950s, and in New York made large obsessively patterned paintings and soft sculptures and staged public happenings in the 1960s; she returned to Japan in 1973. Ono Yōko, who moved in New York's experimental circles and performed in Japan before leaving, produced instruction-based works and the performance Cut Piece (1964), in which audience members cut away her clothing; her book Grapefruit was first published in Tokyo in 1964. These artists belonged to an international avant-garde in which Fluxus, happenings, and conceptual art linked Tokyo, New York, and Europe. Japanese collectives such as Hi-Red Center (Akasegawa Genpei, Nakanishi Natsuyuki, and Takamatsu Jirō) staged actions in the center of Tokyo during the 1964 Olympic period, including a "cleaning event" in Ginza; Akasegawa was later prosecuted over his reproductions of banknotes, a case that tested the boundary between art and counterfeiting. These networks mattered because they allowed Japanese artists to work outside national frameworks and because they made experimental art a means of challenging official narratives of national recovery.

Students should distinguish documentary evidence from staged, experimental, or interpretive images. A photograph of a street fight, a posed portrait of a Hibakusha, and a deliberately blurred night scene have different evidentiary status, and the same image can be a record in one context and an artwork in another. Questions of consent and representation arise especially in images of atomic survivors and of bar workers and base-town residents. Many photographers worked on commission or for magazines, and the publication context, whether weekly magazine, art book, or gallery, shaped the meaning of the image.

Section 95.13 · Division IV — Literature, Architecture, and the Reconstruction of Cultural Identity

Literature after Defeat: War Memory, Disillusionment, and Everyday Experience

Postwar literature began amid destruction and censorship. Paper was scarce, publishers had been consolidated and compromised, and writers who had cooperated with wartime propaganda had to decide what to say about it. Occupation censors reviewed manuscripts until 1949 and monitored publication afterward, with particular strictness about the atomic bombings, so that works by survivors, such as Hara Tamiki's account of Hiroshima and Ōta Yōko's testimony of the city's destruction, reached readers late, in altered form, or in limited editions. When controls lifted, writers could address the bomb, the war, and defeat more openly, but they often chose indirect forms. Sakaguchi Ango's 1946 essay on moral decay after defeat was widely read as a refusal of both nostalgia and official piety; Ōoka Shōhei's Fires on the Plain (1951) drew on his experience as a soldier in the Philippines; and Ibuse Masuji, who built Black Rain, serialized from 1965, from survivor diaries and testimony, produced a novel of Hiroshima that was notable for restraint.

Dazai Osamu (1909–1948) became the emblem of defeat-era disillusionment. His novel No Longer Human (1948) presents a man alienated from social life and unable to sustain relations with others; the book's protagonist describes himself as masked, performing a self he does not feel. Dazai's suicide in 1948 reinforced a popular image of the doomed writer, and the novel's continuing readership, especially among young people, shows that its themes of isolation and shame outlasted the immediate postwar moment. Kawabata Yasunari, who had written before the war, continued with novels such as Thousand Cranes and The Sound of the Mountain in the early 1950s; his prose, restrained and elegiac, treated aging, desire, and the persistence of beauty. Women writers made major contributions. Enchi Fumiko's novels explored female desire and the weight of the past, Hayashi Fumiko's postwar work, which also inspired Naruse's films, chronicled the lives of women in defeat, and Kōno Taeko and others pushed the boundaries of what women could write about sexuality and family. Ariyoshi Sawako's novels later examined social problems such as the care of the elderly.

The literary marketplace was an institution as well as a background. Publishing houses such as Shinchōsha and Kōdansha, newspapers that serialized novels, literary magazines, and prizes such as the Akutagawa and Naoki Prizes, revived after the war, selected, financed, and promoted authors. Paperback series made classics and contemporary works cheap, and weekly magazines launched by publishers from the mid-1950s created a new mass readership and a new need for fiction and journalism. The Akutagawa Prize awarded in 1956 to Ishihara Shintarō for Season of the Sun illustrates how literature, film, and youth culture were intertwined: the same story fed the taiyōzoku films of Section 95.6. Rising literacy and education expanded the reading public beyond the urban elite, though it remained stratified by class, gender, and region.

Writers addressed isolation, shame, collective memory, family obligations, and the search for meaning in a society whose official values had collapsed and been replaced. They did so within a literary culture that debated whether the "I-novel" and other confessional forms were adequate, whether literature should be politically committed, and how Japanese writing stood in relation to Western models. The records they left, novels and essays, memoirs, reviews, and the archives of publishers, are evidence of how educated Japanese processed defeat, but they are literary evidence and do not speak for all social groups. Rural, working-class, colonial, and minority voices were less well represented in the established literary world, a point that should be kept in mind while reading the canonical authors in the next section.

Section 95.14 · Division IV

Mishima, Abe, Ōe, and Endō: Literature, Identity, and Modernity

Mishima Yukio (1925–1970) became the most publicly prominent novelist of the 1950s and 1960s. Confessions of a Mask (1949) made his reputation; The Temple of the Golden Pavilion (1956) fictionalized the 1950 burning of Kyoto's Kinkaku-ji by a young acolyte, using the crime to examine beauty, obsession, and destruction. Mishima's work ranged across novels, plays, essays, and film, and his public persona, which included bodybuilding and a growing political commitment to an emperor-centered nationalism, became part of his fame. In 1968 he founded a small private organization, the Shield Society, and on November 25, 1970, he and several members seized an office at the Self-Defense Forces' Ichigaya base in Tokyo, appealed to soldiers to rise against the postwar order, and then he committed ritual suicide. The event shocked the public, and its meaning is debated: it has been read as political theater, personal aestheticism, and a gesture of despair. It should be contextualized as one writer's act and not used to define postwar literature as a whole; most of his contemporaries followed very different paths.

Abe Kōbō (1924–1993), a writer influenced by existentialism, surrealism, and his training as a physician, wrote The Woman in the Dunes (1962), a novel of a man trapped in a sand pit with a woman who shovels sand to keep the village from being buried. The story, which became Teshigahara's film, is an allegory of modern work, community, and freedom. Ōe Kenzaburō (born 1935) emerged as a writer of the postwar generation, a young Akutagawa Prize winner in 1958, who concerned himself with Hiroshima, democracy, and the responsibilities of intellectuals. His A Personal Matter (1964) tells of a young father's reaction to the birth of a brain-damaged child, drawn from Ōe's own experience as the father of a disabled son, and addresses the temptation to flee responsibility. His non-fiction, including accounts of Hiroshima's survivors and, in 1970, Okinawa Notes, examined the war's legacy for Hibakusha and Okinawans, and positioned him as a critic of the nation's amnesia. Ōe received the Nobel Prize in 1994, long after the period of this chapter.

Endō Shūsaku (1923–1996), a Catholic writer, published Silence in 1966. The novel concerns a Portuguese missionary in seventeenth-century Japan confronting persecution, the ritual of trampling on Christian images (fumi-e), and the silence of God (see Chapter 47). It addressed the problem of faith within a society where Christianity was a small minority, and of moral responsibility under coercion, and its sensitive treatment of apostasy drew both praise and criticism from Christian readers. Endō's concern with weakness and compassion contrasted with the sharper secular alienation of Abe and the political commitment of Ōe. Kawabata's receipt of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1968, the first awarded to a Japanese author, was a landmark of international recognition, and the discussion that followed about whether his work represented an essentially Japanese beauty illustrates the way foreign awards shape domestic debate. Many other writers worked outside the prizewinning circle, in popular fiction, historical novels, and mystery writing, and in the early 1970s new voices, including women writers and writers from Okinawa and the Korean community in Japan, began to be recognized. Their histories belong to later chapters.

Section 95.15 · Division IV

Architecture, Urban Reconstruction, and the Metabolist Movement

Architecture was the most visible form of postwar reconstruction, and also the most politically freighted. Tange Kenzō (1913–2005) became the leading figure. In the late 1940s he won the competition to plan the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park and designed the Peace Memorial Museum and the Cenotaph, arranged along an axis that frames the skeletal Atomic Bomb Dome. The complex translated memory into urban design: it transformed a devastated zone into a place of commemoration and public gathering, and its modernist concrete forms presented peace as a future-oriented project and not only as grief. Critics have asked how a design in which the ruin is framed as an object of contemplation relates to the survivors' own memory, and the debate over whether to preserve the Dome, ultimately decided in favor of preservation, was part of the same cultural negotiation. Tange's other major works, including the Kagawa Prefectural Office (1958) and the Tokyo City Hall of 1957, married modernist structure with references to traditional Japanese timber building, a synthesis that influenced his successors.

Metabolism, a movement of young architects and critics, emerged in 1960. Its immediate occasion was the World Design Conference held in Tokyo in May 1960, where a group that included Kikutake Kiyonori, Kurokawa Kishō, the critic Kawazoe Noboru, the designer Awazu Kiyoshi, and others published a manifesto, Metabolism/1960: The Proposals for a New Urbanism. Maki Fumihiko, a few years younger, was associated with the group. The Metabolists proposed that buildings and cities should be conceived as organisms capable of growth, change, and replacement, with permanent infrastructure supporting replaceable capsules or modules. They used the biological metaphor of metabolism to criticize rigid modernist planning and to imagine a city adapted to rapid change, and their proposals drew on the experience of destruction and rapid rebuilding as well as on the optimism of the growth economy. Tange's own 1960 plan for Tokyo Bay, a vast linear extension of the city over the water, which was a related vision, was not built.

The historian must distinguish realized projects from visionary schemes. Kikutake's Marine City concept of floating urban structures and Kurokawa's helix and capsule designs were largely unbuilt. Realized works include Kikutake's own Sky House (1958), Tange's press and broadcasting centers of the 1960s, Kurokawa's Nakagin Capsule Tower in Tokyo (1972), and several Expo '70 pavilions, in which the Metabolist idea of detachable units was applied on a limited scale. The capsule tower's later history, in which its capsules were rarely replaced as the concept envisioned, illustrated the gap between theory and the economics of real estate. Chapter 91 treated transport and economic infrastructure; this section concentrates on architectural ideas and the cultural vision of the city. Metabolism cannot be understood without noticing its blind spots: the visions of expandable cities were produced mostly by men for an imagined universal user and rarely engaged the displaced populations, the environmental costs, or the continuing existence of older neighborhoods that actual urban growth involved. They were also, in intention, an export: the movement was promoted internationally and became one of the better-known Japanese contributions to modern architecture.

Section 95.16 · Division IV

Graphic Design, the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, and Expo '70

The 1964 Olympic Games, held in October, were conceived as a demonstration of Japan's recovery to the world and to its own citizens, and design was central. Kamekura Yūsaku, the leading graphic designer of his generation, created the Games' emblem, a red sun disc over the Olympic rings, and a series of posters that combined photography with restrained typography; he worked with a design council that coordinated the visual program across signage, tickets, and publications. A team including Yamashita Yoshirō and Katsumi Masaru developed pictograms for sports events and facilities, a system of wordless symbols intended to help foreign visitors that is generally regarded as a landmark in the history of public signage and was influential in later Games. Tange's Yoyogi National Gymnasium, with its suspended roof structure, became an architectural symbol of the event, and the capital's new expressways, monorail, and the Shinkansen, which opened days before the Games, formed the infrastructure of the showcase (Chapter 91).

The Olympics also carried symbolic meanings that were not purely celebratory. The torch relay began in Okinawa, then under American administration, before proceeding across the main islands, a gesture that implied unity while the islands' political status remained unresolved. The final torchbearer, Sakai Yoshinori, was born in Hiroshima on the day of the atomic bombing, a symbolic choice that fused recovery with memory. At the same time, the construction boom displaced residents, strained workers, and was accompanied by a clearing of the city's visible poverty; artists such as Hi-Red Center ironically staged a "cleaning" in the streets. Pollution, labor conditions, and urban inequality, examined in Chapters 91 and 94, were part of the same story, and the contrast between the polished image and the social costs is among the central tensions of the decade.

Expo '70, held in the Senri Hills outside Osaka from March to September 1970 under a theme of progress and harmony for humankind, extended the project. It drew more than sixty million visitors, a scale that made it one of the largest world's fairs to that date. Its Festival Plaza, with a vast roof designed by Tange, was surmounted by Okamoto Tarō's Tower of the Sun, a sculpture of deliberately primitive and monumental character that argued with the technological rationalism around it. Metabolist architects designed pavilions; corporations and nations displayed new technologies, from moving walkways to communication devices; and avant-garde artists and composers, among them some who had been associated with experimental art, took part. The Expo was therefore a coalition of corporate, state, and artistic interests, and it generated controversy among artists about whether participating in it meant complicity with state-corporate modernization. Its timing, in the year of the renewal of the security treaty and of Mishima's death, gave it a special charge. In the following years the idea of the Expo as a national project returned in Okinawa's Ocean Exposition of 1975, held after the reversion and widely regarded as a developmental showcase for the island prefecture. Assessing these events requires holding two things together: they were extraordinary cultural and design achievements, and they celebrated a modernization whose social and environmental contradictions were being exposed by the movements of Chapters 93 and 94.

Section 95.17 · Division V — Manga, Animation, Mass Culture, and the Global Legacy of Postwar Art

Tezuka Osamu and the Transformation of Postwar Manga

Illustrated storytelling in Japan had a long history, from picture scrolls to Edo-period woodblock books and prewar children's magazines and newspaper strips (Chapters 46 and 65). Postwar manga drew on those traditions but took shape through new publishing conditions. In the late 1940s paper shortages and cheap printing produced an inexpensive "red book" (akahon) market in Osaka, where small publishers sold story comics to children. Tezuka Osamu (1928–1989), a medical student in Osaka who had survived wartime air raids, published New Treasure Island (January 30, 1947) in this market, in collaboration with the established Osaka manga artist Sakai Shichima, from Sakai’s original story concept; its cinematic opening sequence and fast pacing impressed young readers, and it has become a conventional landmark. Tezuka went on to serialize in children's magazines in Tokyo: Jungle Emperor, Astro Boy (from 1952), and Princess Knight (1953), the last of these in a girls' magazine.

Tezuka's innovations included panel compositions that borrowed from film, with close-ups, shifting angles, and sequences that imitated camera movement; long serialized stories with continuing characters; a willingness to treat death, science, and ethics in children's stories; and a rounded, expressive drawing style influenced by American animation. Astro Boy, about a robot boy created to replace a dead son and living among humans who both love and fear robots, combined adventure with questions about discrimination, technology, and responsibility, which resonated in a society living with the legacy of the war and with atomic energy. Princess Knight, about a girl who passes as a boy to inherit a throne, gave gender play a place in popular stories. Tezuka was prolific and employed assistants, which made his studio an early model for manga production.

A different tradition of comic storytelling was that of Hasegawa Machiko, whose Sazae-san began in a Fukuoka newspaper in 1946 and, after moving to the Asahi Shimbun in 1949, ran for decades. Its protagonist, a cheerful young woman living in a multigenerational household, depicted everyday family life with gentle humor. Hasegawa was one of the first women to achieve major success as a manga creator, and she managed her own work and publishing interests; Sazae-san reached a mass audience of adults as well as children and later became a television animation. It illustrates that postwar manga was not only a children's adventure medium and was not the work of one artist.

The central argument of this section is that modern manga grew through changing publishing and reading practices, not solely through the achievements of one creator. Publishing houses such as Kōdansha, Shōgakukan, and Kōbunsha operated monthly children's magazines with serialized comics. Bookstores and newsstands distributed them, and in the 1950s rental bookshops (kashihon'ya) lent inexpensive volumes to children and young workers who could not afford to buy them, creating a market for stories that were longer, more varied, and more adult than those in the magazines. The launch in March 1959 of two weekly boys' magazines, Shōnen Magazine and Shōnen Sunday, converted manga into a weekly industry of serialized titles, deadlines, reader surveys, and rapidly growing circulation. The structure rewarded speed, collaboration, and responsiveness to readers, and it gave editors a central, often undervalued role. The result was a mass medium sustained by institutions as well as by creative genius.

Section 95.18 · Division V

Gekiga, Garo, and the Diversification of Manga Audiences

As the first generation of manga readers grew older, some creators sought forms that would not be limited to children's entertainment. Tatsumi Yoshihiro, working in the Osaka rental-book market, adopted the term gekiga ("dramatic pictures") in 1957 for stories with darker themes, realistic settings, and mature readers, and in 1959 he and several colleagues founded a Gekiga Workshop. Their stories, often about working-class people, crime, and urban loneliness, used plain drawing and a cinematic pace, and they appealed to young workers and students who read them at rental shops. The term reflected a challenge to the reigning idea that comics were childish, and the divide between the two styles, though real, should not be exaggerated, since Tezuka himself wrote serious stories and many creators crossed lines.

Historical Vocabulary
Manga, Gekiga, Shōjo Manga, and Anime

Manga means comics or cartoons; the word is old, but in the postwar period it came to designate serialized story comics as a mass medium. Gekiga, a term of 1957, referred to realistic, dramatic comics for older readers. Shōjo manga means comics for girls, defined by an intended audience and by magazines such as Ribon, Nakayoshi, and Margaret; in the early 1970s a generation of women creators transformed the genre. Anime is the common Japanese term for animation, and in this period it referred chiefly to feature films and television animation.

These are not interchangeable. Manga is a print medium produced by artists, assistants, and editors; anime is a moving-image medium requiring teams, cameras or studios, broadcasters, and sponsors. Many anime were adapted from manga, but the economics, labor, and audiences differed. Students should also resist assuming that the word "anime" carried in the 1960s the global meanings it acquired later.

The magazine Garo, founded in 1964 by Nagai Katsuichi, became the principal venue for alternative manga. Its centerpiece was Shirato Sanpei's Kamui Den, serialized from 1964, a long historical saga set in the Tokugawa period about a ninja, born to an outcast status, who flees his clan; the story dealt with class conflict, peasant uprisings, oppression, and the hierarchy of status, drawing on the Marxist historical scholarship current among university students of the day (see Chapters 43 and 44). The outcast origin of its protagonist links it to the history of Buraku discrimination, though the series was a fictional, adventurous work and not a documentary history. Garo's readers, many of them university students, found in it a companion to the New Left and the counterculture; its circulation, in the tens of thousands, was small in comparison with the mass weeklies. Its contributors later included Tsuge Yoshiharu, whose introspective stories were influential, and Mizuki Shigeru, who combined supernatural folk stories with war memories: Mizuki lost an arm as a soldier in the Pacific War and wrote of that experience in works of the 1970s. In the years that followed, manga was often understood as a medium in which the war's memory could be addressed through popular forms.

Girls' manga developed its own institutions and creators. In the 1950s and 1960s many girls' manga were drawn by men, but by the early 1970s women creators such as Hagio Moto, Takemiya Keiko, Ōshima Yumiko, and Ikeda Riyoko were reshaping the genre with psychologically complex stories, science fiction, historical romance, and explorations of sexuality and gender. Ikeda's The Rose of Versailles (1972) was a large commercial success; Hagio's science-fiction and horror stories broadened the genre's range. These creators, sometimes grouped under the retrospective label of the "Year 24 Group" after their approximate birth year in the Shōwa calendar, worked within commercial magazines and under editorial control, but they achieved extensive creative authority and established a new generation of women artists. Their emergence is a counterpart to the women's movement described in Chapter 94 and shows women's agency within cultural production.

The historian must separate artistic innovation from commercial expansion. The diversification of audiences, from children to adolescents to adults, was driven by demand, by magazine publishers eager to capture older readers, and by creators' ambitions. Weekly magazines multiplied; circulations in the hundreds of thousands, and in some cases over a million by the late 1960s, made manga a mainstay of mass culture. Magazine hierarchies, deadlines, and reader polls created pressures that limited experimentation in the mass market, while alternative publications offered freedom with less money. The coexistence of these systems is the mark of the period.

Section 95.19 · Division V

Television Animation, Popular Music, and the New Consumer Audience

Television broadcasting began in February 1953 with the public broadcaster NHK, followed within months by the first commercial station, and spread rapidly with the founding of additional networks in the following years. Its programming drew on film, radio, and theater; sports broadcasts, quiz programs, and drama attracted audiences, and color broadcasting was introduced in 1960 and became widespread by the late 1960s. Advertising revenue paid for programs, and sponsors sought children as consumers. Animation became attractive for that reason, even though it was expensive. Toei's animation division, founded in 1956, produced animated features beginning with Hakujaden (1958) in color, and in the 1960s young animators such as Takahata Isao and Miyazaki Hayao, who joined Toei in the early part of the decade, gained experience in films such as The Little Norse Prince (1968). The later fame of those artists and of Studio Ghibli belongs to another period and is mentioned only as foreshadowing.

Astro Boy premiered as a weekly television series in January 1963, produced by Tezuka's Mushi Production, which he founded in 1961. To meet a weekly schedule with a modest budget, the studio developed limited-animation techniques: fewer drawings per second, held poses, reused backgrounds and sequences, and strong reliance on sound and dialogue. The approach lowered cost and set a production pattern that other series followed. The series' sponsor, a confectionery company, used premiums and promotions, and the character appeared on toys and other products; this was a model of character licensing that linked the program to retail. Reports of the very low payment per episode and the pressure on animators suggest that the economic model rested on low wages and long hours, and Mushi Production later experienced financial difficulties. The series was exported to the United States in the same year, one of the first Japanese television animations to be broadcast there, an instance of circulation that students should place in the 1960s, not read as the beginning of a global anime market.

Music followed similar trajectories of media integration. Kayōkyoku, the popular song genre of record companies and radio, and enka, a sentimental style linked to rural nostalgia and often performed in kimono, dominated the postwar mass market, with singers such as Misora Hibari achieving enormous national popularity. Kyū Sakamoto's song known abroad as "Sukiyaki" reached number one on the American charts in 1963, a rare Japanese-language success. Folk music, inspired by American models and by political songs of the protest movements, flourished in the later 1960s; Group Sounds bands such as the Tigers and the Spiders adopted the instrumentation of British and American rock, and the visit of the Beatles to Tokyo in 1966, accompanied by controversy over their performance at the Budōkan, a venue associated with martial arts, indicated the cultural stakes. NHK's year-end song contest and weekly music programs on television connected singers to national audiences. The talent agencies, record companies, and broadcasters that managed stars were part of an interrelated industry.

The section's main point is that cultural production increasingly depended on interrelated media industries. A manga could become a television series, which sold toys, advertised chocolate, and generated a theme song; a singer could appear in films, on television, and in magazines; a film could be adapted from a novel in a weekly magazine. The older distinction between separate artistic markets, such as theater, cinema, publishing, and music, weakened. This integration explains why the experimental movements of Division III often defined themselves against it and why the economic changes of Chapter 96 would reshape these industries again.

Cause and Effect

How Growth, Television, International Recognition, and Changing Audiences Transformed Cultural Production

Long-term causes

Prewar mass media and publishing; the trauma of war and defeat; occupation censorship and its end; postwar urbanization; rising literacy and schooling; a tradition of studio production and serialized storytelling.

Short-term causes

High-growth incomes and leisure; the start of television in 1953; the launch of weekly magazines in 1959; the Anpo Crisis and student movements; the Olympics; international festivals; the decline of cinema audiences after 1958.

Event or transformation

Cultural production shifted from a studio-dominated cinema and elite literary culture toward a plural field of television, weekly magazines, independent film, experimental art, and global exchange.

Immediate effects

The studio system declined; independent producers and alternative magazines gained room; manga and television animation expanded; avant-garde artists found both patrons and new audiences; Japanese works circulated abroad.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The media industries of the 1960s established models of licensing, serialization, and cross-media adaptation on which later popular culture built. International recognition of directors, authors, and architects shaped how Japanese culture was perceived abroad. But no single cause explains the change: television did not simply destroy cinema, and international recognition did not drive domestic culture. Economic growth, political conflict, technology, and artists' own decisions interacted, and different groups experienced the change differently.
Section 95.20 · Division V

The Historical Meaning of Postwar Japanese Culture: Memory, Modernity, and Global Influence

This section draws the chapter's strands together. The first lesson of the preceding pages is that the cultural field was divided into very different circuits. At one extreme were the internationally recognized works: Kurosawa's and Mizoguchi's films at European festivals, Kawabata's Nobel Prize, Tange's buildings. At another were the mass media: the double-bill cinema, weekly magazines, television, and popular song, with audiences counted in millions. At a third were the experimental performers, painters, photographers, and independent filmmakers whose audiences were small, young, and urban. The three circuits intersected, since a director might make festival films and genre films, and an avant-garde artist might design a poster or appear on television, but they should not be collapsed. International recognition and domestic popularity are different measures; what foreign audiences admired was often a small fraction of what Japanese audiences watched.

The second lesson is that these works responded to a particular historical situation. War memory appears in Godzilla, Tokyo Story, Fires on the Plain, Black Rain, and the photographs of Nagasaki, though usually from the standpoint of Japanese victimhood, and with less attention to the victims of Japanese empire except in a few works, such as Ōshima's film on a Korean resident or the writings of Ōe on Okinawa. Economic growth appears in office films and family dramas, in Metabolist cities, and in Olympic and Expo spectacle, and also in works that criticize it, from High and Low to the Sanrizuka documentaries. Political conflict supplied the subject of the New Wave and the settings of angura theater. Women's lives were represented by male directors and by women who also wrote, acted, directed, drew, and performed. The history of minority communities, including Zainichi, Okinawan, Buraku, and Ainu people, appears unevenly: sometimes as subjects, occasionally as makers, and often as absences. Ainu people, in particular, were largely represented in postwar popular culture through tourism and ethnographic display, and their own cultural activism belongs to the movements of Chapter 94.

The third lesson concerns the institutions that organized production. Film studios, publishers, broadcasters, advertisers, ATG, patrons such as Yoshihara Jirō, galleries, and state events such as the Olympics and Expo made the field. They both enabled and limited creative work: Shōchiku withdrew Night and Fog in Japan; Nikkatsu dismissed Suzuki Seijun; Mushi Production depended on a sponsor; Expo '70 divided artists. Historians who attend only to individual genius or to heroic rebellion will miss this infrastructure. They should also avoid anachronism: the global media environment of the twenty-first century, and the brands associated with it, should not be projected onto the 1950s. In the period covered here, Japanese films and books circulated through festivals and small distributors; manga was almost entirely a domestic phenomenon; Astro Boy's export was exceptional.

The conclusion follows from these points. Postwar Japanese culture was neither an uncomplicated revival of tradition nor an imitation of Western modernity. Tradition was continually reinvented, as in the jidaigeki, Noh-inflected cinema, and Butoh's engagement with the Tōhoku body; foreign influence was continually transformed, as in the New Wave, Gutai, and limited animation. The field was one of artistic innovation, social negotiation, and competing interpretations of Japan's place in the world. Whether one stresses continuity or novelty depends partly on which circuit one studies, as the following debate shows.

Multiple Perspectives

The Decline of the Studio System and the Rise of New Media

Continuity and Change Over Time

Prewar Traditions and Postwar Artistic Innovation

What Changed

  • Censorship shifted from wartime state control to occupation review and then to commercial and self-regulation.
  • Television and weekly magazines created new mass media; cinema declined as the dominant form.
  • Experimental movements rejected both classical and modernist orthodoxies.
  • Women and young creators entered new roles, in directing, art, and manga.

What Remained Continuous

  • Many of the studio directors, writers, and actors had begun work before 1945.
  • Narrative and theatrical traditions from Kabuki, Noh, and picture-storytelling persisted in new forms.
  • Commercial publishing and studio structures survived defeat.
  • Metropolitan Tokyo remained the center of cultural production, with Osaka and Kyoto as important secondary centers.

Who experienced change most directly? Young urban audiences and creators, and those whose subjects had been suppressed under censorship. Who experienced greater continuity? Established directors, cultural institutions, and many rural viewers, whose cultural life changed more slowly. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the postwar break was real but partial, and that the cultural history of the period cannot be told as a story of either rupture or tradition alone.

Historical Debate

Was Postwar Japanese Culture Defined by Renewed Tradition, International Exchange, or Radical Innovation?

The question: Scholars and critics have offered competing accounts of what defined Japanese culture between 1945 and the mid-1970s. Which description best explains the evidence?

Interpretation A — Renewed tradition: Some critics and historians emphasize the continuity of forms such as the period film, Noh and Kabuki aesthetics, craft-based design, and narrative conventions, and see postwar works as new expressions of an enduring cultural heritage. They point to Ozu's domestic aesthetic, Kurosawa's debts to theater, and Butoh's roots in older bodily practice.
Interpretation B — International exchange: Others stress the circulation of ideas, techniques, and models: American film genres and animation, European literature and art movements, international festival circuits, and the movement of artists such as Kusama and Ono. They argue that postwar Japanese culture is best understood as part of a transnational field.
Interpretation C — Radical innovation: A third approach emphasizes ruptures: the New Wave, Gutai, underground theater, Mono-ha, and new manga forms as responses to defeat, protest, and a changing consumer society that broke with both tradition and imported models.

Evidence: Each interpretation relies on works, artists' statements, critical reception, institutional histories, and patterns of circulation. The three are not mutually exclusive, and most scholars now treat them as overlapping. The weight given to each depends on the circuit under study: festival films, mass media, or the avant-garde.

Why it matters: The choice shapes how historians present Japan's cultural identity, whether as an unbroken tradition, a borrower, or an innovator, and has implications for how later debates about "Japanese culture" and its global influence are understood. It also bears on whose work is counted as central.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan's experience of defeat, occupation, reconstruction, rapid growth, political conflict, and renewed international engagement shaped cinema, literature, visual art, performance, architecture, design, manga, and popular entertainment between 1945 and the mid-1970s. Its central argument is that postwar culture was a field of innovation and negotiation rather than a revival of tradition or an imitation of the West, and that it must be understood through its institutions and audiences as well as its celebrated creators.

The film industry provided the framework of the first decades. Occupation censorship suppressed feudal and militaristic themes and delayed treatment of the atomic bombings; six studios, vertically integrated and linked by block booking, produced several hundred features a year for an audience that peaked in 1958 at roughly 1.13 billion admissions. Within and beside this system, Kurosawa's moral dramas and period films, Ozu's restrained portraits of family change, and the work of Mizoguchi, Naruse, and Tanaka Kinuyo on women's lives achieved artistic distinction. Rashomon's Venice prize in 1951 opened international attention, but most of these films were made for Japanese viewers.

Godzilla (1954), made in the wake of the Lucky Dragon incident, was commercial entertainment, a technical achievement in special effects, and an expression of nuclear anxiety; its 1956 American re-edit changed its meaning. The industry's popular genres, from chanbara and yakuza films to youth and monster films, shared the screen with art cinema. Television, which began in 1953, and broader changes in leisure contributed to the decline of attendance in the 1960s, weakening the studios and opening room for the New Wave, the ATG, and independent and documentary filmmakers such as Ōshima, Imamura, Teshigahara, Matsumoto, Ogawa, and Tsuchimoto.

Experimental performance and art developed in separate but overlapping movements. Butoh began with Hijikata's Kinjiki in 1959 and his work with Ōno Kazuo; angura theater arose in the later 1960s under Terayama, Kara, and Suzuki; Gutai, founded in 1954, transformed materials through action, while Mono-ha, emerging in the late 1960s, arranged materials to reveal relationships. Photography moved between documentation and experiment in Tōmatsu's work on Nagasaki and in Provoke, and artists such as Kusama and Ono were part of international networks. These movements differed in chronology, institutions, and aims, and should not be treated as one avant-garde.

Literature confronted war and defeat through Dazai, Kawabata, Ibuse, and others, and through the work of women writers such as Enchi Fumiko. Mishima, Abe, Ōe, and Endō explored identity, responsibility, and faith in the 1950s and 1960s, and Kawabata's Nobel Prize in 1968 marked international recognition. The literary marketplace of publishers, magazines, and prizes shaped what reached readers, and the unevenness of its inclusion of rural, working-class, and minority voices is part of the history.

Architecture and design offered visions of the modern city. Tange's Hiroshima complex linked reconstruction and memory; the Metabolists proposed cities that could grow and change, though most proposals were unbuilt; Kamekura's design program, the pictograms, and Yoyogi Gymnasium presented Japan at the 1964 Olympics; and Expo '70 expressed the ambitions of the state, corporations, and artists. These events also exposed tensions between celebration and the social and environmental costs of growth, and between national unity and unresolved questions such as Okinawa's status.

Manga and animation grew from changing publishing and reading practices. Tezuka Osamu's cinematic storytelling and Hasegawa Machiko's family humor reached mass audiences through magazines, bookshops, and rental shops; the weekly magazines of 1959 industrialized the medium. Gekiga and Garo extended it toward mature and political themes, and women creators transformed shōjo manga in the early 1970s. Television animation, beginning with Astro Boy in 1963, relied on limited animation, sponsors, and merchandising, and illustrates the integration of media industries. Manga and anime are distinct media with different labor structures and audiences.

Women's agency, Zainichi, Okinawan, and Buraku subjects, and the memory of empire recurred throughout. Women appeared as directors, performers, writers, artists, and manga creators; Ōshima's film on a Korean resident, Lee Ufan's role in Mono-ha, Imamura's southern-island film, Tōmatsu's later attention to Okinawa, and Shirato's outcast hero are examples of how marginal communities entered cultural production, though often unevenly and as subjects rather than makers. Representations of war focused more on Japanese suffering than on the victims of imperial violence, a pattern that later chapters will show was contested.

Historians interpret the period differently. Some emphasize renewed tradition, others international exchange, others radical innovation; the best account holds them together while attending to the different circuits of production and reception. The chapter has also insisted on separating international recognition from domestic popularity and on avoiding anachronistic projections of the later global media market.

The chapter thus completes the picture of high-growth Japan sketched in Chapters 91–94 by showing how Japanese people represented, interpreted, and contested their changing world. It sets up Chapter 96, which turns from the culture of the high-growth era to the oil shock, industrial restructuring, and the road to the bubble economy.

Comparing an Art Film, an Avant-Garde Work, and a Mass-Media Production

This project asks you to compare three works from the period, an acclaimed art film, an avant-garde work, and a commercial mass-media production, as evidence of how culture was made, financed, distributed, and received.

  1. Choose three works. For example, Tokyo Story or Seven Samurai; a Gutai work, a Butoh performance, or an underground play; and an episode of Astro Boy, a studio genre film, or a weekly manga serial.
  2. Reconstruct production. Identify the creators, the institutions that financed and distributed each work, and the year and format in which it first appeared.
  3. Analyze form. Describe the visual and structural features of each work and explain what they show about its aims and audience.
  4. Assess reception. Use reviews, circulation or attendance figures, and later critical assessments to compare each work's domestic and international reception, noting the difference between them.
  5. Write your findings. Prepare a 5–7 page essay or equivalent multimedia presentation that uses careful language ("the evidence suggests," "this remains debated") and avoids presenting estimates as exact figures.

Library resources to use: The National Film Archive of Japan, museum research on Gutai and Metabolism, the Tezuka Osamu official archive, university archives of Butoh and theater, scholarly studies of Japanese cinema, art, literature, and manga, and the MGU History Library's reference collection.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how people made sense of an extraordinary transformation. Within three decades, Japanese society passed from defeat and ruin to affluence, and its cultural producers addressed that passage in forms ranging from the intimate family drama to the monster film, from the burning of a body in a darkened hall to a comic strip serialized weekly for millions. By treating these as products of institutions and audiences as well as of individual talent, the chapter has tried to show that cultural history is part of social history, not an ornament to it.

The chapter also reveals the uneven distribution of voice. The directors, artists, and writers who became famous were largely male, metropolitan, and Japanese-speaking members of the majority; women, Zainichi Koreans, Okinawans, Buraku communities, and Ainu people appear as subjects and, increasingly, as makers, but the record favors the former. Attention to Tanaka Kinuyo, Hasegawa Machiko, the Gutai women, Lee Ufan, and the shōjo manga creators is not merely corrective; it changes the account of where innovation came from.

The developments studied here shaped later history. The licensing and serialization models of manga and television animation became foundations of later popular culture; the memory of the bomb and the war, in film and literature, continued to inform debates over responsibility and peace; the experimental movements influenced later artists; and the international reputation of Japanese cinema and architecture established expectations for what Japanese culture could be. Students should be careful, however, not to read the global media landscape of the twenty-first century back into this period, and the later chapters of this book will show how those expectations were transformed.

Finally, the chapter connects the archipelago to the wider world in both directions: Japanese works traveled to Venice, New York, and television stations abroad, while American film, European art, and international design movements entered Japan. Okinawa's position, still under American administration for most of this period, is a reminder that the map of culture and the map of politics did not always coincide. Students who understand postwar culture as contested, plural, and institutionally constructed are prepared to approach the economic restructuring and the bubble of Chapter 96 without assuming that the culture of the high-growth years was either a seamless success or a mere reflection of the economy.
