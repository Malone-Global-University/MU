Fukushima and the Politics of Twenty-First-Century Japan

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

At 2:46 in the afternoon of March 11, 2011, northeastern Honshū was struck by Japan's largest instrumentally recorded earthquake, one of the largest recorded worldwide. Within minutes the shaking had stopped trains, cut electricity, and sent people into streets and schoolyards. Within the hour, a wall of water was crossing fields, harbors, and town centers along hundreds of kilometers of coast, and at a power station on the Pacific shore in Fukushima Prefecture, the machinery that kept nuclear fuel cool was beginning to fail.

The events of that afternoon, usually called the Great East Japan Earthquake or simply "3/11," were not one disaster but three connected ones: a very large earthquake, a tsunami that caused most of the deaths, and a nuclear accident that displaced more than a hundred thousand people. Historians and social scientists therefore speak of a compound or "triple" disaster. Treating the three as separate is necessary, because they killed and harmed people in different ways, followed different timetables, and raised different questions about responsibility. Treating them as connected is equally necessary, because they struck the same region, overlapped in the same emergency, and together tested the same set of institutions: ministries, utilities, municipal governments, the armed forces, schools, hospitals, and the households of the northeast.

This chapter has two historical tasks, and its governing argument depends on keeping them distinct. The first is to reconstruct the disaster itself: what happened on and after March 11, why the consequences were so severe in some places and less so in others, how communities and authorities responded, and why recovery proceeded unevenly. The second is to examine the political and social history of the 2010s, a decade in which Japan returned to Liberal Democratic Party government under Abe Shinzō, reinterpreted the constitutional limits on military action, fought a long dispute with Okinawa over base construction, revised its labor and immigration laws, and saw new kinds of civic protest. Not every one of those developments was caused by Fukushima. The Article 9 debate, the Henoko dispute, and the immigration reforms all had roots that long predate 2011 and would have unfolded in some form without the disaster. They share a chapter because each illuminates a changing relationship among citizens, state institutions, security, economic interests, and public accountability.

The chapter also continues themes introduced in earlier chapters. The Kobe earthquake of 1995, examined in Chapter 98, had already led many people to ask what the state owed disaster victims and what volunteers and nonprofit organizations could do. The demographic decline and regional aging described in Chapter 97 shaped which coastal towns could rebuild and which struggled to retain residents. The protest traditions of Chapters 93 and 94, from the Anpo demonstrations to the residents' movements at Minamata, formed a background against which post-3/11 activism can be compared. And the long history of Okinawa, traced in Chapters 32 and 33 and again in the occupation and reversion chapters, is essential to understanding why a base-relocation project in the north of the main island became a test of local autonomy.

Students should notice at the outset that the people affected by these events were not a single "public." Fishing households on the Sanriku coast, nuclear-plant subcontractors, mothers in Fukushima City worried about their children, officials in Tokyo, Okinawan farmers and municipal mayors, factory managers short of workers, and technical interns from Vietnam and China experienced the decade very differently. They disagreed with one another, and often with the authorities who claimed to speak for them. The evidence also differs from earlier chapters in kind: seismograph and tide-gauge records, ministry statistics, parliamentary investigation reports, court judgments, referendum returns, newspaper and television coverage, social-media postings, and the testimony of survivors, workers, and officials. Because the events are recent, some questions remain unsettled, and later official figures and legal judgments continue to change what can be said. Wherever this chapter gives a number, treat it as an approximation tied to a stated source and date.

The chapter ends in 2020, with the Reiwa era in its second year and a new pandemic beginning to test public institutions once again. It does not attempt a final assessment of Abe Shinzō, of Japanese national identity, or of the country's future; those belong to Chapter 100. Its conclusion is more limited and, for that reason, more defensible. During the 2010s the Japanese state worked to strengthen its administrative and security capacities even as citizens, local governments, scientists, and courts pressed for greater accountability, transparency, and participation. Neither development canceled the other.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the tectonic setting of the March 11, 2011 earthquake and distinguish earthquake magnitude, seismic intensity, tsunami height, inundation depth, and run-up.
  2. Analyze why the tsunami's human toll varied across the northeastern coast, with attention to local geography, planning decisions, warning systems, and unequal vulnerability.
  3. Evaluate the roles of the central government, the Self-Defense Forces, local authorities, volunteers, and foreign assistance, including Operation Tomodachi, in the emergency response.
  4. Trace the sequence of the Fukushima Daiichi accident from March 11 to 15, distinguishing the conditions of Units 1, 2, 3, and 4.
  5. Distinguish deaths caused directly by the earthquake and tsunami, disaster-related deaths, and documented radiation-health outcomes, and explain why the distinction matters.
  6. Compare the findings of the principal Fukushima investigations and assess the creation of the Nuclear Regulation Authority as institutional reform.
  7. Assess how Japan's electricity system, energy policy, and anti-nuclear activism changed between 2011 and 2020, and which trade-offs remained unresolved.
  8. Explain the debates over collective self-defense, the 2015 security legislation, the Specially Designated Secrets Act, and Okinawan opposition to the Henoko project, distinguishing legal, political, and symbolic outcomes.
  9. Describe labor reform and immigration policy in the 2010s, distinguishing foreign residents, foreign workers, technical interns, and Specified Skilled Workers.
  10. Evaluate primary sources, including investigation reports, statutes, Cabinet decisions, and referendum records, for provenance, institutional interest, and evidentiary limits.
  11. Avoid attributing every political development of the 2010s to a single cause, and avoid reading post-2020 outcomes back into the expectations of 2011–2020.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why were the consequences of the tsunami so different from one coastal community to another, and what does this show about the relationship between natural hazard and social preparation?
  2. In what sense was the Fukushima Daiichi accident a failure of technology, of regulation, of management, or of political accountability, and how do the investigations answer differently?
  3. Why must historians separate deaths caused by the tsunami, deaths related to evacuation and disrupted care, and the documented health effects of radiation?
  4. How did Fukushima change Japan's energy policy, and what trade-offs among safety, cost, imports, and climate goals did it leave unresolved?
  5. What made the post-3/11 protest cycle similar to, and different from, earlier postwar movements such as the Anpo protests?
  6. Was the 2014–2015 reinterpretation of Article 9 a necessary adaptation of security policy or a problematic change of constitutional practice without formal amendment, and what evidence bears on each view?
  7. How should historians weigh a non-binding referendum, a prefectural election, a court judgment, and a Cabinet decision when each addresses the same Okinawan project?
  8. How did labor reform and immigration policy reflect Japan's growing dependence on workers who had historically had limited protection or security, and what remained unchanged?

Historical Context

By 2011 Japan was a wealthy, aging democracy two decades past the collapse of its asset bubble. Chapter 97 described the long period of weak growth, the spread of nonregular employment, and the shrinking and aging of the population. Politically, the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP), dominant since 1955 with brief interruptions, had lost the House of Representatives election of August 2009 to the Democratic Party of Japan (DPJ), which promised to reduce bureaucratic control over policy and reorder spending priorities. The DPJ's three prime ministers, Hatoyama Yukio, Kan Naoto, and Noda Yoshihiko, governed in a divided legislature in which the opposition controlled the House of Councillors after 2010, and the party's early record included a failed attempt to move the Futenma air station out of Okinawa. The disaster arrived while Kan's government was already weakened by a political-donations scandal that had forced his foreign minister to resign days earlier.

The northeast, known as Tōhoku, had long been an economically peripheral region supplying food, labor, and, increasingly, electricity to the Tokyo metropolitan area. Its Pacific coast combined fishing and aquaculture ports, rice and vegetable farming, small manufacturing, and a rapidly aging population, as young people moved to cities. The Sanriku coast of Iwate and northern Miyagi had been struck by catastrophic tsunamis in 1896 and 1933 and by a Chilean-earthquake tsunami in 1960, and coastal communities had built seawalls, held drills, and preserved memorial stones. Further south, the Sendai Plain and the Fukushima coast had experienced fewer recent tsunamis in living memory, though geologists had identified evidence of a major tsunami in 869, the Jōgan event, whose implications for planning were still under discussion. The Fukushima coast also hosted two nuclear stations operated by the Tokyo Electric Power Company (TEPCO), which supplied electricity to Tokyo rather than to the northeast.

Japan's institutions for disaster management had been reshaped by the Great Hanshin earthquake of 1995 (Chapter 98), which prompted reforms in emergency coordination, building standards, and the legal position of volunteers. The Self-Defense Forces had become routine participants in disaster relief. The nuclear industry, meanwhile, had grown from the energy-security policies of the 1970s, which responded to the oil shocks by promoting nuclear generation, and it supplied roughly a quarter to nearly a third of the country's electricity before 2011. Nuclear accidents at Tōkaimura in 1999, falsified-inspection scandals at TEPCO in 2002, and damage to the Kashiwazaki-Kariwa station in the 2007 Niigata earthquake had raised doubts but had not changed the basic policy.

Finally, the security environment and alliance politics of the early 2010s shaped debates that appear in the second half of this chapter. North Korea's missile and nuclear programs, China's military modernization and the dispute over the Senkaku Islands, and the United States' strategic "pivot" to Asia all figured in Japanese defense discussions. The postwar settlement discussed in Chapters 84 and 90 left the Self-Defense Forces constitutionally ambiguous, and the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty concentrated American forces on Japanese territory, disproportionately in Okinawa. Readers should treat these as long-standing conditions rather than as consequences of the disaster.

Section 99.1 · Division I — The Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami

March 11, 2011: The Great East Japan Earthquake

At 2:46 p.m. Japan Standard Time on Friday, March 11, 2011, a magnitude-9.0 earthquake struck beneath the Pacific Ocean off the Sanriku coast of northeastern Honshū. It was the largest earthquake recorded in Japan and among the largest recorded anywhere. The Reconstruction Agency, the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA), and other official bodies give the same basic parameters of time, magnitude, and location, though the first magnitude announced within minutes was lower and was revised upward as analysis continued over the following days. This revision is itself a reminder that early records of a disaster are provisional.

The earthquake belongs to a tectonic setting that has shaped the archipelago since the geological processes described in Chapter 1. Along the Japan Trench, the Pacific Plate descends beneath the plate on which northeastern Honshū sits, a process called subduction. Strain accumulates along the contact between the plates over decades or centuries and is released when a segment ruptures. In 2011 the rupture extended over a zone several hundred kilometers long and roughly two hundred kilometers wide, and displaced the seafloor by many meters. Seismologists had long recognized the trench as a source of large earthquakes, and Japanese scientists had mapped several earlier events, but few had expected a single rupture of this extent. A widely discussed consequence was that official hazard assessments, including those used for coastal planning and nuclear-plant design, had assumed smaller maximum events in some segments of the trench.

Students should distinguish two kinds of measurement. Magnitude expresses the energy released at the source and is one number for one earthquake. Seismic intensity, measured in Japan on the JMA scale (shindo), describes the strength of shaking at a particular place and varies across the landscape. The JMA registered the maximum intensity of 7, the top of the scale, at Kurihara in Miyagi Prefecture, and strong shaking was recorded over an enormous area from Hokkaidō to the Kantō region. Tokyo, some 370 kilometers away, experienced intensity 5 upper, enough to stop the capital's rail network and strand millions of commuters overnight.

The shaking itself, for all its scale, caused comparatively limited structural collapse in the northeast. Japan's building codes, tightened after the 1978 Miyagi-ken-oki earthquake and again after Kobe in 1995, meant that most modern buildings withstood the shaking. Damage was nonetheless serious: landfill areas liquefied in Chiba prefecture and parts of Tokyo Bay, an earthfill dam failed at Fujinuma in Fukushima Prefecture, a ceiling collapsed at a Tokyo hall killing two people, and roads, railways, and factories were damaged. Electricity, telephone, and mobile services failed across the region, and a large share of the northeast lost power. Industrial supply chains, especially for automotive and electronic components concentrated in the region, were disrupted for weeks and affected manufacturers around the world.

For the people of the Pacific coast, the shaking lasted several minutes and was a warning. In towns with a living tradition of tsunami preparation, residents knew that a long, strong earthquake meant moving to high ground. The JMA issued tsunami warnings within minutes. The next section explains why those warnings and local preparations produced very different outcomes. The point to hold in mind here is that magnitude 9.0 describes the earthquake, not the disaster. The scale of human loss resulted from the interaction between a rare physical event and decisions made over decades about where people lived, how coasts were defended, how warnings were transmitted, and how communities prepared.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Earthquake and Tsunami Terms

Subduction zone is a boundary at which one tectonic plate descends beneath another; the Japan Trench is such a boundary. Magnitude measures an earthquake's source energy, while seismic intensity (shindo) measures shaking at a location. Tsunami height, inundation depth, and run-up are different measurements: height is the wave's elevation above normal sea level at a measurement point; inundation depth is the water's depth above ground where it floods land; run-up is the highest ground elevation the water reaches. Reports that mix these terms are not comparable.

Disaster-related death (saigai kanrenshi) is an administrative category in Japanese law. It refers to deaths not directly caused by the physical event but attributed by municipal committees to the worsening of health caused by evacuation, disrupted care, or stress in the aftermath, and certified so that families can receive condolence payments. It is not a medical diagnosis. Resilience, often used in policy language, describes the ability of communities and systems to withstand and recover from shocks; students should ask who defines recovery and for whom.

Section 99.2 · Division I

The Tsunami: Coastal Destruction, Local Geography, and Unequal Vulnerability

The tsunami caused the overwhelming majority of the immediate deaths. The first waves reached parts of the Iwate and Miyagi coast within roughly half an hour of the earthquake, and the waves continued for hours. Measurements differed enormously by location. Tide gauges were destroyed or overtopped in many places, so scientists later reconstructed the event from surveys of debris lines and water marks left on buildings and hillsides. Maximum run-up heights exceeding thirty meters were documented in parts of Iwate, with the highest survey values at about forty meters on some steep coastal slopes. Inundation reached several kilometers inland across the low Sendai Plain and covered an area of roughly five hundred and sixty square kilometers in total.

Geography explains much of this variation. The Sanriku coast, with its narrow V-shaped bays and steep valleys, funnels and amplifies water, so that a wave of moderate height in open water becomes much higher as it enters a confined inlet. These coasts also leave little flat land, so communities were built close to the shore at the heads of bays. By contrast, the broad plains of Miyagi and northern Fukushima allowed water to travel far inland with comparatively modest depth, sweeping across farmland and into suburbs. Both kinds of coast had seawalls and breakwaters, some of the largest in the world, but the waves overtopped or destroyed many of them. The planning assumption behind these defenses was that they would protect against tsunamis of the scale recorded in the previous century, and that evacuation would handle larger events. Over time, the sense of safety created by a high wall may have influenced some residents' choices, though research on this point is mixed.

Warnings and evacuation were central. The JMA issued a major tsunami warning within about three minutes, but its first forecast heights were far lower than what occurred, and the figures were revised upward within roughly half an hour. Subsequent studies suggested that some residents heard the earlier figures and delayed. Communication failures compounded the problem: power outages and damaged equipment silenced municipal loudspeaker systems in some towns, and mobile networks were overwhelmed. At Minamisanriku, a municipal disaster-prevention worker continued broadcasting evacuation calls from the town's disaster-management building until the water overwhelmed it, and she was among those who died. Throughout the coast, ordinary people made rapid decisions with incomplete information, often on the basis of local knowledge, family ties, and whether neighbors were moving.

Outcomes illustrate how planning and local culture shaped survival. In Kamaishi, schoolchildren who had been trained in a disaster-prevention program for years ran to higher ground, in some cases leading younger children, and the great majority survived; the episode became known in Japan as the "miracle of Kamaishi," a label that some educators dislike because it implies luck rather than preparation. At Ōkawa Elementary School in Ishinomaki, by contrast, seventy-four of the school's one hundred and eight pupils and ten staff members died after delaying evacuation for around fifty minutes and then moving toward a bridge approach near the river, rather than to the hill directly behind the school. Bereaved parents sued the city and prefecture. On October 26, 2016, the Sendai District Court found negligence in the response and evacuation during the disaster; on April 26, 2018, the Sendai High Court also emphasized inadequate advance preparations and disaster planning. On October 10, 2019, the Supreme Court declined to overturn the High Court ruling. These were distinct stages of the litigation, not one High Court decision in 2016. The case, widely discussed in Japan, shows how disaster preparedness, local assumptions about the school as a safe shelter, and institutional responsibility could become matters of litigation.

The town of Rikuzentakata in Iwate offers another case. Much of its low-lying center, including the city hall and its famous pine grove at Takata-matsubara, was destroyed, and roughly one in thirteen residents, or about seven percent, died or went missing. The grove's solitary surviving pine became a national symbol, preserved at great cost. For historians, the example also shows how reconstruction decisions, including the town's later plan to raise ground levels with soil moved from nearby hills, turned geography into policy. Victims were not evenly distributed. The elderly formed the majority of the dead, since many lived alone or in the lower town and could not climb quickly to high ground. People with disabilities faced severe difficulty: surveys by municipal authorities and disability organizations found mortality rates among registered disabled residents substantially higher than among the general population. Fishers and fish-processing workers, many of them women, were exposed because of their workplaces near the shore, and foreign residents, among them trainees and spouses, sometimes lacked access to warnings in languages they understood.

The tsunami also demonstrated the limits of any single statistic. As of the most recent official police tallies, the disaster left approximately 15,900 people dead and about 2,500 missing, concentrated in Miyagi, Iwate, and Fukushima. Later official counts also registered more than 3,700 disaster-related deaths. Students should keep these categories separate: the first reflects the direct physical event, the second people whose bodies were never found, and the third deaths that resulted from the conditions that followed. The nuclear accident, discussed from Section 99.5, accounted for few if any direct deaths but produced a different, longer-lasting pattern of displacement.

Section 99.3 · Division I

Emergency Response: Government, Self-Defense Forces, Volunteers, and Operation Tomodachi

At 3:14 p.m. on March 11, 2011—28 minutes after the earthquake—the government established the Emergency Disaster Response Headquarters under Prime Minister Kan Naoto pursuant to the Disaster Countermeasures Basic Act; its first meeting followed at 3:37 p.m. This was distinct from the declaration of a nuclear emergency at 7:03 p.m. under the Act on Special Measures Concerning Nuclear Emergency Preparedness. In practice, the early response was a layered improvisation. Municipal governments, many of which had themselves lost buildings, records, and staff, carried out the first rescues and managed shelters. Police and fire departments, including fire-service volunteers (shōbōdan) who were killed in large numbers while closing floodgates and guiding evacuations, did the earliest search work. Prefectural governments requested assistance from the national government, and the Self-Defense Forces, called up by the Defense Ministry, ultimately deployed the largest number of personnel in their history, on the order of a hundred thousand at the peak.

The SDF's role was both logistical and humanitarian. Its personnel searched for survivors, cleared roads and airfields, ferried supplies by helicopter and ship, and operated field baths and kitchens. Their response involved reserve components for the first time in a major disaster. The scale of this deployment reinforced a public perception, already growing since Kobe, that the armed forces were useful and benign, and it gave advocates of a more active defense posture an argument about readiness. It is important not to overstate this link. The public's gratitude toward the SDF for rescue work did not by itself demonstrate support for broader constitutional change, and later opinion surveys on collective self-defense, discussed in Section 99.14, showed substantial division.

The first weeks were dominated by scarcity. Hundreds of thousands of people moved into schools, gymnasiums, and community halls, where food, water, blankets, medicine, and sanitation were short, and where cold weather in March made conditions harsh. Fuel shortages hampered distribution because roads and refineries had been damaged. Hospitals lost power and lacked supplies, and patients with chronic conditions were at risk. Government agencies and local authorities struggled with the sheer number of missing records: some municipalities had lost the household registers and resident databases needed to identify residents and administer aid. Volunteers, often coordinated through local social-welfare councils, nonprofit organizations, and religious groups, arrived in large numbers, with more than a million estimated to have worked in the affected areas during the first year. Their contribution to clearing mud, cleaning photographs, and supporting shelter life became a subject of study in itself, and the experience contributed to a broader sense that civil society had matured since 1995.

International assistance was substantial. More than a hundred countries and regions and many international organizations offered help, and rescue and medical teams from several countries worked in the northeast. The most prominent was the United States' Operation Tomodachi, in which U.S. forces coordinated closely with the SDF. Japan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs reports that approximately 24,500 U.S. personnel, 24 ships, and 189 aircraft participated at the operation's peak. U.S. aircraft cleared and reopened Sendai Airport's operations, ships delivered supplies and supported helicopter relief, and American forces assisted with radiation-monitoring flights near Fukushima. The operation had political effects. In Japan it was widely welcomed and became an emblem of alliance cooperation, and it was used by both defenders and critics of the alliance as evidence in later debates. Critics pointed out that foreign military involvement also illustrated Japan's dependence on the alliance, while supporters emphasized the value of joint training and trust.

Assessment of the government's performance has been mixed and contested. The Kan government's emergency apparatus was criticized by its opponents and by some of its own officials for confusion in the division of authority, for the proliferation of headquarters and task forces, and for the prime minister's close personal involvement in details of the nuclear crisis. Defenders argued that the situation was unprecedented and that decentralization of response to prefectures and cities was inevitable. Historians who examine local records emphasize that the earliest and most effective responses came from municipal staff, neighborhood associations, fire volunteers, and survivors themselves, who dug people from rubble and organized shelters before outside help arrived. A balanced account therefore does not treat the government or volunteers as solely responsible for rescue: state capacity, especially in transport and logistics, and local community action were interdependent.

Section 99.4 · Division I

Rebuilding Tōhoku: The Reconstruction Agency, Relocation, and the Struggle to Restore Communities

Reconstruction policy took shape quickly. The government formed a Reconstruction Design Council, an advisory body of experts, in April 2011, and released the Basic Guidelines for Reconstruction on July 29, 2011. The guidelines set a ten-year framework divided into an initial five-year "intensive reconstruction period" and a subsequent period, and allocated funding through special budgets supported by temporary tax measures. The Reconstruction Agency, created under legislation enacted late in 2011, began operating on February 10, 2012, with a mandate to coordinate the work of ministries and local governments and to serve as a single point of contact for affected municipalities. The agency identifies a first period of intensive reconstruction from 2011 to 2015 and a second period of reconstruction and revitalization from 2016 to 2020, a framework later extended.

The scale of physical reconstruction was large. Authorities arranged temporary housing, at its peak tens of thousands of prefabricated units, and later built public housing for those who could not rebuild privately. They restored railways, ports, and roads, including a new coastal highway network, and built new seawalls, in places more than ten meters high, along hundreds of kilometers of coast. Many towns adopted a policy of collective relocation to higher ground (bōsai shūdan iten) or raised their ground level by moving soil from hillsides. In Rikuzentakata, conveyor systems carried soil from the hills to the lowlands in a project that became one of the largest earthmoving efforts of the decade. Fishing ports and agricultural land were restored, with new processing facilities and consolidated farmland.

Reconstruction raised a series of unresolved conflicts. Rapid reconstruction clashed with the slower processes of consultation required by communities that had to decide where to live. Seawalls protected against future tsunamis but also cut off views and access to the sea, which residents valued for livelihood and identity, and environmental groups objected to the loss of coastal ecosystems. Costs, often funded by the national treasury, encouraged large, standardized projects even in towns whose populations were shrinking. By the time housing on raised land was ready, some residents had moved elsewhere or had grown accustomed to new lives, and a number of newly prepared lots stayed empty. Planners had to decide whether to build for the population that existed in 2011 or for the smaller population likely to live there by the 2030s.

The social dimension of recovery was uneven. Displaced residents were moved through emergency shelters to temporary housing and then to permanent housing, and with each move neighborhood ties weakened. Studies of temporary housing complexes found high rates of isolation among older residents, particularly men living alone, and a phenomenon of "lonely deaths" (kodokushi) that had already appeared in Kobe after 1995 recurred. Younger households with children made different calculations: they weighed schools, employment, and safety, and many chose not to return to their original towns. The demographic trends described in Chapter 97, namely falling birth rates, aging, and out-migration from rural areas, meant that some coastal communities were losing people before 2011, and the disaster accelerated the trend. Reconstruction therefore interacted with long-term regional decline rather than reversing it.

The most important conceptual point is the distinction between physical, economic, and community recovery. By 2020 the agency could report the near completion of housing, transport, and coastal-defense projects in the disaster-stricken regions, and the number of people living as evacuees had fallen from roughly 470,000 in the weeks after the disaster to approximately 44,000 as of April 9, 2020 (Reconstruction Agency reporting). But restoring buildings and roads did not restore populations, businesses, or social networks, and many fishing and processing firms struggled to regain markets lost during the years of closure. Historians therefore treat "reconstruction" as a set of overlapping processes with different timetables, and they note that whose recovery counted, and by what measure, was itself a political question.

Section 99.5 · Division II — Fukushima Daiichi and the Nuclear Crisis

Japan's Nuclear-Energy System before 2011: Policy, Industry, and Regulatory Weaknesses

Japan's commitment to nuclear power grew from its position as a resource-poor industrial economy. Having experienced the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979, as Chapter 96 explained, the government treated imported fuel as a strategic vulnerability and promoted nuclear generation as a domestic and technologically advanced alternative. Subsidies and siting legislation of the 1970s, notably the "three power-source laws," offered payments to communities hosting plants, and utilities built reactors along the coasts, away from the main cities. By 2010 about fifty-four reactors were in operation, and nuclear power supplied roughly a quarter to nearly a third of Japan's electricity. Japan's Atomic Energy Basic Law of 1955 had limited nuclear activity to peaceful purposes, and the memory of Hiroshima and Nagasaki gave Japanese nuclear policy a distinctive public sensitivity, in which official messaging emphasized safety and the benign "atoms for peace" use of the technology.

The key institutions were TEPCO, one of the ten regional monopoly utilities and the largest; the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI), which oversaw energy policy; the Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency (NISA), the regulator, which sat inside METI's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy; and the Nuclear Safety Commission, an advisory body in the Cabinet Office. The arrangement meant that the ministry that promoted nuclear power also supervised its safety. Critics, including later parliamentary investigators, argued that this overlap, together with the movement of officials into industry roles, weakened independent oversight. Utilities, manufacturers, research institutions, academic experts who sat on advisory committees, and politicians in constituencies with nuclear plants formed mutually dependent networks. Journalists and critics called this web the genshiryoku mura, or "nuclear village." The term is a critical description, not the name of a formal institution, and it should not be taken to mean that every official, engineer, or scientist behaved in the same way. Many engineers inside the industry raised safety concerns, and some regulators pressed for stricter measures.

Safety assumptions rested on a concept of defense in depth: multiple barriers and backup systems. Operators and regulators assumed that the loss of all alternating-current power at a plant, a "station blackout," was so improbable that detailed preparation for prolonged blackout was unnecessary; regulatory guidance before 2011 treated such events as brief. Fukushima Daiichi's design was a 1960s-era boiling-water reactor type, upgraded over time but retaining features such as emergency diesel generators and electrical switchgear located in basements and ground-floor rooms. The plant's tsunami design basis was about 5.7 meters, derived from the 1960 Chilean tsunami and revised slightly in 2002 after a method issued by an engineering society. The station was built on a seaside terrace that had been cut down, in the 1960s, to reduce the pumping height for cooling water, lowering its elevation above the sea.

There were warnings. In 2002 a government research body issued a long-term assessment suggesting that a tsunami earthquake could occur along the Japan Trench off Fukushima. In 2008 TEPCO's own engineers, applying that assessment in a trial calculation, estimated that a tsunami of more than fifteen meters could reach the site. The company did not act on the calculation, seeking further study by an academic society, and later investigators debated whether and how seriously it was considered at senior levels. Geologists had also drawn attention to the 869 Jōgan tsunami. In 2007 the Niigata earthquake damaged TEPCO's Kashiwazaki-Kariwa plant, an event that revealed the limits of seismic assumptions, and TEPCO had been found to have falsified inspection records in 2002. None of these produced a fundamental overhaul.

The governing question is therefore not whether a tsunami could have caused damage, but why a technologically sophisticated system remained vulnerable to a combination of foreseeable hazards and institutional failures. Students should distinguish four layers of responsibility: technological risk (the physical design and its limits), management decisions (TEPCO's choices about investment and response to warnings), regulatory oversight (NISA's willingness and ability to require upgrades), and political responsibility (the policies, incentives, and public messaging that sustained the system). Different investigations, examined in Section 99.8, weighted these layers differently.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Nuclear Accident and Regulatory Terms

Reactor shutdown (or scram) means the insertion of control rods to stop the fission chain reaction; it does not stop the heat produced by radioactive decay, which continues for days and requires cooling. Station blackout is the loss of all alternating-current power at a plant. Core damage (loosely, "meltdown") refers to overheating that damages or melts the fuel; the term describes a range of conditions. Radiation dose is the energy absorbed by the body from ionizing radiation, measured in units such as millisieverts; dose depends on exposure and is not the same as the amount of radioactive material released. An evacuation zone is an area where residents are ordered to leave and may not return without permission. Regulatory independence means the separation of the safety regulator from agencies that promote the industry it supervises.

The English word "meltdown" is imprecise and sometimes sensational. This chapter uses "core damage" or "fuel melt" for the specific conditions in Units 1–3, and distinguishes them from the explosion in Unit 4's building, where the reactor was not operating.

Section 99.6 · Division II

The Fukushima Daiichi Accident: Power Failure, Reactor Damage, and Emergency Measures

Six reactors stood at Fukushima Daiichi when the earthquake struck. Units 1, 2, and 3 were operating and shut down automatically when the shaking began. Units 4, 5, and 6 had been shut down previously for maintenance or inspection; in Unit 4, all the fuel had been transferred from the reactor to the storage pool in the building. The earthquake damaged the connections to the external electrical grid, and the plant lost off-site power. Emergency diesel generators started as designed. These first few minutes are important: the plant survived the earthquake itself without evidence, according to most investigations, of fundamental loss of cooling.

About forty minutes after the earthquake, the tsunami arrived. Waves of roughly thirteen to fifteen meters flooded the site, which stood at ten meters above sea level in the area of Units 1–4, well above the design basis. Seawater entered turbine buildings and flooded the basements where emergency generators, batteries, and electrical switchgear were located. Within minutes most of the plant's alternating-current power and, at Units 1, 2, and 4, direct-current power as well, was lost. The result was a station blackout, and, for Units 1–3, the loss of the means of removing decay heat. Units 5 and 6 retained one working air-cooled generator and could be brought to safe shutdown, and the nearby Fukushima Daini plant, which received less severe flooding, kept power for part of its systems and was brought to cold shutdown.

The chronology that followed differs by unit. In Unit 1, an isolation condenser, a passive cooling system, was not working effectively, and investigators concluded that fuel began to overheat and melt within hours; hydrogen produced by the reaction of hot fuel cladding with steam collected in the reactor building, and a hydrogen explosion destroyed the upper part of the building on the afternoon of March 12. In Unit 3, a cooling system continued to operate for roughly a day and a half before stopping; workers failed to restore adequate cooling, fuel was damaged, and a more violent hydrogen explosion occurred on the morning of March 14. In Unit 2, a steam-driven cooling system ran for about three days, longer than in the other units, but then failed; the reactor's pressure could not be reduced in time to inject water, and fuel was seriously damaged. Unit 2 did not suffer a hydrogen explosion in its own building, but the largest releases of radioactive material are generally associated with events in the early hours of March 15, when containment integrity was apparently lost. Workers and officials managed to inject seawater into reactors by pumping through fire hoses and fire engines, an improvised measure taken after resistance, delay, and a short interruption.

A safeguard in understanding the accident concerns Unit 4. A hydrogen explosion destroyed the upper part of the Unit 4 reactor building early on March 15. But Unit 4's reactor was not operating and contained no fuel; the damage was caused by hydrogen that flowed from Unit 3 through shared venting pipes, not by an operating-core meltdown. The explosion nevertheless raised serious fears because the spent-fuel pool in the damaged building contained a large inventory of fuel and its cooling had stopped. Water was supplied by improvised means, including a concrete pump truck and helicopter and fire-truck water, and the pool, as it turned out, held more water than feared because of water that had leaked in from an adjacent area during maintenance. Students should therefore be careful to say that fuel melted in Units 1, 2, and 3, and that Unit 4 was a building-level crisis.

The people who confronted the accident included TEPCO operators and subcontracted workers who worked in the dark with flashlights and vehicle batteries; the plant manager, Yoshida Masao, who coordinated response and later died in 2013; firefighters from Tokyo and other cities who were sent to cool the reactors; and SDF helicopter crews and fire units. A small number of workers received doses above the level at which the government raised exposure limits for emergency work, and a larger number were affected by physical hardship. Controversy surrounds the relationship between the Prime Minister's Office and TEPCO. Prime Minister Kan visited the plant on March 12 and later set up a joint response headquarters at TEPCO's offices after concluding that information was not flowing; the NAIIC and others disagreed about whether the visit interfered with operations, and about whether TEPCO had proposed withdrawing all staff on March 14–15, as Kan believed. The National Diet investigation specifically reported finding no evidence that TEPCO had decided on a complete withdrawal, while also recording misunderstandings between the prime minister's office and TEPCO about which personnel might be relocated. This documented finding must be distinguished from the conflicting recollections and arguments about the communications and Kan's intervention. In April 2011 Japanese authorities rated the accident at level 7, the highest on the International Nuclear Event Scale, the same rating as Chernobyl, although estimates of the release of radioactive material are a fraction of those from the 1986 accident.

For students the point is to grasp a causal sequence without technical overload: the earthquake cut external power; the tsunami destroyed the backup power and the means of cooling; decay heat damaged the fuel; hydrogen and steam escaped from damaged containment and exploded in some of the buildings; radioactive materials were released to the air and, in smaller part, to the sea. The accident's timeline is documented in chronologies prepared by TEPCO, the Japanese government, the OECD Nuclear Energy Agency, and the International Atomic Energy Agency, and the later reconstruction relied on more detailed analysis than was possible in the emergency.

Section 99.7 · Division II

Evacuation, Radiation Exposure, Public Health, and Displaced Communities

The evacuation expanded in steps as the accident worsened. On the evening of March 11, authorities ordered residents within three kilometers of the plant to leave; on the morning of March 12 the zone was extended to ten kilometers, and that evening to twenty. Residents between twenty and thirty kilometers were told on March 15 to stay indoors, and in April the government designated a "deliberate evacuation area" beyond the twenty-kilometer zone, including the village of Iitate, where radioactive material had been deposited by a plume traveling northwest. Roughly 160,000 people at the peak were displaced from Fukushima Prefecture, including both those under government orders and those who left voluntarily, especially mothers with children.

Evacuation was itself dangerous. Many people left with a day's belongings expecting to return within days. Hospitals and care homes in the zone housed frail patients, and their transfer over long distances, in conditions of cold, fuel shortage, and poor communication, was a serious hazard; dozens of patients and residents of facilities near the plant died in the days following the evacuation. Families were separated, and moves among shelters broke neighborhood ties. Schools were relocated, and children scattered across the country faced bullying in some cases because of their origin. Farmers and fishers lost their livelihoods, and workers lost jobs. The uncertainty about whether and when return would be possible was itself harmful, producing documented high rates of depression, alcohol use, and other health problems among evacuees.

The central public-health distinction is among three categories. Deaths caused by the earthquake and tsunami were the great majority of the casualties. Disaster-related deaths, which numbered in the thousands in Fukushima, resulted from the conditions of evacuation and displacement, including worsening chronic illness, interrupted care, and stress. And health outcomes attributable to radiation exposure were, according to the major scientific assessments, not documented to have occurred. The UNSCEAR 2020/2021 report concluded that no adverse health effects among Fukushima residents had been documented as directly attributable to radiation exposure from the accident, and that future increases in cancer incidence attributable to radiation were unlikely to be discernible. This conclusion rests on dose estimates for the public that were generally low relative to levels where effects are observed, thanks in part to evacuation and food restrictions. It does not negate the serious physical, psychological, social, and economic consequences of displacement. It also does not say that radiation exposure carries no risk at any level; it says that the doses were low enough that effects are unlikely to be measurable in the population.

Fukushima Prefecture and Fukushima Medical University established the Fukushima Health Management Survey, which includes basic surveys of external dose estimates, check-ups, and mental-health assessments, and a thyroid ultrasound program for people who were children in March 2011, about 360,000 individuals. The thyroid program has detected several hundred cases of thyroid cancer. UNSCEAR and many scientists concluded that this increase reflects the effect of screening, which detects small cancers that would not have been found otherwise, rather than radiation effects; they note that the doses to the thyroid were low and that the pattern of cases does not correspond with estimated doses. Others, including some physicians and citizen groups, have questioned the interpretation or argued that the program's design makes conclusions difficult. The survey also illustrates a methodological problem: intensive screening can generate anxiety and overdiagnosis, even as it provides reassurance to some families. Communicating uncertainty humanely is part of the story, and Section 99.12 returns to it.

Monitoring and food safety were additional arenas. The government set provisional standards for radioactive cesium in food, tightened them in April 2012, and inspected rice, vegetables, seafood, and milk. Consumers were suspicious, prices of Fukushima produce fell, and some countries restricted imports. Radiation measurements were taken by the authorities, by universities, and by citizen groups, and differences between methods and locations produced confusion. Students should keep the evidence clear: monitoring data showed that contamination was highly uneven, with concentrated hotspots, and that restrictions reduced doses; they did not eliminate public anxiety, which had many sources, including distrust of authorities.

Section 99.8 · Division II

Investigating Responsibility: The National Diet Commission and the Creation of New Nuclear Regulation

Four principal investigations examined the accident. The government appointed an Investigation Committee on the Accident at the Fukushima Nuclear Power Stations, chaired by the engineer Hatamura Yōtarō, which issued an interim report in December 2011 and a final report in July 2012. TEPCO conducted its own internal investigation, reporting in 2012. A private Independent Investigation Commission established by the Rebuild Japan Initiative Foundation, led by journalists and scholars, published a report in 2012 drawing on interviews with officials, including Prime Minister Kan. And the National Diet established the Fukushima Nuclear Accident Independent Investigation Commission (NAIIC), the first independent parliamentary investigation commission in Japan's modern constitutional history, chaired by the physician and scientist Kurokawa Kiyoshi, which reported on July 5, 2012. The International Atomic Energy Agency later published a comprehensive technical assessment in 2015.

The NAIIC's mandate was broader than the government committee's, its members were chosen by the Diet and drawn from outside government, and it held public hearings and compelled testimony. It concluded that the accident was a "manmade" disaster in the sense that it was foreseeable and that TEPCO, regulators, and the government had failed to take available precautions. It criticized the relationships among TEPCO, NISA, and the Cabinet Office as allowing the regulator to be dominated by the industry, a condition often called regulatory capture, and found that the government's crisis management was hampered by confusion and lack of preparation. It explicitly argued that damage to certain safety-related equipment from the earthquake, including possible coolant leakage, could not be excluded on the evidence then available. This was the commission's uncertainty-based concern, not a demonstrated alternative causal sequence; other investigations found the tsunami-driven loss of power and cooling central. In the English-language chairman's foreword, Kurokawa described the disaster as “Made in Japan” and attributed underlying failures in part to cultural conventions such as obedience to authority and reluctance to question it. The Japanese-language prefatory text uses materially different cultural phrasing and should not be quoted or paraphrased as if it contained the identical English sentence. The sweeping explanation drew criticism for substituting national-cultural generalization for a more specific account of institutional incentives, legal structures, and decisions by identifiable organizations.

Comparison of the reports shows a measure of agreement and several differences. All found that the plant was not adequately prepared for a long-duration station blackout and a large tsunami, that regulators had not required sufficient upgrades, and that emergency communication and coordination failed. The government committee gave more emphasis to technical and organizational failings within the plant and the lack of a unified emergency framework, while the NAIIC put greater weight on the political and regulatory structure and on the long history of regulatory weakness. The private commission focused on the decision-making of the Prime Minister's Office. Disagreements concerned the role of the Prime Minister's intervention, the question of earthquake damage before the tsunami, and how far culture or institutional design explained the outcome. Historians advise students to read the reports as products of institutions with different mandates, not as interchangeable accounts.

The institutional response was the creation of the Nuclear Regulation Authority (NRA). The Nuclear Regulation Authority Establishment Act (Act No. 47 of 2012), promulgated on June 27, 2012, created the new regulatory body, which began work on September 19, 2012, as an external agency of the Ministry of the Environment with independent authority, replacing NISA and the Nuclear Safety Commission. The law explicitly addressed the problem of overlapping promotion and regulation by separating the regulator from METI. The NRA's five-member commission, chaired initially by the geologist Tanaka Shunichi, was drawn from outside the industry. In July 2013 it implemented new regulatory standards that required measures against severe accidents, stronger protection against tsunamis and earthquakes, assessment of active faults under reactor sites, backup power and cooling, filtered venting, and a forty-year operating limit with one possible twenty-year extension. Restart required NRA safety review; in practice, utilities and governments also navigated expectations of agreement from host communities, but local consent was not itself a statutory NRA safety-approval criterion.

The reform was significant but limited. The NRA's independence was institutional, but critics noted that its staff included former officials of the earlier agencies and that its budget and personnel relied on government support. Emergency planning was not within its power to enforce, since evacuation planning for areas around plants was the responsibility of local governments and the Cabinet Office. Reviews proved slow and contentious, and the NRA's decisions were challenged in court by residents and local governments. Even so, historians generally regard the creation of the NRA as a real change in the structure of regulation, and its strictness delayed and in some cases prevented the restart of reactors. The governing conclusion of this division is that the accident was a compound technological and institutional failure, not simply an unavoidable consequence of a natural event.

Analysis: The statute reveals how lawmakers diagnosed the problem: overlapping functions and insufficient independence. It cannot tell historians how independent the NRA proved in practice, which requires evidence from its decisions, staffing, budget, and conflicts with ministries and utilities. The statute also does not decide the harder political question of who bears responsibility for local emergency plans or whether restarts should occur.

Historical Debate

Was Fukushima Principally an Engineering Failure, a Regulatory Failure, or a Failure of Political Accountability?

The question: Investigators and historians agree that the accident had multiple causes, but they differ about which should be treated as primary and how responsibility should be assigned.

Interpretation A — Engineering and management failure: Some analysts emphasize design choices, such as the placement of backup power equipment and inadequate protection against flooding, the lack of effective means of cooling after loss of power, and TEPCO's decisions not to act on its own 2008 tsunami calculation. On this view, the central error was the operator's failure to manage a known risk.
Interpretation B — Regulatory and political failure: Others argue that a system that allowed the promoter to supervise the plant, and that placed limited demands on operators, made management failure likely. They emphasize the NAIIC's findings about regulatory capture and the role of politicians and ministries in sustaining a policy that discouraged questioning of safety.

Evidence: Both rely on the same records: the 2002 and 2008 assessments, internal TEPCO documents, regulatory minutes, and investigators' interviews. They differ over whether the regulator could realistically have compelled action and over the weight to give to managers' knowledge. Court cases have reached different conclusions; a criminal trial of former TEPCO executives ended in acquittal in 2019, while civil suits produced divergent judgments about the state's responsibility, including later rulings beyond this chapter's boundary.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether reform focuses on technical standards, institutional design, or political accountability, and how the costs of the accident are distributed among the utility, the government, and taxpayers.

Student task: Identify two facts from this division that support Interpretation A and two that support B, and explain whether the interpretations are mutually exclusive.

Section 99.9 · Division III — Energy, Reconstruction, and Anti-Nuclear Mobilization

Japan's Energy Debate after Fukushima: Nuclear Shutdowns, Renewable Power, and Reactor Restarts

The accident reorganized Japan's electricity system within months. Reactors that had stopped for inspections could not restart without local consent, and the government ordered the Hamaoka plant shut in May 2011 because of seismic risk. By May 2012 all of Japan's reactors were offline. Two reactors at Ōi in Fukui Prefecture were restarted in July 2012 under provisional standards, amid protests, and stopped again in September 2013, leaving Japan without nuclear generation until August 2015. Utilities replaced the lost output mainly with imported natural gas, coal, and oil. Fossil-fuel imports surged, Japan recorded its first annual trade deficit in decades in 2011, and electricity prices rose; greenhouse-gas emissions increased in the following years before declining. Conservation campaigns, including summer power-saving measures and business adjustments such as "Cool Biz," reduced consumption, and a wave of rolling blackouts in March 2011 gave way to a sustained emphasis on efficiency.

Renewable energy was the principal policy innovation. The feed-in tariff, which obliges utilities to buy renewable electricity at guaranteed prices, began in July 2012 and led to rapid growth of solar installations, from a very small base to a significant share of generation within several years. The price guarantees were generous, and costs were passed to consumers through surcharges, which led to debates about subsidies and to later reductions of tariff rates. Integrating variable sources into a grid divided between 50-hertz and 60-hertz regions, and controlled by regional monopolies with limited interconnection, was difficult. Electricity market liberalization, including retail competition from 2016, was another post-3/11 reform, in part prompted by criticism of the utilities' dominance.

The government's energy plans reflected competing arguments. Under the DPJ, the government in 2012 discussed an aim of phasing out nuclear power by the 2030s, but the LDP government that returned in December 2012 rejected that target, describing nuclear power as an important base-load source. The Strategic Energy Plan of 2014, the 2015 long-term supply-and-demand outlook, and the revised plan of 2018 described nuclear power as an important base-load source and set a goal for the 2030 electricity mix that included a nuclear share of about 20 to 22 percent and renewables of 22 to 24 percent. Supporters of restarting reactors cited energy security, the cost of fossil imports, climate commitments, and the economic competitiveness of industry. Opponents cited safety, the unresolved problems of waste, the unrealistic character of evacuation plans, and the decline in the cost of renewable power. Public opinion surveys through the decade generally found that a majority favored reducing or ending nuclear power, though support for immediate abolition was lower, and electoral outcomes were driven by many other issues.

The first commercial reactor to restart under the new regulatory arrangements was Unit 1 of the Sendai Nuclear Power Station in Kagoshima Prefecture, operated by Kyushu Electric Power, which resumed operation on August 11, 2015. Others followed slowly. By 2020 about nine reactors had returned to operation, at Sendai, Genkai, Ikata, Takahama, and Ōi, while many more were in review, idled, or ordered to close. Courts played a role: judges in some district courts issued injunctions against operating reactors, including an order affecting Takahama in 2016, though these were frequently overturned on appeal. Students must distinguish several statuses: reactors permanently decommissioned (including Fukushima Daiichi's six units and a number of older reactors that owners decided not to bring into compliance), reactors idled awaiting review, reactors approved but not yet restarted because of local opposition or litigation, and reactors in operation.

Section 99.10 · Division III

The Anti-Nuclear Movement: Protest, Citizen Science, and the Political Legacy of 3/11

Anti-nuclear activism existed before 2011: local movements had opposed plant sites for decades, such as at Kaminoseki and Maki, and Japan's atomic-bomb survivors' movement, discussed in earlier chapters, had a distinct tradition of opposing nuclear weapons. After March 2011, however, concern about nuclear power moved into the mainstream. In September 2011 a large rally in Tokyo, with organizers' estimates of tens of thousands, included writers such as Ōe Kenzaburō. A petition organized by prominent figures gathered several million signatures calling for an end to nuclear power. In spring 2012, as the Noda government prepared to restart the Ōi reactors, weekly Friday-evening demonstrations outside the Prime Minister's residence grew into the largest in decades; organizers claimed a crowd of two hundred thousand on June 29, while police estimates were far lower, a discrepancy that is a standard example of the need to compare sources. In August 2012 Prime Minister Noda met representatives of the protest organizers, an unusual event.

The movement was diverse. It included veteran activists from labor and environmental organizations, but also many first-time participants: mothers worried about children's exposure, office workers, students, artists, and residents of Fukushima who had been displaced. Local campaigns opposed restarts in communities near nuclear stations and in neighboring municipalities that were not included in formal consent requirements. Lawyers brought civil suits seeking injunctions against reactor operations and damages from TEPCO and the state. A distinctive feature was citizen science: groups such as Safecast and citizen radiation-measuring stations built or acquired detectors, mapped contamination, and published data, challenging official information when they thought it inadequate. These groups did not always agree with each other or with government scientists, and they drew on the expertise of volunteer physicists and engineers.

The movement mobilized people with different political outlooks around different concerns. Some emphasized radiation risk, others institutional distrust, others the energy system or Japan's postwar nuclear policy, and some framed their objection in explicitly antinuclear-weapons terms. Many participants stressed that they were not ideological. Compared with earlier postwar movements, the post-3/11 cycle was less organized around political parties and labor unions, which had been central to the Anpo protests of 1960 (Chapter 92) and the New Left of the late 1960s (Chapter 93). In this respect it resembled the residents' and environmental movements of the 1960s and 1970s (Chapter 94), which began with local damage and distrust, but it used the internet and social media on a scale that earlier movements could not. Scholars generally identify the period after 2011 as a new cycle of protest, with large antinuclear demonstrations followed by mobilization on constitutional and security issues, discussed in Section 99.16.

Students should avoid assuming that Japan was uniformly opposed to nuclear power. Opinion polls showed a majority favoring reduction, but many people also worried about electricity costs and supply, and communities that hosted plants depended on them for jobs and revenue. Local elections sometimes produced candidates opposed to restarts, as in Niigata Prefecture in 2016, and sometimes ones who accepted them. National elections returned the LDP, which supported restarts, with large majorities in 2012 and later, because voters had many reasons for their choices. The movement's achievements are therefore debated. It helped produce the NRA, shaped public opinion, and made restart decisions slow and politically costly, but it did not prevent restarts or change the party in power.

Section 99.11 · Division III

Fukushima's Long Reconstruction: Compensation, Return, Decontamination, and Local Economies

Recovery in the areas affected by radioactive contamination followed a different logic from recovery from the tsunami. The government zoned the evacuated areas into categories according to estimated annual dose, and began decontamination under a 2011 special-measures law, which authorized the removal of topsoil, washing of buildings, and clearing of forest margins in residential areas. Millions of cubic meters of contaminated soil were stored in black plastic bags in temporary sites and later moved to an interim storage facility built in the towns of Ōkuma and Futaba, which began receiving material in 2015. Decontamination reduced dose rates, but it was costly, left forests largely untreated, and raised unresolved questions about the final disposal of the material.

Evacuation orders began to be lifted in stages from 2014. Naraha town was among the first fully reopened in 2015, and large portions of Namie, Iitate, Tomioka, and Kawamata followed in 2017, with partial reopening of Ōkuma in 2019. A formal lifting of an order is not the same as return. Residents' decisions depended on the condition of homes, which had often been vandalized by wildlife or looters or had deteriorated, on the availability of shops, clinics, and schools, on employment, and on family circumstances. In many towns return rates were low, concentrated among older residents, while younger families had settled elsewhere. Some towns recruited newcomers, including workers in decommissioning and reconstruction, and promoted new industries, such as robotics, renewable energy, and agricultural experiments. The picture was uneven: some communities regained a substantial population, while others remained largely empty.

Compensation became a central political and legal issue. Under a 2011 law, TEPCO was responsible for compensating victims, and a government-backed facility, the Nuclear Damage Compensation and Decommissioning Facilitation Corporation, was established to provide funds. In 2012 the government injected capital into TEPCO and became its majority voting shareholder, effectively placing the company under state control. Payments covered lost wages, property, and psychological distress ("consolation money"), but the process was complicated, and standards differed between those evacuated under government order and those who left voluntarily. Thousands of residents joined lawsuits against TEPCO and the state. Courts' rulings varied: several lower courts found the state partly responsible for failing to require tsunami precautions, while later decisions, outside this chapter's boundary, reached different conclusions. These cases illustrate that the question of accountability continued long after the investigations.

Fukushima's agriculture and fishing struggled with consumer suspicion even after monitoring showed that most products met standards. Rice was tested bag by bag, and fisheries operated on a limited, experimental basis for years. Other countries maintained import restrictions for some years. Contaminated water remained a technical and political problem: groundwater flowing into the damaged buildings, storage of treated water in tanks, and countermeasures such as a ground-freezing wall. Decommissioning of the damaged reactors was planned for several decades, and the locations and condition of melted fuel debris remained uncertain through 2020. A decision on release of treated water into the sea was taken only in 2021 and lies beyond this chapter, except as retrospective context. The distinction to preserve is between engineering progress, which was real but slow, and economic and social reconstruction, which depended on trust and choice.

Section 99.12 · Division III

Information, Scientific Expertise, and the Reconstruction of Public Trust

Public trust in institutions was damaged early. In the first days, the government spokesperson's repeated assurances that there was no immediate health threat, together with delayed release of dispersion forecasts and conflicting descriptions of the reactors' condition, produced a perception that authorities were concealing information. The SPEEDI system, designed to forecast the spread of radioactive material, produced results that were not used in the initial evacuation decisions and were released to the public later, which investigators criticized. TEPCO's statements were revised repeatedly. Media coverage varied widely; major outlets relied on official briefings and expert commentators, while weekly magazines, independent journalists, foreign press, and online media gave more skeptical accounts, and some of these gave unreliable ones. Social media circulated both valuable information and rumor.

Scientific expertise became contested. Some scientists who appeared in the media to reassure the public were later criticized for understating uncertainty, and some who warned of grave danger were criticized for exaggeration. Medical professionals, local governments, and universities collaborated with the prefecture on health monitoring, but some residents were wary of surveys run by officials, and others disagreed with the advice they were given about acceptable dose limits. The government's decision to set a twenty-millisievert-per-year threshold for the evacuation of some areas and, notably, for schools, prompted protests by parents and some scientists, leading to adjustments. Citizen groups, physicians who monitored children's health independently, and international organizations all participated. The debate was not merely between experts and laypeople, because experts disagreed with each other, and members of the public differed in the level of risk they were willing to accept.

Two principles recur in the evidence. First, residents were entitled to transparency, to participation in decisions about their communities, and to independent verification, and the failure of authorities to provide these damaged credibility. Second, scientific conclusions about radiation risk depend on evidence and methods that do not change because of public sentiment, which makes honest communication of uncertainty difficult. The Fukushima Health Management Survey shows both. Its long-term monitoring offered some residents reassurance and provided data for researchers, while its sensitive thyroid screening generated difficult interpretive and ethical questions, including whether to screen asymptomatic people and how to explain overdiagnosis.

Historians therefore analyze trust through institutional behavior: whether data were open, whether compensation and monitoring were consistent, whether communities were consulted, and whether officials admitted errors. It is not enough to say that citizens either trusted or rejected science. Many residents trusted local doctors but not national ministries, used official data alongside citizen measurements, or trusted data but not the motives of institutions. The result was a more complicated landscape of public expertise, in which scientific authority had to be earned through transparency rather than assumed.

Cause and Effect

From Hazard to Civic Mobilization

Long-term causes

Subduction-zone seismicity; coastal settlement patterns; postwar nuclear-energy policy and the 1970s oil shocks; overlapping promotion and regulation; regional demographic decline; an established tradition of local and environmental protest.

Short-term causes

The unexpected scale of the March 11 rupture; tsunami heights exceeding design assumptions; a plant site with vulnerable backup power; ignored warnings of 2002 and 2008; communication failures during the emergency.

Event or transformation

A compound disaster of earthquake, tsunami, and nuclear accident that exposed weaknesses in infrastructure, regulation, crisis management, and public information.

Immediate effects

Mass casualties and displacement; shutdown of nuclear generation; new investigations; creation of the Reconstruction Agency and the NRA; large volunteer mobilization; the first of a series of large protests.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The disaster contributed to a changed energy policy, new regulatory standards, and a more skeptical public, but it did not cause every later political development. Executive consolidation, security-policy change, and immigration reform followed their own causes. The sequence from hazard to mobilization was not automatic: it depended on decisions by officials, companies, courts, and citizens, and on the political opportunities available to each.
Section 99.13 · Division IV — Constitutional Politics, Security Policy, and Okinawa

Political Leadership after 3/11: The Democratic Party, Abe's Return, and the Transformation of Government

The Democratic Party of Japan had come to power in 2009 promising to replace bureaucratic dominance with political leadership. The disaster tested that promise under the worst conditions. Prime Minister Kan Naoto's government was criticized for improvised structures, for his intervention in the nuclear response, and for tensions with TEPCO and with ministry officials. It was also defended by some for acting under unprecedented pressure and for decisions such as the request to shut down the Hamaoka plant. Kan's support was weak even before the disaster, and opposition parties, including the LDP, refused to cooperate fully; he announced his resignation after passing key legislation in the summer of 2011. Noda Yoshihiko succeeded him on September 2, 2011.

Noda's government negotiated with the LDP and Kōmeitō over a plan to raise the consumption tax from five to eight and then ten percent, a measure intended to fund social security, and passed the legislation in 2012. It also pushed for reactor restarts at Ōi, and set up the NRA. But the DPJ split over the tax increase, its public support fell, and Noda dissolved the lower house in November 2012. In the general election of December 16, 2012, the LDP won 294 of 480 seats, and with its coalition partner Kōmeitō held more than two-thirds, while the DPJ fell to 57. Turnout, at under sixty percent, was the lowest in the postwar period to that date. The result was less a surge of enthusiasm for the LDP than a collapse of the DPJ, and an electoral system favoring large parties, since the LDP's share of the party-list and single-seat votes was much smaller than its seat share. Abe Shinzō, who had served a brief first term in 2006–2007, became prime minister again on December 26, 2012.

It is important to separate the immediate consequences of 3/11 from the longer-standing conditions that contributed to the DPJ's defeat. These included the party's failure to deliver on its 2009 manifesto, the reversal of its promise on consumption tax, its failed approach to Futenma, rivalries within the party, and a divided Diet. The disaster and its handling were part of the story, but opinion polls indicate that voters in 2012 weighed the economy, taxes, and leadership as well as nuclear policy. The opposition to nuclear power, strong in opinion surveys, was divided among several small parties, which did not benefit significantly.

Abe's second administration lasted until September 2020, the longest continuous premiership in Japanese history, supported by victories in the 2013 and 2016 upper house elections and the 2014 and 2017 lower house elections. Several features of this period are relevant to this chapter. The government strengthened the executive: the Cabinet Office and the Prime Minister's Office gained power over appointments, including the Cabinet Bureau of Personnel Affairs created in 2014 to manage senior civil servants, and a National Security Council was established in December 2013 with a national security secretariat. Economic policy, known as Abenomics, combined monetary easing, fiscal stimulus, and structural reform, and set a framework for labor and immigration policy examined later in this chapter. The long tenure produced policy continuity and, critics argued, weakened the autonomy of the bureaucracy and led to scandals concerning favoritism in government decisions, which the opposition pursued. The chapter's task is to explain these policies within the history of the 2010s. Chapter 100 will take up Abe's wider legacy, his assassination in 2022, and later controversies.

Section 99.14 · Division IV

Article 9, Collective Self-Defense, and the Security Legislation of 2015

Article 9 of the 1947 Constitution renounces war as a sovereign right and the use of force to settle international disputes and states that land, sea, and air forces will not be maintained. As Chapters 84 and 90 explained, successive governments nonetheless maintained the Self-Defense Forces (SDF), justifying them as the minimum force necessary for individual self-defense, an inherent right of any state. They argued that the exercise of collective self-defense, meaning the use of force to defend an ally under attack even when Japan itself has not been attacked, exceeded the constitutional limit. This interpretation was articulated by the Cabinet Legislation Bureau, the body that advises the government on constitutional questions, and was reaffirmed by governments for decades. The U.S.–Japan alliance therefore rested on an asymmetry: the United States was obligated to defend Japan, while Japan provided bases and later logistical support, but its forces did not fight for American forces.

Abe, who had long regarded this restriction as an obstacle to Japan's role as a normal country, formed an advisory panel on the question. His preference was formal constitutional revision, but amending the Constitution requires a two-thirds majority in both houses of the Diet and a majority in a national referendum, which he could not secure. His government therefore pursued reinterpretation. A first attempt to loosen the amendment procedure itself (Article 96) failed to win support in 2013. On July 1, 2014, the Cabinet adopted a decision that reinterpreted Article 9 to permit a limited exercise of collective self-defense. Under the decision, force could be used when an armed attack occurred against a foreign country in a close relationship with Japan and the result threatened Japan's survival and posed a clear danger of fundamentally overturning people's right to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness, when no other appropriate means existed, and when the use of force was limited to the minimum necessary. The decision also addressed gray-zone situations and participation in international peace operations.

The government introduced legislation in May 2015 to implement the decision, a package of amendments to ten existing laws and a new law on international peace support, collectively called the Peace and Security Legislation. It was accompanied by revised U.S.–Japan defense cooperation guidelines in April 2015. The House of Representatives passed the bills in July, and the House of Councillors approved them in the early hours of September 19, 2015, after a chaotic final session. The laws entered into force on March 29, 2016. The government argued that the security environment had changed because of North Korea's missile and nuclear programs, China's military expansion, and the nature of modern threats, and that the new framework would strengthen deterrence and the alliance. It stressed that the three conditions were strict and that Japan would not participate in wars of aggression or in conflicts such as the Gulf War.

Critics included opposition parties, many constitutional scholars, and a large part of the public. They argued that the changes exceeded the limits of what Article 9 allowed and that a Cabinet decision could not rewrite the Constitution. In a notable episode in June 2015, three constitutional scholars invited to testify before the lower house's commission, including one chosen by the governing parties, stated that the proposed laws were unconstitutional. Other critics objected to the ambiguity of the criteria, especially the idea of a threat to the "survival" of Japan, to the pace and manner of passage, and to the risk that Japan would be drawn into American wars. Opinion polls through 2015 found majorities opposed to the legislation and a majority saying that the government's explanations were insufficient, though polls also showed substantial support for strengthening Japan's defense capabilities in general. Supporters, including many scholars and security specialists, argued that the reinterpretation was within the range of legitimate interpretation, that the government retained constraints, and that the previous position was untenable in practice.

The principal historical question is whether the reforms represented a necessary adaptation of Japan's security policy or a problematic alteration of constitutional practice without formal amendment. The evidence permits some clear distinctions. The change was a reinterpretation, not an amendment, and the text of Article 9 remained unchanged. The Diet legally enacted the laws, so the legislative procedure was formally valid, though its quality was disputed. No court invalidated the laws; Japanese courts have been reluctant to rule on the constitutionality of security measures. Beyond those points, the two interpretations rest on different premises about the Constitution's purpose and the nature of the threats Japan faces, and the chapter does not treat them as equally supported on every specific legal or factual claim: for example, the claim that Japan is thereby committed to fight in any American war is not supported by the text of the conditions, while the claim that the scope of the "survival-threatening situation" is open to broad interpretation by government is a point acknowledged by many commentators.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Article 9, Collective Self-Defense, Local Autonomy, and Civic Activism

Article 9 is the constitutional provision renouncing war and the maintenance of war potential, interpreted since the 1950s to allow forces limited to self-defense. Individual self-defense is a state's use of force to defend itself; collective self-defense is the use of force to help an ally under attack. Reinterpretation of a provision by the Cabinet differs from amendment, which requires supermajorities in the Diet and a national referendum. Kichi (military base) is the everyday word for the installations that, in Okinawa, include U.S. facilities; in political usage it carries the weight of local experience. Local autonomy (chihō jichi) refers to the constitutionally recognized self-government of prefectures and municipalities, whose relation to national security policy is contested. Civic activism describes organized participation by citizens outside parties and government, including demonstrations, petitions, volunteering, and litigation.

Section 99.15 · Division IV

The U.S.–Japan Alliance and Okinawa: Futenma, Henoko, Local Autonomy, and Military Burden

Okinawa, the former Ryukyu Kingdom described in Chapters 32 and 33, had been annexed by Japan in the 1870s, devastated in the Battle of Okinawa in 1945, and administered by the United States until reversion on May 15, 1972 (Chapter 90). Reversion did not remove the U.S. military presence. Roughly seventy percent of the land area used exclusively by U.S. forces in Japan lies in Okinawa Prefecture, which holds less than one percent of the country's land. Residents have long experienced noise, accidents, environmental contamination, and crimes by service members. The rape of a schoolgirl by three American servicemen in September 1995 triggered a mass protest of tens of thousands in October and pressure on both governments. In April 1996 the two governments announced the planned return of Marine Corps Air Station Futenma, located in the middle of the city of Ginowan, on condition that a replacement facility be provided within Okinawa.

After years of negotiation, a 2006 realignment plan identified the coast at Henoko, in the city of Nago, adjoining Camp Schwab, as the site of a replacement airfield built on reclaimed land in Ōura Bay, with two runways in a V shape. Plans were controversial from the start. Opponents, including local residents, environmental organizations, and many prefectural politicians, argued that the project would destroy coral and seagrass habitat, harm the dugong, and entrench rather than reduce the burden on Okinawa. They noted that the seabed included areas of soft soil requiring extensive and costly reinforcement. The DPJ's Prime Minister Hatoyama in 2009–2010 raised the possibility of relocating Futenma outside the prefecture but abandoned the effort in the face of opposition from U.S. officials and national bureaucrats, a failure that damaged his government and deepened Okinawan disillusionment. Governor Nakaima Hirokazu, after receiving funding and development commitments from the Abe government, approved the landfill application at the end of 2013.

In November 2014, Onaga Takeshi, a former LDP politician and mayor of Naha who had broken with his party on the issue, won the governorship against Nakaima, with a platform opposing the construction at Henoko and a coalition known as "All Okinawa" that included conservatives, liberals, and the left. Onaga revoked the landfill approval in October 2015, citing defects in the original decision. The national government responded with administrative measures and litigation, and the dispute went through the courts. In December 2016, the Supreme Court ruled against the prefecture in a case concerning the validity of the revocation, and construction resumed. Onaga died in August 2018, and his successor, Tamaki Denny, won the September 2018 election with a record number of votes on the same platform. The national government began depositing soil into the bay in December 2018. The conflict was thus a legal and political struggle over whether the national government's security authority superseded the autonomy of the prefecture, in a system in which courts largely upheld national authority.

On February 24, 2019, the prefecture held a referendum on the reclamation project, after residents collected tens of thousands of signatures to demand it. Several municipalities initially declined to administer it, and the ballot was revised to offer three options: agree, disagree, and neither. About 72.15 percent of valid votes opposed the landfill, and turnout was approximately 52.48 percent. The result was politically significant, and the prefectural government communicated it to the national government and to the United States. But it was not legally binding on the national government, which declared its intention to continue the project, citing the need to remove the danger of Futenma. Some commentators noted that the vote showed opposition among those who voted, while supporters of the project observed that nearly half of eligible voters had not voted and that earlier local elections had produced mixed results, including in Nago. Students should avoid assuming that Okinawans held a single view: residents differ over the alliance, base-related employment and economic benefits, the balance between Okinawan identity and Japanese identity, and the best way to reduce the burden, and the 2018 Nago mayoral election, for example, was won by a candidate supported by the national government.

Security arguments for the relocation emphasized that Futenma is a hazard in a densely populated area, that the Marines' presence in Okinawa deters regional threats, and that the alliance depends on facilities in a strategically located prefecture. Opponents argued that the military value of the Henoko base was uncertain, that the unfinished project could not solve Futenma's danger for years, and that the process demonstrated the national government's willingness to override local consent. Aircraft accidents, including the crash of a Marine MV-22 Osprey off Nago in December 2016 and the fall of a helicopter window onto a school playground in 2017, kept the issue in public view. Those events and the 2016 murder of an Okinawan woman by a civilian employee at a U.S. base increased pressure on political leaders. The Henoko dispute is thus both a particular conflict and a window onto the broader question of how a democracy balances national security with local consent.

Section 99.16 · Division IV

Security, Government Secrecy, Civil Liberties, and Constitutional Protest

A second pillar of the Abe government's security agenda was information security. The Act on the Protection of Specially Designated Secrets was passed in December 2013 and took effect on December 10, 2014. It allowed ministers to designate information in four categories, namely defense, diplomacy, counter-espionage, and counter-terrorism, as specially designated secrets for periods of up to five years, renewable, and it provided penalties of up to ten years' imprisonment for leaking designated secrets, with lesser penalties for others who obtained them through improper means. The government said the law was necessary for sharing intelligence with allies, particularly the United States, which had complained about the weakness of Japan's secrecy rules, and for the new National Security Council.

Journalists, lawyers' associations, the opposition parties, and civil-liberties organizations objected that the definition of the categories was broad, that the law gave bureaucrats discretion to classify information with insufficient external review, and that it could chill investigative reporting and the public's right to know. Concerns also included the speed of passage, with the upper house vote taken after limited debate and amid protests, and the provision on journalists, which the government said would not be used against legitimate news gathering. Supporters argued that safeguards, including review bodies and provisions protecting reporting, would prevent abuse. Evidence on the law's effects is mixed: through 2020, prosecutions were few, but critics argued that the effects of a deterrent are difficult to measure, and the volume of designated secrets, which was large, remained difficult to scrutinize.

The 2015 security legislation produced the largest protests since the antinuclear demonstrations of 2012. Constitutional scholars, lawyers, and former officials issued statements. Women's groups, including a network called Mothers Against War, a group of academics, and many ordinary citizens joined. Students formed SEALDs (Students Emergency Action for Liberal Democracy) in 2015, a group that attracted media attention for its rallies outside the Diet, its use of social media, its slogans in a hip-hop style, and its spokespeople, such as Okuda Aki. A large demonstration outside the Diet on August 30, 2015, was estimated by organizers at about one hundred and twenty thousand and by police at a much lower number, again illustrating the need to compare sources. These movements used legal tools as well: petitions to the Diet, lawsuits challenging the laws' constitutionality, and cooperation with opposition parties, which formed an electoral alliance in the 2016 upper-house elections that achieved limited results.

The protests resembled earlier movements in their concern with Article 9, with the memory of the Anpo protest of 1960 (Chapter 92), and with the language of defending democracy. They differed in important ways. Unlike the 1960 movement, they were not organized primarily by the Socialist Party and labor federations, which had weakened since the 1990s, and they emphasized individual participation, nonviolence, and polite, visually distinctive protest. Participants were not necessarily opposed to the SDF or the alliance; many criticized the process and the reinterpretation rather than defense itself. SEALDs dissolved in August 2016, having claimed that it had completed its purpose. The protests did not stop the legislation, and the LDP remained in office, but they affected public discourse and the political positioning of opposition parties, and they demonstrated that the 2010s were a period of contestation, not of uncontested executive power. Opinion about constitutional amendment remained divided through 2020: Abe's proposal in 2017 to add an explicit reference to the SDF to Article 9 did not proceed to a referendum.

Section 99.17 · Division V — Labor, Immigration, Civic Change, and Historical Synthesis

Work in the 2010s: Labor Reform, Overwork, Gender, and Economic Security

Chapter 97 described how the Lost Decades expanded irregular employment, leaving about a third of the workforce in nonregular jobs with lower pay and security. In the 2010s, the labor market shifted in a new direction: as the population of working age shrank, unemployment fell to historically low levels, and by 2018–2019 there were more job openings than applicants, with the ratio rising to about 1.6. Labor shortages became a defining concern, particularly in construction, caregiving, retail, food service, and logistics. Employers hired more women, older workers, and foreign workers. The share of nonregular employment, however, did not fall; it rose to just under forty percent by 2019, because many newly employed women and older workers took part-time or fixed-term jobs.

The Abe government's economic strategy included an emphasis on women's participation, sometimes called Womenomics. It set goals for women in leadership, including a target of thirty percent of leadership positions by 2020, which was not met, expanded childcare capacity, and enacted a 2015 law requiring larger employers to publish action plans to promote women's advancement. The female labor-force participation rate for those aged fifteen to sixty-four rose from about sixty-three percent in 2012 to over seventy percent in 2019. Observers, however, distinguished stated goals from measurable outcomes: the increase was concentrated in part-time and irregular work, the gender wage gap remained wide, and women continued to bear most unpaid care work. Waiting lists for daycare became a political flashpoint in 2016, when an anonymous online post complaining about a failed application for a daycare place went viral and prompted parliamentary debate. Free early-childhood education was introduced in October 2019, funded by a rise in the consumption tax.

Overwork (karōshi, death from overwork) remained a concern. A law to promote measures against karōshi took effect in 2014. In 2015 a young Dentsu employee, Takahashi Matsuri, died by suicide after working long hours, and in 2016 labor authorities recognized her death as work-related, causing public outrage and a government investigation of the company. This case, along with campaigns by bereaved families and lawyers, helped create the political conditions for reform. The Work Style Reform legislation, passed in June 2018 and promulgated in July, was the most important labor reform of the decade. It introduced legal limits on overtime for the first time: in general 45 hours per month and 360 hours per year, with a maximum under special circumstances of 100 hours in a single month and 720 hours per year, and an average of 80 hours over multiple months. It also required employers to ensure that employees took a minimum number of paid leave days, introduced rules on rest intervals, and aimed to reduce disparities between regular and nonregular workers by establishing the principle of "equal pay for equal work" in the legislation on part-time and fixed-term workers.

Implementation was staged. The overtime caps took effect for large firms in April 2019 and for small and medium-sized firms in April 2020, while construction, truck driving, and physicians were given a delay until 2024 because of labor shortages. Provisions on equal treatment applied from April 2020 for large firms and April 2021 for smaller ones. The legislation also created a "Highly Skilled Professional" system exempting some well-paid specialists from overtime regulation, which unions opposed as a threat to protections. Employers responded in varied ways: some reduced hours and improved productivity, others shifted work to unpaid overtime or to subcontractors, and many small firms said they could not comply without more workers. Labor unions, which organized less than a fifth of workers and were concentrated in large firms, argued that the law's limits were too high, since 100 hours in a month exceeds the level at which health authorities had recognized karōshi, and that enforcement by a small inspectorate would be difficult.

The central issue is whether labor-policy reforms changed the underlying distribution of economic security established during the Lost Decades. The evidence through 2020 suggests partial change: hours limits and equal-treatment principles created new legal tools, and a tight labor market improved bargaining power for some workers, but the dual structure of regular and nonregular employment persisted, wages grew slowly, and the share of workers with job security did not expand. Passage of legislation, its staged implementation, and measured effects are three different things, and historians must keep them separate.

Section 99.18 · Division V

Immigration and Foreign Workers: Labor Shortages, Legal Reform, and Social Inclusion

Japan had long avoided a formal immigration policy. Postwar governments sought to limit permanent settlement, while permitting entry in specific categories to meet labor needs. Zainichi Koreans and Chinese, whose presence resulted from colonial and wartime history (Chapters 79 and 94), formed the largest long-term foreign population for decades. From 1990, a revision of immigration law allowed people of Japanese descent from Brazil, Peru, and other countries (nikkeijin) to live and work in Japan, and their numbers grew. The Technical Intern Training Program, founded in 1993, officially transferred skills to workers from developing countries but functioned in practice as a source of labor for small manufacturers, farms, and construction firms. By the 2010s, foreign workers had become an increasingly important part of the labor force.

Students must distinguish several categories. Foreign residents are all people with legal residence status, including permanent residents, spouses, students, and workers; the number rose from about 2 million in 2012 to about 2.9 million in 2019. Foreign workers are the subset who are employed, whose number, reported by the labor ministry, rose from about 680,000 in 2012 to about 1.66 million in October 2019, and about 1.72 million in 2020. They included skilled professionals, people with family-based or ethnic-descent statuses who could work without restriction, foreign students working part-time, and technical interns. Interns numbered several hundred thousand by 2019 and came mainly from Vietnam, China, and other Asian countries; foreign students, many from Vietnam and Nepal, numbered over 300,000 and often worked in convenience stores, food delivery, and factories. Their employment was concentrated in manufacturing, construction, agriculture, food processing, retail, and services, and in regions with severe labor shortages.

The technical intern system attracted criticism from labor advocates, lawyers, journalists, and international observers. Interns paid large fees to brokers in their home countries, incurred debts, were tied to a single employer, and in some cases experienced unpaid wages, confiscation of passports, harassment, and unsafe conditions. Several thousand interns each year disappeared from their workplaces. A 2016 law, effective in 2017, created a supervisory organization, extended the maximum period to five years for qualified interns, and established penalties for abuse, but the structure of employer dependence remained. The system's formal justification, skills transfer, differed from its labor-supply function, and the government held that Japan did not have an "immigration policy."

The 2018 revision of the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act, passed in December 2018, created a new framework. The Specified Skilled Worker residence statuses began on April 1, 2019, along with the elevation of the Immigration Bureau to the Immigration Services Agency. Specified Skilled Worker (i) allowed foreign workers who passed skills and Japanese-language tests, or who had completed the technical intern program, to work for up to five years in designated sectors with labor shortages, initially fourteen, without bringing family. Specified Skilled Worker (ii), for more advanced skills, initially limited to a few sectors such as construction and shipbuilding, allowed renewable status and family accompaniment, which could lead to long-term residence. The government projected up to about 345,000 workers over five years but, because of slow testing, limited host-country arrangements, and the pandemic, only a few thousand had entered by the end of 2019 and about fifteen thousand by the end of 2020, many of them converted from interns.

Students should keep these statuses distinct from permanent residency, citizenship, refugee status, and the technical-intern system. Japan recognized very few refugees, a few dozen per year, despite thousands of applications, and naturalization remained limited. The problems of language access, workplace protection, recruitment practices, family separation, and employer dependence persisted, and local governments and civic groups, such as support centers for migrants, often took the lead in providing language classes and counseling. A 2016 law against hate speech, the first national law of its kind, addressed discriminatory demonstrations targeting Zainichi Koreans and other residents, though it contained no penalties. The section therefore shows a growing practical reliance on foreign labor combined with institutional constraints that limited migrants' rights and integration. Debates about national identity and Japan's demographic future are reserved for Chapter 100.

Section 99.19 · Division V

A New Generation of Civic Activism: Digital Organization, Local Movements, and Public Participation

The movements of the 2010s shared tools and, in some cases, participants, but not a single ideology. Volunteers after 3/11 built on the nonprofit sector that had grown since the 1998 NPO Law (Chapter 98). Antinuclear activists, constitutional protesters, Okinawan autonomy campaigners, labor advocates, migrant-support groups, and recovery organizations used demonstrations, public meetings, petitions, litigation, and digital communication. Lawyers played a visible role in suing over nuclear restarts, security laws, compensation, and migrant labor abuses. Local governments, including those of Okinawa and some nuclear-host or neighboring municipalities, became actors in national politics. Differences among these movements were as important as their similarities: Okinawan campaigns had a distinct history of regional grievance and identity, while the antinuclear and constitutional protests mobilized mainly urban, mixed-age and often first-time participants.

Social media changed how information moved. Twitter and Facebook, and the messaging application LINE, spread news of the disaster, coordinated volunteers, publicized demonstrations, and allowed citizens to document official decisions, such as the live-streaming of Diet protests or the sharing of radiation measurements. Online platforms gave women, younger citizens, and people outside major organizations a way to speak, as with the viral 2016 daycare post and the networks of mothers concerned about radiation or the security laws. They also created new forms of harassment, misinformation, and hostility: rumor about radiation and about evacuees circulated alongside accurate information, and activists, journalists, and minority groups, including Zainichi Koreans, were targeted by online abuse. Digital engagement was unequal by age and access, and it did not always translate into sustained organization or electoral power.

The period expanded the definition of civic responsibility. After 2011, volunteering was widely understood as a civic virtue; after 2012 and 2015, protest and petition came to be seen by many participants as a legitimate extension of that responsibility, including demands for transparency, participation, and accountability from government and corporations. Displaced residents, migrant workers, and Okinawans also asserted claims of their own. The limits were real. The LDP and its partners won every national election of the decade, many movements lacked institutional power, and participation across the political spectrum, including nationalist groups and organizations hostile to critics of the government, also grew. The most defensible conclusion is that civic activity was broader and more varied, not that it was uniformly progressive or uniformly effective.

Section 99.20 · Division V

From Fukushima to Reiwa: The Historical Meaning of Japan's Political Transformation, 2011–2020

The triple disaster revealed an interaction among physical vulnerability, energy infrastructure, institutional oversight, emergency administration, and reconstruction. The earthquake and tsunami were extreme physical events, but their consequences depended on the location of settlements, defenses, warning systems, and preparation. The nuclear accident combined a foreseeable hazard with weaknesses in design, management, and regulation. Reconstruction showed that physical rebuilding differs from the restoration of populations and communities, especially in a region already aging and shrinking. Nuclear policy settled into an uneasy compromise: a stricter regulator, slow restarts, growth in renewable power, continued dependence on fossil fuels, and a public that was skeptical but divided. Confidence in scientific expertise depended on institutional transparency and not simply on the quality of the science.

The decade's political developments followed their own logics but fit into a larger pattern. The LDP's return and Abe's long tenure produced a stronger executive, a National Security Council, and security legislation reinterpreting Article 9, with the Specially Designated Secrets Act, while opposition took the form of mass protest, litigation, scholarly criticism, and the resistance of Okinawan voters and officials, who kept the Henoko project contested. Labor and immigration policy responded to a shrinking workforce: the Work Style Reform introduced overtime caps and equal-treatment principles, and the Specified Skilled Worker system formalized reliance on foreign labor without declaring an immigration policy. Civic activism became more varied, with digital tools and new actors, but remained fragmented.

The transition from the Heisei to the Reiwa era is a chronological marker, not an automatic rupture. Emperor Akihito, who had addressed the nation by video on March 16, 2011 and who visited disaster areas with Empress Michiko, expressed in a 2016 broadcast his concern about continuing to fulfill his duties as he aged. A special law of 2017 allowed his abdication, the first in more than two centuries, which took place on April 30, 2019. His son Naruhito acceded on May 1, 2019, and the era name Reiwa began. The change generated public celebration and debate but did not alter the structure of politics or economy. In 2020 a new challenge appeared: the COVID-19 pandemic, whose first cases in Japan were confirmed in January, led to a state of emergency in April and to the postponement of the Tokyo Olympics, which had been promoted by the government as a symbol of recovery from 3/11. These events tested the same issues of crisis management, communication, and trust, but they belong to a new chapter of history.

The chapter's argument can now be stated precisely. During the 2010s, Japan's governments worked to strengthen administrative and security capacities, including executive coordination, regulatory reform, defense policy, labor-market adjustment, and immigration management. At the same time, citizens, scientists, local governments, courts, and media pressed for public accountability, local autonomy, scientific transparency, and participation. These tendencies were simultaneous and interacting. The record does not support the conclusion that Japan definitively overcame its institutional weaknesses, nor that it entered an irreversible crisis. It supports the view that the relationship between state and society was contested and in flux, which is a prelude to the broader questions of identity, memory, culture, international relations, and the future that Chapter 100 addresses.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Great East Japan Earthquake, tsunami, and Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident of March 2011 and the political and social history of Japan in the following decade. Its governing argument is that the triple disaster exposed vulnerabilities in infrastructure, nuclear-energy institutions, regulation, and political leadership, and that the 2010s saw simultaneous efforts to strengthen state administrative and security capacities and to expand demands for accountability, local autonomy, transparency, and participation. It also insists that not every political development of the decade was caused by Fukushima.

The earthquake of March 11, 2011, a magnitude-9.0 rupture on the Japan Trench subduction zone, produced intense shaking but relatively limited structural collapse in a country with strict building codes. The tsunami that followed caused the overwhelming majority of the roughly 15,900 deaths and about 2,500 missing. Its effects varied greatly with geography, from the funnel-shaped bays of the Sanriku coast to the broad Sendai Plain, and with preparation, warning, and decisions made in minutes, as cases such as Kamaishi, Ōkawa Elementary School, and Rikuzentakata show. The elderly, people with disabilities, and workers near the shore were disproportionately vulnerable.

The emergency response combined national and local effort. The Kan government's crisis headquarters, the largest deployment of Self-Defense Forces in history, municipal staff, fire volunteers, over a million volunteers, and foreign assistance, including the U.S. military's Operation Tomodachi, all contributed, and the response is better understood as an interdependence of state capacity and community action than as the work of either alone. Reconstruction, coordinated from 2012 by the Reconstruction Agency, rebuilt housing, transport, and coastal defenses on a large scale, but it could not by itself reverse depopulation, and it showed that physical, economic, and community recovery are different processes.

At Fukushima Daiichi, the earthquake cut external power and the tsunami destroyed backup power and cooling, leading to fuel damage in Units 1, 2, and 3, hydrogen explosions in the buildings of Units 1, 3, and 4, and large releases of radioactive material. Unit 4, whose reactor was not operating, was a building-level and spent-fuel-pool crisis. The accident developed within an institutional system in which promotion and regulation overlapped, safety assumptions treated prolonged station blackout as improbable, and earlier warnings were not acted upon. The evacuation of about 160,000 people at its peak led to a distinctive pattern of harm: disaster-related deaths in the thousands and prolonged displacement. The UNSCEAR assessment found no documented adverse health effects directly attributable to radiation exposure, a conclusion that does not negate the serious consequences of displacement and uncertainty.

Investigations by the government, TEPCO, a private commission, and the National Diet commission agreed on major failures of preparation and regulation while differing in emphasis and on contested points such as the role of the prime minister and possible earthquake damage. The creation of the Nuclear Regulation Authority in September 2012 was a significant attempt to separate regulation from promotion, and its standards slowed and sometimes prevented restarts. Energy policy afterward balanced safety, cost, imports, and climate goals without resolving them: nuclear generation fell nearly to zero, fossil imports rose, renewables grew under the feed-in tariff, and the first restart came in August 2015.

The anti-nuclear movement, with its demonstrations, citizen radiation measurement, local campaigns, and lawsuits, was among the largest protest cycles of the postwar decades and combined first-time participants with veteran activists. It did not prevent restarts or change the governing party, but it shaped regulation, public opinion, and a more skeptical public relationship with experts. Fukushima's long recovery, involving decontamination, compensation, and the lifting of evacuation orders from 2014, showed that formal reopening differs from return and that trust depends on institutional behavior.

Politically, the DPJ's defeat in December 2012 and Abe Shinzō's return ushered in the longest premiership in Japanese history, with a stronger executive, a National Security Council, the Specially Designated Secrets Act, and the July 2014 reinterpretation of Article 9 to permit limited collective self-defense, enacted in the 2015 security legislation. These measures were contested by constitutional scholars, opposition parties, and mass protests including SEALDs, though not reversed. The text of Article 9 remained unamended. In Okinawa, the Henoko dispute pitted prefectural governors Onaga and Tamaki and the 2019 referendum, in which about 72 percent of valid votes opposed the landfill, against a national government that held legal authority to continue.

Work and migration changed in response to labor shortages. The Work Style Reform of 2018 introduced overtime limits and equal-treatment principles, implemented in stages from 2019, without eliminating the dual structure of regular and nonregular employment. The 2018 immigration revision created the Specified Skilled Worker statuses in April 2019, formalizing reliance on foreign labor while leaving problems in the technical intern system and the absence of an official immigration policy. Civic activism broadened through digital tools, local movements, and litigation, but remained fragmented.

The chapter concludes that Japan in 2011–2020 should be understood neither as having definitively overcome its institutional weaknesses nor as having entered an irreversible crisis. The Heisei–Reiwa transition on May 1, 2019 and the arrival of COVID-19 in 2020 were markers, not ruptures. The period's central historical significance is the contested renegotiation of the relationship among citizens, state institutions, security, economic interests, and public accountability, a theme that Chapter 100 carries into its assessment of identity, memory, culture, and the future.

The UN committee whose 2020/2021 assessment found no documented radiation-attributable health effects among residents.
Fukushima Health Management Survey
The prefectural program of dose assessment, health checks, and thyroid screening for residents.
Feed-in tariff
A policy guaranteeing prices for renewable electricity, begun in Japan in July 2012.
Sendai restart
The August 11, 2015 restart of Sendai Unit 1, the first under the new regulatory standards.
Abenomics
The economic program of the second Abe government combining monetary easing, fiscal stimulus, and structural reform.
Collective self-defense
Use of force to defend an ally under attack; limited forms were permitted by the July 1, 2014 Cabinet decision.
Peace and Security Legislation (2015)
The package of laws implementing the 2014 decision, approved September 2015 and in force March 2016.
Specially Designated Secrets Act
The law, effective December 10, 2014, allowing designation and protection of certain defense and security information.
SEALDs
The student movement of 2015–2016 that protested the security legislation.
Futenma and Henoko
The U.S. Marine air station in Ginowan and the planned replacement facility in Nago, Okinawa.
All Okinawa
A cross-party current opposing the Henoko project that supported governors Onaga and Tamaki.
2019 Okinawan referendum
The February 24, 2019 prefectural vote in which about 72.15% of valid votes opposed the landfill; not legally binding.

The 2018 legislation introducing overtime limits and equal-treatment principles, implemented in stages from April 2019.
Karōshi
Death from overwork, a policy and legal concern in the 2010s.
Technical Intern Training Program
The 1993 program that in practice supplied labor to small firms, criticized for abuse and employer dependence.
Specified Skilled Worker
Residence statuses begun April 1, 2019 for designated labor-shortage sectors, with limits on duration and family accompaniment for the first category.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a natural catastrophe became a test of institutions. The earthquake and tsunami were not preventable, but the scale of loss and the course of the nuclear accident depended on human decisions about where to build, how to regulate, how to warn, and how to prepare. Understanding 3/11 as a compound disaster avoids both fatalism and simple blame, and it shows why careful distinctions among kinds of death, kinds of evidence, and kinds of responsibility are essential to historical writing about recent events.

The chapter also reveals a decade in which the relationship between state and citizens was actively renegotiated. The government strengthened executive coordination and security capacity, and the public pressed for transparency, participation, and local self-government. Neither tendency cancelled the other, and neither can be reduced to a single cause. The Okinawan struggle over Henoko, the debates over Article 9, and the new reliance on foreign labor each show how long-standing structures, from the alliance to the dual labor market, interacted with new pressures.

The developments examined here shaped what followed. They informed energy policy debates that continued after 2020, the memory and litigation of the accident, the political position of Okinawa, and the practices of protest and online organization. They also connect Japan to a wider world through the experience of foreign responders, international scientific bodies, the alliance, and migrant workers. The northeast, Okinawa, and the foreign communities of Japan's cities appear here as historical actors rather than as peripheries.

Students who understand the 2010s as a period of contested change, neither a restoration nor a collapse, are prepared for Chapter 100, which turns to the broader questions of identity, memory, culture, international relations, and the future that the events of 2011–2020 helped to frame.
