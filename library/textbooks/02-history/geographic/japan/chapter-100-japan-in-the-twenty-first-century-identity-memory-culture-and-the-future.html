Japan in the Twenty-First Century: Identity, Memory, Culture, and the Future

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the morning of July 8, 2022, a former prime minister stood on a street corner in the city of Nara, in the old heartland of the Yamato court, speaking in support of a candidate in an upper-house election. He was shot and died the same day. In a country with some of the lowest rates of gun violence in the world, the event was a national shock; its aftermath would reopen questions about religion, money, political power, and accountability that Japanese society had not settled.

The man was Abe Shinzō, the longest-serving prime minister in Japanese history, and the place was not arbitrary for a textbook that began in the Jōmon archipelago and has now reached its hundredth chapter. Nara was a capital in the eighth century (Chapter 14). Its streets lie close to the great keyhole tombs and the sites where Chapters 7 and 8 traced the emergence of Yamato rule. The event of 2022 was entirely contemporary: a man with a homemade firearm and a grievance about his family's finances. But the debates that followed about legitimacy, religion, the state, and public memory belong to long histories this book has followed.

This chapter has two responsibilities. The first is to explain Japan in the twenty-first century on its own terms: its political controversies, its demographic transformation, its changing understandings of family and gender, its recognition and misrecognition of minorities, its disputes over the imperial and wartime past, its extraordinary cultural reach, and its relations with China, the Koreas, the United States, and the wider Indo-Pacific. The second is to conclude the textbook. A final chapter cannot merely append another set of recent events; it must ask what the preceding ninety-nine chapters add up to, and which of the patterns they described persist, mutate, or disappear. Section 100.20 takes up that task directly.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines political leadership and social transformation: the Abe legacy and its violent end, the Unification Church controversy, population change, and debates over gender and family. Division II turns to identity and memory: the Ainu, the other communities that make Japan plural, the politics of war memory, and the commemorations of Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and the eightieth anniversary of 1945. Division III studies cultural globalization through manga, anime, games, music, film, literature, contemporary art, and fashion. Division IV examines Japan's international relationships. Division V considers heritage, technology, competing visions of the future, and the concluding synthesis.

Students should understand at the outset the limits of writing about the present. The narrative here runs through November 2026, and the date of this edition is November 13, 2026. Events of the 2020s have been documented in government records, court judgments, statistical releases, and the press, but they have not yet been examined with the distance, the archives, and the scholarly controversy that characterize earlier periods. Some developments described below, such as court decisions, are completed. Others, such as legislation, diplomatic disputes, and economic conditions, are ongoing and may have changed by the time a student reads this. Projections and scenarios are labeled as such. Wherever the chapter gives a figure, it is an official or institutional estimate, identified by source, and should be checked against the latest release.

The evidence is correspondingly different from that of earlier chapters. It includes cabinet records and prime ministerial statements, judgments of the Tokyo District Court, Tokyo High Court, and Supreme Court, vital statistics from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, census releases from the Statistics Bureau, industry reports from bodies such as the Association of Japanese Animations, UNESCO records, strategy documents, OECD assessments, survivors' testimony, and the work of journalists and scholars. Each has an author and an interest. Government documents establish what the government decided and declared, not whether those declarations were accurate or adequate. Court judgments establish legal findings under particular statutes, not the whole truth about an organization. Industry statistics are produced by industries that want to appear successful.

Two assumptions that have recurred in this textbook's warnings must be named again. One is the assumption that Japan is a homogeneous, timeless nation. Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 introduced a different picture: an archipelago populated through multiple movements, in which "the Japanese people" was a later formation. Chapters on the Emishi, the Ainu, Ryukyu, the Buraku, Zainichi Koreans, colonial subjects, and migrants have kept the plurality visible. The other is the assumption of either exceptional success or inevitable decline. Japan in 2026 is wealthy, aging, technologically capable, politically contested, culturally influential, and strategically anxious, all at once. Neither a story of decline nor a story of triumph describes it adequately.

The chapter's governing question is this: how has Japan's long historical development shaped its contemporary identity, culture, institutions, and international relationships, and how are the Japanese people responding to the challenges and opportunities of the twenty-first century? The answer is not a conclusion that can be stated in a sentence, and the chapter does not offer one in the opening. It builds toward it through twenty sections and returns to it in the last.

Edition note: This chapter's contemporary account is current to November 13, 2026; the OECD International Migration Outlook dated November 12, 2026 is within that cutoff, while scheduled events after November 13 are not narrated as completed. Court decisions and election results described here are completed events; legislation, diplomatic disputes, economic conditions, and demographic projections may have changed. Where figures come from preliminary releases, the text says so.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the political legacy of Abe Shinzō and the significance of his assassination and its aftermath, distinguishing documented events from partisan assessment.
  2. Explain the legal basis, procedure, and limits of the dissolution of the Unification Church's religious-corporation status, and distinguish it from the history of Aum Shinrikyō.
  3. Analyze demographic change in the 2020s using census data, vital statistics, and projections, distinguishing population size from productivity and living standards.
  4. Assess the legal and social state of gender, family, and LGBTQ debates, distinguishing legislation, court rulings, local policy, and attitudes.
  5. Interpret the 2019 Ainu Policy Promotion Act and Upopoy, and distinguish cultural recognition from the redress of historical and contemporary inequality.
  6. Compare the distinct histories of Okinawan, Zainichi Korean, Buraku, and migrant communities without treating them as one category.
  7. Distinguish academic research, official positions, testimony, and political rhetoric in disputes over imperial and wartime history and in the commemorations of 1945.
  8. Explain how anime, manga, games, music, film, literature, art, and fashion became globally influential, and evaluate claims about Cool Japan and soft power.
  9. Analyze Japan's relationships with China, the Koreas, the United States, and the wider Indo-Pacific as products of security, economic, cultural, and memory-related forces.
  10. Evaluate heritage and tourism, technological change, and competing scenarios for Japan's future, separating demonstrated capability from projection.
  11. Synthesize Japan's history from the Jōmon to the Reiwa era around persistent relationships such as center and periphery, hierarchy and reform, and exchange and identity.
  12. Evaluate primary sources, statistics, and interpretations of very recent events for their creators, purposes, and limits.

Historical Context

A student reaching Chapter 100 carries ninety-nine chapters of background, and this section selects only what contemporary developments depend on. The archipelago was settled in several waves and by several routes (Chapter 2). Jōmon foragers, Yayoi farmers, and the chiefdoms of the Kofun age (Chapters 3 to 7) left populations and traditions that survive in the diversity of Japan's regions, including the Ainu of the north and the peoples of the Ryukyu Islands in the south, who developed in distinct ways (Chapters 16, 32, 33). The Yamato court, the ritsuryō state, and the imperial institution (Chapters 8 to 13) established a form of sovereignty that persisted, in changing roles, until the twenty-first century. The reigning emperor, Naruhito, who acceded in 2019 at the start of the Reiwa era, occupies a constitutional position defined in 1947 (Chapter 84) very different from that of any earlier tennō.

Warrior government, from the Kamakura bakufu through the Tokugawa (Chapters 24 to 50), established a pattern in which political authority was shared among courts, shogunates, domains, temples, and villages, and in which regional communities retained distinct economies and identities. The Meiji Restoration (Chapter 53) centralized the state, created prefectures, a conscript army, a constitutional monarchy, and a modern education system, and also annexed Hokkaido and Okinawa and acquired Taiwan and Korea (Chapters 54 to 62). The empire that resulted, its wars, and its collapse in 1945 (Chapters 75 to 81) remain central to how contemporary Japanese and their neighbors understand one another. The occupation (Chapters 82 to 90) produced the 1947 Constitution with its Article 9, the U.S.–Japan security treaty, and the political settlement under which the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) dominated governance from 1955 (Chapter 92).

The economic miracle and bubble (Chapters 91 and 96) gave Japan a wealthy industrial society and a global reputation for manufacturing. The collapse of the asset bubble in 1990 and the long stagnation that followed (Chapter 97) brought irregular employment, banking crisis, falling fertility, and population aging. The earthquake and sarin attack of 1995 (Chapter 98) and the triple disaster of 2011 (Chapter 99) tested institutions and trust. Abe's second premiership began in December 2012, and his security legislation and economic program were treated in Chapter 99. This chapter picks up the story after Abe's departure from office in 2020. It also draws on the chapters on culture (Chapters 46, 49, 65, 70, 88, and 95), whose earlier history of kabuki, ukiyo-e, cinema, jazz, manga, and anime is the foundation for Division III.

Three features of the recent past frame everything below. First, population decline began in earnest around 2008, when Japan's population peaked at roughly 128 million, and has since accelerated. Second, the political order, though it has produced a large number of prime ministers, has been remarkably stable at the level of party control: the LDP has governed for nearly all of the period since 1955, with brief interruptions in 1993 to 1994 and 2009 to 2012. Third, the regional security environment has deteriorated, with China's military growth, North Korean missile and nuclear programs, and Russia's war against Ukraine from 2022 reshaping Japanese strategic debate. The COVID-19 pandemic, which led to the postponement of the Tokyo Olympics from 2020 to 2021, added another shock whose social effects are still being studied.

Section 100.1 · Division I — Political Leadership and Social Transformation

The Abe Era, Its Political Legacy, and the Assassination of 2022

Abe Shinzō governed twice. His first premiership (2006 to 2007) ended after about a year amid scandal and illness. His second, from December 2012 to September 2020, became the longest continuous tenure in the history of the office, a fact that mattered precisely because it was unusual: Japan had had seven prime ministers in the six years before 2012. Chapter 99 narrated the major measures of those years. The question here is how historians are beginning to assess the era as a whole. Its components were an economic program, "Abenomics," built on monetary easing, fiscal flexibility, and structural reform; a strengthening of the prime minister's office, aided by the 1990s electoral and administrative reforms and by the creation of the National Security Council in 2013 and a cabinet personnel bureau in 2014; a security agenda that included the 2014 reinterpretation of collective self-defense and the 2015 legislation; and a persistent but unrealized ambition to revise the Constitution.

Assessments diverge, and they diverge on evidence. Supporters point to political stability, an improved employment rate (including higher female labor-force participation), a more active diplomacy, including the concept of a "free and open Indo-Pacific," and a strengthened security posture. Critics point to the failure to reach the 2 percent inflation target on a sustained basis in his tenure, the limited growth of real wages, the concentration of power in the prime minister's office, and a series of accountability controversies, among them the Moritomo Gakuen and Kake Gakuen land and veterinary-school affairs and the cherry-blossom-viewing party scandal, in which officials' handling of records and testimony drew criticism. Neither list settles the matter. Whether Abenomics improved Japan's long-term prospects, or only extended an easy-money period while leaving structural questions unresolved, remains a subject of economic and political debate.

Leadership after Abe was marked by turnover within continuity. Abe left office in 2020, citing illness. Suga Yoshihide, his chief cabinet secretary, served from September 2020 to October 2021, a tenure dominated by the pandemic and the delayed Tokyo Olympics. Kishida Fumio succeeded him and served until October 2024; his government oversaw the December 2022 security strategy, a budgetary commitment to defense, and a response to a scandal over unreported political-fund revenues in LDP factions, among them the faction once led by Abe, that damaged public trust. Ishiba Shigeru, elected party leader in 2024, led a government that lost its lower-house majority in October 2024, and the ruling bloc also lost its upper-house majority in 2025; he resigned that year. The pattern should be stated carefully. Individual leaders changed frequently, and the party's grip on parliament weakened in some elections, but the LDP remained the central organizing force of Japanese government, and no opposition party consolidated an alternative.

On July 8, 2022, while Abe was speaking at a campaign event outside Yamato-Saidaiji station in Nara in support of an LDP candidate for the House of Councillors, he was shot twice by a man using a homemade firearm. He died of his wounds that day. The suspect, Yamagami Tetsuya, was arrested at the scene. The national response combined shock, mourning, and immediate debate over security for political figures. The police acknowledged shortcomings in the protection arrangements, and the National Police Agency undertook a review. The LDP won the election that followed. Yamagami was sentenced to life imprisonment by the Nara District Court on January 21, 2026; his lawyers filed an appeal to the Osaka High Court on February 4, so the district-court judgment should not be treated as the final appellate disposition as of this edition. Students should note the distinction between the court's judgment of an individual crime and the political questions the case raised: the assailant's stated motive was a grievance against a religious organization, which Section 100.2 examines.

The government's decision to hold a state funeral for Abe, on September 27, 2022, was itself controversial. There was no statute specifically authorizing state funerals for former prime ministers, and the funeral for a politician who had been divisive provoked protests, litigation, and polling that showed public opinion divided. Supporters argued that the length of his service and his diplomatic standing justified the honor, and foreign dignitaries attended in large numbers. Critics argued that it lacked a clear legal basis, bypassed the Diet, and was an honor imposed on a divided public. Historians will use the episode to study how the state distributes recognition, and how the memory of a figure so recently active is shaped by the people who govern in his name.

Abe's political heirs did not abandon his agenda. Takaichi Sanae, a long-time ally who had held ministerial posts under him, won the LDP presidency in October 2025 after Ishiba's resignation, and on October 21, 2025, was appointed prime minister, the first woman to hold the office. The LDP had lost its coalition partner, Kōmeitō, and secured the support of the Japan Innovation Party (Ishin). In early 2026 she called a snap election; the ruling party won a large majority in the lower house, and she was reappointed prime minister in February 2026. Her appointment was a genuine historical milestone in a country where women remain underrepresented in elected office, and the next sections return to its meaning. Her policies, including a security buildup, a fiscal stance she calls "responsible proactive," and a hard line in some foreign-policy matters, must be assessed as policies, separately from the symbolism of her gender. How a democratic society should assess the legacy of a powerful leader whose influence persists after his death is a question the Abe case poses without answering. A reasonable approach is to separate three things: documented decisions and their consequences, the controversies that surrounded them, and the way successors use his memory.

Section 100.2 · Division I

The Unification Church Controversy: Religion, Political Influence, and Public Accountability

The Unification Church, which now uses the name Family Federation for World Peace and Unification, was founded in Korea in 1954 by Moon Sun Myung and established in Japan in the 1960s. It grew in the 1970s and 1980s and developed contacts with conservative Japanese politicians, partly through anti-communist organizations. In the same period it drew criticism for recruitment practices and for "spiritual sales," in which followers were pressed to buy objects at inflated prices to remove ancestral or spiritual afflictions. A network of lawyers began assisting families who said they had been financially ruined. Their work produced civil judgments but did not result in sustained political or regulatory action for decades.

The assassination of 2022 changed the situation. Yamagami told investigators that his mother had given the church large sums, leaving the family bankrupt, and that he blamed the church and believed Abe had links to it. Whatever the connection between Abe and the church in fact was, and Abe had sent a video message to an affiliated organization's event, the revelation that a former prime minister's killer targeted him because of a religious organization led the press, the public, and the political parties to examine the church's ties with politicians. LDP surveys found that well over a hundred of its Diet members had had some contact with the organization or its affiliates. The extent and meaning of those contacts varied widely, from attendance at events to electoral help, and historians should distinguish them rather than treating them as equivalent.

The government responded in several ways. It established a consultation system for victims. In December 2022 the Diet passed legislation to prevent wrongful solicitation of donations. And the education ministry, which supervises religious corporations, exercised its power of inquiry under the Religious Corporations Act (Shūkyō Hōjin Hō). The government stated that civil torts, and not only crimes, could count as "violations of laws and regulations" that harm public welfare, a reinterpretation that opened the way to a dissolution petition. In October 2023 the ministry petitioned the court. The Tokyo District Court ordered the dissolution in March 2025. The Tokyo High Court upheld the order on March 4, 2026, and the order took effect, beginning liquidation proceedings. The Supreme Court rejected the church's appeal in a ruling dated June 22, 2026, reported on June 23. The courts found that the church's donation solicitation, over decades, had caused large financial and psychological harm; the High Court estimated the damages between 1973 and 2016 at about 7.4 billion yen. The decision was the first time Japan's top court finalized the dissolution of a religious corporation on the basis of civil torts rather than criminal conduct.

The legal distinctions are essential. A dissolution order removes the organization's status as a religious corporation, with its tax advantages and its legal personality for holding property, and begins liquidation of its assets. The court stated that it does not itself prohibit belief, worship, or voluntary religious gatherings, and that any practical impediments to members' religious activity are indirect effects rather than legal prohibitions. Believers remain free to practice. Critics, including some civil-liberties and religious-freedom advocates, nevertheless argue that the reinterpretation of the statute and the speed of the process set a precedent that could be used against disfavored religions, and that the court should have weighed religious-freedom effects more heavily. Supporters answer that the harm to victims was documented over many years and that the law's purpose is public welfare. The student should hold both positions in view and note that the courts examined the evidence of harm and ruled on it.

The controversy is distinct from that of Aum Shinrikyō (Chapter 98). Aum carried out mass murder and was dissolved after criminal prosecution of its leaders. The Unification Church case involved no comparable violence by the organization, and the legal basis was civil liability for a pattern of exploitative fundraising. The two cases have different histories, different misconduct, and different procedures. They do share an underlying question: what can a state that guarantees freedom of religion do about an organization that harms people? The answer in 2026 was that it could dissolve the corporation while leaving individual belief alone, and that the question of political accountability, meaning what politicians knew, accepted, or gained from such organizations, remained incompletely answered by the courts, which were not asked to decide it.

Section 100.3 · Division I

Demographic Adaptation: Population Decline, Aging, Regional Survival, and Immigration

Chapter 97 explained the origins of fertility decline and aging. The 2020s bring the consequences into focus. Japan's population peaked around 2008 at roughly 128 million and has fallen since. The finalized vital statistics for 2025 recorded 671,270 births and a total fertility rate of 1.14, well below the level of about 2.1 needed to sustain a population without migration. The May 29, 2026 preliminary release of the 2025 census counted approximately 123.05 million residents, with decreases in 1,558 of 1,719 municipalities (90.6 percent); subsequent 2026 basic-tabulation releases should be consulted separately rather than treated as identical to the preliminary series. At the other end of life, Japan has among the world's highest life expectancies, and the share of the population aged sixty-five and over exceeds a quarter. The combined effect is a society with fewer children, fewer working-age adults, and more elderly people, whose needs fall on families, municipalities, and social insurance.

The effects differ by place. Metropolitan areas, above all Tokyo, continue to attract young people for education and employment, so that depopulation is steepest in rural districts and on remote islands. Some municipalities now have too few residents to maintain schools, clinics, bus services, or volunteer fire brigades. Researchers have used the term genkai shūraku, "marginal settlements," for villages in which most residents are elderly and communal functions are at risk; the concept has been criticized as fatalistic, and some communities have disproved forecasts through in-migration. Vacant houses (akiya) are a visible consequence. Schools merge or close, and hospitals struggle to staff obstetrics and pediatrics. The population is also unevenly distributed across generations, so decisions about pensions and health insurance involve a transfer from a smaller working population to a larger retired one.

Policy responses have multiplied. The government has expanded childcare provision, child allowances, and parental-leave incentives, and has framed fertility decline as a national priority. It has encouraged longer working lives, raising the proportion of older people in employment, and has promoted regional revitalization through incentives for relocation and remote work. Municipalities have experimented with subsidies for young families, shared transport, and consolidation of services. The results are contested. Evidence from other wealthy countries suggests that cash incentives have modest effects on fertility when the costs of housing, education, and long work hours remain high, and Japan's own history of falling fertility spans decades of varied policy. Marriage is a major factor, since childbearing outside marriage is uncommon in Japan, and marriage rates have fallen with later and less secure entry into stable employment (Chapter 97).

Immigration is the most politically sensitive option. Japan has long resisted describing itself as a country of immigration, yet the foreign-resident population has grown, and the OECD’s November 2026 International Migration Outlook described a 2025 foreign population of approximately 3.8 million under its comparative statistical definition. The Immigration Services Agency’s administrative residence register counted 4,125,395 at the end of December 2025 and 4,281,026 at June 30, 2026, including mid-to-long-term and special permanent residents but excluding short-term visitors. These are different statistical populations and observation dates, not interchangeable totals. Programs created since 1990, including the trainee system, the Specified Skilled Worker visas introduced in 2019, and a reformed scheme to replace the technical-intern program that has been criticized for abuses, have drawn workers from Vietnam, the Philippines, Indonesia, Nepal, and other countries into care, agriculture, construction, and manufacturing. Section 100.6 treats these communities as members of society and not only as labor supply. The question of scale is political: Takaichi's government, like others, has debated controls on foreigners alongside labor needs.

Population decline should not be read as a predetermined catastrophe. A smaller population is not by itself a poorer one: living standards depend on productivity, on how the elderly are supported, and on institutions. Projections, such as those of the National Institute of Population and Social Security Research, depend on assumptions about fertility and migration and have been revised repeatedly. What is certain is the age structure of people already born. What is uncertain is whether automation, higher participation by women and older people, immigration, and institutional change will offset the shrinkage of the workforce. The historian's task is to examine the choices, and the interests and regions affected by them.

Section 100.4 · Division I

Gender, Family, LGBTQ Rights, and the Changing Meaning of Equality

Women's educational attainment in Japan is high: women accounted for 46.1 percent of undergraduate university students in the final 2025 MEXT School Basic Survey, while female labor-force participation has risen substantially since the 2010s. The undergraduate enrollment share should not be confused with the share of entrants across all forms of tertiary education. Yet the gap between formal equality and outcomes remains wide. Women hold a small share of senior management posts and of seats in the Diet, and a large proportion of women workers are in irregular employment (Chapter 97), with lower pay and weaker security. Unpaid care of children and elderly relatives falls disproportionately on women. International indices of gender equality rank Japan well below its peers among wealthy countries; in the World Economic Forum's index for 2025 it ranked 118th of 148 economies (score 0.666); in the separately constituted 2026 edition it ranked 117th of 145 (score 0.670), with political representation a persistent weakness. The differing numbers of economies mean the ranks are not a perfectly constant-sample comparison. Indices are constructed with particular weightings and should be read critically, but the pattern they show is consistent with other data.

The appointment of Takaichi Sanae as prime minister on October 21, 2025, was therefore a historic moment. It does not by itself indicate a transformation of gender relations. Takaichi rose within the conservative wing of the LDP, has opposed some reforms favored by feminist advocates, such as allowing married couples to keep separate surnames, and her cabinet's composition and policies are matters of ordinary political assessment. Her election showed that a woman could win the highest office in the governing party and that voters accepted it, while the number of women in the Diet remains low. The historian's distinction is between symbolic breakthroughs and institutional change.

Family law is a central arena. The Civil Code requires married couples to share a single surname, and in practice about ninety-five percent of couples adopt the husband's. Supreme Court rulings in 2015 and 2021 upheld the rule while leaving the matter to the Diet, and business groups and many local governments have since urged change. In 2024 the Diet passed legislation introducing the option of joint custody after divorce, a reform of the earlier sole-custody system, which took effect on April 1, 2026. The amendment permits courts and parents, subject to the child's welfare and applicable safeguards, to choose joint or sole parental authority after divorce; it does not automatically require equal time in each parent's home. Meanwhile, the family itself has changed: single-person households are the most common household type, marriage is later, and more people never marry. The model of the male breadwinner and full-time homemaker, which shaped postwar institutions (Chapter 91), describes a diminishing share of households.

The debate over sexual orientation and gender identity has moved from the margins to the courts. Municipalities and prefectures have introduced partnership-recognition systems since Shibuya and Setagaya in 2015, and by the mid-2020s a large majority of the population lived in areas covered by one. They provide certificates that can help with housing or hospital visitation but do not confer the legal rights of marriage, such as inheritance. In 2023 the Diet passed a law promoting public understanding of sexual orientation and gender diversity; it set out principles of awareness and education but did not prohibit discrimination, did not legalize same-sex marriage, and was criticized by activists as weak and by some conservatives as going too far. The same year the Supreme Court held that a requirement for surgical sterilization as a condition of legal gender change was unconstitutional.

Marriage equality is being decided through litigation. From 2021, plaintiffs in several district courts argued that excluding same-sex couples from marriage violates the Constitution's guarantees of equality and of marriage based on mutual consent. District courts delivered mixed judgments, with several finding the situation unconstitutional or in a state of unconstitutionality, and five high-court panels—in Sapporo (March 2024), the first Tokyo case (October 2024), Fukuoka (December 2024), Nagoya (March 2025), and Osaka (March 2025)—found the exclusion unconstitutional on overlapping but not identical grounds. A separate Tokyo High Court panel ruled the exclusion constitutional on November 28, 2025. The cases were headed toward or under Supreme Court review as of the edition cutoff, with no general judicial legalization of same-sex marriage. Students must treat each judgment separately, noting its court, date, reasoning, and whether it ordered a remedy, since none created a right to marry. Public-opinion polls have shown majority support for same-sex marriage, especially among younger respondents, but the government has resisted legislating, and opposition within parts of the LDP has been strong.

The section's conclusion is that contemporary Japanese gender and family debates concern the allocation of rights, economic responsibilities, and social recognition, and not only personal preference. Who earns, who cares, whose surname is used, who can marry, and who is recognized as a family are questions of law and economics, and they connect to the demographic questions of Section 100.3.

Section 100.5 · Division II — Identity, Minority Recognition, and Historical Memory

The Ainu in Contemporary Japan: Indigenous Recognition, Cultural Revival, and Historical Justice

The Ainu history traced across this textbook is one of long-standing trade and conflict with Wajin communities, the expansion of the Matsumae domain, the Shakushain war of 1669, the Kunashiri–Menashi uprising of 1789, and, after 1869, colonization of Hokkaido by the Meiji state, which classed the Ainu as "former aborigines," allotted them farmland, restricted their hunting and fishing, and imposed Japanese-language schooling (Chapters 48 and 54). The Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899 remained in force until 1997. By then activists, including those described in Chapter 94, had challenged assimilation, and a 1997 court ruling in the Nibutani dam case acknowledged the Ainu as an indigenous minority whose cultural interests had been neglected, while the Cultural Promotion Act of 1997 replaced the 1899 statute with a culture-focused framework.

The twenty-first-century movement has built on this foundation. In 2008 the Diet passed a resolution calling on the government to recognize the Ainu as an indigenous people of the northern archipelago. The Ainu Policy Promotion Act of 2019, which came into force that year, was the first Japanese statute to describe the Ainu explicitly as an Indigenous people. It provides for national and local measures to promote Ainu culture, to support regional economies, and to abolish discrimination, including grants to municipalities that formulate plans. A Hokkaido government survey in 2017 counted roughly 13,000 Ainu in Hokkaido, a figure widely regarded as an undercount because many people do not disclose their identity, and many Ainu live outside Hokkaido, notably in the Tokyo region.

Upopoy, the National Ainu Museum and Park in Shiraoi, opened on July 12, 2020. The name is an Ainu word describing singing together. Its museum presents the history, language, and culture of the Ainu from the perspective of Ainu people, and the park houses a reconstructed village, craft workshops, and performance spaces. The opening came at the start of the pandemic, limiting initial attendance. The institution is also a center for a broader project of cultural revival: language classes (the Ainu language is classified by UNESCO as critically endangered), the transmission of oral epics (yukar), the revival of embroidery, woodcarving, and the mukkuri mouth harp, and new work by Ainu musicians and artists. Popular culture, including the manga and film Golden Kamuy, has stimulated public interest, with mixed responses among Ainu people, some of whom welcomed it as visibility and others of whom criticized its representations.

The tensions are substantial. Critics note that the 2019 law recognizes Indigenous status but does not establish rights to land, resources, or self-determination of the kind in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, which Japan endorsed in 2007. Fishing is a flashpoint: Ainu claims to a right to catch salmon for ceremonial and subsistence purposes have clashed with prefectural fishing regulations, and litigation has not produced the recognition claimants sought. The return of human remains collected by universities in the early twentieth century for anthropological research has been a long struggle, with remains gradually moved toward a memorial facility at Upopoy and returned to some communities. Tourism raises further questions: when Ainu culture is a cultural-tourism product, who controls its presentation, who earns income, and how are complex living communities represented to visitors? Ainu people disagree among themselves about these questions, and the community is not uniform.

The central question is whether recognizing a culture addresses the historical and contemporary inequalities experienced by its people. Evidence from surveys has shown gaps in higher-education attendance and income between Ainu and others in Hokkaido, and experience of discrimination in marriage and employment. Recognition and a museum are real achievements and many Ainu people value them. They do not, by themselves, restore land, redress dispossession, or ensure that Ainu communities control decisions affecting them. Upopoy is evidence of change and a limit on change at once.

Historical Vocabulary
National Identity, Pluralism, Indigenous Recognition, Multiculturalism, Citizenship

National identity is a historically constructed sense of belonging to a nation; as Chapter 2 emphasized, the category "Japanese" is a later formation and not a timeless inheritance. Pluralism describes a society recognizing multiple cultural, ethnic, or religious communities. Indigenous people is a term in international usage for communities with ancestral ties to a territory that predate a state's expansion and that retain distinct cultural institutions; in Japan, the Ainu are officially recognized as such, while Okinawans are not formally classified in this way despite some UN bodies' recommendations. Multiculturalism in Japanese official usage is tabunka kyōsei, "multicultural coexistence," a policy idea emphasizing cooperation and local services, not a legal regime of group rights. Citizenship in Japan is defined by nationality, acquired principally by descent, and dual nationality is not generally permitted; residents without nationality, including long-settled Zainichi Koreans, therefore hold different legal statuses from citizens.

These terms matter because the same community can be described in different ways, and the choice of term has legal and political consequences.

Section 100.6 · Division II

Multicultural Japan: Okinawan, Zainichi Korean, Buraku, and Migrant Identities

The image of Japan as an ethnically homogeneous nation has a history. It was strengthened in the postwar period when the loss of the empire reduced the number of citizens of colonial origin and when much public discourse emphasized national uniformity. It never accurately described the archipelago, as the earlier chapters have shown. Contemporary Japan includes several categories of people who are not interchangeable, and this section keeps their different histories apart.

Okinawans and other Ryukyuan peoples are citizens, with a history as the independent Ryukyu Kingdom (Chapters 32 and 33), annexation in 1879, the devastation of 1945 (Chapter 80), and American administration until reversion in 1972 (Chapter 90). Okinawa Prefecture contains less than one percent of Japan's land but roughly seventy percent of the exclusive-use area of U.S. military facilities in Japan, and issues such as the relocation of Marine Corps Air Station Futenma to Henoko, crimes by service members, noise, and environmental contamination have repeatedly set the prefectural government and many residents against Tokyo. Okinawan identity is itself plural: it includes an Okinawan self-identification within Japan, a distinct cultural revival including languages of the Ryukyuan family, which UNESCO classifies as endangered, and, for some, advocacy of greater autonomy or independence, a minority position. In diplomatic and security debates, Okinawa is a place where the choices described in Section 100.15 are most directly felt.

Zainichi Koreans descend largely from people who came to Japan, or were brought, during the colonial era (Chapters 62 and 79), and who lost Japanese nationality in 1952 (Chapter 90). Many held the status of special permanent resident, created in 1991. Their number has declined through naturalization, marriage, and demographic change to a few hundred thousand, and their identities range from loyalty to South Korean or North Korean institutions to a self-understanding as long-term residents of Japan with no strong tie to either state. They have faced discrimination in employment, marriage, and housing. Hate speech directed at them led to a 2016 law against it, which has no criminal penalties but has been used by local governments to restrain demonstrations. Since the 2000s, the circulation of South Korean popular culture in Japan, discussed in Section 100.14, has coexisted with continued prejudice.

Buraku communities, descended from groups classed as outcaste in the Tokugawa system (Chapter 43), were legally emancipated in 1871, yet continued to face discrimination in marriage and employment, a pattern addressed by the Suiheisha (Chapter 69) and by postwar liberation movements (Chapter 94). Special measures from 1969 to 2002 improved housing and infrastructure in designated districts. A 2016 law committed the government to promote the elimination of Buraku discrimination, which persists in the form of background checks and lists of Buraku districts circulated online. The issue is distinct from migrant status or ethnicity, since Burakumin are not an immigrant group and are not distinguishable by physical features; their stigmatization depends on the identification of ancestry and place of origin.

Recent migrants form the fastest-growing category. The OECD’s November 2026 comparison described a 2025 foreign population of about 3.8 million; the Immigration Services Agency’s own registration series counted 4,125,395 foreign residents at year-end 2025 and 4,281,026 at June 30, 2026. The latter includes mid-to-long-term residents and special permanent residents and excludes short-term visitors. Chinese, Vietnamese, Koreans, Filipinos, Nepalis, and Brazilians, among others, appear in the register. Its total must also be distinguished from foreign workers (an overlapping subset, not the whole register), international students (a residence-status category), and short-term visitor arrivals (counted separately). Local governments, not the national government, have taken the lead in providing language classes, interpretation, and school support; the notion of tabunka kyōsei, "multicultural coexistence," has been official policy since the mid-2000s but remains more an administrative concept than a legal framework. Children of immigrants, including those who do not attend school regularly, are a recurring concern. There are also growing numbers of people of mixed parentage, including those with Japanese and Black, Filipino, or Korean parents, and naturalized citizens, whose public presence, notably in sport and entertainment, has prompted debate about who is considered Japanese.

The result is not a single minority category. These groups have distinct legal statuses, institutions, histories, and relationships to the state. Their experiences are not interchangeable and no community is internally uniform. What they share is that their existence contradicts a narrow definition of national belonging and that Japanese citizenship and residency policy continues to be contested.

Section 100.7 · Division II

Remembering Empire and War: Textbooks, Yasukuni, Colonial Responsibility, and Historical Disputes

Historical memory is not a separate topic from politics: in East Asia, disputes over the imperial and wartime past help define diplomacy, domestic politics, and education. The documented facts are not themselves in doubt in the way sometimes suggested. Japanese forces occupied Korea and Taiwan and large parts of China and Southeast Asia (Chapters 60 to 62, 75 to 79). The Imperial Japanese Army killed large numbers of civilians and prisoners at Nanjing in 1937 to 1938, a conclusion established by Japanese and international documentary evidence, military diaries, and the Tokyo trial. The scale is debated: scholars' estimates range from tens of thousands to upward of two hundred thousand, depending on the geography and period counted and the definition of victims, and the Chinese government's figure of 300,000 is an official number rather than a scholarly consensus. The military system of sexual slavery commonly associated with "comfort women" involved women from Korea, China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Indonesia, the Netherlands, and Japan, among others, and the degree of military involvement in recruitment and operation is documented in government and military records. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans and Chinese were mobilized for labor in Japan and the empire (Chapter 79). Denial of these events is not a legitimate competing interpretation; disagreement among historians concerns scale, terminology, causation, legal responsibility, and the interpretation of particular documents.

Official statements have been central. The Kōno statement of 1993 acknowledged the involvement of military authorities in the comfort-station system and expressed apology. The Murayama statement of 1995, on the fiftieth anniversary, expressed "deep remorse" and "heartfelt apology" for colonial rule and aggression, and became a reference point for subsequent governments. Abe's statement of 2015 for the seventieth anniversary affirmed previous cabinets' positions in general terms while adding that future generations should not be predestined to apologize; it received different readings in Japan and abroad. In December 2015 the Japanese and South Korean governments reached an agreement on the comfort-women issue that was later contested in South Korea. These statements are best read as documents of political positioning: they matter to diplomacy, and they differ in wording and emphasis, and historians should compare them closely.

Textbooks are a recurring battleground. Japan's system of authorization by the education ministry, a postwar and prewar inheritance, produces books that reflect compromise, and revisionist groups have since the 1990s promoted alternatives that minimize or reinterpret wartime wrongdoing, with limited adoption. Neighboring countries protest passages they consider inadequate, and Japanese scholars and teachers have pushed back against both nationalist revisions and official pressure. Museums reflect similar disputes: the Yūshūkan museum at Yasukuni presents a narrative of the war that has been criticized by historians, while the Peace Museums in Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and Okinawa emphasize civilian suffering, and some have been criticized for underplaying Japanese aggression. Memorial landscape thus reflects different priorities.

Yasukuni Shrine, founded in 1869 to honor those who died in service of the emperor (Chapter 55), enshrines about 2.5 million war dead and, since 1978, fourteen individuals convicted as Class A war criminals by the Tokyo tribunal. Visits by prime ministers and ministers evoke protests from China and South Korea, who view them as endorsing militarism, while supporters in Japan argue that they are private acts of mourning. The Emperor has not visited since 1975. The shrine is neither only a religious site nor only a political one; its significance depends on the observer.

Heritage recognition has become part of the dispute. The Sado Island Gold Mines were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2024, with Japan's nomination focused on the Edo-period mining system. South Korea argued that it ignored the forced labor of Koreans during the wartime period at the mines. Japan agreed to present the history of Korean workers in an exhibition on the island and to hold memorials, and the details of the exhibition and ceremonies were themselves subjects of disagreement. A similar controversy attended the Gunkanjima (Hashima) site, inscribed in 2015. These cases show that memory is contested in the realm of tourism and heritage and not only in diplomacy.

Historical memory matters because states use it, communities carry it, and it shapes whether present relationships can be repaired. The section's safeguard is analytic: distinguishing academic research, official diplomatic positions, victim testimony, political rhetoric, and popular memory, and recognizing that contested political interpretation does not require false equivalence between established evidence and unsupported denial.

Section 100.8 · Division II

Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Peace Activism, and the Eightieth Anniversary of 1945

The atomic bombings of Hiroshima on August 6, 1945, and Nagasaki on August 9 (Chapter 80) are commemorated in Japan as events of civilian suffering and as the foundation of a postwar peace identity. The survivors, hibakusha, have been central to this memory. They formed local and then national organizations, and in 1956 the confederation Nihon Hidankyō was founded. For decades its members testified, campaigned for medical support and for the abolition of nuclear weapons, and met discrimination in marriage and employment because of fears about radiation. The institutions of memory include the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum and Park, the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum, annual ceremonies on the anniversaries, and international gatherings, among them the Group of Seven leaders' summit hosted in Hiroshima in May 2023.

In October 2024 the Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded the Peace Prize to Nihon Hidankyō. The award recognized its work to show that nuclear weapons must never be used again. It came when the number of survivors was declining, with an average age above eighty-five, and at a time of renewed nuclear threats in the world. It also drew attention to a tension in Japanese policy. Japan, the only country to have suffered atomic attack in war, advocates abolition while relying on the United States' extended nuclear deterrent, and it has not joined the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, a position survivors' groups criticize. The passing of the generation with direct memory raises questions about how oral testimony, objects, buildings, and records will be preserved, and how younger people will inherit the memory. Projects employing recorded testimony, digital archives, and "legacy successors" who learn survivors' stories to retell them are one response.

The eightieth anniversary of the end of the war in August 2025 was a substantial moment of public reflection. It produced memorial ceremonies, television programs, exhibitions, and debates about what lessons should be drawn. Official ceremonies on August 15 at the Nippon Budōkan, with the Emperor's expressions of remorse and the prime minister's address, are annual ritual events whose wording is watched closely for changes. In October 2025 Prime Minister Ishiba Shigeru released a reflection on the war, which examined the failure of political institutions and of civilian leadership to restrain the military and prevent war. It was issued as a personal statement and not as a cabinet resolution, which affects its authority, and it was differently received. The statement is valuable evidence of how a contemporary leader interpreted institutional responsibility.

Comparing perspectives reveals the range of meanings. For hibakusha, remembrance means testimony and the demand that no one else suffer as they did. For historians, it means preserving evidence and placing the bombings within the whole history of the war, including Japanese aggression, strategic bombing of Japanese cities, and the decisions of the United States, in an analysis which is itself debated. For many younger citizens, the war may be a distant subject encountered in school and media. For people in China, Korea, and Southeast Asia, the eightieth anniversary is a memory of occupation and liberation more than one of Japanese victimhood, and they sometimes object when commemoration of Japanese suffering seems to eclipse Japanese responsibility. These meanings are not mutually exclusive, but they are not identical.

Remembrance therefore has several functions that should be distinguished: honoring victims, preserving evidence, acknowledging responsibility, strengthening commitments to peace, and constructing national identity. A commemoration can serve some of these while neglecting others, and the debates examined in this division often arise from disagreement over which function a particular act of memory is serving.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Memory, Reconciliation, Responsibility, Cultural Heritage

Historical memory refers to how societies, communities, and institutions remember and narrate the past, as distinct from the critical reconstruction of it by historians; the two interact but are not identical. Reconciliation is a process of repairing relationships after conflict, which may involve apology, compensation, truth-telling, and educational cooperation, and is not the same as agreement about history. Responsibility may be legal, political, or moral, and may be attributed to individuals, institutions, or states without implying collective guilt by ethnicity or nationality. Cultural heritage consists of tangible and intangible inheritances that communities and institutions choose to preserve, and its recognition, as with UNESCO listings, involves judgments about what to include.

Each term is used in public debate with different meanings by different parties, so students should ask which sense is intended.

Section 100.9 · Division III — Japanese Culture in a Globalized World

Manga and Anime: From Japanese Popular Culture to a Global Cultural System

Chapter 95 traced the postwar history of manga and animation: Tezuka Osamu's innovations, the weekly magazines, the television series of the 1960s, and Studio Ghibli. The twenty-first century added a different kind of change, in scale and in structure. Works that circulated first among domestic audiences, and then among fans abroad through informal means, became global products distributed by international companies. Pokémon (from 1996), One Piece (from 1997), Naruto (from 1999), and later Demon Slayer reached audiences across continents, and the films of Miyazaki Hayao, including Spirited Away (2001), which won an Academy Award, and The Boy and the Heron (2023), which won another, achieved critical standing alongside commercial success. In 2020, the film Demon Slayer: Mugen Train became the highest-grossing film in Japanese box-office history to that date, a reminder that the domestic market remains large.

The global system depends on institutions. Publishers such as Shueisha, Kodansha, and Shogakukan commission and serialize manga and license translations. Animation studios, often small and working for production committees (seisaku iinkai) of publishers, broadcasters, toy makers, and others, make the series. Streaming platforms, notably Crunchyroll and Netflix, changed distribution by making new episodes available worldwide soon after Japanese broadcast, displacing earlier models of delayed licensing and of unauthorized fan translation, though piracy persists. Conventions, merchandise, and music created a participatory culture in which fans produced translations, art, and commentary. The Association of Japanese Animations publishes annual industry reports, and these show a market in which overseas revenue has become a larger share, though readers must check definitions of what is counted.

"Cool Japan" is the name for both a phenomenon and a policy. The phrase, drawn from a 2002 essay about Japan's "gross national cool," was taken up by the government in the 2010s, which established a Cool Japan strategy and a public-private investment fund to promote creative industries abroad. The program has been criticized for spending, for results that seemed modest, and for the premise that the state can create cultural appeal that arose largely from commercial and fan activity. Scholars treat it as an example of efforts to convert cultural prestige into economic and diplomatic advantage, which Section 100.16 considers under the heading of soft power.

The industry's labor conditions are a counterweight to its glamour. Animators, many working as freelancers, have been paid per frame or per piece, often at low rates and with long hours, and young animators leave the field. Concentration of profit in committee members rather than studios, the weakness of intellectual-property control for creators, and the competition from foreign studios, including those subcontracted for Japanese series, are structural problems. A related shift is the use of digital production and, more recently, generative artificial intelligence, which raises questions about authorship and labor. At the same time, anime and manga are not a single genre or a uniform representation of Japanese society: they include children's comedy, romance, science fiction, sports, historical drama, horror, slice-of-life, and works by women and independent creators. The global attention given to a few franchises should not be confused with the whole field.

Section 100.10 · Division III

Video Games, Digital Entertainment, and Japan's Global Creative Economy

Japan became a center of video-game development in the late 1970s and 1980s with Taito, Namco, Nintendo, and Sega. Nintendo's Famicom (1983) and its Western counterpart revived the industry after the American market collapse of 1983, and franchises such as Super Mario, The Legend of Zelda, Final Fantasy (Square, now Square Enix), Resident Evil (Capcom), and Pokémon (Game Freak and Nintendo) became recognizable worldwide. Sony's PlayStation, launched in 1994, took Japanese consoles into a new era of three-dimensional games. By the 2000s the center of the global industry was shifting: American and European developers and, later, Chinese and Korean companies grew in importance, and Microsoft, Apple, and Google entered with new platforms.

The twenty-first century saw major structural changes. Handheld and mobile devices, digital distribution, online play, and streaming of gameplay reshaped how games were sold and used. Nintendo's Wii (2006) and Switch (2017) found success by emphasizing accessibility and portability rather than raw technical capability, and the Switch became one of the best-selling consoles of all time. Mobile games, a large domestic business with microtransactions, changed revenue models. Japanese publishers have also partnered with Western developers, and independent developers worldwide have drawn inspiration from Japanese games while building their own styles. Streaming and fan communities contributed to the visibility of Japanese titles and to practices such as speedrunning and modding.

Games are an artistic and narrative form as well as a commercial one. Japanese designers have contributed distinctive approaches to level design, character, music (composers such as Koji Kondo and Uematsu Nobuo gained worldwide followings), and storytelling, with role-playing games developing particular conventions of narrative structure. Studios vary from corporate giants to small teams. Labor conditions are also a concern, including long hours in some studios, and the industry's relationship to unions and to creators' credit has been uneven.

The global circulation of games shaped international perceptions of Japan, often independently of creators' intentions. A player abroad may associate Japan with a fantasy aesthetic, with characters like Mario or Pikachu, or with imagined versions of Japanese history such as samurai and ninja drawn from a long tradition of popular depiction (see Chapter 49), a point where the textbook's warnings about stereotypes apply. The central question is how Japan became a producer of cultural experiences that audiences worldwide consume, modify, and interpret. The answer lies in firms with strong intellectual-property management, a large domestic market that supported experimentation, the transferability of design styles, and the technologies of distribution, rather than in any national essence.

Section 100.11 · Division III

J-Pop, Cinema, Literature, and Transnational Cultural Exchange

The term J-pop emerged in the 1990s to describe mainstream Japanese popular music that drew on Western pop and rock but was marketed as domestic, and it has since come to include numerous genres: rock, hip-hop, electronic music, city pop, and the idol industry. Idol groups, managed by large agencies, build fan relationships through live events and merchandise, and the system has been criticized for restrictive contracts and for the exploitation of young performers; the scandal over sexual abuse by the founder of Johnny & Associates, publicly acknowledged in 2023, led to the dissolution of that company's old structure and to wider reconsideration of the industry. Utada Hikaru, who emerged in the late 1990s, and the electronic group Perfume are among artists with international followings, while YOASOBI, which began as a duo producing songs based on written stories and reached worldwide audiences with the song "Idol" (2023), illustrates the circulation of music through anime, streaming platforms, and social media.

The relationship between music and anime or games is a feature of the contemporary system: opening themes, soundtracks, and tie-ins reach audiences who might not otherwise hear Japanese music. The revival of 1980s "city pop" among overseas listeners through video-sharing algorithms is a case in which the circulation was unplanned and driven by foreign audiences. Streaming has also affected the domestic market, which had remained oriented to physical formats longer than other large markets. These examples show cultural exchange running in multiple directions, with Japanese works reinterpreted abroad and Japanese artists adopting global styles.

Cinema has also continued to draw international attention. Kore-eda Hirokazu's Shoplifters won the Palme d'Or at Cannes in 2018, and Hamaguchi Ryūsuke's Drive My Car (2021), adapted from a story by Murakami Haruki, won the Academy Award for Best International Feature. Yamazaki Takashi's Godzilla Minus One (2023) won an Academy Award for visual effects, in a tradition of the monster film first examined in Chapter 95. Alongside such acclaim, the domestic film industry depends on animation and franchise films, and independent directors often work with small budgets. Cultural recognition abroad does not necessarily translate into domestic commercial success, and vice versa.

Contemporary literature is similarly global. Murakami Haruki is among the most translated living novelists. Tawada Yōko, who writes in both Japanese and German and lives in Berlin, exemplifies a literature that crosses linguistic boundaries, and Kawakami Mieko, whose novel Breasts and Eggs appeared in English in 2020, writes on women's bodies and social pressures. Translators, publishers, and prizes shape which works circulate. Many Japanese works that reach readers abroad are selected and framed by foreign editors and marketing categories, and the translation itself is an act of interpretation. The Akutagawa Prize and other domestic prizes remain important to the careers of writers at home.

These examples let students ask what happens when artistic works cross linguistic and cultural boundaries. The answer is neither that Japanese culture is exported unchanged to passive audiences nor that it is lost in translation. Foreign audiences read works through their own contexts, producing meanings the creators did not intend, and creators in turn respond to foreign reception. Cultural exchange is a two-way process.

Section 100.12 · Division III

Superflat, Contemporary Art, Japanese Fashion, and the Reinvention of Aesthetic Traditions

In 2000 the artist Murakami Takashi presented the idea of Superflat, an exhibition and a theory proposing that Japanese visual culture, from Edo-period painting and prints (Chapter 46) to postwar manga and anime, shared a flat, graphic treatment of space and an absence of the hierarchical depth of Western perspective painting. He linked this to the postwar experience of defeat, consumerism, and a culture in which the boundary between "high" and "low" art had weakened. The theory is a provocative interpretation and a marketing concept, and art historians have debated it. It has been criticized for presenting a generalized Japanese aesthetic and for accepting the commodification it describes. Murakami's studio, Kaikai Kiki, produces paintings, sculptures, and merchandise in collaboration with luxury brands, a practice he presents as part of the argument and critics treat as an instance of it.

Nara Yoshitomo, whose paintings of children with an ambiguous, wary stare, have been shown internationally, developed his work from the punk music and countercultural influences of the 1980s and 1990s and studies in Germany, and he is not reducible to the Superflat label. Other artists, among them Kusama Yayoi, whose international fame in her nineties exemplifies the late recognition of a long career, and younger artists working with new media, participate in international institutions and markets. A museum or gallery abroad may present them as representatives of Japan, and they may accept or resist that role.

Fashion is an equally important field. Miyake Issey, who died in 2022, Kawakubo Rei (Comme des Garçons), and Yamamoto Yōji arrived in Paris in the early 1980s with designs that challenged Western conceptions of the body, silhouette, and color, and they remain influential. Street fashion in Harajuku, Shibuya, and elsewhere generated subcultures, among them lolita, gyaru, and visual kei, that circulated through magazines and later social media. Streetwear brands collaborate with global labels, and vintage and secondhand markets have also become a sector of international interest. Craft traditions, such as indigo dyeing, are used by designers and revalued by consumers. The result is a field in which luxury, subculture, craft, and mass retail interact, including large Japanese fast-fashion firms.

These developments challenge assumptions that tradition and innovation belong to different cultural worlds. Designers cite kimono construction, folded cloth, and indigo; artists cite ukiyo-e; and traditional craftspeople adopt new markets and tools. At the same time, the historian should not treat Japanese artistic creativity as the expression of an unchanging national essence. Each of these movements arose in particular institutions, markets, and circumstances; each was shaped by international influences from Europe and America; and each changed over time.

The conclusion of Division III is that Japan's global cultural influence emerged from a changing system of artists, companies, technologies, international audiences, and institutions. Japanese culture became globally influential in part because it proved capable of adaptation, reinterpretation, and exchange, qualities that appear throughout this book, from the adoption of Chinese writing to the invention of kana, and from the integration of Buddhism to the reworking of Western music in the jazz kissa.

Section 100.13 · Division IV — Japan and the Contemporary International Order

Japan and China: Economic Interdependence, Strategic Competition, and Historical Memory

Diplomatic relations between Japan and the People's Republic of China were normalized in 1972, and the Treaty of Peace and Friendship followed in 1978 (Chapter 92 and later chapters). In the decades that followed Japan became a major source of investment, technology, and development aid for China's economic opening. After 2000 the relationship changed. China's economy grew rapidly and surpassed Japan's in size in 2010, and China's military modernization, including naval expansion, altered the regional balance. Yet the economies remained deeply connected: China has been among Japan's largest trading partners for many years, Japanese firms built supply chains through Chinese manufacturing, and large numbers of people traveled between the two countries as tourists, students, workers, and residents. Chinese visitors were a major component of Japanese inbound tourism before the pandemic.

Three sources of friction are intertwined. The first is memory. The Japanese imperial past, including the invasion of China, Nanjing, and wartime atrocities, is a continuing subject of Chinese public education and official commemoration, and Chinese protests have followed Japanese textbook decisions and prime ministerial visits to Yasukuni Shrine (Section 100.7). The second is territory. The Senkaku Islands, which China and Taiwan call Diaoyu (Diaoyutai), are uninhabited and administered by Japan, which maintains that they are Japanese territory and that no dispute exists to be resolved; China claims them. A collision between a Chinese fishing boat and Japanese coast guard vessels in 2010 and the Japanese government's purchase of three of the islands from a private owner in 2012, which led to anti-Japanese demonstrations in China, were turning points, and Chinese coast guard presence in surrounding waters has become routine. The third is security. China's military modernization, activity near Japan's southwestern islands, and the possibility of conflict over Taiwan, a place only about a hundred kilometers from Japan's westernmost island Yonaguni, have become the principal driver of Japanese defense planning.

The Taiwan question sharpened in November 2025, when Prime Minister Takaichi told the Diet that an attack on Taiwan involving warships and the use of force could constitute a "survival-threatening situation" under the 2015 security legislation, the legal category that permits Japan to exercise collective self-defense. Chinese officials demanded a retraction, which she declined to give, and Beijing responded with diplomatic protests and measures affecting travel and trade, as reported at the time. Students should recognize that this episode reflected a legal distinction long present in Japanese policy discussion, and that the novelty lay in a prime minister stating a hypothetical case so explicitly. On November 13, 2026, the government announced that she was scheduled to visit the Philippines and China on November 16–19, including the APEC leaders’ meeting in Shenzhen on November 18–19. This is a scheduled visit, not an accomplished diplomatic outcome at the November 13 edition cutoff.

Japan's 2022 National Security Strategy identifies China as the greatest strategic challenge Japan has faced while also describing the importance of constructive and stable relations and of dialogue. How can two states remain economically connected while disagreeing profoundly about security, territory, and history? Several factors operate at once: mutual dependence on trade and investment; business and local-government ties; people-to-people links; and an interest in avoiding escalation. Their coexistence is not a contradiction but a characteristic of the contemporary relationship, though it is vulnerable to rapid deterioration, as episodes of Chinese import bans, such as that on Japanese seafood after the 2023 release of treated water from Fukushima, have shown.

Section 100.14 · Division IV

Japan and the Koreas: Colonial Memory, Reconciliation, Popular Culture, and Security

Japan's relations with South Korea and North Korea are distinct diplomatic histories. The Republic of Korea and Japan normalized relations in 1965 through a treaty and an accompanying agreement in which Japan provided economic assistance and the two governments stated that claims were settled. Japan has maintained that this settled the legal question of compensation, while many Koreans have argued that it did not address the claims of individual victims of forced labor and sexual slavery, whose cases were not represented in the negotiations of a military government. Since the 1990s the issues have repeatedly returned: the comfort-women question, forced-labor lawsuits, the status of the Dokdo/Takeshima islets, and textbooks.

Tension peaked in the late 2010s. In 2018 South Korea's Supreme Court ordered Japanese companies to compensate former forced laborers, and in 2019 Japan tightened export controls on certain materials used by Korean semiconductor firms, while South Korea briefly threatened to end an intelligence-sharing agreement. The relationship improved after Yoon Suk Yeol's government in 2023 proposed that a Korean foundation pay compensation, and Kishida and Yoon exchanged visits. The subsequent political upheaval in South Korea in 2024 and the election of Lee Jae-myung as president in 2025 did not result in a rupture. The 2025 sixtieth anniversary of normalization was marked by a cautious pragmatism on both sides, though underlying disagreements, such as the interpretation of the forced-labor rulings and the Sado commemorations, did not disappear.

Cultural exchange runs alongside political disagreement and sometimes contradicts it. The Korean Wave began in Japan with the drama Winter Sonata in the early 2000s, followed by K-pop groups, television dramas, films, cuisine, and cosmetics. Young Japanese consumers of Korean popular culture are a large and stable audience, and Korean visitors are among Japan's largest tourist groups. Japanese manga, anime, and games are in turn widely consumed in Korea. This exchange reflects the market logic of cultural industries and the interests of young consumers, which can diverge from government positions. It does not eliminate prejudice, including the hate speech discussed in Section 100.6, nor does political tension eliminate exchange. The two coexist because culture, politics, and economics operate through different institutions and constituencies.

North Korea presents a different set of problems. Japan has no diplomatic relations with Pyongyang. The 2002 summit between Prime Minister Koizumi Jun'ichirō and Kim Jong-il led to North Korea's admission that it had abducted Japanese citizens in the 1970s and 1980s, and five abductees returned that year. The issue of the remaining abductees remains unresolved, and families, such as those who lost Yokota Megumi, have been public advocates for decades; Takaichi attended a rally on the issue in November 2026. North Korea's nuclear tests and missile launches, including launches over Japanese territory in 2017 and 2022, are a major element of Japanese security policy. Cooperation among Japan, South Korea, and the United States was institutionalized in the 2023 Camp David summit, but the three governments' positions are not permanently aligned, since they depend on domestic politics and on the policies of each administration. Japanese government documents provide the official position; students must supplement them with South Korean and independent scholarship.

The central question is why memory, economic cooperation, cultural exchange, and security concern lead Japan's relationships with the Koreas in different directions. Part of the answer is that each is governed by different actors: courts, ministries, firms, fans, and veterans' and victims' organizations. Part is that the same history supports different present-day stories.

Section 100.15 · Division IV

The U.S.–Japan Alliance, Indo-Pacific Strategy, and Japan's Changing Security Role

The alliance originated in the occupation. The 1951 security treaty, signed with the peace treaty at San Francisco, allowed U.S. forces to remain, and the 1960 revision, which provoked the Anpo protests (Chapter 92), made it a mutual arrangement in which the United States committed to defend Japan and Japan provided bases. Article 9 of the Constitution limits Japan's use of force, and governments have interpreted it to permit a self-defense force but not offensive capability, a position changed incrementally. The United States maintains tens of thousands of troops in Japan, with Okinawa hosting the largest concentration of facilities, and Japan provides host-nation support. The alliance has been the foundation of Japanese security policy for seventy years and has also been a source of domestic controversy, in debates over bases, accidents, and the possibility of being drawn into American wars.

The strategic changes of the 2020s are significant. In December 2022 the Kishida cabinet adopted a new National Security Strategy, with accompanying defense documents. The strategy states that Japan faces the most severe and complex security environment since the end of the war. It authorizes the acquisition of "counterstrike capability," meaning long-range missiles able to hit launch sites in enemy territory, and sets a goal of raising defense and related spending to about two percent of gross domestic product, a large increase in a country that had kept its defense budget near one percent for decades. It also emphasizes economic security, cybersecurity, supply-chain resilience, and cooperation with partners. Subsequent governments have pursued and in some respects accelerated this program. Japan has also joined with Britain and Italy in developing a next-generation fighter aircraft, relaxed restrictions on arms exports in stages, and established a joint operations command for its forces.

Relations with other partners have also expanded. Japan belongs to the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue with the United States, Australia, and India, and has deepened defense cooperation with Australia, the Philippines, which concluded a reciprocal access agreement with Japan in 2024, Britain, and others. It continues to value the "free and open Indo-Pacific" concept proposed by Abe. In the United States, the political environment has been less predictable: the second Trump administration's tariffs and its emphasis on burden-sharing created uncertainty about the reliability of commitments and about economic terms, and Japan negotiated a trade agreement with Washington in 2025. The Japanese government has responded by affirming the alliance while diversifying partnerships, an approach that some analysts describe as hedging and others as a strengthened network.

Debates within Japan continue. Supporters argue that the changes are necessary responses to China, North Korea, and Russia, and that a counterstrike capability is consistent with a defensive posture. Critics argue that the change erodes the postwar constitutional restraint, was adopted through cabinet decision rather than legislation or constitutional amendment, and raises costs at a time of fiscal strain and demographic constraint. Okinawans object that the burden of bases remains unequal. Questions about democratic accountability include how the Diet reviews defense spending and how citizens influence decisions that may involve use of force. Chapter 99 described the 2014 reinterpretation of collective self-defense and the 2015 legislation in detail; the point here is to place them in a longer trajectory in which Japan's strategic role has been enlarged step by step through reinterpretation, legislation, and budgets.

Section 100.16 · Division IV

Japan's Place in Asia and the World: Economic Diplomacy, Development, and Cultural Influence

Japan's international role cannot be reduced to its three most prominent bilateral relationships. Its postwar reentry into Asia was economic: reparations and loans to Southeast Asian countries, official development assistance, and the investment of firms that built regional production networks (Chapters 90 and 91). The Fukuda Doctrine of 1977 pledged Japan to a non-military role and to equal partnership with Southeast Asia. Today, ASEAN countries are major trading and investment partners, and Japanese companies, banks, and development agencies are present in infrastructure, manufacturing, and finance across the region, competing with Chinese, Korean, and other investors. Japan was a principal architect of the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) after the United States withdrew from the earlier agreement in 2017, and it also belongs to the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, which includes China.

"Economic security" has become a central term. It refers to measures to protect supply chains for critical goods such as semiconductors, rare earths, and energy, to guard technology, and to reduce vulnerability to coercion. Japan imports nearly all its fossil fuel and much of its food, and the closure of nuclear plants after 2011 increased its dependence on imported energy (Chapter 99). The government has promoted domestic and allied semiconductor production, including partnership with the Taiwanese firm TSMC in Kumamoto, and has legislated on economic security. The tension between openness and resilience is a defining issue: Japan's prosperity depends on trade, and its security planners worry about dependence.

Soft power, a term coined by the political scientist Joseph Nye, refers to influence arising from attraction and reputation instead of coercion or payment. Japan's cultural industries, education, research partnerships, and development assistance contribute to it, but it is a different thing from formal diplomatic authority and economic leverage. Foreign students come to Japanese universities, the government has set targets for more, and exchange programs and the Japan Foundation promote the language. Japanese communities abroad, such as those in Brazil, Peru, and the United States, descend from earlier emigration (Chapter 62 and others), and some members have returned as workers. Visitors, migrants, and students carry ideas and practices both ways.

The historical conclusion is that Japan's relationships with the world are organized through economic institutions, cultural exchange, technological partnerships, and human mobility, in addition to military policy. The postwar picture of Japan as an economic power with a limited military role has been revised as the military dimension has grown, but the other dimensions have not disappeared.

Section 100.17 · Division V — Heritage, Innovation, and Japan's Future

Cuisine, Traditional Arts, Tourism, and the Global Presentation of Japan

Food has become one of the most visible dimensions of Japan's international image. Sushi, ramen, and other dishes are widely available abroad in adapted forms, and Japanese restaurants have multiplied. Many of the dishes now regarded as classic have relatively recent histories: ramen derives from Chinese noodle dishes adapted in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and its modern national popularity grew after the war, in part from the black-market and street-stall culture of Chapter 85 and wheat imports from the United States. Regional cuisines, and the food of Okinawa and Hokkaido in particular, are distinct and have their own histories. In 2013 UNESCO inscribed washoku, "traditional dietary cultures of the Japanese," on its list of intangible cultural heritage. The inscription concerns a set of social practices, relating to the preparation and consumption of food, seasonal observances, and respect for natural resources, rather than an endorsement of all Japanese dishes, and critics noted that it risks fixing a living and varied practice into a single image.

Traditional performing and visual arts continue as living practices. Kabuki, nō, and bunraku (Chapters 30 and 46) are performed by professional lineages and institutions, with state support and with challenges of audience size and succession; kabuki experiments with new works and collaborations while sustaining the training of actors born into theatrical families. Tea ceremony schools, ceramics, calligraphy, and crafts such as lacquerware and textiles depend on workshops whose artisans are aging, and some have found new markets abroad and among younger consumers. Festivals (matsuri) face the depopulation of the communities that sustain them, and some have been preserved by volunteer visitors or reorganized. These arts show that "tradition" requires continuing institutions, apprenticeships, finance, and audiences, and that it changes in the process.

Tourism is the most visible link between heritage and the economy. Inbound visitor numbers rose sharply in the 2010s, a result of visa relaxation, a weak yen, rising prosperity in Asia, and promotion, then fell during the pandemic when borders were largely closed, and recovered to new records, exceeding 36 million in 2024. The benefits are real: revenue for regional businesses, work for local communities, and funds for preservation. The tensions are also real. Overtourism has crowded sites in Kyoto, Kamakura, and Mount Fuji, causing local residents to complain about congestion and behavior, and municipalities have introduced restrictions, fees, and access controls. Heritage-tourism development raises questions about who benefits and who has a voice, as in the Ainu case discussed in Section 100.5.

Expo 2025 Osaka–Kansai, held on an artificial island in Osaka Bay from April 13 to October 13, 2025, was a contemporary case study. It recorded approximately 29.02 million visits, including those admitted with accreditation passes, which is a different quantity from ticket sales and from the number of unique individuals. The Expo's theme was "Designing Future Society for Our Lives," and its centerpiece, a large circular timber walkway, the Grand Ring, was a feat of traditional wood-joinery combined with contemporary engineering. The planning had been criticized for cost overruns, for delays in construction, and for low early ticket sales, and the final attendance was therefore read as a success by supporters. The Expo presented technology, design, sustainability, and the image Japan wished to project, a successor to the 1970 Osaka Expo (Chapter 91) and a comparison students can use to see how that image has changed.

Heritage is not merely what a society inherits. It is also what communities, institutions, and markets choose to preserve, represent, and transmit, with the participation or exclusion of particular people. That is the conclusion of the section.

Section 100.18 · Division V

Technological Change: Robotics, Artificial Intelligence, Industry, and Environmental Sustainability

Japan's technological reputation rests on the late twentieth century: automobiles, consumer electronics, semiconductors, and industrial robots (Chapters 91 and 96). Japanese firms remain leaders in precision machinery, materials, components for chip manufacturing, industrial robotics, and automotive technology, including hybrid vehicles. But in the digital industries that grew after 2000, platform companies, cloud services, and consumer software, the largest firms are American and Chinese, and Japanese consumer-electronics makers lost ground to Korean, Taiwanese, and Chinese competitors. The government has responded with an industrial policy that is notable for its return: subsidies for semiconductor plants, such as the TSMC facility in Kumamoto, support for the domestic chip venture Rapidus, a Digital Agency established in 2021 after the pandemic revealed weaknesses in public-sector information systems, and a 2025 law promoting research and use of artificial intelligence.

Automation is often presented as a solution to labor shortages. Robots are used widely in factories, and experiments in logistics, agriculture, and eldercare, including lifting and monitoring devices, are under way. The evidence for their effect is mixed. Care work is difficult to automate fully, and many technologies have been adopted slowly or used as supplements to human care. Automation may increase productivity but also change the nature of jobs and may not reach small firms. The distinction between demonstrated capability and promised breakthroughs matters; Expo-style demonstrations of robots and prototypes show possibilities, not widespread adoption.

Environmental conditions shape technological choices. Japan has committed to carbon neutrality by 2050, and its energy debate involves renewable energy, the restart of nuclear reactors that passed new safety reviews after 2011, imported liquefied natural gas, and the cost of decarbonization. The decommissioning of the Fukushima Daiichi plant will take decades, and the release of treated water into the ocean, which began in 2023, has been controversial at home and abroad. Extreme heat, intense rainfall, and typhoons have increased in frequency, and the possibility of a major Nankai Trough earthquake is a subject of formal government warnings. The Noto Peninsula earthquake on New Year's Day 2024 showed again the vulnerability of aging rural communities. In November 2026 the government inaugurated a new Disaster Management Agency, an institutional response to a long series of disasters.

Technology's effects on employment, care, education, cities, and rural communities depend on how it is used and who owns it. Remote work, digital administration, and online education can reduce the disadvantages of distance, though they can also entrench inequalities in access and skills. The historian should resist both technological optimism and fatalism. The decisions that determine outcomes are social and political, concerning investment, regulation, labor, and the allocation of gains.

Section 100.19 · Division V

Competing Visions of Japan's Future: Demography, Democracy, Economic Renewal, and Social Inclusion

This section is analytical and not predictive. Its purpose is to show the structure of the choices Japan faces and the assumptions behind competing answers, not to say what will happen. Four questions organize the debate.

Population and community. How can Japan maintain essential services and living standards while its population ages and declines? One vision emphasizes raising fertility through support for families, childcare, and employment reform. Another emphasizes adapting to a smaller population through compact cities, consolidation of services in regional hubs, and a "managed retreat" from the most remote settlements. A third emphasizes immigration. These are not mutually exclusive, but they imply different budgets, rely on different evidence, and distribute benefits and costs differently across regions and generations.

Economic transformation. What might raise productivity and incomes? Proposals include stimulating wage growth, reforming labor markets to reduce the gap between regular and irregular workers, supporting startups and digital industries, strengthening domestic semiconductor and energy industries, and deepening trade integration. The current government's fiscal approach emphasizes public investment and tax measures, while critics worry about public debt, which is large relative to the economy, and about a weak yen and inflation. The assumptions about interest rates, growth, and political tolerance for taxation are decisive and uncertain.

National identity. How might immigration, minority recognition, citizenship, and family change alter understandings of Japanese society? Some advocate a more explicit immigration policy with pathways to settlement, rights, and integration support; others favor stricter controls and an emphasis on assimilation or on temporary labor. Debates over same-sex marriage, separate surnames, and Ainu and Okinawan rights concern whether the definition of the national community should broaden. Political currents differ, and recent elections have shown that both inclusion and restriction have popular support.

Political institutions. How might Japan reconcile democratic accountability, national-security concerns, local autonomy, and institutional reform? Questions include constitutional revision, which requires approval by two-thirds of both houses and a national referendum; the balance between the Diet and the cabinet on security decisions; political funding reform after the scandals of 2023 to 2024; the weight of rural votes in a metropolitan society; and the decentralization of authority.

Optimistic scenarios assume that productivity gains from technology and labor reform, combined with immigration and higher labor participation, can sustain living standards despite a smaller population. Pessimistic scenarios assume that fiscal strain, regional collapse, and political polarization will overwhelm adaptation. Conditional scenarios identify the policies on which outcomes depend. Each has trade-offs of cost, feasibility, equality, political consent, and the distribution of benefits across generations and regions. Students should not be asked to identify an inevitable future.

What can historical knowledge contribute? It can show that institutions were built by choices that could have been different, that societies have adapted to demographic and economic shocks before, that policies often have unintended effects, and that the groups most affected are often those with least power over decisions. It can prevent the use of false analogies and of national-character explanations. It cannot supply certainty about developments that have not occurred. That limit is part of its value, since it encourages humility about prediction.

Historical Debate

Is Twenty-First-Century Japan Best Understood Through Continuity, Transformation, or Both?

The question: Observers of contemporary Japan often emphasize either deep continuity, in party rule, bureaucratic institutions, family and corporate structures, and cultural practices, or fundamental transformation, in demography, gender relations, migration, technology, and security policy. How should historians weigh them?

Interpretation A — Continuity within change: Some scholars stress the persistence of institutions: LDP dominance since 1955, the imperial institution, the constitutional framework, the role of ministries, and long-term patterns of regional inequality. They argue that apparent changes, such as new security documents or new prime ministers, are incremental adjustments within stable structures.
Interpretation B — Transformation beneath continuity: Others stress that demographic change, the transformation of employment, the growth of migration, changes in family and gender, and the reorientation of security policy are altering the conditions under which the institutions operate, so that the same labels conceal different realities. They argue that continuity of form can accompany discontinuity of function.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same evidence: election results, institutional records, demographic data, legislation, and social surveys. They differ in whether they emphasize the persistence of names and procedures or the change in the social base and function. Many scholars combine the two, observing that Japan's history, as this textbook has shown, has repeatedly involved institutions that endured by changing.

Why it matters: The choice affects expectations. If continuity dominates, reform is difficult and predictions should be conservative; if transformation dominates, earlier models may not apply and the range of possible futures is wider.

Student task: Choose one institution, such as the imperial household, the LDP, the family system, or the village, and use evidence from at least three chapters of this textbook to argue whether its twenty-first-century form is better understood as continuity or transformation.

Section 100.20 · Division V

Japan from Jōmon to Reiwa: Continuity, Transformation, and the Meaning of Japanese History

A hundred chapters ago this book began with an archipelago, not a nation. Its earliest people arrived by several routes over tens of thousands of years, made stone tools, crossed seas, and then made pottery among the earliest in the world (Chapters 1 to 4). That beginning mattered for a reason that returns in the last pages: nothing about Japan's later identity was present, fully formed, in its prehistory. This section organizes the book's final synthesis around five transformations.

First: the creation and development of Japanese societies. Jōmon communities, Yayoi migrants and farmers, the chiefdoms of Yamatai and the Kofun age, and the Yamato polity formed through interaction with Korea and China, from which writing, Buddhism, metallurgy, and models of government came (Chapters 5 to 13). The ritsuryō state and the imperial court of Nara and Heian built institutions and a literary culture, in which women writers produced works still read today (Chapters 14 to 19). Identities, including the idea of a "Japanese" polity, developed historically through exchange, migration, and political invention; they did not arrive complete. Even in these early centuries, the Emishi, the Ainu, and the peoples of the southern islands followed their own trajectories, which the court's frontier expansion affected but did not absorb.

Second: the diversification of political authority. From the tenth century, central authority was shared and contested: shōen estates, provincial warriors, the Kamakura bakufu, the Hōjō regents, the Ashikaga shogunate, the Sengoku daimyō, and finally the Tokugawa bakuhan system (Chapters 20 to 50). The emperor's role varied, as symbol, source of legitimacy, ritual head, or court figure, without being identical to governing capacity. Villages, temples, merchants, and domains were political actors. Status distinctions hardened and were contested, with the outcaste and the peasant petitioner as examples. Ryukyu, linked by trade to China, Korea, and Southeast Asia, was a distinct kingdom (Chapters 32 and 33), and the Ainu traded and fought on their own terms (Chapter 48). The period shows a long pattern of authority exercised through negotiation among centers and peripheries.

Third: the modern transformation. Foreign pressure, domestic crisis, and civil war ended the Tokugawa order, and the Meiji state centralized authority, abolished domains and the samurai order, built an army, schools, railways, and a constitution, and promoted industrial capitalism (Chapters 51 to 59). It also annexed Hokkaido and Okinawa, took Taiwan, annexed Korea, and fought wars that created an empire, with colonial administration, assimilation policies, resistance, and exploitation (Chapters 60 to 62). Mass society, labor and tenant movements, the suffrage campaign, and party government expanded participation while the Peace Preservation Law and the independence of the military limited it (Chapters 63 to 74). The road to total war, the atrocities of the 1930s and 1940s, and the destruction of 1945 (Chapters 75 to 81) were neither inevitable nor accidental; they came from choices within institutions whose weaknesses earlier chapters described. Modernization produced real achievements and coercive consequences, and it cannot be told without both.

Fourth: defeat, reconstruction, and postwar reinvention. Defeat, occupation, and reform produced a new constitution, women's suffrage, land and labor reform, and the dissolution of the zaibatsu, alongside hunger, black markets, and the social experience of occupation (Chapters 82 to 90). The Reverse Course, the Korean War, and the alliance with the United States set the course of the postwar order. The high-growth economy and the 1955 system produced a wealthy society, while protest, pollution disasters, minority activism, and a flourishing culture revealed its conflicts (Chapters 91 to 96). The postwar order was constructed by Japanese and foreign actors together and was contested from the start.

Fifth: the contemporary challenge. The bubble's collapse, long stagnation, irregular employment, the earthquake and sarin attack of 1995, and the triple disaster of 2011 tested institutions (Chapters 97 to 99). This chapter has added demographic decline, political scandal and assassination, the first female prime minister, recognition of the Ainu, a more plural society, the politics of memory, a security buildup, and cultural influence that spreads Japanese works through global networks. How Japan should adapt is under debate, as it has been repeatedly in the past.

Several persistent relationships run through these five transformations. Center and periphery: from Yamato and the frontier, through Kyoto and the domains, to Tokyo and the depopulating regions, the relationship between metropolitan power and distant communities has been constant, though its terms have changed. Continuity and adaptation: institutions such as the imperial household, the village, the temple, and the political party have lasted by changing function. Hierarchy and reform: status systems, from the Kofun chiefs to the household registers, have been challenged by petitions, movements, and laws, with partial and uneven results. International exchange and national identity: writing, Buddhism, firearms, constitutions, jazz, and anime all came from or went to the wider world and were remade; identities were built in these exchanges. Technological innovation and human welfare: the rice paddy, the arquebus, the railway, the factory, the robot, and the algorithm each raised questions about who benefited. State authority and civic participation: peasants, merchants, workers, women, and minorities have repeatedly pressed claims on states that granted some and denied others.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Japanese Institutions and Identities from Earlier Periods to the Present

What Changed

  • The imperial institution moved from ritual and political ruler to court symbol, constitutional sovereign, and, since 1947, a symbol of the state with no governing powers.
  • Political authority moved from clans and courts to warriors, to a centralized empire, and to parliamentary democracy under a popular-sovereignty constitution.
  • Hierarchies of status were abolished in law, and the franchise extended to women in 1945.
  • The borders of the state expanded to an empire and contracted again; the population rose from a few million to a peak of about 128 million and is now falling.

What Remained Continuous

  • The existence of an imperial line, with continuing ritual functions at the Ise and Imperial Household institutions.
  • Regional inequality and the pull of capital cities on population.
  • Selective adoption and adaptation of foreign ideas and technologies.
  • Exposure to earthquakes, volcanoes, typhoons, and tsunami, and the institutions of disaster response.
  • Discrimination against groups defined by descent, ethnicity, or origin.

Who experienced change most directly? Women, who gained rights and entered paid work; former outcaste and colonial populations; and rural communities facing depopulation. Who experienced greater continuity? Residents of the metropolitan centers, whose institutions have changed more gradually, and those who experienced persistent discrimination. Why the pattern matters: It shows that continuity is not stasis. Many Japanese institutions have lasted precisely because they changed.

It would be wrong to conclude that Japanese history shows uninterrupted continuity, since the archipelago's societies have been remade repeatedly through migration, war, revolution, and defeat. It would be equally wrong to conclude that it shows inevitable modernization, since the same century that built parliaments and railways also built an empire and a war machine, and the path through them was contingent. It does not reveal an exceptional national identity, since "Japanese" was made and remade and includes people whose histories run through Hokkaido, Okinawa, Korea, China, and many other places. Nor is it a simple succession of regimes, for beneath each regime were villages, workers, women, merchants, and minorities whose lives did not change at the same time or in the same way. The institutions and cultural traditions treated in these chapters, from court poetry and warrior government to tea, kabuki, factory unions, jazz, and anime, have continually changed through conflict, exchange, experimentation, and reinterpretation.

A student who has followed the book from the Jōmon pit-dwellings to the screens of the Reiwa era has seen a history in which nothing was final and almost nothing was foreordained, and has been asked, chapter by chapter, to separate evidence from legend, interpretation from fact, and the powerful from those they governed; what remains, as the present becomes the past and the next generation's choices turn into the next chapter, is a history that is not a finished story but an unfinished process, made by people, institutions, inherited conditions, and their changing relations with the wider world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Japan in the twenty-first century, current to November 13, 2026, and has also concluded the textbook. Its central argument is that contemporary Japan negotiates between its historical inheritance and a changing society, between national identity and diversity, between memory and international relations, and between technological achievement and demographic, economic, and political constraint. Neither a narrative of decline nor one of exceptional success describes it.

In politics, Abe Shinzō's long tenure left a contested legacy of economic experiment, executive strength, and security change. His assassination on July 8, 2022, in Nara led to a debate over his state funeral and to scrutiny of religion, money, and politics. Leadership passed through Suga, Kishida, and Ishiba to Takaichi Sanae, who on October 21, 2025, became the first female prime minister and was reappointed after a landslide in February 2026. The LDP's continuous centrality coexisted with frequent changes of leader.

The Unification Church controversy ended in the dissolution of the church's religious-corporation status, ordered in 2025, upheld by the High Court in March 2026, and finalized by the Supreme Court in June 2026 on grounds of civil torts. The courts distinguished the corporation from individual belief. The case differs from Aum Shinrikyō, and it left questions of political accountability to politics.

Demographic change is the deepest structural trend. Births in 2025 were 671,270, the total fertility rate was 1.14, and the preliminary census counted about 123.05 million people, with decline in over ninety percent of municipalities. Responses include family policy, longer working lives, regional measures, automation, and migration, whose scale is politically contested. Population decline is not a predetermined catastrophe, since outcomes depend on productivity and institutions.

Gender and family debates concern the allocation of rights and responsibilities. Women's education and employment have risen, yet political representation, management, pay, and care burdens remain unequal. The 2023 law on sexual orientation and gender diversity promoted awareness without creating antidiscrimination rights or marriage equality, and courts have reached differing conclusions in marriage cases. Takaichi's appointment was a milestone whose policy meaning is separate from its symbolism.

The Ainu were recognized as an Indigenous people in the 2019 act, and Upopoy opened in 2020, but recognition differs from redress of dispossession. Okinawans, Zainichi Koreans, Burakumin, and migrants have distinct histories and statuses, and the 3.8 million foreign residents recorded by the OECD in 2025 are a distinct category from foreign workers and visitors. The homogeneous-nation image has never been accurate.

Memory of empire and war remains politically potent. The Kōno, Murayama, Abe, and Ishiba statements, textbooks, Yasukuni, and the Sado Mines inscription show disputes among historians, governments, victims, and publics. The Nobel Peace Prize to Nihon Hidankyō and the eightieth anniversary of 1945 highlighted survivors' testimony and the challenge of preserving memory as witnesses die.

Japanese popular culture, including manga, anime, games, J-pop, cinema, literature, contemporary art, and fashion, became globally influential through a system of firms, technologies, platforms, fans, and policy, not a national essence. Its labor conditions, definitions of revenue, and two-way reception need critical attention.

Internationally, Japan is economically tied to China while contesting security, territory, and memory with it; it shares cultural exchange with South Korea despite historical disputes; it faces North Korean missiles and the abduction issue; and it has strengthened its alliance with the United States and partnerships across the Indo-Pacific under the 2022 strategy. Economic diplomacy, development, soft power, and human mobility are as important as military policy.

Heritage and technology present opportunities and trade-offs. Washoku, traditional arts, and Expo 2025 (about 29.02 million visits) illustrate how heritage is chosen and presented, with tourism tensions. Robotics, semiconductors, AI, energy, and disaster preparedness shape economic prospects, but demonstrated capability must be distinguished from promise.

Competing visions of the future involve population, economy, identity, and political institutions, and no future is inevitable. Historical knowledge improves decisions without prediction.

The final synthesis traced five transformations: the creation of Japanese societies, the diversification of political authority, the modern transformation, defeat and postwar reinvention, and the contemporary challenge. Persistent relationships, such as center and periphery, hierarchy and reform, and exchange and identity, link them. Japanese history cannot be reduced to uninterrupted continuity, inevitable modernization, exceptional identity, or a succession of regimes.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the present is a historical period with its own uncertainties. The assassination of a former prime minister, a court's dissolution of a religious corporation, the appointment of a woman as prime minister, the recognition of an Indigenous people, and a record year of visitors are connected, in different ways, to the long histories of authority, religion, gender, frontier, and exchange that earlier chapters traced.

The chapter also shows how unevenly change is experienced. A metropolitan professional, a rural elder, a migrant worker, an Ainu artisan, an Okinawan resident near a base, and a foreign student inhabit the same country differently. The unity implied by the name Japan has always been a construction that included, excluded, and negotiated among many communities.

It connects Japan to its neighbors and the world. Memory, trade, culture, and security tie Japan to China, Korea, the United States, and Southeast Asia, and the circulation of anime, food, and people shows that influence runs in several directions. What survives from earlier periods is not an unchanging essence but a habit of adaptation, together with persistent problems of hierarchy and inequality.

The chapter, and the book, leaves the reader at an open point. The institutions and choices described here will become evidence for later historians, who will see what we cannot. The task of the student, as in every chapter, is to read evidence carefully, to hold interpretation apart from fact, and to remember that the future remains to be made.
