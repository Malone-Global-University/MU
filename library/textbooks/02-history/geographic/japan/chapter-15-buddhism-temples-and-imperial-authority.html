Part III — Nara and Heian Japan
15

Buddhism, Temples, and Imperial Authority

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 752, at the eastern edge of the capital of Heijō-kyō, a bronze figure of the Buddha Vairocana, many times the height of a person, was brought to ceremonial completion before a gathering that included clerics from the temples of the realm, court aristocrats, and members of the imperial family. Nothing of this scale had been attempted in the archipelago. The project had absorbed metal, timber, labor, and money on a level that only a state could assemble, yet it had also drawn on monks, fundraisers, foundry specialists, and contributors who were not officials at all.

The Great Buddha of Tōdaiji is often presented as the summit of Nara-period religion and as a monument to the power of Emperor Shōmu. Both descriptions contain truth, but both can mislead. The image and its hall were the culmination of a decade in which the court had turned to Buddhist institutions with unusual intensity: ordering the establishment of monasteries and nunneries in the provinces, sponsoring the copying and recitation of sutras, and committing itself to a colossal act of patronage. Yet the same decade shows that Buddhism was not simply a tool held in the emperor's hand. Monasteries were communities with their own leaders, learning, property, and interests, and clerics could and did become actors in court politics.

Chapter 14 examined how the Nara state operated as an administrative system: its capital, its officials, its provinces, its roads, and its taxes. Chapter 15 takes up the religious material that chapter deliberately held back and asks how Buddhism became part of that machinery of government, and what happened when Buddhist institutions and clerics acquired political weight of their own. The question is not how Buddhism reached the archipelago. That transformation, including the Soga patronage, the temple building of the sixth and seventh centuries, and the roles of continental monks and craftsmen, belongs to Chapters 10 through 12. The question here is what changed when the ritsuryō state began organizing, financing, and regulating Buddhism on a much larger scale.

The central argument of the chapter is that Nara Buddhism was neither merely a private faith nor simply an instrument of the state. Rulers sponsored temples, sutra recitation, provincial monasteries and nunneries, ordination institutions, and monumental projects such as Tōdaiji because they believed, sincerely and within the assumptions of their age, that Buddhist ritual could protect the realm and support legitimate rulership. Precisely because they did so, monasteries accumulated personnel, wealth, land, intellectual authority, and institutional continuity. The relationship between court and clergy was therefore mutually reinforcing and inherently tense. A distinction runs through the whole chapter: state-sponsored Buddhism was not a single state-controlled church. Different temples, teachers, and traditions pursued different aims, and the court regulated more than it commanded.

The chapter also insists that Buddhism did not displace the older ritual world of the kami. The Nara court maintained the Department of Deities (Jingikan) and its calendar of kami rites even as it sponsored Buddhist ceremonies, and the case of the deity Hachiman shows Buddhist and kami traditions becoming entangled in the eighth century. Nor is the story restricted to the capital region. The provincial temple system tied Buddhist institutions to the same provincial administrative map studied in Chapter 14, so that religious infrastructure was extended into the provinces, at uneven speed and with uneven results, alongside the offices of provincial government.

The evidence for this chapter is unusually varied for the eighth century, and students should learn to distinguish its kinds. The chronicle Shoku Nihongi, compiled by court officials later in the century and completed in 797, records edicts and political events but reflects the viewpoint of the court and its successors. Temple traditions, such as those preserved by Tōdaiji itself, record institutional memory that may shape earlier events to fit later needs. The Shōsōin repository in Nara preserves an extraordinary body of administrative documents and objects, including records of sutra-copying offices. Archaeology of temple sites, pagoda foundations, and provincial remains adds material evidence. Missing from nearly all of it are the voices of the laborers, donors, and ordinary nuns and monks whose work made these institutions function.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Nara Buddhism as one of the principal institutions of authority in the eighth-century state, and should be able to explain why that partnership generated new centers of religious and political power that the court could not always control. The chapter closes with the Dōkyō affair of the 760s, one of the most contested episodes in early Japanese political history, and with a paradox that shapes the chapters that follow: the court made Buddhist institutions powerful because it needed their ritual, intellectual, and institutional resources, so their power was not an accidental failure of the system but partly a consequence of its success.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Nara court moved from patronage of particular temples to a broader system of state-sponsored Buddhism, and why that system was not a single state-controlled church.
  2. Analyze the political, epidemic, and fiscal crises of the 730s and 740s as contexts that intensified Buddhist patronage without reducing Shōmu's Buddhism to a reaction to disaster.
  3. Describe the provincial temple system established by the 741 edict and explain how it related to provincial administration and to local elites.
  4. Trace the development of Tōdaiji and the Great Buddha project from 728 to the 752 consecration.
  5. Interpret the symbolism of Vairocana and the Kegon (Avataṃsaka) vision for imperial authority while avoiding the oversimplification that the Buddha “was” the emperor.
  6. Identify the many participants in the Great Buddha project, including Rōben, Gyōki, artisans, laborers, and donors, and evaluate how their roles complicate a top-down narrative.
  7. Assess the relationship between Buddhist ritual and kami worship, using Hachiman as a case study of accommodation rather than replacement.
  8. Explain why monasteries became powerful institutions even when no cleric held political office.
  9. Evaluate how ordination, clerical registration, and the arrival of Ganjin shaped state regulation of monks and nuns.
  10. Distinguish the “Six Nara Schools” as scholastic traditions from modern denominations.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Dōkyō affair and the 769 Usa Hachiman oracle, applying source criticism to the Shoku Nihongi.
  12. Connect the chapter's findings to neighboring chapters on the Nara state, the provinces and frontiers, and the move to Heian-kyō.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Nara rulers use Buddhist institutions, rituals, monasteries, and clergy to strengthen imperial authority?
  2. Why did the same partnership create new centers of religious and political power that the court could not always control?
  3. In what sense was the Great Buddha a project of the state, and in what sense was it a project of many other people?
  4. Why might an eighth-century government have regarded investment in ritual as a form of governance rather than a distraction from it?
  5. How did Buddhist and kami ritual traditions interact in the Nara court, and why is a simple Buddhism-versus-Shinto binary misleading?
  6. What made a monastery powerful even without political office, and how did the court try to regulate clerical life?
  7. How should historians read a chronicle such as the Shoku Nihongi when it describes a monk who lost a political struggle?
  8. How did the provincial temple system depend on, and reshape, relationships between the court and provincial elites?

Historical Context

By the early eighth century Buddhism was no longer a contested foreign cult centered mainly on particular lineages. As Chapters 10 through 12 showed, its introduction had provoked political conflict among Yamato elites, and its early patrons, notably the Soga, had used temples, images, and continental specialists to build prestige. Sovereigns and royal princes, including the figures associated with the reign of Suiko and Prince Umayado, patronized temples and Buddhist learning. Continental monks, texts, artisans, and ritual knowledge, much of it arriving through Korean peninsular connections, gave the archipelago its first generation of major temples. By the late seventh century, the court itself was sponsoring temple foundations, and the rulers of the period after the Jinshin War supported Buddhist ceremonies on a scale unlike anything earlier.

Chapter 14 then described the framework within which Nara Buddhism operated. The Taihō and Yōrō codes organized government through ranked officials, central ministries, and a provincial system linked to the capital by roads. Heijō-kyō, established in 710, housed the court, the bureaucracy, and, increasingly, great temples transferred or newly founded around the city, including Daianji, Yakushiji, and Kōfukuji. The codes contained a section on monks and nuns, commonly known as the Sōniryō, which reflected a state determined to regulate clergy rather than leave religious life entirely to private arrangement. In other words, the legal architecture for state involvement in Buddhism existed before the great projects of Shōmu's reign.

Two features of the eighth-century religious landscape deserve emphasis. First, the Nara court did not have a single religion. It maintained a bureaucratic office for kami rites, the Department of Deities (Jingikan), alongside its Buddhist patronage, and it drew on continental Confucian and Chinese ritual models as well. Students should therefore avoid picturing a religious “switch” from an older religion to Buddhism. Second, Buddhism in this period was scholastic and ritual as much as devotional. The imported traditions included texts of doctrinal argument, the monastic discipline known as vinaya, and sutras regarded as powerful in themselves. Sutras such as the Golden Light Sutra (Konkōmyō-kyō) and the Lotus Sutra promised protection for rulers who upheld the teaching. Historians often describe the use of such texts as Buddhism for the protection of the realm (chingo kokka), a useful shorthand provided one avoids implying that eighth-century Buddhism formed one coherent state ideology.

Finally, the political situation of the 720s and 730s was unsettled. The reigns of Genshō, Shōmu, and their successors were marked by contention over succession, the ambitions of the Fujiwara and other lineages, and the difficulties of a tax-based state whose population and harvests were vulnerable to epidemic and crop failure. These conditions, discussed in Section 15.2, explain much about the timing and scale of Buddhist patronage. Material on rural society and frontier populations belongs to Chapter 16, and the later move to Heian-kyō is the subject of Chapter 17; this chapter concentrates on Buddhism, its institutions, and the authority relations involved.

Section 15.1

From Court Buddhism to State-Sponsored Buddhism

The story of Buddhism at the Nara court does not begin with Nara. Chapters 10 through 12 traced how Buddhism entered Yamato politics through Korean peninsular connections, how the Soga and their rivals fought over its acceptance, and how rulers associated with Suiko and Prince Umayado patronized temples and learning. What changed by the early eighth century was not the presence of Buddhism at court but its scale, its institutional density, and the degree to which the state regarded it as part of governance itself. Monks, nuns, sutras, images, and Buddhist ceremonies had become embedded in court life. The question now is what happened when a ritsuryō state, equipped with codes, registers, provincial offices, and a tax system, set out to organize and fund Buddhism across the realm.

Three earlier developments matter for what follows. First, the great temples of the late seventh century had already established the idea that a sovereign might found a major monastery as an act of state as well as devotion. Second, continental monks, texts, artisans, and ritual knowledge had created a reservoir of expertise that could be drawn on for large-scale construction and for the training of clergy. Third, the codes of the early eighth century had already defined a legal relationship between the state and the clergy through the Sōniryō, which treated monks and nuns as persons whose status, conduct, and movements were the concern of government. State involvement in Buddhism was therefore neither new in Shōmu's reign nor a purely royal preference. It was built into the legal and institutional culture of the state.

At this point a crucial distinction must be stated and then held throughout the chapter: state-sponsored Buddhism is not the same as a single state-controlled church. The court funded certain temples, appointed or confirmed certain clerical leaders, designated certain institutions as official, and set rules for ordination and conduct. But there was no unitary religious hierarchy answerable to a single head. Great temples such as Kōfukuji, Yakushiji, Daianji, and Tōdaiji had their own histories, patrons, and scholarly traditions. The Fujiwara supported Kōfukuji, and the imperial house had its own temple interests. Individual monks moved among institutions and sometimes between official and unofficial roles. What emerges is a field of institutions with overlapping relations to the state, not a bureaucratic church.

Evidence for this system comes from the legal codes, from the Shoku Nihongi, and from temple records. It is worth remembering whose perspective each represents. The codes describe how the state intended to regulate clergy, not how regulation worked in practice. The chronicle records edicts and appointments from the court's point of view, and a later generation of officials edited its language. Temple records reflect the priorities of the institution that kept them. Historians therefore reconstruct state-sponsored Buddhism by comparing these sources and by supplementing them with archaeology and with administrative documents such as those preserved in the Shōsōin.

Section 15.2

Crisis, Emperor Shōmu, and the Search for Protection

Emperor Shōmu came to the throne in 724 as a ruler already committed to Buddhism. To explain his patronage as a reaction to disaster is to misread both him and the evidence. What the crises of the 730s and 740s did was intensify the use of Buddhist ritual and institutions as technologies of protection and legitimation, and give the court an urgent reason to do so on a larger scale. The crises were several, and they overlapped.

The first was political. In 729 Prince Nagaya, a senior royal figure and minister, died after an accusation of forbidden sorcery in a politically charged succession struggle that Chapter 14 introduced; historians debate the extent of Fujiwara initiative and the motives behind the accusation. The second was epidemiological. Between 735 and 737 a smallpox epidemic, spreading through the capital and provinces, killed a very large but uncertain proportion of the population; modern estimates vary widely and rest on limited evidence. In 737 the four Fujiwara brothers who dominated the government died in the epidemic, shattering the political balance. The epidemic also reduced the number of taxpayers and workers on whom the state relied, creating fiscal difficulty. Harvest problems compounded the strain. The third crisis followed in 740, when Fujiwara no Hirotsugu, based in Kyushu, rose in rebellion against the court. The rebellion was suppressed, but Shōmu, apparently shaken, began a period of repeated moves of the court to Kuni, Naniwa, and Shigaraki before returning to Heijō in 745. The reasons for these moves are debated, and probably included security, symbolic renewal, and factional politics as well as religious concerns.

Against this background the court's turn to Buddhist ritual can be understood. In the assumptions of the eighth century, disease, drought, and rebellion were not only material events but signs and consequences of a disturbed relation between rulers and the powers governing the world, whether kami, buddhas, or Heaven in the Chinese political vocabulary. Rites, prayers, sutra recitation, and the sponsorship of religious institutions were regarded as effective forms of action. A government that wished to restore order would naturally deploy them. The sutras the court favored, especially the Golden Light Sutra, explicitly promised that the Four Heavenly Kings would protect rulers who upheld the teaching and honored the sangha. Historians describe this pattern as Buddhism for the protection of the realm, though, again, it should not be treated as a single systematic ideology. Different texts, different clerics, and different courtiers understood protection in different ways.

Students should also keep in mind that religious and political motives were not sharply separable for the participants. A modern reader may ask whether Shōmu was “really” pious or “merely” political. That question imposes categories the sources do not support. The court's Buddhist policy was at once an expression of belief, a strategy of legitimation, and a practical mobilization of resources. The Cause and Effect feature later in the chapter distinguishes the intensified patronage associated with these crises from longer institutional processes, including ordination reform, that were already underway.

Section 15.3

The Provincial Temple System: Kokubunji and Kokubunniji

In 741 Shōmu ordered the establishment of provincial monasteries and nunneries in the provinces of the realm. The monasteries, the kokubunji, and the nunneries, the kokubunniji, were intended to house communities of clergy who would recite sutras and perform rites for the protection and prosperity of the state. According to the traditional account, each monastery was to have a community of monks, each nunnery a smaller community of nuns, and each province was to erect a pagoda and to hold copies of designated sutras, notably the Golden Light Sutra and the Lotus Sutra. The monasteries were associated with the protection of the realm by the Four Heavenly Kings and the nunneries with the expiation of wrongdoing.

The system deserves attention because it links Chapter 15 directly to Chapter 14. The provincial administrative system centered on the provincial headquarters, the kokufu, where the governor and his staff supervised taxation, corvée, and local order. The provincial temples were meant to reproduce religious infrastructure across that administrative map: kokufu, provincial government, and provincial Buddhist institution together. The court was attempting to make the state visible and audible in the provinces through ritual as well as through officials and registers. Tōdaiji later stood at the head of this network as the “head” monastery (sōkokubunji), a status the temple's own tradition ties to the 741 edict.

It would be a serious error to imagine the entire network built instantly. An edict is an order, not a construction report. Implementation took years, and in some provinces decades. It depended on the resources of each province and on the cooperation of provincial elites, especially local magnates from families who had served as district administrators (gunji) and who had wealth, labor connections, and stakes in local prestige. Sites were chosen near the provincial headquarters, but not always adjacent to them, and archaeological investigations of provincial temple remains show variation in size, layout, and dating. The court repeatedly issued further instructions, incentives, and complaints about slow progress, which is itself evidence that compliance was uneven. In some regions, existing local temples were designated as provincial temples rather than newly built.

For local elites the system offered opportunity and burden. Contributing to construction or supporting the community could win court favor and rank, and the temples became centers of literacy, sutra copying, and Buddhist learning in the provinces. Yet construction and maintenance also required labor and materials extracted from a population already burdened by taxes and by the aftermath of epidemic. Provincial governors were responsible for the results, so the temples became another dimension of the relationship between the center and the provinces that Chapter 16 will explore from the perspective of rural society and frontiers.

Historical Vocabulary
Kokubunji and Kokubunniji

Kokubunji (“provincial monastery”) and kokubunniji (“provincial nunnery”) were the official Buddhist institutions ordered for each province by Shōmu's edict of 741. They were tied to provincial government, funded through provincial resources, and charged with sutra recitation and rites for the protection of the realm. The monasteries are traditionally linked with the Golden Light Sutra and the nunneries with the Lotus Sutra.

The modern word “national temple” can mislead. These were not branches of a centralized church. They were provincial institutions with varying resources and local relationships, connected to the court by patronage, regulation, and the head-monastery status of Tōdaiji. Their importance lay in extending Buddhist ritual infrastructure across the administrative map of the state.

Section 15.4

Tōdaiji and the Great Buddha

Tōdaiji is the center of gravity of this chapter because its history brings together nearly every theme: state patronage, provincial administration, cosmic symbolism, mass labor, clerical leadership, and political risk. The institution's own history, preserved by the temple, places the provincial temple edict of 741, the 743 Great Buddha proclamation, construction after the return to Heijō, completion of the image in 749, and consecration in 752 within one expanding state-sponsored project. Following the temple's tradition, the origins of the site lie in 728, when a mountain temple associated with Crown Prince Motoi, Shōmu and Kōmyō's infant son who died young, was established east of the capital. The site thus began as a small imperial-family foundation with mourning and memorial significance, not as a national monument.

The elevation came in stages. In 741 Kinshō-ji, the predecessor of Tōdaiji, was elevated as Yamato's provincial monastery under the new provincial-temple system. The institution that developed into Tōdaiji subsequently served as the chief temple of that network. In the tenth month of 743, while the court was at Shigaraki, Shōmu proclaimed his intention to construct a colossal image of Vairocana (in Japanese, Birushana or Rushana), as discussed in the Primary Source Window below. Work began at Shigaraki but shifted to Nara after the court returned to Heijō in 745. The casting was a complex technical undertaking. The image was cast in stages in bronze, over several years, and substantially completed in 749; the discovery of gold in Mutsu Province in the northeast that year, a rare and welcome resource for gilding, was celebrated by the court. The Great Buddha Hall was completed around 751. The consecration ceremony of 752, often called the “eye-opening,” was presided over by a foreign monk, Bodhisena (Bodai Senna), from India via China, with Shōmu, who had abdicated in 749 in favor of his daughter Kōken, attending as retired sovereign. The ceremony included performances from several continental and local traditions, and objects from the event survive in the Shōsōin.

What did this image mean? The Vairocana of the Avataṃsaka (Kegon) tradition is a cosmic Buddha whose light pervades the universe and within whom all worlds and beings are interconnected. For a ruler wishing to organize people and resources on an unprecedented scale, such a figure offered a powerful image of ordered and interdependent universality. Shōmu himself, in the proclamation, connected the project to the well-being of all beings and to the idea of a collective undertaking. But it is important to avoid the tempting formula that the Great Buddha “symbolized the emperor as Buddha.” The sources do not support such a simple equation, and historians debate how far Vairocana and the sovereign were linked in the minds of contemporaries. A more defensible statement is that the monument connected imperial patronage, Buddhist cosmology, the protection of the realm, and the court's claim to mobilize the population and resources of the archipelago for a common purpose.

The project also had costs. Metal, charcoal, timber, and food had to be assembled and transported. Later accounts and temple tradition report very large numbers of workers, and while the figures given in later sources are not reliable and should be treated with caution, there is no doubt that the effort was enormous by contemporary standards. Some historians see in the Great Buddha both an act of devotion and a fiscal strain on a state already coping with epidemic and population loss. The court's ability to undertake the project also depended on cooperation from many participants outside the formal apparatus of government, as the next section shows.

Historical Vocabulary
Vairocana (Birushana) and Kegon

Vairocana (Japanese Birushana or Rushana) is the cosmic Buddha of the Avataṃsaka Sutra (Japanese Kegon-kyō), an immense text that describes a universe of interpenetrating worlds. The Kegon tradition, which studied and elaborated this teaching, was one of the scholastic traditions active in Nara, and Tōdaiji became closely associated with it.

Vairocana's cosmic character made the image appropriate to monumental patronage that aimed at universal scope. Students should nevertheless avoid two errors. One is equating the Buddha simply with the emperor. The other is treating Tōdaiji merely as the headquarters of a Kegon “sect” in the modern denominational sense. Nara Buddhism was organized around scholastic traditions and institutional networks, not around exclusive denominations.

Primary Source Window

Shōmu's 743 Proclamation of the Great Buddha

Creator: The court of Emperor Shōmu; preserved in the Shoku Nihongi, compiled by court officials and completed in 797 · Date: 743 (tenth month), Shigaraki · Type: Imperial proclamation preserved in an official chronicle

Context: Shōmu announced his intention to build a great image of Vairocana while the court was at the temporary palace of Shigaraki, during the period of moves between capitals. The text survives not as an original document but as recorded in the later-compiled Shoku Nihongi, which means that it reaches us through an official editorial process. No verified translation is quoted here; the following is a paraphrase of its main themes.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): The proclamation presents the project as a means of bringing benefit to all living beings and speaks in universalizing terms. It notes that the sovereign holds the wealth and power of the realm and could accomplish the task by that authority, but that this would not express the sincere intent of the work. It invites those who wish to join the project willingly to do so, however modest their contribution, and it instructs officials not to disturb or coerce the people in the name of the undertaking.

Analysis: The document reveals how the court wished the project to be understood: as a collective and voluntary religious act with universal aims, not simply an exercise of imperial command. The appeal to voluntary participation and the warning to officials against coercion suggest that the court anticipated resentment and needed to present the project as legitimate, though we cannot tell from the text how far practice matched rhetoric. As a court text preserved in a chronicle, it represents the viewpoint of the sovereign and his officials. It tells us little about the laborers who cast the bronze, the taxpayers whose goods were requisitioned, or the provincial contributors who did or did not give willingly. The wording of the version we have may also reflect later editing.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the purpose of the Great Buddha and about the way the sovereign wished it to be built?
  2. Who created the source, for what audience, and through what process did it reach us?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the relationship between the court and the population?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Section 15.5

Building Sacred Power: Rōben, Gyōki, Artisans, and Donors

The Great Buddha was not created by imperial decree alone. A top-down narrative in which the emperor commands and the realm obeys captures only part of the story. Behind the monument lay a range of participants: senior clerics, court officials, foundry specialists, carpenters, transport workers, provincial contributors, donors, and a mass of laborers whose names are unrecorded.

Among the clerics, Rōben (689–773) stands out. A monk closely associated with Tōdaiji and with the Kegon teaching, he is credited by the temple's own tradition as a major figure in realizing the project. Rōben represents institutional Buddhist leadership: a cleric working within the temple and the official framework, providing doctrinal legitimacy, ritual leadership, and continuity. His long career, extending to 773, spans the entire arc from the founding of the site through the aftermath of the Dōkyō period and illustrates how monks could become durable participants in court religious life.

Gyōki (668–749) represents a different route to influence. Gyōki was a monk who had worked among the common people, organizing groups, building facilities such as bridges, embankments, and rest houses, and gathering followers. In 717 the government had issued a directive restricting unauthorized preaching and the activities of monks operating outside official supervision, and Gyōki's activities were among those affected. By the 740s, however, his relationship to official authority had changed. He and his networks became valuable to the Great Buddha project, particularly in mobilizing contributions and support (a practice sometimes called kanjin, the solicitation of donations), and he was eventually given the highest clerical rank, Daisōjō, in 745. He died in 749.

Students should resist the old textbook caricature of Gyōki as a popular saint persecuted by an oppressive government and then suddenly embraced. The evidence suggests a more complex development. The government's earlier restrictions reflected genuine concern about unregulated religious activity and about people leaving the tax rolls, while its later use of Gyōki's networks reflected pragmatic recognition that such networks could mobilize resources. Gyōki himself was neither simply a rebel nor simply a government agent. His career illustrates a general pattern in this chapter: the court both regulated and drew upon Buddhist leaders and communities that operated with considerable autonomy.

The material work fell to specialists and laborers. Bronze casting required foundry expertise, furnaces, and enormous quantities of metal and fuel, and the temple later organized a construction office to supervise building of the wider complex. Carpenters raised the Great Buddha Hall, which was among the largest wooden buildings of its time. Transport workers moved timber and metal to the site, often from distant provinces, adding to the burden on provincial populations. The state could draw on labor obligations and provincial resources, while other participants contributed through donation networks or were supported by temple and state resources. The proportions and conditions are difficult to reconstruct, and the work was dangerous, as bronze casting and construction at this scale always are.

Multiple Perspectives

Building the Great Buddha: Five Positions

Note: these perspectives are reconstructed from institutional evidence and general historical inference, not from first-person testimony. The groups did not possess equal power or equal freedom of action.

Emperor Shōmu: For the sovereign, the monument promised protection of the realm and a public demonstration of the court's ability to organize people and resources around a shared religious purpose, at a moment when epidemic and rebellion had shaken confidence in the state.

Provincial contributors and taxpayers: Local elites might hope for prestige and favor by supporting the project, while some ordinary households could face additional demands for labor and goods at a time of epidemic aftermath and fiscal strain.
Section 15.6

Buddhist Ritual, Kami, and the Protection of the Realm

A chapter on state Buddhism risks implying that Buddhism replaced an older religion. The evidence shows something different. The Nara state still maintained the Department of Deities (Jingikan), the office responsible for kami rites and for the seasonal ceremonies that the codes treated as part of good government. Imperial rituals connected to ancestral kami and to agricultural cycles continued, and the court's religious life included Chinese-influenced ceremonies as well. Buddhist patronage was added to this world, not substituted for it. The relationship between Buddhist and kami traditions is best described as one of early accommodation and interaction, not as either conflict or a mature synthesis. The later, much more developed pattern often called shinbutsu shūgō (the combination of kami and buddhas) was a product of centuries of medieval development, and one should not read it back into the eighth century in complete form.

A particularly instructive case is Hachiman, a deity worshipped at Usa in northern Kyushu, in a region with strong ties to the Korean peninsula and to continental religious practice. Scholarship emphasizes that Hachiman's connection to the Great Buddha project was crucial to the deity's dramatic rise in court religious importance in the eighth century. According to the tradition recorded in the sources, the deity issued a favorable oracle regarding the project and traveled, in the form of a ceremonial procession, to the capital in 749, receiving court rank and, later, a shrine within the Tōdaiji precinct. The episode is important for two reasons. It shows that a provincial kami cult could be raised into the national religious framework through Buddhist patronage. And it shows that Buddhist institutions and kami cults could reinforce each other's prestige: Hachiman gained court recognition, while Tōdaiji gained an endorsement that presented the project as supported by a powerful native deity.

The ritual repertoire of Nara Buddhism included sutra recitation and copying, rites against disease and disaster, ceremonies for the well-being of the sovereign, memorial services, and large public assemblies. The court's sutra-copying offices (shakyōjo), whose administrative records survive in the Shōsōin, employed scribes, proofreaders, and other staff who copied texts for temples and for the court. Such rites and copying projects were understood as productive acts, generating merit and protection. This raises a question that students should consider directly: why would a government invest scarce resources in ritual?

The answer is that, within eighth-century assumptions, ritual was political action, not decorative ornamentation around “real” politics. Rulers derived legitimacy in part from their ability to perform ceremonies that maintained cosmic and social order, and a ruler who neglected them would have been vulnerable to criticism. Ritual also created occasions where the court could gather elites, display hierarchy, distribute rank and gifts, and communicate its authority. At the same time, participation in Buddhist rites could bind clerics to the court and turn institutions into stakeholders in the regime's survival. Recognizing the political logic of ritual does not require denying that participants sincerely believed in its efficacy. Both dimensions coexisted.

Everyday Life and Environment

Novices, Scribes, and Temple Artisans

The lives of aristocrats and high clerics dominate the chronicles, but a great temple was also a workplace for many people of lower status. A novice entering a temple as a young person would have been part of a community organized around study, ritual, and labor. Novices learned to read and recite texts, assisted senior monks, and worked toward ordination, which the state increasingly controlled. Some entered for religious vocation, some for family reasons or the prospect of education and security, and some, in a state that taxed households and levied labor, may have seen clerical status as a means of escape from tax obligations, a concern that helps explain why the government regulated ordination so carefully. The situation of nuns is documented less fully, but the nunneries of the provincial system and of the capital region gave some women a religious career within the state's framework.

Scribes in the sutra-copying offices are among the best-documented workers of the period, because administrative records preserved in the Shōsōin list assignments, materials, and work output. These records reveal a bureaucratic workplace with supervisors, salaried scribes, quotas, and inspection, and they show that copying sutras was organized labor as well as devotion. Temple artisans, from metalworkers to carpenters and painters, also occupied a social world that the chronicles rarely describe. These records remind students that religious institutions were embedded in the economy of the state and that their activity depended on people of modest rank whose names mostly do not survive.

Section 15.7

Monasteries as Institutions

To understand why Buddhist institutions became politically consequential, one must look beyond ritual and consider what a great monastery actually was. A major Nara temple was a religious community, a landholder, an employer, a workshop complex, a school, a library or archive, a ritual institution, an administrative organization, and a recipient of donations and government patronage. These functions reinforced each other. Land and donations supported clergy and workers; workers maintained buildings and produced objects; clergy studied, copied texts, and performed rites that attracted patrons; patrons gave more land and gifts.

Tōdaiji illustrates the pattern. It developed specialized offices, a construction administration supervising the growth of the wider complex, monks' quarters, teaching facilities, and substantial resources. Its records and the Shōsōin collection show links between the temple, the court, and the wider economy. Other great temples were similarly complex. Kōfukuji, for instance, had close ties with the Fujiwara and served as an institutional base for that lineage's religious and political interests. In this sense, a monastery was a locus of power even if no monk held a government post.

What made a monastery powerful without political office? The answer lies in a combination of institutional continuity, resources, literacy, ritual expertise, property, personnel, and relationships with elites. A monastery outlived individual rulers and ministers. It held records and taught reading and writing, giving it a relationship to the literate culture on which government depended. Its ritual experts were needed by the court in moments of crisis. Its property, including lands granted by the court, gave it an economic base. Its clergy had networks across the archipelago through temple connections and, after the provincial temple system, across the provinces.

Students should be careful not to overstate these developments. The great estates that later temples and shrines controlled, and the armed monastic communities of the medieval centuries, belong to a different historical stage, examined in Chapter 20 and later chapters. In the Nara period the state still held significant control through land regulation and taxation, and temples' holdings were subject to limits and adjustments. Yet the direction of development is clear: by concentrating resources and prestige in religious institutions, the court created entities with a capacity for independent action that could later be used in ways the state neither planned nor welcomed.

Section 15.8

Monks, Nuns, Ordination, and the Regulation of Clergy

Becoming a recognized monk or nun in eighth-century Japan was not simply a personal spiritual decision. The state tried to regulate ordination, clerical registration, ranks and offices, authorized monasteries, and the movement and conduct of clergy. The reasons were practical as well as religious. In a tax-based state, the clergy were exempt from many obligations, so uncontrolled entry into clerical life could reduce revenue and labor supply. Unregulated preachers and wandering religious specialists could also be seen as sources of disorder, as the 717 measures affecting Gyōki suggest. Central supervision operated through the Genba-ryō, a bureau of the Ministry of Civil Affairs that handled Buddhist temples and clerical registers, and through the Sōgō, the hierarchy of senior clerical officials (such as the sōjō, sōzu, and risshi) formally under its jurisdiction, who supervised the Buddhist community and served as intermediaries between the court and the clergy.

Within this regulatory framework came the arrival of Ganjin (Chinese: Jianzhen, 688–763), a Chinese vinaya master invited to Japan to provide a fully valid ordination lineage. The search for qualified ordaining masters predated the Great Buddha project: the Japanese monks Yōei and Fushō had gone to Tang China in 733 for this purpose, and Ganjin's journey, after several failed attempts at the crossing, was famously arduous. He reached Kyushu in 753, arrived at the capital in 754, and presided over formal ordination at Tōdaiji, where retired Emperor Shōmu, Empress Kōmyō, Empress Kōken, and others reportedly received the precepts. A permanent ordination institution, the Kaidan-in, followed at Tōdaiji, and Ganjin later established Tōshōdaiji. By 761, additional official ordination platforms were created at Yakushiji in Shimotsuke province in the east and Kanzeonji in Kyushu, extending access to formal ordination beyond the capital region.

Ganjin's significance must be described precisely. He did not introduce Buddhism to Japan, which had arrived centuries earlier. He transformed ordination and vinaya institutions, giving Japanese Buddhism a formal ordination lineage in line with continental practice. Nor is it correct to say that every legitimate Japanese monk after 754 had to be ordained personally by Ganjin; the system expanded through platforms and authorized preceptors, and other routes to clerical status persisted. The analytical point is that ordination gave monks religious status while making clerical identity legible to the state. A person ordained at an official platform was recognized within a system that also defined obligations and privileges.

Historical Vocabulary
Ordination, Kaidan, and Clerical Registration

Ordination is the formal ceremony by which a person becomes a fully recognized monk (sō) or nun (ni) by receiving the monastic precepts. A kaidan is the ordination platform or precinct where this ceremony is conducted under the supervision of qualified preceptors. Clerical registration refers to the state's recording of recognized clergy, which allowed the government to distinguish authorized monks and nuns, with their privileges and obligations, from unauthorized religious practitioners.

Modern readers may think of ordination as a purely internal religious matter. In Nara Japan it was also an administrative status, which is why the court cared about who was ordained, where, and under whose authority.

Section 15.9

Buddhist Learning and the “Six Nara Schools”

If temples existed only for ritual and politics, they would be much less interesting to historians of thought. But Nara monasteries were also centers of study. Traditions of learning, later grouped under the label of the “Six Nara Schools,” included Sanron, Jōjitsu, Hossō, Kusha, Kegon, and Ritsu. Sanron drew on Madhyamaka philosophy and its Chinese elaboration; Jōjitsu was a related tradition often studied alongside it; Hossō concerned the analysis of consciousness and was closely associated with Kōfukuji; Kusha studied the Abhidharma treatise of Vasubandhu; Kegon centered on the Avataṃsaka Sutra and became associated with Tōdaiji; and Ritsu focused on monastic discipline, a concern that Ganjin's arrival strengthened.

A major warning applies. These should not be described as six modern-style denominations with mutually exclusive memberships. The label “Six Nara Schools” is a later convenience. In the eighth century, these were primarily scholastic traditions or bodies of teaching studied within institutional settings, and monks could engage with more than one. Tōdaiji itself should not be presented merely as the church headquarters of a Kegon sect in the modern sense; it was a large institution with multiple study traditions and multiple functions. The later development of exclusive sectarian identities belongs to the medieval period, when new movements examined in Chapter 26 created denominations of a different kind.

Learning mattered politically because monasteries accumulated intellectual authority. They trained people in texts, languages, doctrine, ritual, law, and administration. Clerics knew Chinese, understood continental learning, and had access to books and scholarly networks. In a state that relied on Chinese-style law and writing, such knowledge was valuable, and clerics could advise, serve as ritualists, and provide legitimacy. This intellectual capital helps explain why clergy could become politically important, and why the court's patronage of learned monasteries was inseparable from the risk that some clerics would come to hold influence beyond their institutional role.

Section 15.10

Dōkyō and the Problem of Religious Political Power

By the 760s the issue was no longer simply whether rulers could use monks. It became what happened when a monk gained extraordinary access to rulership. The figure at the center of the question is Dōkyō, a cleric who rose to prominence in the reign of Empress Kōken and her second reign as Empress Shōtoku.

According to the traditional account, Dōkyō gained influence with the retired Kōken after attending her during an illness in the early 760s. The political situation was changing. Fujiwara no Nakamaro, a powerful minister who had supported the intervening emperor Junnin, rebelled in 764 and was defeated. Kōken returned to the throne as Empress Shōtoku, and Dōkyō received unprecedented positions, including a high ministerial title, and in 766 the title Hōō, “Dharma King.” Buddhist ceremonial and offices were woven into government in a way earlier courts had not seen, and the monk exercised power through his relationship with the sovereign.

The crisis came in 769. The traditional account holds that an oracle attributed to Hachiman at Usa declared that Dōkyō should be made sovereign. Wake no Kiyomaro, an official, was sent to Usa to confirm the oracle. He returned with a contrary report stressing that succession belonged to the imperial line, and he was punished for it, reportedly exiled and given a humiliating name. Then in 770 Empress Shōtoku died, Dōkyō lost his position, and he was sent to Shimotsuke, where he died in 772. Succession went to Kōnin, a figure from a different branch of the imperial family, in a political realignment that Chapter 17 will take up.

This account requires strong source criticism. Much of our narrative derives from the Shoku Nihongi, compiled after Dōkyō's fall by officials who served the successor regime. Its treatment of him as a threat to the throne may reflect the political interests of those who wrote it, not neutral reportage. Students should not state as unquestioned fact that Dōkyō tried to seize the throne, nor that he personally fabricated the oracle or certainly intended usurpation. The precise roles of Dōkyō, Shōtoku, officials at Dazaifu, Hachiman clergy, Wake no Kiyomaro, and court factions remain subjects of interpretation.

Historical Debate

Was the Dōkyō Affair a Buddhist Challenge to Imperial Rule?

The question: Did Dōkyō and the 769 oracle represent an attempt by a cleric to take the throne, or should the episode be understood differently? The problem is partly one of evidence: the sources that describe the affair were shaped by its political aftermath.

Interpretation A — A clerical threat to the political order: On this reading, the concentration of court rank, ceremonial status, and personal influence in a monk was unprecedented and dangerous. The oracle reflects an attempt, or at least a serious possibility, that clerical authority might displace the imperial line, and the reaction at court reflects a defense of established succession.
Interpretation B — A succession and factional struggle: On this reading, the affair was fundamentally a court struggle over succession in which Buddhist office and oracle claims provided political tools. Empress Shōtoku had no designated heir, aristocratic factions competed, and the narrative of a monk seeking the throne was constructed or amplified by winners to discredit a rival and justify the new order.
Interpretation C — Religion and politics inseparable in the Nara court: On this reading, the episode shows why the categories of “religious” and “political” cannot be cleanly separated in the eighth century. Hachiman, Buddhist clergy, aristocrats, and sovereign authority all participated in one political-religious arena, and the oracle question could be raised only because of that shared world.

Evidence: The interpretations depend on the Shoku Nihongi, on records of appointments and titles, and on what is known of Hachiman's role at Usa and Tōdaiji. The overlapping evidence supports elements of each interpretation, and these interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: How historians read the affair shapes how they explain the later avoidance of a comparable concentration of formal political authority in a cleric, the shift in the political landscape after 770, and the relation between religious institutions and imperial authority in later periods.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation and explain what additional evidence, if it existed, would help resolve the question. Consider whose interests the surviving chronicle served.

The chapter's paradox can now be stated plainly. The court made Buddhist institutions powerful because it needed their ritual, intellectual, and institutional resources. Their power was therefore not an accidental failure of the system; it was partly a consequence of the system's success. The Dōkyō affair did not end Buddhism's role in imperial government, and it should not be read as a simple turning point against Buddhism. Later chapters will show a court that continued to sponsor Buddhism, including new movements from the ninth century, while also seeking to prevent monks from holding such extraordinary concentrations of political rank. Whether the subsequent relocation of the capital was in part a reaction to Nara's powerful temples is a question for Chapter 17, which must weigh multiple causes and should not be reduced to that single explanation.

Cause and Effect

Crisis and the Expansion of Buddhist Institutions

Long-term causes

A century and a half of court patronage of Buddhism; a legal framework regulating clergy; continental religious knowledge and craft expertise; and the idea, drawn from sutras, that rulers who protected the teaching would receive protection in return.

Short-term causes

Political conflict after Nagaya's death, the smallpox epidemic of 735–737, the deaths of the Fujiwara brothers, fiscal strain, Hirotsugu's 740 rebellion, and the court's instability during its moves between capitals.

Event or transformation

The provincial temple edict of 741 and the Great Buddha proclamation of 743 expanded state Buddhist patronage, while an independently developing effort to regularize ordination culminated in new formal ordination institutions.

Immediate effects

Buddhist institutions spread into provinces, drawing on local elites and resources; Tōdaiji became a major center of ritual and learning; Hachiman rose in court religion; monks and nuns became more closely registered and regulated.

Long-term consequences

Monasteries became durable institutions with resources and intellectual authority, creating new centers of power. The Dōkyō crisis of the 760s revealed how far clerical influence could reach at court.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the expansion. Piety, legitimation, crisis management, and the momentum of institutions all mattered, and the participants themselves did not separate these motives as neatly as modern readers might.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked how Buddhism became one of the principal institutions of authority in the Nara state, and when that relationship became politically dangerous. It began from the recognition that Buddhism was already embedded in court life by the early eighth century, and that the question was therefore what changed when the ritsuryō state began to organize, fund, and regulate it on a larger scale. The chapter's governing distinction was that state-sponsored Buddhism was not a single state-controlled church but a field of institutions with overlapping relationships to the court.

The crises of the 730s and 740s, including the Nagaya conflict, the smallpox epidemic of 735–737, the deaths of the four Fujiwara brothers, fiscal strain, and Hirotsugu's rebellion, intensified the use of Buddhist ritual and institutions as technologies of protection and legitimation. Shōmu was already a Buddhist ruler, and disaster did not convert him; it deepened and enlarged a commitment that suited both his beliefs and his need to restore confidence in the state.

The 741 edict for provincial monasteries and nunneries attempted to reproduce Buddhist infrastructure across the administrative map studied in Chapter 14, linking the provincial headquarters, provincial government, and provincial temple. Implementation was slow and uneven, depending on local resources and elites, and the system reveals both the ambition and the limits of central authority.

Tōdaiji and the Great Buddha formed the center of this project. From its origins as an imperial-family mountain temple in 728, through the elevation of its predecessor temple in 741, the proclamation of 743, the casting completed in 749, and the consecration of 752, the temple came to embody a claim that Buddhist cosmology, imperial patronage, and the mobilization of the realm's resources could work together. The Vairocana image should be understood as linking these elements, not as a simple identification of the emperor with the Buddha.

The project was not the work of a sovereign alone. Rōben, Gyōki, court officials, foundry specialists, carpenters, transport workers, provincial contributors, and unnamed laborers all played roles, and their interests were not identical. Gyōki's career, moving from official restriction to high rank, resists both the caricature of a persecuted saint and the picture of a government agent.

Buddhism did not replace the older ritual world of the kami. The Jingikan continued to function, and the rise of Hachiman through the Great Buddha project shows Buddhist and kami traditions interacting and reinforcing each other. Ritual, in the assumptions of the age, was political action, and it provided both legitimacy and occasions for the court to display and exercise authority.

Monasteries became powerful because of what they were: communities, landholders, workshops, schools, archives, and ritual specialists whose continuity outlasted individual rulers. The state responded by regulating ordination, registration, ranks, and conduct, and Ganjin's arrival in 753–754 gave that regulation a formal vinaya-based institutional basis at Tōdaiji and later at other platforms. Learning in the traditions later called the Six Nara Schools further concentrated intellectual authority in monastic settings, though these were scholastic traditions and not modern denominations.

The Dōkyō affair of the 760s tested the limits of religious political power. Dōkyō's rise under Empress Shōtoku, his title of Hōō in 766, and the Usa Hachiman oracle of 769 are known chiefly through a chronicle written by his successors' officials, and historians debate whether the episode was a clerical challenge to imperial rule, a succession and factional struggle, or evidence of the inseparability of religion and politics in the Nara court. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

The chapter's paradox is that the court made Buddhist institutions powerful because it needed their ritual, intellectual, and institutional resources, so their power was partly a consequence of the system's success. That paradox carries forward into the next chapters: Chapter 16 will examine how the state and its institutions appeared in provinces and frontier zones, and Chapter 17 will consider why the court left Nara and how a new political order took shape at Heian-kyō.

Investigating a Nara Temple and Its Evidence

This project asks you to investigate one institution or source group from Nara-period Buddhism, connecting specific evidence to the larger question of how Buddhist institutions and imperial authority related.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state built its authority not only through offices, laws, and taxes but through institutions of ritual and learning. The Nara court turned to Buddhism as a resource of legitimacy and protection, and in doing so it created communities whose continuity, property, and intellectual authority would outlast the rulers who founded them. The story of Tōdaiji is the story of a state acting on the world and a set of institutions acting back on the state.

The chapter also connects different regions and populations. The provincial temple system tied the Kinai to provincial capitals, Hachiman tied Kyushu to Nara, and the contributions of laborers and donors tied households across the realm to a project at the capital. At the same time, the chapter has drawn on continental connections: the texts, techniques, and monks that shaped Nara Buddhism came through Korea and China, and figures such as Ganjin and Bodhisena show that these connections continued to matter in the eighth century. The developments of this chapter belong to a wider East Asian Buddhist world and were not merely local.

Later developments would revisit its questions. The relationship of monasteries to the state, the accumulation of temple resources, the mingling of kami and buddha traditions, and the concern about clerical political influence recur in the Heian court and beyond. This chapter should not be read as explaining the move from Nara in a simple way, and it does not show that Buddhism was a failed experiment in state religion. What it shows is a durable pattern in which political authority and religious institutions needed each other and could not fully control each other.

Students who understand this relationship are prepared for Chapter 16, which turns from the center to the provinces and frontiers, asking what the state and its institutions looked like to people beyond the court, including provincial elites, rural communities, and the Emishi. And they are prepared for Chapter 17, where the court's move to a new capital and the emergence of the Heian political order must be explained through many causes, of which the legacy of Nara's powerful temples is only one.
