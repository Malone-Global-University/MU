Part XI — Empire, Militarism, and Total War

Manchukuo, Militarism, and the Transformation of the State

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On March 1, 1934, in the new capital of Hsinking (Changchun), Puyi, the last Qing emperor, was enthroned as emperor of Manchukuo. The ceremony gave the state a monarch. It did not give him control of it.

Two years earlier, in March 1932, the Kwantung Army had proclaimed Manchukuo in territory it had seized after the Manchurian Incident of September 1931. Chapter 74 explained that conspiracy and the collapse of party government that followed. This chapter asks what was built afterward: how an army-created regime in Northeast China actually worked, and how military influence became embedded in the institutions of Japan itself.

Two transformations run through the chapter. In Manchuria, a formally independent state, staffed by Chinese, Manchu, Mongol and Japanese officials, was governed in practice through Japanese advisers, military oversight, railway and industrial power, settlement schemes, and police coercion. In Japan, the end of party cabinets, factional struggle within the army, the repression of dissent, the attack on constitutional theory, and the February 26 Incident of 1936 shifted power toward the services without abolishing the Constitution, the Diet, or the civilian bureaucracy.

The chapter's central argument is that military power expanded less by replacing existing institutions than by entering, redirecting, and exploiting them. The two stories were connected, since Manchukuo supplied territory, resources, and a laboratory for state-directed development, but one did not inevitably produce the other. The chapter ends in early 1937, before the full-scale war of Chapter 76.

Evidence includes diplomatic records, the proceedings of the Tokyo War Crimes Tribunal, National Diet Library documentation, Diet debates, Mantetsu research, court-martial records, newspapers, and memoirs. Most were created by officials, and the voices of Manchukuo's farmers, laborers, and resisters survive mainly through those officials' reports and later testimony. Figures here are approximate.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Manchukuo was proclaimed and why its legitimacy was disputed.
  2. Distinguish the formal institutions of Manchukuo from the Japanese authority that directed them.
  3. Analyze the role of Puyi and of the Concordia Association's ideology of harmony among peoples.
  4. Trace Mantetsu, heavy industry, and planning, separating plans from results.
  5. Evaluate Japanese agricultural settlement and its effect on local cultivators.
  6. Describe the differing experiences of Chinese, Manchu, Mongol, Korean, and Japanese residents, and the resistance to Japanese rule.
  7. Explain why the end of party cabinets did not end constitutional politics.
  8. Compare the Imperial Way and Control factions without treating either as unified.
  9. Assess the Minobe controversy and thought control as constraints on public debate.
  10. Interpret the February 26 Incident and the restoration of the active-duty minister rule.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of Manchukuo and of Japan's militarization.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How can a state be formally independent and yet governed by another power, and what evidence reveals the difference?
  2. Why did Japanese planners need a Qing emperor and a rhetoric of harmony among peoples?
  3. What distinguishes plans for Manchukuo's economy and settlement from what was accomplished?
  4. How did different communities in Manchukuo experience the same policies?
  5. Why did military influence grow while parties, elections and the Diet continued to function?
  6. How far were factional rivalries about ideology, and how far about institutional interests?
  7. Why did the defeat of the February 26 rebels strengthen military leverage rather than restore civilian supremacy?
  8. How should historians connect Manchukuo to changes in Japan without treating the later war as inevitable?

Historical Context

Japan's presence in southern Manchuria dated from the Russo-Japanese War (Chapter 61), which gave it the Kwantung Leased Territory and the South Manchuria Railway, protected by the Kwantung Army. Manchuria was also home to tens of millions of Han Chinese, many of them recent migrants from Shandong and Hebei, along with Manchus, Mongols, Koreans, and Russians, and it was contested among Chinese warlord rule, Soviet interests, and Japanese ambitions (Chapter 62). Chapter 74 described how officers in the Kwantung Army, acting beyond Tokyo's authority, seized the region in 1931–1932, and how the assassinations of 1932 ended party-led government.

At home, the Meiji Constitution never made cabinets responsible to the Diet (Chapter 59). Party cabinets of the 1920s rested on custom, not law, and on constitutional interpretation that army and navy leaders, the Privy Council, the court and the House of Peers could all challenge. The Depression and rural distress (Chapter 73) intensified demands for national "renovation," and the repression of the left under the Peace Preservation Law (Chapter 69) provided tools later turned against a broader range of critics.

Division I — The Construction of Manchukuo
Section 75.1

From Military Occupation to the Establishment of Manchukuo, 1932

By early 1932 the Kwantung Army controlled the main cities of Manchuria, but a military occupation of Chinese territory was diplomatically costly. Officers such as Itagaki Seishirō and Ishiwara Kanji, who had planned the Incident, preferred a nominally independent state under local figures to direct annexation. The army recruited Chinese and Manchu officials, among them Zheng Xiaoxu, Zhang Jinghui, and Xi Qia, and organized "independence" movements in provinces. On March 1, 1932, Manchukuo was proclaimed, with the capital at Changchun, renamed Hsinking ("new capital"). Puyi became chief executive on March 9.

Legitimacy was disputed from the start. The League of Nations' Lytton Commission (1932) criticized the claim that the state arose from a spontaneous local movement. Japan recognized Manchukuo on September 15, 1932, signing a protocol that allowed Japanese troops to remain, and withdrew from the League in 1933. Few other states extended recognition; Germany, Italy, and some Japanese partners later did. China never accepted the regime.

Japanese planners faced a practical problem after the main cities and rail junctions had been occupied. Direct annexation would have been difficult to reconcile with international treaties and Japan's stated commitment to China's territorial integrity. Leaving military units to administer an indefinite occupation would also have placed routine taxation, schools, policing, and communications in the hands of an army designed for other tasks. An allegedly sovereign Manchurian state offered a way to translate battlefield success into a durable political order while reducing the appearance of annexation. Kwantung Army officers encouraged local declarations of independence and cultivated prominent figures from the old northeastern political hierarchy. This process had indigenous participants, but its timing, territory, and coercive framework were set by Japanese power. Calling Manchukuo simply a product of Japanese orders misses local bargaining; treating it as a freely expressed popular will ignores the conditions under which those bargains were made.

The three northeastern provinces had previously been governed in the name of the Chinese Republic by Zhang Xueliang and his officials. They were not an empty frontier awaiting a new state. Merchants, landowners, rural communities, soldiers, schools, local administrations, and migrant families had connections to Chinese national politics and to the economy of North China. Japanese occupation disrupted those networks. Figures willing to serve in the new government sometimes hoped to preserve offices, protect property, or restore a vision of Manchu authority; others were recruited under political pressure. Zheng Xiaoxu, a former Qing official, became prime minister, lending the regime an established bureaucratic pedigree. Local collaboration should therefore be analyzed as a spectrum of choices shaped by ambition, coercion, and survival rather than as proof of genuine collective consent.

Manchukuo's founding proclamation on March 1, 1932, preceded Puyi's installation as chief executive on March 9. The capital's designation as Hsinking presented an image of political renewal, while the South Manchuria Railway and Japanese military installations secured the new administration's most important communications corridors. Flags, declarations, ceremonies, seals, and ministerial appointments were not superficial decorations: they allowed officials to collect taxes, issue orders, recruit personnel, and address foreign governments in the language of statehood. But the same institutions could be directed by Japanese commanders and advisers. Historical analysis must therefore separate the reality of administrative capacity from the much different question of independence.

The League of Nations' investigators considered whether local populations had freely supported separation from China and whether Japanese action could legitimately be described as self-defense. Their conclusion did not validate the regime's origin as spontaneous independence. Japan nonetheless signed a recognition protocol with Manchukuo on September 15, 1932. The protocol supplied a formal interstate vocabulary for a relationship in which Japanese military protection was effectively indispensable. Recognition by Japan was not equivalent to widespread international recognition, nor did Japan's diplomatic withdrawal from the League resolve the sovereignty dispute. The central question for students is how a dependent government can function extensively while its claim to independence remains contested.

Even the name 'Manchukuo' requires explanation. It made a region's historically contingent identity appear to be the natural foundation for a nation-state. The government circulated official maps, new administrative categories, and stories of common belonging, while existing Chinese jurisdictions and overlapping local loyalties were reorganized. A state can acquire documents and offices rapidly without acquiring legitimate consent. That is why the founding deserves examination through several kinds of sources: military orders show the planners' intentions, government proclamations show the regime's claims, and diplomatic or local records can reveal resistance, accommodation, and uncertainty beyond the staged ceremonies.

Section 75.2

Puyi, the Restoration of Monarchy, and the Problem of Sovereignty

Puyi had been removed from the Forbidden City in 1924 and lived in Tianjin under Japanese protection. He joined the new state hoping for a Qing restoration. Japanese planners valued him for his dynastic legitimacy among some Manchus and as a figure who could not easily be accused of being a Japanese creation, though that claim persuaded few outside observers. His enthronement on March 1, 1934, with the reign name Kangde, fulfilled part of his ambition. But Japanese officers refused him a Qing-style court, controlled his household through the Imperial Household Office, and dictated his ritual role, including accommodation to Japanese imperial symbols. Puyi's own later memoirs describe his powerlessness, but they were written under Chinese communist custody after 1945 and must be read critically.

Puyi's career illustrates the different meanings of legitimacy available to the regime. He had inherited the Qing throne as a child, abdicated in 1912, and retained ceremonial court privileges in Beijing until his expulsion from the Forbidden City in 1924. His subsequent residence in Tianjin placed him within reach of Japanese diplomats and military officers who believed an ex-emperor could give Manchukuo a historical lineage. Puyi's own priority was not necessarily the same as theirs. He dreamed of recovering dynastic dignity and perhaps something resembling the old Qing imperial order. Japanese military planners wanted a ruler whose presence could be advertised abroad and who would not obstruct strategic and administrative decisions.

That divergence appeared in the titles and rituals of power. Manchukuo first designated Puyi chief executive rather than immediately restoring a monarchy. His accession in March 1934, with the reign title Kangde, brought imperial robes, palace ceremonies, court appointments, and carefully staged public symbolism. Yet the state's structure was not a recreation of the Qing empire. Its bureaucracies were modern in form and heavily penetrated by Japanese officials; the Kwantung Army retained decisive security authority. Court protocol consequently became an instrument for controlling the sovereign as well as presenting him to the public. Japanese advisers influenced appointments, travel, audiences, military symbolism, and the range of issues on which the monarch could act without consultation.

The language of a restored Manchu dynasty also created problems. Manchuria's population was predominantly Han Chinese, with substantial Korean, Manchu, Mongol, and other communities. The Qing dynasty had once ruled China as a whole; the new state claimed only a portion of the northeast under the shadow of an invading army. Not all Manchus favored Puyi, and many residents did not identify the regime's founding with the recovery of a lost national state. Other state-building narratives attempted to solve the problem by emphasizing multiethnic harmony and regional renewal rather than strict dynastic restoration. The regime could invoke monarchy, independence, and modern administration simultaneously because each appealed to different audiences.

Puyi's autobiography and subsequent interviews are valuable but complicated. His later accounts were prepared after his defeat, imprisonment, and reeducation in the People's Republic of China. They contain personal observations about control over his household and officials, but they also reflect retrospective attempts to explain his responsibility and adaptation to the politics of his later life. A historian should compare his recollections with contemporary diaries, orders, Japanese diplomatic correspondence, Chinese press reports, and personnel records. The point is not that Puyi possessed no personal preferences. It is that a person's legal designation as sovereign does not establish the ability to command soldiers, determine budgets, or dismiss the officials on whom the government depends.

Historical photographs of Puyi's enthronement can mislead if treated as transparent images of power. Uniforms, ritual objects, guests, and architectural backdrops were selected to communicate both dynastic continuity and modern authority. They tell historians something about how the regime wished to be seen. They do not show who could change an industrial appropriation, overrule a military commander, or negotiate foreign relations. To evaluate sovereignty, students should compare ceremonial evidence with who controlled the police, budget, ministerial appointments, and military garrisons. The distance between these forms of evidence is the central interpretive problem of Puyi's reign.

Section 75.3

The Kwantung Army, Japanese Advisers, and the Government Behind the Government

Manchukuo's formal structure included an emperor, a State Council headed by a prime minister, and ministries led by Chinese or Manchu ministers. Beneath each minister, however, Japanese vice-ministers ran departments, and the State Council's General Affairs Board, a Japanese-dominated body, controlled budgets, personnel, and policy drafts. The commander of the Kwantung Army also served as ambassador to Manchukuo, giving him the authority to approve or veto major decisions. Cabinet-level Chinese officials generally signed what Japanese staff prepared. Historians therefore speak of a "government behind the government," and many call Manchukuo a puppet state. Others prefer descriptions such as "colonial state," arguing that the label "puppet" underplays the role of civilian bureaucrats and local elites, a point taken up in the Historical Debate below.

Manchukuo's administrative design made indirect rule more efficient than it could have been under a loose occupation. The State Council and ministries supplied recognizable offices for finance, education, police, communications, and economic affairs. Chinese and Manchu ministers occupied visible positions, signed regulations, and met delegations. Behind those titles, Japanese vice-ministers and senior bureau chiefs shaped files before they reached the formal ministers' desks. General Affairs officials controlled the preparation of budgets, the circulation of personnel decisions, and the order in which projects would be considered. Administrative power was therefore exercised at the level of drafting, appointments, and finance, not merely through occasional instructions from uniformed officers.

A particularly revealing contemporary report was prepared by American diplomats studying the government in 1934. It described Japanese chiefs of general-affairs bureaus as holders of the actual working authority, recruited through a chain centered on the Japanese ambassador. The ambassador was simultaneously commander of the Kwantung Army and governor of the Kwantung Leased Territory. Combining these posts concentrated military coercion, diplomatic influence, control over Japanese personnel, and authority in the railway and leased zones. The report noted that Japanese officials formed a minority of the civil administration, yet held a disproportionate share of decision-making positions. Numerical minority, therefore, was entirely compatible with political dominance.

Such control depended on routine cooperation by many people who were not simply puppets in their individual behavior. Chinese clerks and officials kept registers, collected revenue, supervised schools, and negotiated with local elites. Some used their offices to preserve careers or protect constituencies; others exploited administrative opportunities for profit; others were constrained by threats of dismissal or physical force. Japanese military administrators themselves disagreed with civilian technicians, railway managers, business executives, and fellow officers over tax policy, development priorities, and organizational efficiency. The government could administer a large territory because it operated through these overlapping professional and local networks, even when its highest strategic choices came from the Kwantung Army.

Students should distinguish three different kinds of authority. Formal sovereignty answers who signs laws and represents the state. Administrative control asks who drafts those laws, selects officials, and directs resources. Coercive power asks who commands troops, police, prisons, and emergency measures when a decision is resisted. In Manchukuo, the answers frequently pointed in different directions. The Japanese military could veto strategic choices; administrative specialists set the practical terms of many civilian programs; the nominal sovereign and ministers publicly legitimized them. Calling the system a puppet state captures its foreign domination, but studying its bureaucratic machinery explains how that domination became ordinary government rather than a succession of battlefield orders.

Later tribunal evidence described meetings in which Japanese officials settled policies before those decisions appeared in Manchukuo's formal governmental proceedings. Such testimony supports the argument that procedures of a sovereign cabinet could serve as the public confirmation of agreements reached elsewhere. But a postwar criminal judgment is also an interpretive record produced after the regime collapsed; its findings should be compared with contemporary administrative descriptions. The independent 1934 American diplomatic report is particularly useful because it was composed while these structures still operated and described the concentration of authority in the Japanese ambassador and Kwantung Army commander.

Historical Vocabulary

Colonial Authority and "Puppet State"

A puppet state is a nominally sovereign regime controlled by an external power. The term fits Manchukuo's dependence on the Kwantung Army, but it can obscure the role of economic planners and local collaborators and the way the state functioned as an administration over millions of people. Scholars therefore use it alongside "colonial state." Collaboration likewise covers motives from conviction to coercion and survival.

Section 75.4

The Concordia Association, Five-Races Harmony, and Imperial Political Ideology

The Concordia Association (Kyōwakai), founded in July 1932, was designed as a mass organization combining a political party's mobilizing function with the claim that Manchukuo needed no parties. Its ideology, minzoku kyōwa, proclaimed harmony among Japanese, Han Chinese, Manchus, Mongols, and Koreans (sometimes counted as "five races"), together with an official "kingly way" (ōdō) language drawn from Confucian rhetoric. In practice the association's leadership was dominated by Japanese, and its membership expanded through officials, schools, and local organizations, often by compulsion. Its propaganda contrasted with unequal wages, access to rations, land, and offices that favored Japanese. The rhetoric nevertheless mattered: it allowed Japan to claim a post-Western, multiethnic order, and it shaped schooling and festivals.

The Concordia Association was created to solve a problem that police control alone could not solve: how to manufacture public belonging for a state without a widely accepted popular origin. Its name invoked cooperation, and its organizers drew on languages of moral renewal, Confucian benevolent rule, and the overcoming of Western imperialism. The formula of harmony among five peoples—Japanese, Han Chinese, Manchu, Mongol, and Korean—could be printed on posters and repeated in schools and public ceremonies. It also implied a sharp contradiction. If the state was a common enterprise of equal populations, why were Japanese officers and advisers disproportionately powerful, and why could residents not openly debate the sovereign status of the territory?

Unlike a parliamentary party seeking power through competitive elections, the Kyōwakai was intended to mobilize support without permitting alternative organized political programs. Local branches could coordinate ceremonies, youth activity, village campaigns, and official messages. Membership was not always voluntary, and participation could be entangled with access to government employment or favors. The association's form mattered because it translated the ideological ambitions of the founders into personnel networks, meeting halls, printed materials, and expectations of public conduct. In this sense an idea such as ethnic harmony became an administrative practice: citizens and residents were asked to perform loyalty as part of everyday life.

The association drew on concepts from several intellectual traditions. The 'kingly way' appealed to a classical distinction between government by virtue and government by mere force. Japanese reformers sometimes spoke of a new civilization transcending both Western colonialism and divisive national politics. Chinese or Manchu participants could interpret this language differently or challenge it privately. The result was not a consistent philosophy agreed on by all the inhabitants. It was a shared official vocabulary masking unequal capacity to define policy, police opponents, and distribute resources. The very category 'five races' simplified the populations of the northeast; it assigned identities from above and overlooked variation within each community.

Propaganda should be analyzed by asking who created it, how it circulated, and what happened when audiences encountered it. A festival photograph can demonstrate that people attended an event, but not that they freely endorsed its message. A membership roll shows organizational reach, not private conviction. Accounts of discrimination in wages, land, education, or public employment disclose the limits of the promise of equality, although those inequalities varied across locality and status. The most careful interpretation neither dismisses the rhetoric as meaningless nor accepts it at face value. It examines how it facilitated an unequal political order and how residents used, ignored, modified, or resisted its claims.

Residents could respond to official harmony campaigns in ways that do not fit a simple division between belief and rejection. An official might repeat state slogans in order to secure a permit, a teacher might comply with prescribed ceremonies while privately disapproving, and a local notable might use government networks to bargain for a public project. These possibilities require evidence; they are not proof of any single person's conduct. Their value as historical questions is to shift analysis away from official membership totals toward the contexts in which choices were made. Political incorporation can be simultaneously coercive, opportunistic, and ideologically meaningful.

Section 75.5

Territorial Consolidation, Rehe, and the Limits of Chinese Sovereignty

Japan claimed that Rehe (Jehol), a province inland of the Great Wall, belonged to Manchukuo. In February–March 1933 Japanese and Manchukuoan forces occupied it, and fighting continued around Great Wall passes. Under the Tanggu Truce of May 31, 1933, Chinese forces withdrew from a demilitarized zone in northeastern Hebei, de facto accepting Japanese military dominance of the Great Wall frontier without formally recognizing Manchukuo. The agreement drew Chinese domestic criticism of the Nationalist government's priorities, and it left North China open to later pressure. Those later events belong to Chapters 76 and after.

Rehe mattered because Manchukuo's announced borders were never merely cartographic. Japanese and Manchukuoan officers considered the province to the southwest part of their strategic sphere, while the Chinese government rejected their claim. Its approaches to the Great Wall linked the northeast to North China and to routes toward Beijing. In early 1933 the Kwantung Army mounted operations that pushed beyond the initial 1931–1932 occupation. Armed resistance and changing local loyalties complicated the advance; provincial administrators and Chinese commanders confronted pressures from a war being fought while the international diplomatic dispute remained unresolved.

Japanese forces took the provincial capital Chengde in March 1933. The subsequent fighting at Great Wall passes demonstrated that control of Manchuria did not end the military's demand for secure frontiers. The logic of protecting railways and settlers could be extended to roads, mountains, and buffer districts beyond the proclaimed state's perimeter. This was a recurrent mechanism of imperial expansion: a government described an earlier seizure as necessary for security and then treated adjacent territory as necessary for the security of the first seizure. It is historically misleading to draw one neat line and assume military ambitions stopped there.

The Tanggu Truce of May 31, 1933, established a demilitarized zone in northeastern Hebei and imposed military restrictions on Chinese forces. It was an armistice-like arrangement made under severe pressure, not a peace treaty in which the Nationalist government formally recognized Manchukuo as a sovereign state. In China, critics regarded it as another erosion of territory and national standing. In Japan, military leaders could point to the truce as evidence that their campaign had secured strategic goals without an immediate general war. The difference between a diplomatic recognition of sovereignty and a military acceptance of a temporary frontier condition is central to understanding the settlement.

The border's human significance deserves equal attention. Communities on either side of the Great Wall had families, trade routes, farms, and religious connections that long predated the new demilitarized zone. Military advances interrupted travel and market access; garrisons and police could subject residents to additional screening, requisitions, or suspicion. The region remained politically unsettled. Although this chapter ends before July 1937, the experience of 1933 provides a context for later pressure on North China. The strongest causal claim is not that the Tanggu agreement caused a later full-scale war. It is that it institutionalized one unequal military frontier, which later politicians and commanders had to confront.

A geography exercise should distinguish the South Manchuria Railway corridor from the railway's wider connections to northern Manchuria and Korea, and locate Rehe relative to the Great Wall. Territorial claims were shaped by the ability to move troops along infrastructure, not simply by ethnic labels attached to an area. The Tanggu demilitarized zone also demonstrates that an agreement drawn on a map can preserve a profound imbalance: military restrictions applied to Chinese forces while the consequences of earlier Japanese advances remained in place. Students should trace whose movements were restricted and who retained the power to resume operations.

Division II — Colonial Economic Development, Settlement, Society, and Resistance
Section 75.6

The South Manchuria Railway, Infrastructure, and Colonial Economic Power

Mantetsu, founded in 1906, was more than a railway. It ran ports, hotels, mines, steelworks, and the railway zone's towns, schools, and hospitals, and it maintained a large research department whose surveys supplied the army and planners. After 1932 the Kwantung Army took over the state-managed lines and expanded railways into the north and east, with strategic aims toward the Soviet border. Mantetsu's President from 1935, Matsuoka Yōsuke, and the new Manchukuo state corporations shifted its role, as industrial development was increasingly directed by the state and the army rather than by Mantetsu alone. Infrastructure served commerce and control together: the same lines carried soybeans, coal, troops, and settlers, and urban planning in Hsinking and Dalian displayed imperial ambitions.

At the center of Japanese economic authority in the northeast stood the South Manchuria Railway Company, usually known as Mantetsu. Its railway lines ran through territories whose status was already entangled with foreign treaties, leased land, and Japanese military protection. Control of a rail line involved far more than tracks: stations needed warehouses and customs arrangements; locomotives needed coal and workshops; industrial firms needed reliable freight rates; towns developed around administrative offices and hospitals. Mantetsu's managers thus occupied a position between private corporate administration and colonial government. Their decisions influenced land values, labor markets, commercial access, and the movement of soldiers.

The railway's research organizations added another dimension. Investigators assembled agricultural, demographic, economic, and political information about regions that Tokyo policymakers often knew only indirectly. Such surveys might identify resources and improve commercial planning, but they could also map communities for taxation, settlement, or security operations. Their categories reflected the questions the investigators had been asked to answer. The existence of detailed information did not automatically make policy objective or benevolent: classification and knowledge could be instruments of power. Historians now use Mantetsu's data with caution, checking sampling methods, administrative definitions, and the gap between official plans and local experiences.

Railway construction after Manchukuo's founding linked the older southern corridor to northern and eastern districts, strengthening military movement toward the Soviet frontier and Korean border as well as expanding the movement of soybeans, coal, machinery, and passengers. Industrial plants around Anshan and Fushun depended on connections to ports and markets. The same system facilitated migration by laborers and settlers, sometimes under conditions of severe inequality or coercion. More trains and larger freight volumes might therefore demonstrate economic activity without indicating that residents benefited equally. The standard language of 'development' should always be paired with questions about whose land was crossed, who supplied labor, and who controlled pricing and access.

Relationships among Mantetsu, the Kwantung Army, the Manchukuo ministries, and Japanese industrial groups were never completely stable. Railway executives defended commercial interests and expertise; army officers sought rapid strategic construction; civilian planners wanted coordinated investments into coal, steel, power, and chemicals. Matsuoka Yōsuke, an advocate of expansion and an important railway leader in the mid-1930s, embodied the political character of economic management. By 1936–1937 the drive toward a more centralized heavy-industrial program reduced the prospect that Mantetsu alone would lead the region's transformation. The outcome was not the elimination of business in favor of the state but a negotiated, coercive mix of corporations, military offices, and administrative planners.

Workforces are largely absent from corporate reports written for investors or strategic planners. Railway maintenance, mine extraction, dock labor, and construction depended on employees whose contractual status, wages, living quarters, and safety varied widely. Chinese and Korean laborers could be especially vulnerable to discrimination or coercive recruitment. For the years before 1937 it is important to distinguish these arrangements from the more systematic forced-labor mobilization implemented later in the war. The rail company can be studied as an economic enterprise only if the labor that made its transport network function is placed beside the balance sheets and photographs of stations.

Section 75.7

Natural Resources, Heavy Industry, and the Emergence of State-Directed Planning

Manchuria held coal around Fushun, iron and steel industries centered on Anshan, oil shale, hydroelectric potential, and major soybean exports. The Kwantung Army and reform-minded officials increasingly favored state-directed industrial planning, while debating the relationship between major Japanese firms, the South Manchuria Railway and special state-supervised companies. Hoshino Naoki, Kishi Nobusuke and other economic administrators helped frame a Five-Year Plan for 1937–1941, formulated around January 1937, emphasizing steel, coal, electricity, and related infrastructure. Those were targets, not projects already completed. Ayukawa Yoshisuke and Nissan became central to a later corporate reorganization negotiated in autumn 1937, outside the main period examined here.

Japanese planners saw Manchuria's raw materials as the basis for industrial and strategic autonomy. Coal at Fushun could provide fuel for railroads and power stations; iron ore and steelmaking around Anshan could support machinery and military production; abundant soybeans offered export revenue; and electricity promised to connect mining, metallurgy, and urban growth. But natural resources did not convert themselves into functioning plants. Mines required labor, equipment, credit, engineering skills, and transport. The region's potential was a central argument for expansion, yet the costs of realizing that potential raised disputes over whether the state, the railway, established zaibatsu, or new industrial groups should direct development.

Technocrats associated with the General Affairs Board and Japanese ministries proposed a pattern of coordinated investment that differed from reliance on ordinary market decisions. They discussed industrial targets, supplies of scarce capital, controlled corporations, priority sectors, and distribution of foreign exchange. Hoshino Naoki and Kishi Nobusuke became especially associated with this approach. The planned economy was not a total abandonment of private ownership. It created privileged combinations of public supervision and corporate management, in which industrial firms could benefit from guaranteed markets and exclusive rights while officials expected compliance with strategic priorities. Decisions about the location of factories, the price of inputs, and access to equipment could be political decisions as much as commercial ones.

The Five-Year Industrial Development Plan associated with 1937–1941 is best taught as a proposed trajectory rather than a balance sheet. Developed through the mid-1930s and formulated around the beginning of 1937, it envisaged greatly expanded heavy industry, energy production, and infrastructure. The plan did not ensure the availability of investment capital, qualified workers, imported machine tools, or international markets. Industry still relied on foreign technology and on flows of materials through Japan and the wider world. As war and military requirements intensified after 1937, targets were repeatedly altered. A retrospective statistic for output in 1940 cannot be read back into a decision taken in 1936.

The Nissan group's later centrality belongs to a carefully dated sequel. Ayukawa Yoshisuke and his industrial network had already experimented with a newer style of conglomerate organization, but the decisive cabinet arrangements for the Manchurian heavy-industrial enterprise were negotiated in autumn 1937. Nissan shifted its center to Hsinking in November 1937, after this chapter's main endpoint. Here the responsible approach is to explain why planners began considering private industrial partners, while reserving the later institutional creation for a forward-looking note. This distinction prevents a common historical error: making plans that were only being argued about in early 1937 appear to be already accomplished projects.

One way to test the claims of planners is to compare three documents: a proposed output target, the recorded allocation of finance or machinery, and the output actually measured after a project began. Such comparisons reveal whether bottlenecks came from transport, labor, equipment, or shifting military priorities. A large projected increase in steel production may express a strategic preference rather than an achievable forecast. Historians should also ask how the costs were distributed: public authorities could subsidize privileged corporations, while landholders and workers faced taxes, relocation, or stringent discipline. Planning shaped the economy even where numerical goals were not met.

Section 75.8

Japanese Agricultural Settlement, Land Acquisition, and Rural Displacement

Japanese agricultural settlement began with small experimental communities in 1932, including armed settlers and former soldiers, and was promoted as a solution to rural hardship in Japan as well as a security policy for the Kwantung Army. Advocates such as Katō Kanji sought to move land-poor farm households. In 1936 officials adopted a much larger twenty-year target of one million farm households, or roughly five million people. Acquisition for new settlements could displace Chinese and Korean cultivators through coercive purchases, low compensation and administrative claims that cultivated or seasonally used land was vacant. Conditions for Japanese settlers themselves could be harsh, and success depended on labor and infrastructure that advertisements often overlooked.

Agricultural colonization brought together several agendas that did not always fit. Rural reformers in Japan wanted to relieve land shortages and household debt; military officers wanted settlers in exposed frontier districts who could support Japanese claims and serve as a defensive screen; colonial officials wanted more taxable and productive farms. These motives reinforced one another in public rhetoric, yet the interests of migrants, administrators, and the people already cultivating the land were often in direct conflict. Katō Kanji and other agrarian advocates presented settlement as an opportunity for disciplined family farming and national renewal. The Kwantung Army treated village locations as strategic choices, particularly in districts bordering the Soviet Union or zones of armed resistance.

Early projects beginning in 1932 were relatively small. Recruitment involved former soldiers and civilian families, and settlement authorities advertised acreage and the chance for economic independence. But farming practices developed for Japan did not always suit the climate, soil, and labor arrangements of northeast China. Travel costs, unfamiliar crops, dangerous winters, and unstable security discouraged or defeated some settlers. Women's experiences were distinct: moving a household demanded childcare, water collection, food processing, and the maintenance of social ties in settlements that sometimes lacked schools and medical services. Administrative records often counted household heads or departure groups more readily than the difficulties faced by wives and children.

Obtaining land was not simply the transfer of vacant public property. Officials and intermediaries could classify cultivated or seasonally used fields as 'waste,' arrange purchases under the threat of military authority, or set compensation far below the value residents attached to continued possession. Chinese and Korean farmers might lose their crops, irrigation arrangements, tenancy agreements, or village burial grounds even when paperwork recorded a sale. Some became agricultural laborers for incoming settlers, while others moved to new areas or joined protests and armed opposition. The legal language of purchase therefore does not establish that a transaction was voluntary. Nor should all colonized farmers be portrayed as identical: tenure, wealth, nationality, and local relationships influenced their possibilities for resistance and negotiation.

The 1936 program envisioned the migration of one million Japanese agricultural households over twenty years. It was a goal, not a census of colonists. One published study drawing on a contemporary migration yearbook identifies 1,667 households in the four experimental years 1932–1935; the figure excludes later and differently classified programs and must not be equated with a count of all Japanese residents in Manchuria. Comparing that small experimental cohort with the enormous projected program shows the scale of the change in policy, but it would be misleading to announce that the twenty-year plan had 'failed' before its first full year. Later settlement programs, youth groups, and village transfers grew mainly after the period covered here.

Another measurement problem concerns settlers' identities. A Japanese employee of a railway firm, a merchant resident since before 1931, an experimental farm household, and an agricultural youth recruited years later were all Japanese inhabitants of Manchuria but belonged to different demographic categories. Combining them into a single number would inflate the success of the agricultural program. The source register should therefore specify both the unit counted and the dates. Enrollment in a departure program, arrival in Manchuria, continued residence, and establishment of a viable farm are four separate outcomes, each requiring different records.

Section 75.9

Chinese, Manchu, Mongol, Korean, and Japanese Lives under Manchukuo

Han Chinese made up the large majority of Manchukuo's roughly thirty million inhabitants; Manchus, Mongols (including those in the Xing'an provinces, with a degree of autonomy), Koreans, and a Japanese community of several hundred thousand were minorities. Japanese lived with higher wages and privileged access to food, housing, and education. Koreans, subjects of the Japanese empire, occupied an intermediate and contradictory position: some worked as police, interpreters, or settlers under Japanese protection, while many were poor farmers, often rice cultivators, who suffered discrimination and tension with Chinese neighbors, and some joined the resistance. Schools taught Japanese alongside local languages. Chinese merchants, laborers (including large seasonal migration from North China), and rural households adapted, bargained, and endured, and religious institutions were supervised.

The word 'population' conceals the diversity of Manchukuo's society. The majority were Han Chinese, many belonging to families that had migrated north from Shandong or Hebei over previous generations. Manchus had varied relationships to the Qing past and to the new regime's attempt to invoke it. Mongol communities occupied different political, pastoral, and agricultural settings. Koreans had crossed into the region for decades, some seeking land and work and others escaping colonial pressures within Korea. Japanese residents included not just soldiers and settlers but railway staff, managers, police, teachers, technicians, and their dependents. Each group's legal standing and economic opportunities depended on occupation, locality, and the institutions with which they had to deal.

Urban centers reveal the contradictions of imperial development. Hsinking was redesigned to project modern administrative order, with government avenues, parks, public buildings, and plans for transportation. Housing, water, and public services did not reach all districts equally. Japanese professional households could access schools and employment channels unavailable to ordinary Chinese laborers; nearby Chinese markets continued to operate within a new regime of permits, police, and taxes. Merchants sometimes profited from infrastructure or supplying troops; others faced price controls, seizures, and new competitors. Rapid urban growth created opportunities, but unequal power shaped who could exploit them. A modern city plan is evidence of planning capacity, not proof that living conditions improved for everyone.

Korean residents occupied an especially complicated position. The Japanese empire claimed a protective interest in Koreans as colonial subjects, yet Koreans in Manchuria were not automatically privileged in the same way as Japanese settlers and officials. Many were rural families cultivating paddy rice under precarious tenure. Some worked with Japanese institutions as interpreters or policemen; others joined Chinese and Korean anti-Japanese movements. Chinese suspicion sometimes targeted Korean communities because of the military's claims to defend them, while Korean farmers themselves experienced land loss, exploitation, and cultural pressure. No single label—collaborator, colonizer, or victim—can adequately describe this population.

Schooling and public language demonstrate how an unequal state could nonetheless shape daily routines. Official curricula introduced state rituals, emperor-oriented symbols, and the language of harmony; Japanese language instruction helped administration and employment, but access and classroom experiences varied. Families continued to use Chinese dialects, Korean, Mongolian, or other languages at home, while religious communities maintained local practices under uneven supervision. Women worked on farms, in households, markets, and factories, usually without corresponding political authority. To reconstruct these experiences, historians must combine census statistics, local testimony, school records, labor reports, letters, and oral histories. Government claims about ethnic harmony and order should be tested against these less official forms of evidence.

Contemporary censuses also reflected political definitions. The designation of a resident as Japanese, Korean, Manchu, Chinese, or Mongol might differ between administrative tables and the person's own identity, especially where family migration or imperial legal status crossed borders. These categories cannot be treated as timeless racial divisions. Their use in resource allocation, school admission, and policing could make them powerful regardless of their instability. A sound social history should preserve that tension: people lived complicated identities, while governments classified them in simplified ways and then sometimes made unequal treatment depend on those classifications.

Section 75.10

Policing, Counterinsurgency, Local Resistance, and Colonial Violence

Manchukuo's stability rested on coercion. Armed resistance in 1932–1934 included former Northeastern Army units, "anti-Japanese volunteer armies," bandits, and, increasingly, communist-led guerrillas, including Korean and Chinese fighters, such as the Northeast Anti-Japanese forces that later formed a united army. The Kwantung Army and Manchukuoan forces carried out counterinsurgency, including the concentration of rural populations into guarded "collective hamlets" (shūdan buraku). A police system with Japanese officers, a secret service, and the military police surveilled the population, detained suspects, and used torture. Villages were punished collectively in documented incidents such as the Pingdingshan massacre of September 1932. This section does not attempt the full history of wartime atrocity, which Chapter 79 treats.

Resistance to Manchukuo took several forms, with different organizations, motivations, and military capacities. Officers and soldiers formerly linked to Chinese northeastern forces joined volunteer armies; local fighters defended villages or commercial routes; some armed groups combined resistance with older bandit networks. Communist-led units gradually became more important, including Chinese and Korean participants. Their activities ranged from attacks on rail lines and garrisons to intelligence gathering, moving food, and supporting underground networks. It is inaccurate to describe all opponents as communist, just as it is inaccurate to treat the entire countryside as united behind resistance. Communities could be pressured by both occupying forces and armed groups, and many sought simply to preserve their livelihoods.

The Kwantung Army and Manchukuoan security organizations responded with sweeps, forced registration, informants, detention, and armed punitive operations. The military police, colonial police specialists, and local security forces could overlap in jurisdiction, often with weak mechanisms for correcting abuses. Japanese reports frequently labeled opponents 'bandits'—a category that could encompass organized nationalists, armed opportunists, and civilians accused of helping guerrillas. Historians must examine who applied that designation and the evidence behind it. Counterinsurgency also meant transforming the physical landscape through road construction, watch stations, checkpoints, and guarded or consolidated settlements; these changes interfered with ordinary trade and farming even when no battle was taking place.

The Pingdingshan killings near Fushun in September 1932 offer a documented example of punitive violence against civilians. Reports sent by Chinese authorities described Japanese troops killing inhabitants of villages in the area following resistance activity. An American consul investigating the accounts later concluded that a substantial number of unarmed villagers had been killed and that surrounding settlements had been destroyed, while warning that the exact number could probably never be established. This distinction is essential: the massacre is documented, but individual casualty totals differed markedly between the Japanese army, witnesses, and Chinese reports. A sound first-edition chapter should not choose the highest or lowest figure without explaining its source.

Repression reshaped political life in ways that are difficult to count. A farmer might avoid travel to a market after being stopped at a checkpoint; a family might have to provide food or labor for security forces; a teacher or shopkeeper might be investigated because of a relationship with a suspected resister. Fear could suppress opposition in one place while encouraging recruitment to guerrilla groups elsewhere. The state also tried to impose a monopoly over accounts of violence, using censorship and propaganda to portray military campaigns as the restoration of order. Comparing army communiqués with diplomatic reports and survivors' testimony reveals both the mechanisms of rule and the unequal preservation of sources. The experience of occupation cannot be reconstructed solely from administrative successes or battlefield statistics.

The Pingdingshan episode is especially useful for training source criticism because reports emerged from actors with opposed political interests. Chinese reports sought international attention for civilian deaths. Japanese army accounts minimized or justified violence as an anti-bandit operation. An American consul weighed contradictory figures and concluded that the number was uncertain but the killing of unarmed villagers was established. Rather than asking students to select one unsupported casualty estimate, the chapter can ask which claims are independently corroborated and how each author learned what was happening. This method both documents violence and respects the limits of the surviving record.

Section 75.11

The Saitō and Okada Cabinets: Government after the End of Party Rule

After Inukai's assassination in May 1932, Elder statesman Saionji Kinmochi recommended Admiral Saitō Makoto, a moderate, as a "national unity" prime minister, with ministers from both parties, bureaucrats, and the services. Saitō (1932–1934) and Okada Keisuke (1934–1936) governed without party leadership but with party support in the Diet. Elections continued: the 1936 general election gave the Minseitō a plurality and the proletarian parties gains. The court, the Privy Council, the elder statesmen, and civilian ministries remained influential, and budget disputes with the army were frequent. The end of party cabinets thus shifted the balance, but it did not abolish constitutional politics.

The assassination of Inukai Tsuyoshi in May 1932 ended a sequence of cabinets formed primarily by the party commanding the lower house. It did not dissolve the parties or repeal the Meiji Constitution. The two major parties continued to compete in elections, negotiate budgets, and hold parliamentary seats. Yet the conventions that had made party leaders plausible candidates for the premiership were no longer reliable. Saionji Kinmochi, the surviving elder statesman consulted in the appointment of prime ministers, recommended the former naval officer Saitō Makoto as an acceptable compromise. Coalition-like cabinets could include party politicians while placing their leadership under a nonparty premier.

These governments confronted several centers of authority. The emperor possessed constitutionally defined prerogatives, but advice from court officials and elders influenced how they were exercised. Military ministries controlled personnel and demanded budgets; civil ministries still administered taxes, education, the police, and public works. The Privy Council and House of Peers could obstruct legislation. Cabinet ministers had to coordinate among these offices without a simple parliamentary rule enabling the elected majority to command them. A navy admiral as prime minister did not mean that the navy possessed unlimited power: army and navy priorities frequently diverged, and financial officials resisted some demands from both services.

Saitō's cabinet attempted to steady politics following assassination and the Manchurian expansion. Its authority depended partly on broad parliamentary support and partly on the absence of an alternative able to command the military's cooperation. The 1934 succession to Okada Keisuke reflected continued reliance on respected naval figures as political moderators. Officials pursued routine legislation and administration amid mounting conflict about constitutional theory, military assignments, and national policy. The endurance of ordinary government work matters because institutional erosion often proceeds alongside functional public administration, making it difficult to identify a single day on which 'democracy ended.'

Election results offer a corrective to a simple dictatorship narrative. In February 1936 voters still elected a House of Representatives, with the Minseitō emerging as the largest party and smaller labor-oriented parties gaining representation. Women remained excluded from the parliamentary franchise, and election procedures and political speech were subject to significant restrictions. Nevertheless these contests could express civilian criticism and political competition. The central change was not the instantaneous disappearance of electoral politics; it was the widening gap between winning seats and controlling strategic government decisions. The February uprising soon demonstrated how violence and military organization could override the expectations produced by a recent election.

The premiership was not the only office through which political power could be exercised. Finance ministers negotiated expenditures and tried to restrain military demands; the Home Ministry supervised police and elections; the Education Ministry shaped academic discipline; local assemblies handled daily governance. Some officials supported expansion while disagreeing with political terrorism, while others supported constitutional government yet accepted the Manchurian settlement. The surviving administrative machinery was not politically neutral, but neither was it merely an empty shell. Tracking its decisions helps explain the gradual redistribution of authority more accurately than portraying 1932 as the arrival of a complete dictatorship.

Section 75.12

The Imperial Way and Control Factions: Rival Strategies for Military Reform

The Kōdōha, associated with Generals Araki Sadao and Masaki Jinzaburō, stressed spiritual renewal, devotion to the emperor, and a confrontation with the Soviet Union, and drew support from young officers who blamed politicians and capitalists for rural suffering. The Tōseiha, associated with Nagata Tetsuzan, Tōjō Hideki and others, favored bureaucratic reorganization, economic planning, and mobilization of national resources, working through the army ministry and cooperating with civilian officials. The labels were partly applied by opponents, memberships were fluid, and personal rivalries and generational resentments blurred ideology. Students should not treat either as a party with a program.

Descriptions of the army's Imperial Way and Control factions can make the 1930s appear to be a contest between two clearly defined political parties. In reality they were overlapping networks of officers, patrons, staff positions, and intellectual tendencies. The Kōdōha label gathered figures who emphasized spiritual purity, emperor-centered renovation, personal leadership, and a confrontation with what they described as corrupt party politics and capital. Araki Sadao and Masaki Jinzaburō were important figures, but their careers and relationships were not identical. Radical junior officers sometimes regarded senior sponsors as insufficiently bold even while borrowing their language.

The Tōseiha label is generally associated with officers favoring administrative coordination, industrial mobilization, and strengthened military institutions through regular bureaucratic channels. Nagata Tetsuzan was a significant organizer of this approach; other officers associated with centralized planning did not necessarily share every policy or personal allegiance. It would be misleading to call the Control faction 'moderate' simply because its advocates opposed an unauthorized coup. Many supported aggressive military expansion, tighter political supervision, and extensive rearmament. The difference was partly over method: whether the desired national transformation would emerge from a dramatic purge of 'evil advisers' or from personnel control, budget planning, and coordination with bureaucracies and industry.

Regional and generational divisions helped intensify the conflict. Officers feared that rival networks would determine appointments and promotions, and accusations of factional favoritism threatened the army's claim to a common imperial mission. Junior officers living among recruits from hard-hit rural districts could point to poverty, indebtedness, and public scandals as proof of the need for national renovation. Their sense of moral urgency had real social roots, but it did not establish that rural people uniformly supported the violent political program. The army was both a national institution and a workplace in which careers and loyalties mattered.

The concept gekokujō, often rendered 'the low overriding the high,' links the 1931 Manchurian precedent with the later attempted uprising. It names a pattern of insubordination, not a formal army doctrine. Superiors who tolerated one unauthorized action because it achieved a strategic goal could weaken discipline elsewhere. Yet the February 26 rebels eventually met emphatic opposition from the emperor and senior commanders. The contrast shows that the outcome of insubordination depended on institutional reactions as well as on ideology. The military's eventual predominance cannot be explained merely by one faction 'winning'; it emerged from the reallocation of personnel and the increase of the army's corporate leverage after an internal crisis.

Foreign policy is another reason the factional labels cannot be made too rigid. Discussion of a northern confrontation with the Soviet Union, resources in Manchuria, and the potential for conflict in China crossed personal and bureaucratic networks. An officer favoring technical planning might nonetheless advocate risky expansion, while a critic of party finance might oppose a particular coup. Researchers often reconstruct factions retrospectively from later appointments and purges. Contemporary letters and personnel records must therefore be examined to determine whether an individual actually identified with a named group at a given moment. The labels clarify relationships but can also exaggerate coherence.

Historical Vocabulary

Kōdōha, Tōseiha, and Gekokujō

Kōdōha ("Imperial Way faction") and Tōseiha ("Control faction") are analytical labels for loose networks. Gekokujō, "the low overcoming the high," describes junior officers acting independently of their superiors, as in Manchuria in 1931. The terms matter because they shifted over time and were used polemically.

Section 75.13

Political Repression, Thought Control, and the Narrowing of Public Debate

The Peace Preservation Law (Chapter 69) was used against communists and, increasingly, others. Mass arrests of 1933 and the death in custody of the writer Kobayashi Takiji in February 1933 deepened fear. In June 1933 the leaders of the imprisoned Communist Party, Sano Manabu and Nabeyama Sadachika, announced their tenkō (ideological conversion), and thousands of others followed under pressure, family appeals, and the appeal of nationalism. In 1933 the Education Ministry dismissed the Kyoto University law professor Takigawa Yukitoki over his writings on criminal law, provoking faculty resistance that ultimately failed. Publishers were pressed, books banned, and the Special Higher Police expanded. Criticism did not vanish: newspapers, party politicians, and some intellectuals continued to speak, but organized opposition was weakened and the range of safe speech narrowed.

Japan's coercive apparatus against political dissent predated the Manchurian Incident. The Peace Preservation Law of 1925, broadened in 1928, gave the state tools for prosecuting movements accused of seeking fundamental changes to the national polity or private property system. Police collected memberships, infiltrated organizations, questioned suspects, and pressed defendants to renounce their commitments. Following waves of arrests, political movements had difficulty maintaining regular meetings, newspapers, and legal representation. The suppression of the Communist Party was especially severe, but the effects extended to publishing houses, student associations, trade unions, and scholars whose work authorities regarded as dangerously subversive.

The death of proletarian writer Kobayashi Takiji in police custody in February 1933 became an emblem of the risks of political opposition. It is important, however, not to make him stand for every dissenter or to portray repression as a uniform process. Some authors continued to publish critical work using fiction, historical analogy, or cautious language. Some police departments and prosecutors differed in their practices, while ministries sometimes disagreed about how much intellectual freedom to allow. The threat of surveillance or arrest could produce self-censorship far beyond the number of people actually imprisoned.

Tenkō, conventionally translated as ideological conversion, is best understood through the pressures under which declarations were made. In June 1933 the imprisoned Communist Party leaders Sano Manabu and Nabeyama Sadachika publicly abandoned their earlier revolutionary position, and their statements became a model for authorities seeking similar recantations. Some converts genuinely reconsidered their beliefs; others calculated that changing their public position might reduce punishment or protect relatives. Appeals to family duties and national belonging were deployed alongside policing. Counting statements of conversion does not allow historians to infer that each person's private beliefs changed in the same way.

The Takigawa Incident at Kyoto Imperial University in 1933 showed that even a respected academic could face government pressure. The Education Ministry intervened against law professor Takigawa Yukitoki, provoking protests from faculty and students. The dispute concerned control over university teaching and research, not merely the views of one individual. Attempts to defend the university's academic autonomy failed to reverse the essential administrative outcome. University administrators, journalists, lawyers, and civil servants learned that intellectual controversy could be turned into an employment and institutional survival crisis. This atmosphere formed the background for the more focused constitutional struggle against Minobe in 1935.

The courtroom, school, and newspaper were connected sites of political regulation. A censorship order could limit a book's circulation even where its author had not been tried; an employment dismissal could silence colleagues who feared a similar fate; a police interrogation could make family members vulnerable to public stigma. These mechanisms help explain why a statistical count of prosecutions cannot by itself measure the narrowing of debate. They also show why continuing publication of some dissenting writing does not disprove the existence of severe repression. Both the surviving criticism and the institutional pressures acting on it must be interpreted together.

Section 75.14

Minobe Tatsukichi, the Emperor-as-Organ Controversy, and the National Polity

Minobe Tatsukichi, a constitutional scholar, argued that the emperor exercised state powers as an organ of the state within the constitutional order, an established interpretation long influential among jurists and officials. In February 1935 Kikuchi Takeo attacked this position in the House of Peers; political opponents, military figures and nationalist organizations then mobilized against it. Under pressure, the Okada cabinet issued declarations repudiating the theory on August 3 and October 15, 1935. Minobe’s works faced restrictions and he resigned from the House of Peers in 1935; he survived an assassination attempt in 1936. The controversy weakened the public standing of a constitutional interpretation that had helped support restraints on unchecked authority.

Minobe Tatsukichi's emperor-as-organ theory had long been a prominent way of explaining constitutional government under the Meiji Constitution. In this jurisprudential model, the state possessed legal personality and the emperor exercised sovereign powers as its highest organ, subject to constitutional forms. The word 'organ' did not mean that the emperor was unimportant or disloyal to the polity. It referred to a technical category used in legal theory to distinguish an institutional office from the entire legal order. For decades the approach had been taught at universities and used by officials who believed the constitution could accommodate cabinet responsibility, parliamentary legislation, and modern administrative practices.

Nationalist opponents recast this doctrinal argument as an affront to the emperor's dignity. Kikuchi Takeo attacked Minobe in the House of Peers in February 1935; veterans' groups, politicians, and military figures extended the controversy into meetings, newspapers, and political pressure on the cabinet. The Seiyūkai's opposition role gave the campaign partisan incentives as well as ideological ones. Not everyone who challenged Minobe had the same view of the appropriate constitutional order. Some wanted to discredit the sitting government; others regarded abstract legal categories as incompatible with a sacred national polity; still others saw a chance to remove intellectual obstacles to broader military power.

The Okada cabinet first sought to separate scholarly debate from government decisions, but pressure intensified. On August 3, 1935, the government issued its first declaration clarifying the national polity and rejecting the use of the emperor-as-organ theory. The second declaration, issued October 15, adopted a still firmer repudiation. These dates are established in Japan's National Archives and contemporary diplomatic reporting. They should not be confused with later reminiscences that put the second statement in December. Minobe's books faced restrictions and he withdrew from the House of Peers in 1935; in 1936 an assailant wounded him. The sequence reveals how a dispute about interpretation could end with official sanctions and threats to personal safety.

The significance reaches beyond one scholar's career. Constitutional language helped determine whether civilian officials could defend their authority by appealing to established legal principles. By branding a once respected interpretation as contrary to the national polity, the campaign weakened a vocabulary for restraining extraordinary claims of military or imperial prerogative. Yet rejecting Minobe did not repeal the constitution, abolish the Diet, or settle all disagreements among officials. The change lay in the range of permissible argument and the political cost of making it. Source criticism should distinguish Minobe's own writings, parliamentary speeches by his opponents, the cabinet's official declarations, and later accounts of the confrontation.

The two 1935 statements are themselves a useful source comparison. The first declaration defined the emperor's position against an alleged constitutional misreading. After continued pressure, the October statement more emphatically condemned the theory. The wording and setting demonstrate that government speech could change as political forces pressed their demands. Students should distinguish these official texts from Minobe's technical writings and from accusations made against him. A historian who relies exclusively on opponents' summaries may mistake a theory about the legal structure of sovereignty for a claim that the emperor had no political authority.

Division IV — The February 26 Incident and the Transformation of State Power
Section 75.15

Military Factional Conflict and the Aizawa Incident of 1935

As army minister, General Hayashi Senjūrō in 1935 reassigned officials and removed General Masaki from his post as Inspector General of Military Education in July, with Nagata Tetsuzan, chief of the Military Affairs Bureau, as a central figure. On August 12, 1935, Lieutenant Colonel Aizawa Saburō, a Masaki supporter, killed Nagata in his office. Aizawa's public trial before an army court became a platform for criticism of the "control" clique. The affair weakened discipline and heightened tensions among officers, including the First Division in Tokyo, which was scheduled to deploy to Manchuria.

The Aizawa Incident developed within a prolonged struggle over army personnel policy. Senior officers wanted greater coordination of military planning and appointments, while others feared that a rival faction was taking control of an institution supposed to serve the emperor above politics. In July 1935 General Masaki Jinzaburō was removed from the post of Inspector General of Military Education. Supporters regarded the dismissal as evidence that officials associated with Nagata Tetsuzan were systematically excluding Imperial Way sympathizers. The dispute was therefore not simply about one general's career; it touched the hierarchy through which officers were educated, promoted, and assigned to influential positions.

On August 12 Lieutenant Colonel Aizawa Saburō entered Nagata's office in the Army Ministry and killed him with a sword. The attack turned factional suspicion into an open act of political violence inside army headquarters. Aizawa's justifications emphasized service to the emperor and opposition to supposed corrupt influences, but an assassination was not an authorized mechanism of military discipline. Senior commanders faced an uncomfortable dilemma: severe punishment might intensify grievances among radical officers, while indulgence might signal that ideological anger could justify attacks on superiors. The military's efforts to preserve unity increasingly made ordinary rules of command appear negotiable.

Proceedings against Aizawa became a forum for wider allegations about army leadership. The courtroom could be used to denounce Nagata's associates, challenge the legitimacy of appointments, and attract sympathy for a defendant presented by supporters as motivated by moral outrage. The public character of some testimony intensified the political impact, even while the armed forces continued normal deployments and training. Aizawa's case was still unresolved when younger officers began moving toward the February 1936 uprising. It would be misleading to say the trial caused that revolt on its own; the two emerged from a common set of factional disputes and uncertainties about senior command.

Another immediate factor was the planned transfer of Tokyo's First Division to Manchuria. Radical officers feared that their units would be scattered before they could act and that sympathetic senior patrons would soon be retired or reassigned. They interpreted the political crisis through a language of national renovation, promising to remove alleged enemies of the emperor and rectify rural distress. The danger of their plans was not hidden from everyone in authority, but information was fragmentary, and officials differed over which warnings should be believed. The following events demonstrate the consequences of permitting a heavily armed institution's internal disputes to spill into the political selection of national leaders.

Accounts produced after February 1936 can make the Aizawa affair seem merely a predictable prelude to the uprising. That hindsight obscures uncertainty. Officials in summer 1935 could not know which of several possible responses—prosecution, administrative reshuffle, retirement, reconciliation, or renewed violence—would follow. The institution had tools for disciplining its members, but leaders weighed their use against internal solidarity and factional pressure. Tracing these options gives the story analytical depth: the crisis was not a mechanical clash of personalities, and the outcome depended on decisions by officers who still possessed alternatives.

Section 75.16

February 26, 1936: The Military Rebellion in Tokyo

In the snowy early hours of February 26, 1936, about twenty junior officers led some 1,400 troops in attacks on government and police sites in central Tokyo. They killed Finance Minister Takahashi Korekiyo, Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal Saitō Makoto, and Inspector General of Military Education Watanabe Jōtarō; wounded Grand Chamberlain Suzuki Kantarō; and attacked the prime minister's residence, where they killed Okada's brother-in-law, mistaking him for Okada, who survived in hiding. The rebels occupied the Diet area, the army ministry, and police headquarters. Influenced by the thought of Kita Ikki, they sought a "Shōwa Restoration" under the emperor, with the removal of "evil advisers." They issued manifestos and demanded a new government, but had no plan to govern or to take control of the key institutions of the emperor's household and the navy.

The uprising began before dawn on February 26, 1936, during heavy snowfall in Tokyo. Young army officers led roughly 1,400 soldiers into central government districts, directing attacks on officials they identified as obstacles to an imperial restoration. They killed former prime minister and Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal Saitō Makoto, Finance Minister Takahashi Korekiyo, and General Watanabe Jōtarō. Grand Chamberlain Suzuki Kantarō was gravely wounded but survived. At Prime Minister Okada Keisuke's residence, assailants killed his brother-in-law Matsuo Denzō, mistaking him for Okada. The premier escaped and remained concealed while the rebels asserted that he had been eliminated. These mistaken and incomplete outcomes mattered because the assassination plan did not remove all its intended opponents.

The officers occupied key streets and administrative sites around Nagatachō and Miyakezaka, bringing a portion of the capital under armed control. Their proclamations called for a 'Shōwa Restoration' and the removal of corrupt officials around the emperor. They drew on nationalist and emperor-centered language, and some had studied the ideas of Kita Ikki, but their programs were not identical. They expected their seizure of the center and assassination of influential figures to compel senior officers and the imperial court to accept a reorganized government. Yet they had not secured reliable cooperation from the navy, a unified army high command, or the emperor. Nor had they established an effective civilian administration able to operate if their demand for imperial approval failed.

Several social explanations require separation. Many soldiers were conscripts from rural communities that had suffered from the depression, and the officers invoked village hardship as moral justification. This does not prove that the conscripts shared their commanders' political objectives. Some simply followed the orders of superior officers and later said they did not know the larger plan. Meanwhile civilians in the capital encountered roadblocks, restrictions on movement, rumors, and uncertainty over the government's survival. The revolt therefore had very different meanings for its intellectual supporters, its officer organizers, rank-and-file participants, ordinary Tokyo residents, and relatives of those attacked.

In the first hours the reaction of army leadership was uneven. Some commanders hoped to prevent bloodshed and perhaps accommodate the officers' motives without recognizing their actions as lawful. Others saw a direct threat to command and constitutional order. The uncertainty was visible in official wording that initially treated the young officers' intentions sympathetically or ambiguously before the government's response hardened. The rebels' failure to win immediate political recognition did not end the danger, because they still controlled armed units near the center of government. The question shifted from whether their grievances might be considered to whether their weapons and troops would be allowed to dictate a government.

The killings affected policy directly. Takahashi, as finance minister, had resisted unlimited military demands because the budget and currency had constraints. His death removed a major voice in fiscal bargaining at a moment of growing rearmament. Saitō's murder deprived the court of a senior adviser whose standing extended beyond party politics. Watanabe was a central figure in military education and appointments. These choices of targets reveal an attempt to reshape the political structure rather than simply punish personal enemies. Yet targeting powerful figures did not guarantee a functioning alternative government; the conspirators underestimated the importance of institutions beyond their occupied streets.

Section 75.17

Emperor Hirohito, the Suppression of the Uprising, and the Consolidation of Army Control

Emperor Hirohito rejected the uprising and demanded its suppression. Senior officers initially hesitated, but martial law and preparations by loyal army and naval forces increasingly isolated the insurgents, who surrendered on February 29. Trials before special military courts differentiated principal officers from enlisted soldiers who had followed orders. A July 7, 1936 diplomatic report recorded death sentences for thirteen officers and four civilians; most of those condemned in these proceedings were executed that month. Kita Ikki and Nishida Mitsugi were executed in later, separate proceedings in 1937. The subsequent purge weakened officers associated with the Imperial Way faction and strengthened senior military administrators, even as civilian control remained fragile.

Hirohito's response was decisive in the immediate suppression of the February 26 uprising. The attackers had killed or wounded men closely associated with the court and government, and their invocation of loyalty to the emperor did not persuade him to authorize their program. He demanded that the rebellion be put down. That reaction should not be confused with a general judgment that the emperor opposed every earlier military initiative or the later expansion of Japan's war. It is a specific historical fact about a particular armed revolt, to be evaluated alongside his different actions and responsibilities in other crises. Senior officers had to reckon with the fact that the sovereign whose name the rebels invoked explicitly denied them legitimacy.

Military leaders prepared a response while making efforts to induce surrender. Martial law was imposed, loyal forces were mobilized, and the navy prepared to defend important facilities. Broadcasts, notices, and commands told enlisted soldiers to abandon the uprising and return to their units. Some troops had little knowledge of the conspiracy and feared punishment for disobeying either their own officers or the central army command. The rebels faced a narrowing set of choices as support failed to materialize. By February 29 the uprising had been suppressed. Its defeat did not erase the shock created by the murders, the spectacle of soldiers in the streets, or the political impression that the army could interrupt national government.

Special military proceedings followed. A contemporary American embassy report on July 7, 1936 recorded that **13 officers and four civilians** had been sentenced to death among those prosecuted for participation; other defendants received imprisonment or suspended penalties, and some soldiers were acquitted after findings that they had merely obeyed orders. These are not seventeen officers. Most of those sentenced to death were executed on July 12, 1936; the cases against civilian ideological figures Kita Ikki and Nishida Mitsugi proceeded separately, and they were executed in 1937. Students should distinguish indictment, sentence, confirmation, and execution dates, rather than treating all defendants as one group.

Army leaders used the aftermath to reassign or retire officers associated with the Imperial Way network. The outcome is paradoxical only if one assumes that all military power was located with the rebels. Their defeat enabled a different constellation of senior officers, personnel managers, and reform-minded bureaucrats to present themselves as guardians of order. Civilian politicians, however, confronted a military organization able to claim both the authority of suppressing rebellion and the right to shape future cabinets. The balance shifted away from revolutionary junior officers and toward organized institutional power—not toward a restoration of party-led control. This interpretation connects the uprising with the rule changes of May 1936 without making the coup's failure an automatic or predestined cause.

The treatment of enlisted men also illustrates the distinction between moral responsibility and military hierarchy. Courts could find that a conscript obeyed an officer without knowing that he was participating in a political rebellion, even though his presence helped the officers occupy public buildings. Higher-ranking planners possessed different information and choices. Historical explanations should not reduce the entire uprising to a faceless mass of 'rebels' or assume every participant had equal responsibility. The July 1936 sentencing report is therefore valuable not just for the corrected numbers but for the differences in how the military justice system characterized degrees of participation.

Section 75.18

The Hirota Cabinet, the Restoration of the Active-Duty Minister Rule, and Military Leverage

Hirota Kōki, a diplomat, formed a cabinet in March 1936 under army pressure over its composition. On May 18, 1936, the cabinet restored the requirement that the army and navy ministers be active-duty generals and admirals, a rule that had been relaxed in 1913. Because a service could refuse to supply a minister, it could prevent a cabinet from forming or force one to resign. When Ugaki Kazushige, a former army minister, was asked to form a cabinet in January 1937, the army refused to name a minister and he failed, though army opinion was itself divided. Hayashi Senjūrō formed the next cabinet in February 1937.

Okada's government could not continue normally after the February crisis. Hirota Kōki, formerly foreign minister and a senior diplomat, became prime minister in March 1936. The selection and composition of his cabinet showed the practical bargaining required to secure cooperation from the services. Army leaders were increasingly insistent that ministerial appointments and policy commitments reflect military priorities. The cabinet was not devoid of civilian politicians or administrators, and Hirota was not a mere uniformed subordinate. Its vulnerability arose from constitutional arrangements that gave the services institutional means of leverage independent of electoral results.

The most consequential formal change was the restoration, on May 18, 1936, of the requirement that army and navy ministers be serving officers on active duty. This had originally been imposed in 1900 and relaxed in 1913. It had not been continuously operative throughout the 1920s and early 1930s. Under the restored system a service could deny a proposed cabinet an eligible minister or withdraw cooperation during a crisis. A prime minister might have electoral support and imperial authorization to attempt forming a government but still lack the personnel needed to complete the cabinet. The power was not an unlimited legal right to control all policy; it was a highly effective veto at a strategic point in government formation.

This leverage was tested when Ugaki Kazushige, himself a former army minister, received the imperial command to assemble a cabinet in January 1937. Army opposition blocked the provision of an army minister and Ugaki abandoned the attempt on January 29. His memorandum protesting that the emperor's order had been obstructed is preserved in the National Diet Library. The episode demonstrates the distinction between the formal authority to appoint a premier and the practical capacity to assemble a functioning ministry. The obstruction involved real decisions by identifiable officers; it was not an abstract consequence mechanically generated by constitutional text.

Hayashi Senjūrō subsequently became prime minister in February 1937. His selection did not end arguments over budgets, diplomacy, or policy. Members of the Diet could still scrutinize ministries, and opposition politicians remained capable of criticizing the army. The new personnel requirement nevertheless altered the strategic position of every future candidate for the premiership. It incentivized politicians to anticipate military acceptance in advance and gave the services new means to press demands without openly staging a coup. That is why the 1936 minister rule belongs among the institutional consequences of militarization rather than merely as an obscure technical regulation.

Ministerial appointment rules were consequential because forming a cabinet required participation by the very institution a prime minister might wish to restrain. A refusal to provide an officer could prevent a new cabinet from starting, while ministerial resignation could create a crisis for an existing one. The leverage was more indirect than tanks outside parliament, but it could be employed repeatedly and with fewer political costs. Comparing the 1913 relaxation and 1936 restoration demonstrates how apparently technical eligibility provisions can alter the distribution of authority in a constitutional system without changing its most visible institutions.

Section 75.19

Rearmament, National Policy, and the Approach of the China War

In August 1936 the Hirota cabinet endorsed Basic Principles of National Policy, combining southward ("naval") expansion with northern ("army") priorities, expansion of armaments, and the consolidation of Manchukuo as a base. Military budgets rose, and bureaucrats of the Planning Office and the army ministry increasingly drafted production-expansion plans linked to Manchukuo's. On November 25, 1936, Japan signed the Anti-Comintern Pact with Germany. Political arrangements at the start of 1937 were unstable: parties opposed the army's influence, and the Diet was dissolved in March 1937. The chapter ends here; war with China began in July 1937 (Chapter 76).

By late 1936 the Japanese government was attempting to reconcile strategic aims that pulled in different directions. The army worried about the Soviet Union and the security of Manchukuo; the navy prioritized maritime supply routes, the Pacific balance, and access to resources farther south. Both sought greater industrial and financial capacity. The August 1936 Basic Principles of National Policy provided an official framework, but a broad statement could not resolve disputes over procurement, future expansion, and how far the civilian economy should be organized around military needs. Its significance lies partly in the ambition to bring diplomacy, military planning, industrial policy, and imperial territory into closer alignment.

The planned development of Manchukuo belonged to this wider effort. Officials argued that coal, iron, steel, transport, and agricultural production in Northeast China could support defense and reduce dependence on vulnerable imports. In practice the plans themselves demonstrated dependence on specialists, capital, foreign technology, railways, and labor recruitment. Economic bureaucracies and military ministries increasingly collaborated over forecasts and targets, but their agreements could conceal conflicts about who would pay and who would control the resulting enterprises. The process was thus a transition toward more intensive state-directed mobilization, not yet the wartime economy of the 1940s.

In diplomacy Japan's signing of the Anti-Comintern Pact with Germany on November 25, 1936, signaled an anti-Soviet orientation and new international alignments. The treaty's declared concern with Communist International activities was not identical to a binding commitment to fight a joint war, and its later significance should not be projected back unchanged onto 1936. Japanese officials also had to manage relations with Britain, the United States, the Soviet Union, and China, whose interests often conflicted with the empire's ambitions in the northeast. International choices remained contingent, despite the growing influence of military calculations.

The political order entering 1937 was fragile rather than uniformly settled. Ugaki's failure showed a concrete military veto in cabinet formation, while the Diet and political parties continued to exist and government policy could still be contested. Hayashi's cabinet dissolved the lower house in March 1937, leading to an election beyond the chapter's core narrative. The Manchukuo industrial plan had been framed, but much of its implementation lay ahead. The full-scale fighting that began at the Marco Polo Bridge in July 1937 belongs to Chapter 76. This ending should leave readers with a historically precise problem: the state had become more capable of military mobilization and less capable of restraining military initiatives, but the particular path from early 1937 to total war was not yet fixed.

The chapter should end by distinguishing several meanings of militarization. Rearmament meant increased weapons, industrial capacity, and military expenditure. Institutional militarization meant stronger service influence over appointments and policy. Social militarization meant patriotic messages, training, and pressure on associations and schools. These processes developed at different speeds; none required the total disappearance of civilian life. By early 1937, the Japanese state had moved substantially along all three dimensions, but much of the machinery associated with later total war was still under development. Chapter 76 can then begin with a genuine historical transition rather than a foregone conclusion.

Division V — Historical Interpretation and Chapter Review
Section 75.20

Manchukuo, Military Power, and the Transformation of the Japanese State: Synthesis and Review

Comparing the two arenas reveals a common pattern: military power entered existing institutions. In Manchukuo it worked through a formal state, ministers, and a mass association; in Japan through cabinet formation, budgets, and the ministers' veto, while elections and the Diet persisted. Economic planners, bureaucrats, and some businessmen participated in both, and ordinary people, from displaced Manchurian farmers to Japanese villagers drawn toward emigration, were affected. Chinese resistance continued throughout. Manchukuo shaped Japanese planning, but Japan's turn toward war in 1937 was not fixed by 1936.

The two halves of this chapter show that military power can be embedded in government without suspending every civilian institution. In Manchukuo, the regime used the language of sovereignty, an emperor, ministries, a mass association, and economic plans. Yet effective authority frequently rested with Japanese advisers, army commanders, and corporations connected to imperial strategy. In Japan, constitutional forms, elections, civil ministries, and legal argument persisted, while the army became more capable of determining which cabinets could form and which policies were politically sustainable. Neither system can be explained by a simple contrast between 'military' and 'civilian': planners, politicians, bureaucrats, companies, police, and local collaborators were central participants.

Colonial society reveals the unequal social distribution of those changes. A railway engineer, Chinese cultivator facing a coerced land sale, Korean migrant farmer, Japanese settler's wife, Manchu official, and Chinese guerrilla confronted the same state from positions of markedly different power. Ideals of ethnic harmony were tested against land tenure, employment, education, policing, and the ability to express disagreement. Government records make its officials visible; testimony and local histories are necessary to recover other perspectives. Historians must neither erase local collaborators' agency nor accept their participation as proof that the political order was freely chosen.

The February 26 Incident demonstrates the limits of inevitability. Radical officers tried to transform the state by violent insurrection; the emperor opposed them and loyal forces defeated them. Yet the institutional aftermath expanded military leverage through appointments, factional purges, and the restoration of the active-duty minister rule. That outcome resulted from choices made after the revolt, not from the mere fact that a revolt occurred. The Minobe controversy similarly shows how political pressure could discredit a legitimate constitutional interpretation without formally repealing the underlying constitution.

To synthesize the evidence, compare the Manchukuo General Affairs Board with the Japanese Army Ministry: which decisions could each shape, and what checks remained? Separate a **plan** from an **outcome** when evaluating migration and industrialization. Reconstruct the sequence from the 1935 Minobe declarations through February 1936 and January 1937. Finally, test three possible explanations for militarization—constitutional structure, institutional competition, and deliberate political choices—against examples from both Manchukuo and Japan. No single cause explains all of the changes. These questions prepare the study of Japan's war in China without suggesting that every later action was predetermined.

Chapter Summary

Between 1932 and early 1937, Japan's transformation into a militarized imperial state proceeded through the construction of Manchukuo and the redistribution of political authority at home. Manchukuo, proclaimed in March 1932, was formally independent, with Puyi as chief executive and then emperor, but the Kwantung Army and Japanese officials in the General Affairs Board held decisive power.

Ideology disguised hierarchy: the Concordia Association's rhetoric of harmony among peoples coexisted with unequal access to land, wages, and office. Territorial consolidation through the Rehe campaign and the Tanggu Truce extended Japanese control to the Great Wall.

Manchukuo's economy centered on the South Manchuria Railway, coal and steel, and a state-directed plan for 1937–1941, whose targets must be distinguished from results. Settlement schemes projected a million households but displaced local cultivators long before they reached their goals.

Chinese, Manchu, Mongol, Korean, and Japanese residents experienced the regime differently, and the regime relied on policing, counterinsurgency, and violence against resisters.

In Japan, the Saitō and Okada cabinets governed without party leaders while elections and the Diet continued. The Imperial Way and Control factions competed over the army's future, and repression, the tenkō movement, and the Takigawa Incident narrowed public debate.

The attack on Minobe Tatsukichi's theory in 1935 weakened a constitutional doctrine that had sustained party government.

The assassination of Nagata and the February 26 Incident of 1936 formed the principal domestic turning point. Hirohito rejected the rebellion and it was suppressed, yet the army's institutional leverage grew, as the restoration of the active-duty minister rule and the failure of Ugaki's cabinet in 1937 showed.

The Basic Principles of National Policy and the Anti-Comintern Pact tied expansion and rearmament together. Historians debate whether Manchukuo was chiefly a military colony or a bureaucratic experiment; the evidence supports elements of both.

The chapter's lesson is that military power expanded by entering existing institutions, and that the war of 1937 was not predetermined. Chapter 76 follows the transformed state into full-scale war.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that militarization was not a single coup but a layered process. Authority moved through institutions that continued to exist, which made it harder for contemporaries to see where power had gone.

It also connects regions: Manchuria, Korea, North China, and Tokyo became parts of one system, and people far from the capital bore many of its costs. Manchukuo's farmers, laborers, and resisters belong to Japanese history because Japanese policy shaped their lives.

Later developments, including full-scale war in China (Chapter 76), mobilization (Chapter 77), and wartime colonial violence (Chapter 79), grew from these arrangements, though they were not inevitable. Continuities, from planning bureaucracies to administrative personnel, survived 1945 in altered forms, a theme for later chapters.
