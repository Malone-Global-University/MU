Part XIV — Japan after the Bubble
98

Kobe, Aum Shinrikyō, Religion, Crime, and Social Anxiety

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

At 5:46 on the morning of January 17, 1995, most residents of the Kobe–Awaji corridor were asleep. Within seconds, tens of thousands of older wooden houses, an elevated expressway, rail lines, water mains, and gas pipes were failing at once. Two months later, on March 20, commuters boarding subway trains toward Kasumigaseki, the heart of Japan's central government, were exposed to a chemical nerve agent released by members of a religious organization. Different in cause, scale, and meaning, the two events nevertheless arrived within sixty-two days of each other.

This chapter treats 1995 as a turning point, but it asks students to be careful about what kind of turning point it was. The Great Hanshin–Awaji Earthquake was a natural hazard converted into an urban catastrophe by the way people had built, lived, and governed along a densely settled coast. The Tokyo subway sarin attack was an intentional act of terrorism planned and carried out by identifiable members of Aum Shinrikyō. Chronological proximity does not establish a common cause. What the two events shared was an effect: they unsettled assumptions, widely held at home and abroad, that Japan was a uniquely safe society whose institutions could be relied upon to anticipate danger and respond competently when it arrived.

The chapter does not stop with those two tragedies. It follows their institutional aftermath through the late 1990s and 2000s: the reform of disaster administration, the rise of volunteer and nonprofit activity, the amendment of religious-corporation law, the 1999 statute regulating organizations that had committed indiscriminate mass murder, the debates over juvenile justice that followed the 1997 Kobe child murders, the surge and decline in police-recorded crime, the development of victims' rights, school-safety policy after the 2001 Ikeda attack, and the public conversation about bullying, school refusal, and social withdrawal. Each of these belongs here because it helps explain a historically significant change in law, public policy, journalism, community organization, or perceptions of danger. This chapter is not an encyclopedia of notorious crimes.

Its central analytical distinction is among four things that are easily blurred: actual threats, perceptions of danger, media representations, and governmental responses. Recorded crime, for instance, rose in the late 1990s and early 2000s, but the rise consisted largely of theft and was shaped by changes in police practice. Public fear and recorded crime did not simply move together. A fifth element must always remain in view: the experiences of victims and survivors, who are too often reduced to casualty totals while the notoriety of perpetrators dominates public memory.

The chapter also insists on three safeguards. First, Aum Shinrikyō was one organization with a documented authoritarian structure and criminal record; its crimes cannot be treated as representative of Japan's many lawful new religions, Buddhist and Shinto institutions, or religious minorities. Second, social withdrawal and mental-health difficulties are not predictors of violent crime, and the chapter examines them as social and institutional problems in their own right. Third, because the casualty figures for both the earthquake and the subway attack have been revised or depend on definitions, the chapter specifies which figure belongs to which date and source. It also omits technical details about chemical weapons, which are unnecessary for historical understanding.

Evidence for this chapter is unusually abundant and unusually uneven. It includes Cabinet Office and prefectural chronologies of the earthquake, engineering reports, statutes and Diet records, police and prosecutorial records, court judgments, annual white papers on crime, the Public Security Intelligence Agency's published materials on Aum, television and newspaper coverage, survivor testimony, and oral-history works such as Murakami Haruki's Underground (1997). Government retrospectives, written years afterward, must be distinguished from documents contemporary to the events. Statistics reflect the definitions of the agencies that compiled them, and testimony reflects the position of the person speaking.

Chapter 98 follows Chapter 97's account of economic stagnation, nonregular employment, and demographic change, whose background it uses without repeating. It prepares for Chapter 99, which examines the 2011 earthquake, tsunami, and Fukushima nuclear disaster and the political debates that followed. The chapter ends around 2010 with a mixed conclusion: Japan's reputation for public safety did not disappear, but the meaning of safety became more complicated, encompassing the reliability of institutions, the protection of rights, and the capacity of communities to respond collectively to crisis.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Great Hanshin–Awaji Earthquake caused such extensive destruction, distinguishing seismic hazard from social and structural vulnerability.
  2. Assess the documented strengths and failures of the government response in January 1995 without reducing them to a claim that nothing was done.
  3. Analyze the emergence of volunteer and nonprofit activity after Kobe and the legal framework created by the 1998 NPO Law.
  4. Trace the development of Aum Shinrikyō from 1984 to 1995 and the escalation of its violence, distinguishing documented organizational mechanisms from psychological generalization.
  5. Reconstruct the March 20, 1995 attack, distinguishing contemporaneous casualty figures from later revised totals.
  6. Evaluate competing legal principles in the 1995 and 1999 measures that followed Aum: public safety, religious freedom, association, proportionality, and state authority.
  7. Distinguish investigative reporting, entertainment-oriented coverage, moral panic, and documented evidence in media treatment of Aum.
  8. Interpret victim and survivor testimony as a form of evidence differing from official and courtroom records.
  9. Compare organized crime, juvenile violence, and school attacks as separate problems producing distinct legal and policy responses.
  10. Evaluate crime statistics critically, distinguishing recorded offenses, rates, clearance, theft, and violent crime from public perceptions of danger.
  11. Contextualize preparedness reform after 1995 through the comparison of the Kobe and Chūetsu earthquakes.
  12. Explain why the crises of the 1990s and 2000s cannot be reduced to a single narrative of declining public safety.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why could a technologically advanced society suffer such extensive urban destruction from a foreseeable seismic hazard, and who bore the consequences most heavily?
  2. What did Kobe reveal about the capacity of the state, and what did it reveal about the capacity and limits of community-based action?
  3. How did a lawful religious environment of constitutional freedom and many new religions coexist with, and fail to prevent, the violence of one organization?
  4. What institutional failures allowed Aum's escalation from 1989 to 1995, and which were failures of information, jurisdiction, or assumption?
  5. How should a democracy regulate organizations that have committed mass violence without weakening the religious freedom of everyone else?
  6. How do media coverage, official statistics, and public fear relate to one another, and why do they diverge?
  7. How did the experiences and advocacy of victims change law and public memory?
  8. Was 1995 a crisis of institutional legitimacy, a crisis of preparedness, or something that those labels do not capture?

Historical Context

By the early 1990s Japan had been among the wealthiest societies in the world for a generation. Its reputation for public safety was real: homicide rates were low by international comparison, the neighborhood police box (kōban) was a familiar feature of city life, and commuters moved through crowded transit systems with little fear of violence. The reputation also formed part of a larger self-image, constructed during the postwar decades, of an orderly, cohesive, and technically competent society. Chapters 91 and 96 traced the economic and cultural foundations of that image; Chapter 97 described how the collapse of the asset bubble from 1990 strained it through banking crisis, restructuring, and growing insecurity in employment.

The archipelago's seismic setting, described in Chapter 1, was never in doubt. Japan sits where several tectonic plates meet, and the Great Kantō Earthquake of 1923 (Chapter 71) had killed more than 100,000 people. After 1923, building codes, fire-resistant construction, and public education gradually developed, and the 1948 Fukui earthquake and 1978 Miyagi-oki earthquake produced further revisions. Yet many of those lessons were absorbed unevenly. Public discussion in the early 1990s focused heavily on the anticipated Tōkai earthquake east of Kobe, while the Kansai region was widely regarded as less likely to suffer a severe shock. This assumption, the physical reality of older housing stock, and a disaster administration designed around smaller, localized events set the stage for January 1995.

Religion, too, had a long modern history. The Meiji state had organized Shinto into a state cult (Chapter 77), and the Occupation had abolished that arrangement and written religious freedom and the separation of religion and state into the 1947 Constitution (Chapter 84). Postwar decades saw rapid growth of new religious movements, large organizations such as Sōka Gakkai and Risshō Kōseikai, and, from the 1970s, smaller spiritual groups appealing to young people. Meanwhile, the state's reluctance to scrutinize religious bodies, a reaction against wartime control, shaped how police and local governments approached any organization claiming religious status.

Finally, the 1990s were a decade of institutional uncertainty. The "1955 system" of Liberal Democratic Party dominance had ended in 1993 (Chapter 92), and the government in office in January 1995 was a coalition led by Socialist Prime Minister Murayama Tomiichi. Bureaucratic reputations, once taken for granted, were under scrutiny. Students should avoid reading this setting teleologically: the events of 1995 did not occur because Japan was already in decline, and later reforms did not follow from a single master plan.

Division I — Kobe and the Crisis of Urban Safety Section 98.1

January 17, 1995: The Great Hanshin–Awaji Earthquake

At 5:46 a.m. on January 17, 1995, a rupture beneath the northern end of Awaji Island and the waters of Akashi Strait shook southern Hyōgo Prefecture. The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) assigned the earthquake a magnitude of 7.3 and measured seismic intensity 6 at Kobe and Sumoto. Subsequent JMA field investigations established intensity 7 in parts of Kobe, Awaji Island, and neighboring districts; at the time, this highest category was assigned by field survey rather than by an instrumental intensity-7 reading. Seismologists also use the moment magnitude scale, which for this event is approximately Mw 6.9. The two figures measure related but different things and are not in conflict. The Japanese name for the event, Hyōgo-ken Nanbu Jishin, refers to the "southern Hyōgo" earthquake; the later name, Hanshin–Awaji Daishinsai ("Great Hanshin–Awaji Disaster"), was adopted to describe the disaster as a whole.

The geography mattered as much as the shaking. The affected region ran along a narrow coastal plain between the Rokkō mountains and Osaka Bay, from Awaji Island through Kobe, Ashiya, and Nishinomiya toward Osaka. This corridor contained one of Japan's most heavily populated and industrial strips: a major container port, steel and shipbuilding works, sake breweries, dense residential neighborhoods, and the rail and expressway links connecting western and central Japan. The fault system ran directly beneath or alongside this strip. An earthquake of comparable magnitude in a thinly populated region would have been a regional emergency; here it produced a national catastrophe.

The Cabinet Office/JMA series based on Fire and Disaster Management Agency figures updated in December 2005 records 6,434 deaths; Japan's 2026 disaster white paper reports a revised total of 6,437. The chapter preserves these source-dependent figures separately rather than suggesting a single timeless count. The older total includes disaster-related deaths: Hyōgo Prefecture's 2005 accounting listed 919 such deaths within the prefecture, involving illness, stress, or evacuation conditions rather than direct physical injury. The prefectural and nationwide figures do not have identical geographic or reporting bases. Hundreds of thousands of buildings were damaged; at the height of the emergency more than 300,000 people were in evacuation shelters. These are rounded, commonly cited figures, and students should consult the official chronologies for exact definitions. The vast majority of deaths occurred in the first hours, most from the collapse of houses.

One should not infer that the destruction followed automatically from the earthquake's magnitude. The same shaking produced very different outcomes depending on how buildings were constructed, how old they were, who lived in them, and how the neighborhood was laid out. The next section turns to those differences, and a fuller treatment of the Japanese archipelago's seismic setting may be found in Chapter 1.

Section 98.2

Collapsed Buildings, Fires, Infrastructure Failure, and Unequal Vulnerability

Engineering investigations, including a detailed U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) study of structural, utility, and fire-protection performance, document a consistent pattern. Most deaths occurred in older low-rise wooden houses with heavy clay-tile roofs and limited bracing, many built before the 1981 revision of Japan's seismic building standards. Neighborhoods of such houses, often rented by elderly or lower-income households, were packed tightly along narrow lanes. When the ground floor collapsed, occupants were crushed or trapped; in the dark early morning, many were in bed. Newer buildings built to post-1981 standards generally performed far better, though not uniformly, and some mid-rise buildings failed at weak intermediate stories.

Fires compounded the destruction. Broken gas lines, overturned stoves, and electrical faults ignited blazes in the collapsed districts, particularly in Nagata Ward of Kobe, and thousands of buildings burned. Water mains were broken, hydrants lost pressure, and narrow streets blocked by debris prevented fire engines from reaching many fires. The fire department's capacity, sized for ordinary urban fires rather than dozens of simultaneous ignitions, was overwhelmed.

Infrastructure failure was equally visible. A kilometer-long section of the elevated Hanshin Expressway toppled sideways, and the photograph of the overturned roadway became an emblem of the disaster. Rail lines, including the Shinkansen and private commuter lines, were severed, and the port of Kobe, then among the world's busiest container ports, suffered extensive damage to quays and cranes. Electricity, water, gas, and telephone service failed over large areas, and restoration took days to months depending on the system. These failures interacted: blocked roads slowed repairs, which prolonged shortages, which complicated shelter operation.

Historical Vocabulary
Seismic intensity (shindo), vulnerability, resilience, and civil society

Shindo (seismic intensity) measures the strength of shaking at a particular place, in contrast to magnitude, which measures the energy released at the source. A single earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities. Vulnerability describes how much harm a given hazard will cause, determined by buildings, infrastructure, planning, and the social position of the people exposed. Resilience refers to the capacity of a community and its institutions to absorb a shock and recover. Civil society means the sphere of voluntary associations, neighborhood groups, nonprofits, and citizen networks that exist between households and the state.

These terms matter because they help students separate the natural event from the disaster. The phrase "natural disaster" can conceal the human decisions about housing, planning, and administration that decide who is harmed.

Vulnerability was unevenly distributed. Elderly residents, who made up a disproportionate share of the dead, often lived alone in the oldest housing. Renters could not reinforce houses they did not own. Lower-income households were concentrated in older districts where redevelopment had been slow; people with disabilities faced barriers in evacuation and shelters; and foreign residents, including long-resident Korean communities in Kobe and newer migrants, encountered language barriers and, in some cases, uncertainty about aid. The planning, ownership, and maintenance decisions of earlier decades thus determined the pattern of harm. The central question for this section is therefore not why the earthquake was strong but why a technologically advanced society had left so many people living in structures that could not withstand a hazard that seismologists considered possible.

The 1981 seismic building-code revision is especially important for historical comparison, but it cannot be treated as a magic dividing line between safe and unsafe structures. Construction quality, foundation conditions, additions made after completion, the distribution of soft soils, and how a building was maintained also affected outcomes. Engineering reports identified failures in some reinforced-concrete structures, as well as the catastrophic collapse of many timber houses. The image of the overturned elevated expressway was not just an emblem of a spectacular engineering mistake: the loss of a key arterial route impeded rescue, logistics, and economic activity far beyond the place where its columns failed. In disaster analysis, damage to transportation and lifelines creates secondary consequences even where houses remain standing.

Section 98.3

Government Response, Emergency Services, and the Limits of Disaster Administration

In the first hours, the most effective rescues were carried out by neighbors, not agencies. Studies of survivors, often cited in Japanese disaster scholarship, found that the great majority of those pulled alive from collapsed houses were rescued by family members and neighbors, because professional responders could not reach the districts quickly. This finding should not be mistaken for a claim that officials did nothing. Municipal fire departments, police, hospitals, rail and utility companies, Hyōgo Prefecture, and the national government all acted. Kobe's own city hall and many municipal offices were themselves damaged, and many officials were victims or unable to reach their posts.

The problems that critics identified were nonetheless real and are documented in the Cabinet Office's chronology. Communications failed; telephone lines were overloaded and damaged, and information about the extent of destruction reached central authorities unevenly. Early reports in Tokyo underestimated the casualties. Roads clogged with traffic delayed rescue vehicles. Under the legal framework of the time, the Self-Defense Forces were dispatched at the request of the prefectural governor, and the formal request was recorded at approximately 10:00 a.m., after which units had to travel to a region where roads were blocked. Earlier contacts and preliminary activity mean that the formal request time should not be equated with the first moment any military personnel began responding. The timing and process of the dispatch became a lasting subject of dispute, and the legal and practical questions it raised, namely who could request help, how quickly, and with what information, led to changes in procedure. Offers of help from foreign governments and teams also encountered procedural hurdles.

Underlying these episodes was a structural issue: disaster administration was built for smaller, more localized events and relied on a division of responsibility among municipalities, prefectures, and the national government that assumed local authorities would remain functional. When the earthquake incapacitated local capacity and demanded national coordination, the system strained. The Murayama cabinet established a headquarters for emergency measures, and within the following year the Diet amended the Disaster Countermeasures Basic Act, strengthened emergency headquarters and coordination provisions, and later created a cabinet-level disaster-management post and reinforced the national fire-and-disaster-management agency's cross-regional emergency teams. Seismic retrofitting of existing buildings was promoted by a 1995 law, and communications and information systems were developed.

Chronology clarifies the administrative problem. The government's later lessons archive records a 5:46 a.m. earthquake, a 6:13 a.m. announcement of intensity 6 at Kobe, and a cascade of communications and infrastructure failures. The formal Hyōgo request for Self-Defense Forces assistance was recorded at about 10:00 a.m.; that time needs to be read alongside early local contacts, the condition of roads, and the practical ability of military units to reach the worst-damaged neighborhoods. A request authorized at a particular time did not instantly produce rescuers at every collapsed home. Nor should delay in national-level situational awareness be confused with the conduct of municipal officers, many of whom began responding under severe personal and institutional disruption.

The reform agenda raised a wider question about who should possess emergency authority. A system built around prefectural requests can protect local autonomy and prevent military deployment from becoming routine. Yet it also assumes that elected executives and their staffs can communicate and evaluate an emergency while their own offices, personnel, and information networks may be disabled. Post-Kobe debates consequently concerned prearranged mobilization, the exchange of situation reports, mutual-aid agreements, and arrangements for distributing supplies as much as legal powers alone. Emergency plans on paper are only one kind of institutional capacity; transport networks, trained personnel, rehearsed relationships, and public trust determine whether a plan can operate under crisis conditions.

Section 98.4

Survivors, Volunteers, Reconstruction, and the Emergence of Civil Society

In the days after the earthquake, residents opened schools, temples, shrines, and churches as shelters; shopkeepers distributed stock; neighbors shared water and heaters; and members of local associations tracked missing persons. Volunteers arrived from across Japan, many of them students and young workers, by train where lines ran and on foot or bicycle where they did not. Government retrospectives count more than 1.3 million volunteer participations during the relief effort; these are cumulative person-participations, not necessarily that many distinct individuals, and the coverage period and counting method matter. The press named 1995 "the first year of volunteering" (borantia gannen), and the term remains a shorthand for a perceived change in public attitudes toward voluntary action. Religious bodies, including Buddhist temples, Christian churches, and large new religions, ran shelters and relief; the Japan Red Cross, cooperatives, and professional associations also contributed. A caution is in order: volunteering had a long history in Japan, from neighborhood associations to Buddhist charities, and 1995 was less the invention of civil society than the moment at which it became visible to the national public.

That visibility created pressure for legal recognition. Under the Civil Code then in force, groups wishing to acquire legal personality, hold property, or sign contracts faced demanding requirements, and many volunteer organizations operated as informal bodies. After extended discussion among citizens' groups, parties, and ministries, the Diet enacted the Act on Promotion of Specified Non-profit Activities in March 1998. It took effect on December 1, 1998, and created a simplified route to incorporation for organizations pursuing specified public-interest activities. The law did not itself determine how effective those organizations would be; it provided a legal framework, and subsequent registrations grew considerably over the following years.

Reconstruction produced its own disputes. Survivors first lived in shelters, then in temporary housing built on the periphery or on reclaimed land, often distant from former neighborhoods. Moving elderly residents to isolated sites weakened the social ties on which many depended, and cases of people dying alone in temporary and later public housing (kodokushi) drew national attention. Redevelopment plans for heavily damaged districts raised tensions between residents who wanted rapid rebuilding and planners pursuing large-scale redevelopment; some residents were unable to afford new housing or returned to find their neighborhoods transformed. Government policy initially resisted direct public compensation for private housing loss, a position that citizens' campaigns challenged, leading to later support-law revisions.

Memory institutions developed alongside recovery. The annual Kobe Luminarie light festival, begun in December 1995, memorialized the dead, and the prefecture established museums and research centers to preserve records, survivor accounts, and lessons. The historical argument of this section is that Kobe revealed weaknesses in state capacity while also demonstrating both the capabilities and the limitations of community-based action: neighbors saved lives and volunteers delivered aid, but they could not replace functioning institutions, and the benefits of recovery were unequally distributed.

The relationship between relief and reconstruction needs careful periodization. During the immediate emergency, spontaneous aid and local knowledge often made it possible to deliver food or rescue people despite impassable roads. In subsequent months, the problems became less visible but no less consequential: rent, employment, the loss of small shops, replacement housing, education for displaced children, and the reconstitution of neighborhood networks. A household that had survived the collapse might still lack the financial means to return. Reconstruction statistics describing new units, repaired infrastructure, or aggregate economic output could therefore coexist with profound individual displacement. These different temporal scales help explain why the successful reopening of roads and railways did not signify completed social recovery.

The longer struggle over housing and nonprofit incorporation also demonstrates that volunteers did not simply fill a temporary gap in state services. Some groups developed sustained expertise in counseling, disability assistance, disaster preparation, and advocacy for residents who feared that redevelopment decisions would exclude them. The NPO Law created a relatively accessible form of legal personality, enabling qualifying organizations to enter agreements and administer funds, but incorporation did not remove dependence on donations, volunteers, and uneven municipal cooperation. In retrospect, the significance of 1995 was institutional as well as emotional: it accelerated debate about who could act in the public interest and whether a capable democratic state should regard organized citizens as partners rather than merely as recipients of official instructions.

Division II — Aum Shinrikyō and Religious Terrorism Section 98.5

Religion in Postwar Japan: Constitutional Freedom, New Religions, and Spiritual Communities

Aum Shinrikyō emerged within a religious environment shaped by the postwar settlement. Article 20 of the 1947 Constitution guarantees freedom of religion to everyone, prohibits the state from granting privileges to any religious organization or exercising political authority through one, and forbids the state and its organs from religious education or activity. The Occupation-era Religious Corporations Ordinance, replaced by the Religious Corporations Act of 1951, allowed religious bodies to obtain legal personality by certification, with tax advantages, and the state exercised minimal supervision. The design reflected a determined reaction against wartime control of religion (Chapter 77).

Under this regime the postwar decades saw extraordinary religious diversity. Established Buddhist sects and Shinto shrines continued to anchor family ritual and local festivals, even as many Japanese described themselves as not strongly "religious" in surveys. Alongside them stood the shinshūkyō, "new religions" founded or expanded in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, some of them introduced in Chapter 64. Sōka Gakkai, a lay movement based on Nichiren Buddhism, grew rapidly in the 1950s and 1960s among urban migrants and offered community, welfare, and education. Risshō Kōseikai, Shinnyo-en, Tenrikyō, and many others provided similar networks. Sōka Gakkai's founding of the Kōmeitō party in 1964 made religion's public and political presence a subject of recurring constitutional debate about where permissible political participation ended.

From the 1970s a different kind of religious interest appeared, sometimes called the "new new religions" or the spiritual boom: smaller groups and individual practices emphasizing meditation, yoga, esoteric Buddhism, supernatural powers, and personal transformation. Books on the occult and apocalypse circulated widely, and magazines and television programs gave attention to psychic phenomena. Many participants practiced without any organizational commitment; most groups were benign.

Historical Vocabulary
Shinshūkyō, religious corporation, apocalyptic belief, and freedom of religion

Shinshūkyō ("new religions") is an umbrella label for movements arising since the nineteenth century outside the older Buddhist and Shinto establishments; it covers groups differing enormously in doctrine, size, and politics. A religious corporation (shūkyō hōjin) is a legal status under the Religious Corporations Act that allows property ownership and tax benefits; it is distinct from being a religion. Apocalyptic belief refers to expectation of a coming cataclysm that will end or transform the world; it appears in many traditions and is not by itself linked to violence. Freedom of religion protects belief and association, but Japanese courts and scholars have long recognized that harmful acts such as crimes are not protected by it.

These distinctions matter because later debates after 1995 repeatedly turned on the difference between a group's beliefs, its legal status, and the criminal acts of particular individuals.

An essential distinction governs the rest of this division: Aum Shinrikyō's crimes arose within a specific organization with an authoritarian structure and a documented record of violence. They are not representative of Japanese new religions generally, and the chapter treats lawful religious diversity and the violence of one group as separate subjects.

Religious statistics require the same caution as crime statistics. Government religious-corporation registers count legally recognized organizations such as shrines, temples, churches, and umbrella bodies; they are not direct measures of the number of people who believe in a particular doctrine or participate in religious rites. Different surveys ask about family affiliation, personal belief, religious practice, and formal membership, and these categories produce very different answers. A household may visit a Shinto shrine, maintain Buddhist memorial practices, and identify with no formal religious organization. The post-Aum crisis of confidence therefore affected public language about religion in a society that had never been accurately described by a single measure of religious affiliation.

Section 98.6

The Rise of Aum Shinrikyō: Asahara Shōkō, Recruitment, Doctrine, and Organization

The organization began in 1984 as a small yoga and meditation group in Tokyo founded by Matsumoto Chizuo, born in 1955 in Kumamoto Prefecture, who took the name Asahara Shōkō. In 1987 it adopted the name Aum Shinrikyō and presented itself as a religious movement drawing on Hindu, Buddhist, and esoteric elements, with Asahara as its guru, claiming extraordinary powers and eventually a role as a savior figure. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government certified Aum as a religious corporation in August 1989 after initially hesitating; the decision followed an application process and complaints from critics and families, and later became a focus of debate about oversight.

Aum's teaching combined practices of ascetic training, with a strongly hierarchical system of ranks and initiations, and a growing apocalyptic message predicting catastrophic conflict, in which members would be the survivors. Renunciants (shukke) were encouraged to cut ties with families and live in communal facilities, donating property to the organization. Within the community, disobedience could be punished by confinement or violence, and dissent was suppressed. The organization built an elaborate pseudo-governmental structure of "ministries" and a laboratory and manufacturing capacity, and by the mid-1990s had facilities at Kamikuishiki village at the foot of Mount Fuji, and thousands of members in Japan and a notable following in Russia.

Aum attracted a range of recruits. Prominent among those later prosecuted were highly educated people, including graduates of elite universities, physicians, and scientists, which fascinated commentators. Scholars of religion such as Ian Reader and Erica Baffelli caution against explanations that reduce recruits to a single psychological type or social condition. The documented mechanisms were institutional: the promise of spiritual progress through demanding practice, the appeal of a close community, isolation from outside relationships, financial and personal dependency, escalating commitment, and punishment of doubt. Interpretations that assert that members were simply alienated or naive are unsupported by evidence and risk stigmatizing the many people who were drawn to meditation or spirituality for unremarkable reasons.

In February 1990 Asahara and twenty-four followers ran unsuccessfully as candidates of the "Shinritō" (Truth Party) in the House of Representatives election, and none won. Aum interpreted the humiliating failure as proof of a conspiracy against it, and many analysts regard the period after 1990 as one in which the organization turned more intensely toward isolation, apocalyptic rhetoric, and the pursuit of weapons. The Public Security Intelligence Agency's chronology confirms the principal organizational dates, though it is a later official account and states the government's interpretive framing.

As Aum built institutions of its own, the distinction between ordinary devotion and coercive organization became central. Religious commitment itself does not explain criminal activity, and intense practice may be found in peaceful communities. What investigators later documented at Aum were concentrated personal authority, restrictions on members' contact with outsiders, coercion within facilities, systematic concealment, and the subordination of dissenting members to leaders who claimed exceptional access to truth. Disillusioned former members and worried relatives supplied information that could have challenged the movement's official presentation, although agencies did not always integrate complaints into a coherent picture. Historians must reconstruct this evidence without assuming that every person who joined understood the organization’s secret conduct.

Section 98.7

From Religious Isolation to Organized Violence: The Sakamoto Murders and the Matsumoto Attack

In the late 1980s, families of members began seeking information and the return of relatives from Aum. Sakamoto Tsutsumi, a Yokohama lawyer, represented some of these families and prepared litigation. In early November 1989, he, his wife Satoko, and their fourteen-month-old son Tatsuhiko disappeared from their home; their bodies were found years later, in 1995, after Aum members had confessed. At the time, police did not link the disappearance to Aum, and the failure to investigate more vigorously has been criticized: the evidence at the home included an Aum badge, but investigators did not act on it as a basis for pursuing the organization. After 1995, prosecutors established Aum's responsibility, and members were convicted.

Over the following years, evidence emerged of additional abuses: the confinement and death of members and outsiders, abductions of relatives of defectors, and attempts to intimidate critics. The organization's drive to acquire and use weapons intensified. On June 27, 1994, a night of release of sarin in a residential neighborhood in Matsumoto, Nagano Prefecture, killed eight people and injured hundreds. The target, according to later findings, included judges who were due to rule in a land dispute involving Aum, and the attack hit an apartment complex housing some of them. Seven people died in 1994; an eighth, a woman who had remained in a coma, died in 2008, and the official total is now eight. Contemporary 1994 accounts therefore report seven deaths, and students should check which count a source uses.

The police investigation of the Matsumoto attack is a case study in institutional failure. Early forensic work detected a nerve agent, but authorities focused on a resident, Kōno Yoshiyuki, who had reported the first emergency and whose home contained legally owned chemicals. Police and media treated him as a suspect for months, with heavy damage to his reputation, even as his own wife lay critically injured. Only after the 1995 events did investigators recognize Aum's responsibility, and public apologies were later offered. The episode also shows how a media environment eager to name a culprit could amplify police suspicion.

The central analytical purpose here is to identify warning signs and institutional gaps, not to recount technical details of the weapon. Historians point to limits on information sharing among prefectural police forces, reluctance to investigate a religious corporation, the difficulty of linking disparate incidents, and the absence of anyone assigned to assemble them. These points, drawn from official and scholarly reviews written after the fact, should be treated as interpretations of evidence; hindsight makes the connections appear more obvious than they were at the time.

Section 98.8

March 20, 1995: The Tokyo Subway Sarin Attack

On the morning of Monday, March 20, 1995, five Aum members boarded five trains on three lines of the Tokyo subway during the rush hour. Each carried packages of liquid and punctured them, then left the train. The trains, on the Chiyoda, Marunouchi, and Hibiya lines, were all traveling toward stations around Kasumigaseki, the district housing the national ministries and the National Police Agency. The timing coincided with a pending police operation against Aum, and the prosecution's account, based on court findings, was that the attackers sought to disrupt that operation. Motives remain a subject of interpretation by scholars, including apocalyptic beliefs and organizational defensiveness.

The immediate response was confusion. Passengers and station staff felt sudden symptoms, including difficulty breathing, blurred vision, and collapse, but no one recognized a chemical agent. Some station employees, among them staff at Kasumigaseki and Kodemmachō stations, removed the leaking packages by hand, and two employees died. Trains continued to run for a time, and some contaminated trains carried passengers beyond the first stations. Ambulances were overwhelmed, and thousands of people made their way to hospitals on their own, many by taxi or on foot. Medical staff, who had no briefing on the nature of the agent, learned of it from a physician in Matsumoto and from emergency practitioners who identified the symptoms. The Tokyo Fire Department and police, and later Ground Self-Defense Force chemical-defense units, assisted.

Early records reported twelve or thirteen deaths. Following the death in 2020 of a further victim who had been severely disabled since 1995, commemorations and the Public Security Intelligence Agency now generally report fourteen deaths and more than 6,000 people injured or affected; government compensation records may use a narrower definition. Students must treat these as different counts for different dates and purposes, and not as errors. The attack's victims were ordinary commuters, subway employees, and police and emergency personnel, and the long-term consequences for many extended well beyond the day.

The consequences for public confidence were profound. The attack occurred in the capital's everyday transit system, among people going to work, and it confronted Japan with an unfamiliar form of terrorism: not political assassination or ideological bombing, but an indiscriminate chemical attack by a religious organization. Security in transit, police preparedness for chemical incidents, and the readiness of hospitals all came into question. The attack also shifted public discourse toward religion, which Section 98.11 examines.

The emergency also exposed an information problem inside modern healthcare. Train staff, emergency dispatchers, hospitals, and police initially faced a rapidly developing incident whose cause was uncertain. Patients arrived with different degrees of exposure and symptoms, while ordinary commuter transport became a source of additional danger for those trying to leave affected stations. Officials had to coordinate decisions about station closures, hospital access, protective equipment, and communication before a full account was established. Later reviews connected these difficulties to the development of mass-casualty procedures for unfamiliar hazards, but the practical lesson was broader: highly specialized institutions may work effectively within their normal duties yet require new coordination mechanisms when an event crosses transport, medical, policing, and public-information boundaries at once.

Division III — Religion, Law, Media, and Public Trust Section 98.9

The Investigation of Aum Shinrikyō, Criminal Trials, and Organizational Dissolution

Within hours of the attack, investigators suspected Aum, and on March 22 police began a series of raids on its facilities at Kamikuishiki and elsewhere, ostensibly for an earlier abduction case. The searches uncovered chemicals, equipment, and evidence of the organization's production capacity, and in the following weeks police arrested dozens of members. On March 30 the National Police Agency's director, Kunimatsu Takaji, was shot and seriously wounded by an unidentified gunman; the case remained officially unsolved, and the attack, which investigators associated with Aum in public speculation, is an example of an incident where assertion outran proof. Asahara was arrested on May 16, 1995, found in a hidden compartment at Kamikuishiki.

Prosecutors charged leaders and participants separately for specific offenses: the Sakamoto murders, the Matsumoto attack, the Tokyo attack, killings of members, and others. Over more than a decade, courts convicted many defendants, sentenced a number to death, and imposed prison terms on others. The trials produced extensive records, including testimony from former members, though the long proceedings against Asahara himself yielded little direct account from the defendant. In February 2004, the Tokyo District Court sentenced him to death; his appeal was dismissed on procedural grounds, and the sentence became final in September 2006. Responsibility was assessed by role and offense; membership alone was not a crime, and many members were never charged.

The organization's legal status was addressed separately. The Tokyo District Court ordered Aum's dissolution as a religious corporation in October 1995, and the order was affirmed on appeal; the organization was also declared bankrupt in 1996, with debts to victims far exceeding its assets, which led to the later creation of a special law and fund for compensation. These civil and administrative actions are distinct from criminal convictions, from surveillance, and from the constitutional right to hold beliefs.

Aum continued as an unregistered group. In 2000 its leadership adopted the name Aleph, publicly acknowledged the group's responsibility for the attacks, and pledged compensation; in 2007 a faction under Jōyū Fumihiro formed Hikari no Wa ("Circle of Rainbow Light"), which disavowed Asahara's teachings. Both groups remained under observation. Differences between them, and the existence of members who were not involved in earlier crimes, illustrate the need to separate criminal responsibility, group status, and religious belief.

Retrospective note. Beyond this chapter's main period, Asahara and twelve other convicted members were executed in July 2018. Their deaths ended the criminal cases but did not end debate over how to understand the organization or what questions remained unanswered.

Section 98.10

Religious Freedom, Government Oversight, and the Laws Passed after Aum

Aum's violence raised hard constitutional questions. The Religious Corporations Act of 1951 had given authorities limited power to examine religious corporations, and after 1995 critics argued that lax supervision let a dangerous group operate under legal protection. In December 1995, the Diet amended the Act. The revision required corporations operating in more than one prefecture to be certified by the national authorities, obliged them to submit financial and organizational documents, and gave officials power to request reports and conduct inquiries under specified conditions. Supporters presented the measure as a modest transparency reform. Opponents, including many religious organizations, feared that officials could use the new powers to interfere with unpopular groups and that government oversight of religion might revive wartime controls. Debate was intense, and Sōka Gakkai, as the political base of an opposition party, was a particular object of partisan suspicion; the politics of the reform were shaped by these rivalries as well as by principle.

A second measure addressed Aum directly. The Act on the Control of Organizations Which Have Committed Acts of Indiscriminate Mass Murder, passed in 1999 and in force from December 27, 1999, allowed the Public Security Examination Commission, on the Public Security Intelligence Agency's request, to place under observation for a defined period an organization whose members had committed indiscriminate mass murder in the course of the organization's activity and which remained dangerous. Observed groups must report membership and property, and authorities may conduct on-site inspections; for more serious situations, the Commission can impose restrictions, including on use of facilities and recruitment. The measure was formulated in general terms but, in practice, applied to Aum and its successors.

Competing principles were visible in the debate. Defenders emphasized the safety of the public, the proportionality of administrative monitoring compared with outright prohibition, and the fact that the statute required a judicial-style process of proof and periodic renewal. Critics, including many lawyers and civil-liberties groups, warned that group-based regulation could chill freedom of association and expression, that "dangerousness" is difficult to define, and that powers directed against one organization could be turned against others. The law did not ban belief, outlaw new religions generally, or make membership in a successor group a crime in itself. Courts upheld its application, and observation measures were extended periodically.

The legal challenge was not only whether a religious body could lose its status. Japanese law distinguishes a religious organization's existence as a community of believers from the public-law privileges and legal personality attached to corporate certification. Authorities could revoke the latter without making belief itself a crime. A further line separated investigation of individual criminal offenses from continuing administrative surveillance of an organization. The Public Security Examination Commission rejected an application for dissolution under the Subversive Activities Prevention Act in January 1997; the later 1999 statute instead authorized specified surveillance measures under defined conditions. These distinctions are fundamental for evaluating whether a security response was narrowly tailored or an unwarranted extension of state power.

Debate also revolved around the limits of hindsight. Once criminal acts were established, the argument for stronger scrutiny of Aum appeared compelling. But laws are not written solely for cases already solved: they apply to future organizations whose conduct and beliefs may be contested. Religious associations feared that administrative oversight could penalize unpopular views, expose donor or member information, or be used by officials whose interpretation of public order was political. Victims and families reasonably demanded protection from organizations capable of repeat violence. A balanced legal history must explain both positions, distinguish reporting obligations from forced dissolution, and assess who authorizes inspections, what evidence is required, and what opportunities exist for judicial review.

Section 98.11

Mass Media, Public Sensationalism, and the Crisis of Religious Credibility

After March 1995, Aum dominated television, newspapers, and weekly magazines for months. Cameras camped outside the Kamikuishiki compound; Asahara's image, teachings, and the group's cartoonish songs and costumes filled variety and "wide show" programs; former members and spokespersons appeared repeatedly. Serious investigative reporting coexisted with entertainment-oriented coverage, and the line between them was often blurred. Heavy coverage sustained public attention and produced real information, but sensational treatment tended to center the organization's strangeness and its leaders, rather than the victims, the institutional failures that had enabled the attacks, or the evidence presented in court.

The Matsumoto case had already demonstrated media hazards, and 1996 added another. In March of that year, Tokyo Broadcasting System (TBS) acknowledged that in October 1989 its staff had shown Aum representatives a video of an unaired interview with Sakamoto Tsutsumi, in which he criticized the organization, shortly before Sakamoto and his family were killed. The disclosure raised grave ethical questions about journalistic independence and protection of sources, and TBS's president resigned. What is verified is the showing of the tape. Whether it influenced Aum's decision or timing to kill the family is a different claim that requires stronger evidence; courts found Aum members responsible for the murders, and the extent of the broadcast's causal role was not established as a matter of record. The episode shows both a real professional failure and the need to separate it from conclusions the evidence does not yet support.

The attacks also affected the standing of religion generally. Surveys in the following years showed declining trust in religious organizations and heightened wariness of new religions, and some groups reported discrimination or difficulty recruiting. Scholars of religion criticized media and public tendencies to generalize from one violent organization to religious minorities, and some religious bodies undertook public-communication efforts. Others, such as established Buddhist and Christian denominations, were drawn into discussions about their own social responsibilities.

For students, the section offers a lesson in source criticism. Investigative reporting aims to establish facts through verification and accountability. Entertainment-oriented coverage seeks audience attention. "Moral panic" describes a pattern in which a group or phenomenon is portrayed as a grave threat to social order out of proportion to evidence. Documented evidence, such as police findings, court records, and survivor testimony, can be used to assess all three. A historian must also consider that media representations are themselves historical evidence of public concerns, even when they are unreliable guides to events.

The TBS affair is especially useful for a history of journalism because it reveals an accountability problem internal to the media, not only a tendency toward sensational language. Reporting about a controversial organization depends on gathering information from people who may attempt to manipulate journalists. Showing a confidential interview to the subject of an investigation jeopardizes that information-gathering relationship and the safety expectations of an interviewee. TBS's 1996 acknowledgment prompted criticism of newsroom management, disclosure, and the responsibilities of broadcasters. The temporal connection between the disclosure and the Sakamoto murders was grave, but historical analysis must keep separate what the station admitted, what investigators established about the murders, and conclusions about causation that cannot be demonstrated solely from their sequence.

Section 98.12

Survivors, Bereaved Families, Psychological Trauma, and Public Memory

For the people affected by the attacks, March 20 did not end. Many suffered lasting physical injuries, including damaged eyesight and chronic fatigue, and others endured nightmares, anxiety, and the condition now diagnosed as post-traumatic stress disorder, a category that was only beginning to be widely recognized in Japan after Kobe and Tokyo. Some struggled to keep jobs, since employers were skeptical of symptoms that did not appear on routine tests, and some felt isolated by the sense that their suffering was a matter of public spectacle. Families faced bereavement, caregiving for severely disabled relatives, and, for some, dispute over how to obtain compensation and recognition. Government compensation under existing worker-accident systems and, later, a dedicated relief law, covered many but not all of those affected, and definitions of "victim" shaped who received assistance.

Subway employees and emergency personnel occupied a distinctive position. Station staff had removed leaking packages and aided passengers without protection, and several suffered severe injuries or died. Police, firefighters, ambulance crews, and hospital staff, who treated patients without knowing the cause, also carried long-term consequences, and their experience led to training and equipment for chemical incidents.

Victims and families became advocates. Takahashi Shizue, whose husband Kazumasa, a deputy station master, died after removing a package at Kodemmachō Station, became one of the most visible, speaking publicly, attending trials, and contributing to victim-support efforts and to public remembrance. Her advocacy, and that of other bereaved relatives and survivors, helped shift attention from the perpetrators to the harmed and informed the later movement for crime-victim rights examined in Section 98.16.

Literary and documentary works preserved these experiences. Murakami Haruki, then living abroad, returned to Japan and conducted interviews with dozens of survivors and relatives, published in Japanese in 1997 as Underground; a later companion volume interviewed Aum members and former members. Murakami explained that he wished to hear the ordinary people who had been reduced to a statistic and to listen to a wide range of voices. The Public Security Intelligence Agency also preserves first-person accounts, which show consequences extending well beyond 1995. Survivor testimony differs from official and courtroom records: it conveys experience, emotion, and the long aftermath, but it reflects memory, selection, and the framing of an interviewer or institution, and it is rarely designed to establish legal facts.

The section's closing problem is one of historical remembrance. Public memory tends to attach to the notorious, and the faces and statements of leaders have been far more widely reproduced than those of the people they harmed. A responsible history must give perpetrators and institutions the attention required to explain what happened without allowing their notoriety to dominate the narrative, and must keep victims' lives and voices central.

The distribution of assistance was itself a historical problem. Physical injuries could be visible and documented immediately, while psychological consequences, lost income, interrupted education, and the burdens on relatives persisted after acute medical treatment. Some survivors found it difficult to demonstrate that a later condition resulted from exposure to the attack; others needed rehabilitation or adaptations to employment. Public compensation systems and charitable support rarely tracked the full range of those circumstances automatically. The voices of survivors thus contributed information unavailable in indictment summaries: how a working day was interrupted, what returning to a train station felt like, whether a family could continue working, and how uncertainty about long-term health shaped ordinary choices.

Division IV — Crime, Juvenile Violence, and Public Security Section 98.13

Organized Crime, the Yakuza, and the Anti-Bōryokudan Law

Organized crime had a long history in Japan, traced in Chapters 50, 85, and 87: gamblers (bakuto) and itinerant vendors (tekiya) of the Tokugawa period, the postwar black markets, and the large federated syndicates that consolidated in the 1960s. The groups commonly called yakuza have been open organizations, with offices, emblems, and publications, a feature unusual among organized-crime groups elsewhere. Police instead use the legal and administrative category bōryokudan ("violent groups"). The terms overlap but are not identical: "yakuza" is a popular and self-descriptive label with cultural connotations, while "bōryokudan" is a police definition of groups whose members habitually engage in violent or illegal acts, and it is the basis for legal designation.

In the late 1980s and 1990s, bōryokudan income came from extortion and protection demands on bars and businesses, loan-sharking, illegal gambling, drug trafficking, and, increasingly, involvement in construction, real estate, and debt collection. The bubble economy created openings; after its collapse, groups became involved in disputes over bad loans and property. Individuals with ties to criminal groups also appeared in financial firms. Police estimates in the early 1990s counted tens of thousands of members and quasi-members, concentrated in a few large syndicates.

Historical Vocabulary
Bōryokudan, juvenile justice, kōban, and moral panic

Bōryokudan is the legal and police category for organized criminal groups, defined by law by the share of members with criminal records and by hierarchical structure. Juvenile justice (shōnen hōsei) refers to the system under the Juvenile Act, in which family courts decide on protective measures for offenders under twenty, with an emphasis on rehabilitation. A kōban is a small neighborhood police box staffed by officers who patrol, give directions, and take reports; it is often cited as a basis of Japan's community-policing reputation. A moral panic is a disproportionate public alarm over a perceived threat to social values, typically amplified by media and political reaction.

These terms are not interchangeable with their English approximations: "mafia," "youth court," "police station," and "crime wave" each imply things the Japanese institutions do not.

The Act on Prevention of Unjust Acts by Organized Crime Group Members, commonly called the Anti-Bōryokudan Law, was enacted in 1991 and took effect in March 1992. It authorized prefectural public-safety commissions to designate groups meeting statutory criteria, and prohibited designated groups' members from specified coercive demands, such as intimidation of businesses for "protection" money, and from certain violent conduct, with orders and penalties for violation. The law also established support for people leaving groups and for citizens harmed. It was a significant departure: it defined organized crime in statute, in contrast to earlier practice of prosecuting individual offenses.

Enforcement had consequences. Police scrutiny, civil suits, and later financial and local-government ordinances excluding groups from business made it harder to operate openly, and membership gradually declined over the following decades. Critics of the approach raised concerns about the breadth of administrative designation and, later, about the social exclusion of former members, while some observers noted that groups adapted by restructuring or shifting into less visible fraud. Students should distinguish these actual practices from stylized portrayals in film, manga, and television, which have long romanticized or caricatured the yakuza and have shaped popular perceptions quite apart from what police and courts documented.

A second distinction is necessary between criminal networks and the administrative categories used to measure them. Police reports commonly distinguish full members from quasi-members or associates, and changing rules for counting these people can affect apparent trends. Publicly displayed offices and emblems made some organizations easy to identify, but not every illegal business used a visible syndicate. Efforts to exclude designated groups from financial contracts or public procurement could reduce their opportunities while also changing how they organized transactions. Consequently a fall in reported membership, while important, cannot by itself prove a corresponding fall in every activity associated with organized crime. A careful study compares police membership data with prosecutions, financial investigations, civil complaints, and evidence about the displacement or transformation of offenses.

Section 98.14

The 1997 Kobe Child Murders, Juvenile Violence, and the Debate over Criminal Responsibility

In 1997, two children in Kobe were murdered in separate incidents, and the offender, arrested in June, was a fourteen-year-old boy. The case, which also included taunting messages sent to the press, produced national shock. Because the offender was a minor, Japanese law restricted publicity: family-court proceedings were closed, and his identity was protected. Some weekly magazines nonetheless published his photograph, provoking debate about whether privacy protections should apply to someone who had committed such a crime. This chapter avoids reproducing details of the offenses and withholds the offender's name, consistent with the privacy principles of juvenile law and the dignity owed to the victims' families.

The case sharpened a long debate about juvenile justice. Under the Juvenile Act as it then stood, cases involving offenders under twenty were referred to family courts, which could order protective measures such as probation or placement in juvenile training schools, with an emphasis on rehabilitation and the individual's circumstances. Criminal prosecution in adult court was possible only for offenders aged sixteen and over who had committed serious offenses. A fourteen-year-old could not be prosecuted and sentenced as an adult, even though the Penal Code already set fourteen as the age of criminal responsibility.

Victims' families and reform advocates argued that the closed system left them uninformed and powerless, and that it failed to hold offenders accountable. Defenders of the existing approach, including family-court judges, lawyers, and child-welfare specialists, argued that rehabilitation was effective, that harsher treatment would not deter, and that the case was exceptional. The debate was framed partly by high-profile incidents and partly by statistics, which did not show a dramatic increase in serious juvenile crime.

The Diet amended the Juvenile Act in 2000, with the revisions taking effect on April 1, 2001. The most discussed change lowered the minimum age at which a case could be referred ("reverse-referred") from family court to prosecutors for adult criminal prosecution from sixteen to fourteen, in cases of serious intentional offenses causing death. Other changes strengthened fact-finding, introduced a role for prosecutors in some family-court proceedings, and gave victims limited opportunities to learn of outcomes and be heard. Importantly, the general age of criminal responsibility had been fourteen all along; the reform changed eligibility for prosecution, not the age at which an act could constitute a crime. Later amendments, in 2007 and afterward, adjusted other provisions.

Section 98.15

The Rise and Fall of Reported Crime: Statistics, Public Perception, and Moral Panic

Police-recorded offenses rose steadily from the late 1990s. The Ministry of Justice reports approximately 2.854 million general Penal Code offenses recorded in 2002, a postwar high, excluding specified traffic-related negligence offenses (principally professional negligence causing death or injury in traffic incidents). The broader total, which includes those offenses, was approximately 3.694 million. Students must understand the difference: the narrower figure is the standard measure of non-traffic crime, while the broader one includes a large category of traffic negligence cases that is unrelated to the question of public fear of crime. Figures also differ from rates (offenses per 100,000 residents), arrest counts, and clearance rates, the share of recorded cases solved.

Theft accounted for the great majority of the recorded total, often around four-fifths in the early 2000s, and included bicycle theft, shoplifting, theft from vehicles, and burglary. The rise also reflected changes in reporting and recording. After a series of cases in 1999–2000 in which police were criticized for failing to act on complaints, notably the murder of a young woman in Okegawa, Saitama, who had reported stalking, and a scandal in Kanagawa Prefecture over police misconduct, the National Police Agency instructed forces to accept and register reports more readily. Stalking and domestic-violence laws were enacted in 2000 and 2001, widening the range of conduct treated as criminal. More recorded offenses did not necessarily mean more offenses, and the clearance rate fell sharply as recorded cases swelled while investigative capacity did not.

Public perceptions followed their own course. Surveys in the 2000s showed that many respondents believed that public safety had deteriorated, and exceptional violent crimes, which were heavily covered, shaped these perceptions. Yet homicide rates, which are among the better-measured indicators, remained low by international standards and were not rising dramatically; the evidence does not support a claim that Japan experienced an unprecedented nationwide epidemic of homicide or serious interpersonal violence. Recorded offenses declined after 2002, year by year, in the following decade.

The numbers permit a more precise exercise than the statement that crime rose. The 2003 White Paper on Crime reported 2,854,061 general Penal Code offenses for 2002, compared with 3,693,928 in the larger category that also counted relevant traffic-negligence cases. In the general category, 476,573 recorded cases were offenses other than larceny, showing the numerical dominance of theft. The same publication reported a clearance rate of 20.8 percent for general Penal Code cases; that ratio reflected the number cleared against those recorded under the applicable annual definitions, not the probability that any particular victim would receive justice. Comparing the series over time therefore requires attention to category changes, repeat reports, reporting incentives, and the lag between an offense and clearance.

The question of why recorded offenses rose should remain open rather than being reduced to an administrative explanation. Changes in public willingness to report, police instructions to accept complaints, economic incentives for some property offenses, and genuine changes in incidents may all have contributed, and their relative importance differed by crime type. The subsequent fall was also uneven: theft was a major driver, while more serious offense categories followed distinct patterns. Police data measure contact with the criminal-justice system as well as offending. Victimization surveys, hospital data, and longitudinal comparisons provide additional perspectives, but each brings its own selection problems. Students should resist both the assumption that a crime increase is entirely real and the opposite assumption that all recorded growth is an illusion.

Section 98.16

Policing, Community Safety, Victims' Rights, and Civil Liberties

Japanese policing combined local presence with central coordination. Prefectural police forces, supervised by the National Police Agency, operated neighborhood police boxes and residential police stations, and officers conducted periodic home visits to gather information on residents. Following the crises of the 1990s, police and local governments encouraged community patrols, neighborhood watch groups, and school-safety volunteers, and many municipalities adopted crime-prevention ordinances. Surveillance cameras proliferated in stations and shopping districts, and police developed forensic and investigative resources. These initiatives responded to public anxiety, but they also raised questions of privacy and of how much responsibility for security should fall on citizens.

Public trust in policing was strained by scandals. The 1999–2000 cases in Kanagawa, Niigata, and Saitama revealed concealed misconduct, mishandled complaints, and institutional defensiveness, and in 2000 the government formed a reform council that recommended changes: expanded public safety commissions, complaint procedures, and greater transparency. Concerns also persisted about discrimination, including allegations that foreign residents and minorities faced heavier scrutiny, and about interrogation practices and the use of extended detention, which critics said produced coerced confessions. The tension between effective investigation and the rights of suspects remained unresolved.

A more lasting change concerned crime victims. For most of the postwar period, criminal procedure centered on the state and the defendant, and victims had little formal role, scant information, and inconsistent support. Victim and bereaved-family groups, many formed in the 1990s, including relatives of the Aum attacks and homicide victims, pressed for change. Laws in 2000 allowed victims to give statements in court and received expanded access to case records, and the Basic Act on Crime Victims, enacted in 2004 and effective in April 2005, committed the state to protect victims' dignity, provide information, support compensation, and ensure participation in the criminal process. A 2007 amendment allowed victims to participate in trials, and the victim-participation system began on December 1, 2008, later followed by lay-judge trials from 2009.

By the late 2000s, public security policy therefore involved not only police but a broader network of victim-assistance centers, counseling, community participation, and legal reform. Its achievements and risks both lay in its breadth: greater support for victims and prevention, coupled with enhanced surveillance and the possibility that punishment would be emphasized over rehabilitation.

Police reform after the late-1990s scandals highlighted the fragility of informal accountability. A locally familiar police box could give residents an approachable institution, but relationships alone did not guarantee fair handling of complaints, especially complaints against officers or involving domestic violence and stalking. Formal procedures for documenting allegations, reviewing misconduct, and communicating outcomes mattered because police discretion was exercised within a hierarchy that had incentives to protect its reputation. Likewise, proposals for cameras and community patrols raised questions about whose behavior was monitored and who had an opportunity to challenge a mistake. Public trust depends not only on crime-prevention results but also on transparent rules that govern how prevention is pursued.

The expanding legal position of victims created further choices for criminal justice. Being informed of proceedings and allowed to express the consequences of an offense can protect dignity, but not every victim wishes to participate publicly or relive a traumatic event. Nor does a victims' role negate the presumption of innocence, access to defense counsel, or the court's duty to evaluate evidence impartially. The 2007 procedural reform, implemented on December 1, 2008, permitted participation in defined categories of trials under court authorization. It should be distinguished from the broader Basic Act on Crime Victims of 2004 and earlier witness-protection measures. This chronology shows reform occurring through multiple institutions and statutes, not through a single legislative breakthrough.

Division V — Social Anxiety, Community Safety, and Historical Memory Section 98.17

School Safety, Public Violence, and the Challenge of Protecting Everyday Spaces

On June 8, 2001, a man entered the elementary school attached to Osaka Kyōiku University in Ikeda, Osaka Prefecture, and attacked pupils and teachers with a kitchen knife. Eight children were killed and fifteen other people, children and teachers, were injured. The attack on a place that families regarded as protected produced profound grief and national attention, and the school's community, including surviving pupils, parents, and staff, bore consequences that continued for years. The offender was convicted and sentenced to death, and was executed in 2004. This section, like the others, prioritizes victims, families, school communities, and policy responses over the offender's identity or motives.

The Ministry of Education and local authorities reviewed school security. Policies that followed included controlled entry, registration of visitors, intercoms and cameras, crisis-management manuals, drills for responding to intruders, and arrangements with local police. Schools also strengthened cooperation with parents and neighborhood volunteers, who patrolled routes to school, and expanded counseling and psychological support for pupils and teachers. The Ikeda school itself was rebuilt, and the date became an annual day of commemoration and a reference point in school-safety education. These measures changed a long-standing norm in which Japanese school grounds had been relatively open to the community, and critics asked whether heightened security would alter the relationship between schools and neighborhoods.

On June 8, 2008, seven years to the day, a man drove a truck into pedestrians in Akihabara, Tokyo's electronics and subculture district, then stabbed passersby, killing seven people and injuring ten. The attack, which occurred on a pedestrian-only street during a weekend, prompted renewed discussion of safety in familiar urban environments, of restrictions on knives, and of the circumstances of casual and irregular employment in which the offender had worked, a point discussed in Chapter 97 and in the following section.

Separate incidents should not be framed as evidence of a single nationwide trend without statistical support. Mass-casualty attacks in schools and public spaces were rare, and they differed in circumstances. Their historical importance lies in the public debates and policy responses that followed them, and in the ways they shaped how adults thought about protecting children and public spaces.

The Ikeda response also illustrates the difficulty of balancing two desirable goods: an accessible school woven into its neighborhood and a school able to limit a stranger's access during an emergency. Physical security measures are visible and straightforward to describe, but they depend on staff training, the clarity of responsibility, and how rapidly adults can contact help. Children and teachers may require psychological support long after a damaged building is repaired or a security system installed. School anniversaries, manuals, and training exercises can institutionalize memory, yet a safety measure designed around an exceptional attack may be less relevant to more common risks facing pupils. Historical assessment should therefore ask what problem each reform addressed, what resources it required, and whether its intended benefit was later evaluated.

Section 98.18

Hikikomori, Bullying, Youth Alienation, and Changing Ideas of Social Responsibility

Concerns about young people formed a persistent strand of public discussion from the 1990s. Ijime (bullying) became a subject of national attention after a series of student suicides in the 1980s and 1990s linked to bullying, which prompted school and ministry responses, and later legislation in 2013 beyond this chapter's period. Futōkō (school refusal) rose in the 1990s, and the number of long-term absentees from school became a regularly published statistic. Hikikomori, a term popularized by psychiatrist Saitō Tamaki's 1998 book, refers to prolonged social withdrawal, often at home for six months or more. Estimates of its prevalence, which vary with definition, have run to hundreds of thousands of people, and many are in their twenties, thirties, and older.

These phenomena must be distinguished from criminal behavior. Public explanations often combined them with crime, particularly after publicized cases in 2000 in which offenders were described as withdrawn or reclusive, but the evidence does not show that social withdrawal or mental-health difficulties predict violent offending. Most people experiencing withdrawal harm no one, and associating them with violence adds stigma to families already distressed. This section therefore treats hikikomori as a social and public-health problem, to be understood through the experiences of those affected and through institutional responses.

Explanations varied. Some attributed withdrawal to family dynamics, particularly parental overprotection or the absence of fathers; some to rigid schools and competitive examinations; some to the labor market that, as Chapter 97 explained, closed off stable entry for many young graduates during the "employment ice age"; and some to individual psychological conditions. Employers, schools, psychiatrists, and the media each contributed to constructing the category, and scholars have noted that the concept both named a real experience and shaped how families and professionals understood it. Technological change, including mobile phones, online communities, and games, also altered how young people communicated, and observers disagreed about whether it deepened isolation or created new forms of connection.

Institutional responses developed slowly. Local health centers, nonprofit support groups, "free schools," and from 2009 national support centers offered counseling and outreach. Many of these organizations were founded by parents and volunteers and, after 1998, some adopted NPO status. The emphasis shifted over time from blaming individuals or families toward recognizing shared social conditions and promoting gradual social participation. The diversity of personal experience, including gender, age, and family circumstances, resists any single account.

The categories used by officials also changed over time. School absence was ordinarily counted through a defined number of missed days and reasons supplied by schools, which could not reveal a student's complete circumstances or guarantee that the same definition was applied consistently. Hikikomori surveys used criteria about withdrawal duration and activities outside the home, but definitions and sampling methods varied. A person who avoided employment because of a chronic health condition, someone excluded by bullying, and someone who chose to spend time at home could have very different needs, even if each was discussed under an umbrella label. Such distinctions make administrative statistics useful for describing the scale of requests for assistance but less reliable as measures of individual motivation.

Media portrayals also helped define what counted as a social problem. A story emphasizing an unusual violent incident could invite the public to associate isolation with danger, while a documentary about a family seeking assistance might expose gaps in social services and employment opportunities. Parents and self-advocacy groups challenged narratives that treated withdrawal as proof of weak character or failed upbringing. The development of alternative learning spaces, counseling, and gradual employment support suggested a different approach: rather than force every person through one school-to-work pathway, institutions could accommodate different rhythms of participation and seek to remove barriers. Historians should evaluate these approaches by what services were available and how people described their experiences, not by a presumed national psychological trait.

Section 98.19

Disaster Preparedness after Kobe: Community Resilience, Institutional Reform, and the 2004 Chūetsu Earthquake

After 1995, Japan changed how it prepared for earthquakes. Building-code revisions and the 1995 retrofit-promotion law encouraged strengthening of older structures, though compliance depended on owners' resources and many old wooden houses remained unreinforced. Emergency communication and information systems were improved, and prefectures and cities established disaster-management offices, stockpiles, and emergency-operations centers. Municipal and neighborhood drills expanded, and school and workplace programs taught evacuation and first aid; the January 17 anniversary and September 1 Disaster Prevention Day became occasions for public education. Volunteer coordination centers and disaster-volunteer registration developed from the Kobe experience, and survivors from Kobe traveled to share their knowledge with other regions. The Hyōgo prefectural memorial and research institutions, which opened in the early 2000s, preserved records and trained disaster workers.

The October 23, 2004 Niigata–Chūetsu earthquake (JMA magnitude 6.8) provided a comparative case. It struck a mountainous, relatively sparsely populated region, and its distinctive damage was caused by landslides, ruptured mountain roads, and isolated hamlets; some villages, notably Yamakoshi, were cut off, and residents were evacuated by helicopter. Aftershocks were prolonged, and the final Niigata Prefecture accounting records 68 deaths, including disaster-related deaths associated with illness, stress, and the hardships of evacuation, including people who spent nights in vehicles. A Shinkansen train derailed during the shaking, though without fatalities. The government and engineering studies compared Chūetsu with Kobe: fewer building collapses, but different challenges in access, temporary housing for dispersed communities, and long-term depopulation in an aging countryside.

Areas of institutional improvement were visible by 2004, including wider arrangements for emergency coordination and volunteer activity; however, differences in deaths and building collapses between Kobe and Chūetsu cannot be assigned primarily to reform without accounting for the disasters' different geography, building stock, exposed populations, hazard characteristics, and statistical definitions. What had not was equally evident: rural aging, isolated settlements, evacuee health, and the problem of ensuring that every household was reached. This section stops short of the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, tsunami, and Fukushima nuclear disaster, which belong to Chapter 99.

The geography of the Chūetsu disaster changes what can be inferred from the comparison. Kobe's greatest losses occurred in dense urban neighborhoods where collapsed structures, fires, and ruptured lifelines interacted. In Niigata, heavy shaking destabilized slopes, severed roads into upland settlements, and complicated the evacuation of residents from dispersed communities. The 2004 event also produced repeated aftershocks and long interruptions to ordinary transport. An official count of 68 deaths includes deaths linked to conditions after the initial shaking, and the distinction between direct and related deaths matters when comparing the two earthquakes. Even apparently simple comparisons of damaged buildings require attention to the affected municipalities, dwelling types, and survey dates.

Institutional learning was real but uneven. Following Kobe, Japan expanded instrumental seismic observation; the Meteorological Agency reports that its network grew substantially after 1995 and that seismic intensity 7 began to be assessed through instrument-based systems rather than only the earlier field-survey practice. Local authorities developed communications, mutual-aid systems, and exercises, yet disaster conditions remained capable of isolating residents and undermining the normal flow of information. The usefulness of an earthquake lesson depends upon whether it is adapted to the next location's population, terrain, and infrastructure. In mountainous Chūetsu, access to isolated hamlets and evacuee health could matter more than solutions aimed at urban fire spread.

Section 98.20

Japan after 1995: Social Trust, Public Safety, and the Historical Meaning of Crisis

This chapter has examined natural disaster, terrorism, organized crime, sensational criminal incidents, and anxieties about youth. They differed in cause, scale, and legal consequence, and treating them as one story of declining public safety would obscure those differences. The earthquake exposed vulnerable housing and strained disaster administration. Aum's violence exposed failures of investigation, oversight, and information sharing. Organized crime was addressed through targeted regulation. Juvenile violence was debated far out of proportion to its statistical trend. Recorded crime rose and fell with policing practice. School attacks shaped security policy. Each belongs to a separate historical account, linked by the ways in which people interpreted them.

Relationships among citizens, institutions, and organizations shifted. Volunteers and nonprofits acquired legal standing; religious bodies faced greater scrutiny and, for some, public suspicion; the police were pressed toward transparency yet were given new tasks in prevention; journalists confronted their own professional failures; and victims became recognized actors, with legal rights and public voices. The tension between security and liberty ran through nearly all of these developments: statutes such as the 1999 Organizations Control Act, surveillance, and juvenile-law reform were responses to real harms, but they also raised questions about proportionality and the rights of religious minorities, suspects, and young offenders.

The conjunction of January and March 1995 retains symbolic force, but chronological proximity does not establish a common cause. The earthquake and the attack shared a moment, and observers connected them rhetorically; they did not share an origin. The chapter's conclusion is that these crises exposed vulnerabilities in institutions and social expectations while stimulating civic participation, accountability, and reform. Japan's reputation for public safety did not disappear. The meaning of safety became more complicated, encompassing the reliability of institutions, the protection of rights, and the ability of communities to respond collectively.

The meaning of institutional accountability also shifted over the period. A late-1990s citizen could demand to know why a prefectural disaster request was delayed, why a religious organization escaped scrutiny, or why police failed to register a complaint. A survivor could seek medical recognition or compensation, while a parent might challenge whether school authorities had a realistic emergency plan. These claims shared no single legal foundation, but each asserted that an institution's responsibility did not end with declaring an event extraordinary. Public records, court cases, journalists' investigations, and nonprofit advocacy became means for contesting official narratives. The expansion of such claims should not be romanticized: access to documents, lawyers, health care, and time was uneven, and public institutions did not always respond promptly.

The longer history of 1995–2010 is thus one of changed relationships as well as changed laws. Government agencies, local residents, victims' groups, religious bodies, and media organizations sometimes cooperated, sometimes competed to define what had happened, and sometimes attempted to assign blame. A legal restriction might make a genuine danger easier to monitor while creating new risks for freedom of association; a school gate might protect pupils while narrowing community access; a criminal statistic might inform policy while distorting public fear when its definition was ignored. The historian's task is to recover those choices, not to describe all reform as progress or all heightened anxiety as irrational. This analytical stance provides a foundation for Chapter 99's examination of 2011 without presuming that lessons drawn from Kobe had already solved Japan's disaster-governance problems.

Historical Debate

Did the Events of 1995 Reveal a Crisis of Institutional Legitimacy or a Crisis of Public Preparedness?

The question: Historians and commentators have disagreed about whether the failures of 1995 showed a deep loss of confidence in the competence and legitimacy of Japan's institutions, or whether they exposed specific, correctable gaps in readiness.

Interpretation A — A crisis of legitimacy: Some argue that official slowness after Kobe, the failure to stop Aum despite earlier warnings, and later police scandals together eroded public trust in the bureaucracy and the police, contributing to demands for transparency and reform and to disenchantment with established authority.
Interpretation B — A crisis of preparedness: Others argue that the events exposed particular shortcomings in planning, communication, and jurisdiction, and that institutions responded with reforms; they point to continuing public reliance on police and government services and to evidence that trust fluctuated by institution rather than collapsing.

Evidence: Both views draw on opinion surveys, legislation, official reviews, media coverage, and the work of scholars of disaster, religion, and policing. Survey wording and timing affect conclusions.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the origins of later reforms and the relationship between citizens and institutions, including the 2011 disaster examined in Chapter 99.

A volunteer: Typically young and from outside the region, volunteers provided labor and supplies and encountered both gratitude and the limits of unorganized help; many later became advocates for formal recognition.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Expectations of Public Safety to Expanded Ideas of Risk and Protection

What Changed

  • Disaster administration, coordination, and public preparedness were reformed.
  • Volunteer and nonprofit organizations gained legal standing and visibility.
  • Religious corporations came under greater oversight, and a targeted law addressed Aum.
  • Victims acquired legal recognition and roles in criminal proceedings.
  • Schools and public spaces adopted security measures and community patrols.

What Remained Continuous

  • Low homicide rates by international comparison and the kōban-based policing system
  • Constitutional guarantees of religious freedom and a pluralistic religious landscape
  • Seismic hazard and the unevenness of housing vulnerability
  • Reliance on neighborhood associations and local volunteers

Who experienced change most directly? Survivors, victims and their families, religious organizations under regulation, and pupils and teachers in secured schools. Who experienced greater continuity? Residents of unreinforced housing, rural elderly communities, and those whose experiences of withdrawal or precarity were less visible to policy. Why the pattern matters: It shows reform as partial and unevenly distributed.

Cause and Effect

Institutional Vulnerability, Emergency Response, Public Debate, and Legal Reform

Long-term causes

Older housing stock and uneven seismic standards; a disaster system designed for localized events; limited oversight of religious corporations; fragmented police jurisdiction; a media market oriented toward sensation; slow recognition of victims' interests.

Short-term causes

The Kobe rupture near a dense corridor; Aum's escalating crimes and failure of early detection; high-profile juvenile and school incidents; scandals over police handling of complaints.

Event or transformation

The crises of 1995–2001 prompted public debate over preparedness, trust, religion, youth, and security, and a sequence of legal and administrative reforms.

Immediate effects

Amendments to disaster law, the NPO Law, revision of the Religious Corporations Act, the 1999 Control Act, Juvenile Act revision, school-security reform, and victims' rights legislation.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The reforms strengthened emergency coordination, civic organization, and victims' standing, but also expanded surveillance and state oversight, raising continuing debates over rights. The developments had different causes and cannot be reduced to one explanation; their effects depended on implementation, not only on legislation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how disasters, terrorist attacks, criminal incidents, and public controversies between roughly 1989 and 2010 challenged assumptions about Japan's safety and institutional reliability, and how citizens, religious communities, governments, and civil-society organizations responded. Its central distinction is among actual threats, perceptions of danger, media representations, governmental responses, and the experiences of victims and survivors.

On January 17, 1995, the Great Hanshin–Awaji Earthquake (JMA magnitude 7.3, approximately Mw 6.9, maximum intensity 7) struck a densely populated corridor, killing 6,434 people by the prefecture's official accounting, including disaster-related deaths. The destruction concentrated in older wooden housing, fires spread through damaged districts, and the Hanshin Expressway, rail lines, port, and utilities failed. Vulnerability was socially patterned: elderly, renter, and lower-income residents bore a disproportionate share of harm.

The government response combined real mobilization with documented limits in communication, information, and coordination, including debate over the timing of Self-Defense Forces dispatch. Neighbors rescued many of the trapped, and volunteers arrived in large numbers, leading to the label "Year of the Volunteer." The 1998 NPO Law gave volunteer organizations a legal framework. Reconstruction raised problems of temporary housing, isolated elderly residents, and unequal recovery, and memorial institutions preserved survivors' accounts.

Aum Shinrikyō arose within a religiously plural postwar society protected by Article 20 of the Constitution. Founded in 1984 by Asahara Shōkō and certified as a religious corporation in 1989, it developed an authoritarian structure, apocalyptic teaching, and a record of violence including the 1989 Sakamoto murders and the 1994 Matsumoto sarin attack. Its crimes cannot be taken to represent Japan's lawful new religions.

The March 20, 1995 Tokyo subway attack, which killed 14 people by the figure now commonly reported and affected more than 6,000, shocked a country accustomed to its reputation for safety. Investigation led to Asahara's arrest in May 1995, extensive prosecutions, a death sentence finalized in 2006, and the dissolution of Aum as a religious corporation. The 1995 amendments to the Religious Corporations Act and the 1999 Organizations Control Act raised hard questions about public safety and religious freedom.

Media coverage mixed investigative reporting with sensationalism, and the 1996 TBS disclosure revealed a grave professional failure while illustrating the importance of separating verified facts from unproven causal claims. Victims and bereaved families, among them Takahashi Shizue, turned survivor testimony into advocacy, and works like Murakami Haruki's Underground recovered ordinary people's experiences. Their efforts contributed to the movement for crime-victim rights, which produced the 2004 Basic Act.

Organized crime was addressed through the 1991 Anti-Bōryokudan Law, while the 1997 Kobe child murders led to the 2000 Juvenile Act amendments, which lowered the age of referral to adult prosecution to fourteen without changing the age of criminal responsibility. Recorded offenses peaked at about 2.854 million in 2002, driven largely by theft and by changes in police practice, and then declined; public fear and recorded crime did not move together, and the evidence does not support the claim of a nationwide epidemic of violence.

High-profile attacks such as Ikeda (2001) and Akihabara (2008) prompted security reforms and public debate, while concerns over bullying, school refusal, and hikikomori reflected social pressures, which must not be conflated with criminality. The 2004 Chūetsu earthquake showed both improved preparedness and the distinct vulnerabilities of an aging mountain region.

The chapter's conclusion is that these crises exposed vulnerabilities in institutions and expectations but also stimulated civic participation, accountability, and reform. They had different causes, and chronological proximity does not establish a common one. Japan's reputation for safety survived, but the meaning of safety became more complex, encompassing institutional reliability, rights, and community capacity.

These developments set the stage for Chapter 99, in which the 2011 earthquake, tsunami, and Fukushima disaster tested the preparedness reforms and the relationship between citizens and the state once more.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a society that took pride in public safety confronted evidence of institutional and social vulnerability. The crises of the 1990s and 2000s did not share a single cause, but together they changed what people expected from their government, their police, their media, their religious communities, and one another.

It also reveals that responses to danger carry costs as well as benefits. Stronger oversight of religion, expanded policing, and juvenile-justice reform addressed real harms and also raised enduring questions about rights. The expansion of civil society and victims' standing, in turn, showed that citizens could reshape institutions from outside them.

The developments examined here shaped later history. The preparedness and volunteer networks built after Kobe, and the frameworks for victim support and public accountability, formed the setting in which Japan faced the disasters of 2011 in Chapter 99. At the same time, the chapter connects regions of the archipelago, from the Hanshin corridor to Niigata, Nagano, and Tokyo, and reminds students that local geography decided what safety required.

Finally, the chapter models a method. Separate the threat from the perception, the event from its media representation, and the law from its implementation, and keep victims and survivors in view. These habits are essential for understanding Chapter 100's debates about identity, memory, and the future.
