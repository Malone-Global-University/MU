Rural Crisis, Financial Instability, and the Power of Big Business

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On March 14, 1927, Finance Minister Kataoka Naoharu told a committee of the House of Representatives that the Tokyo Watanabe Bank had failed. According to standard accounts, the bank had not yet closed its doors. By the next morning, depositors were crowding the counters of Watanabe and other banks, demanding their savings in cash.

The episode is a useful way into this chapter because it shows how fragile confidence could be in a modern credit economy. A bank holds only a fraction of its deposits as ready cash, lending the rest to firms and individuals. It can survive indefinitely as long as depositors do not all ask for their money at once. A sentence spoken in the Diet, however inaccurate or premature, could turn private anxiety into a public stampede. Yet the sentence did not create the weakness it exposed. For years, Japanese banks had carried loans to troubled borrowers that could not be repaid, and the public had only partial information about which institutions were sound. The panic of March 1927 was the moment at which long-hidden problems became visible to ordinary depositors.

This chapter examines how Japan's economy, which had expanded rapidly during the First World War, became vulnerable to repeated financial and agricultural crises between about 1920 and 1933, and how the consequences of those crises were distributed. Its central argument is that the crises emerged from the interaction of structural rural inequality, fragile banking relationships, dependence on international markets, and disputed government policies. Their effects were uneven. Some major financial and industrial groups emerged stronger, while farmers, wage earners, and smaller enterprises suffered prolonged hardship. The chapter treats the question of who bore the costs, and who gained, as inseparable from the question of what caused the crises.

Two events organize the chronology, and students should keep them distinct. The Shōwa Financial Crisis of 1927 was primarily a banking and credit crisis. It grew out of unresolved loans from the postwar downturn of 1920, the difficulty of separating earthquake-related debts from older weak credits, and the vulnerability of banks that had lent heavily to a few borrowers. The Shōwa Depression of 1930–1931 was a broader contraction. It was set in motion by the world depression that began in 1929 and made worse by the Japanese government's decision to return to the gold standard in January 1930 at the prewar exchange rate. These crises had different causes, different victims, and different policy responses, and treating them as a single "Shōwa crisis" obscures why the second hit rural households so much harder than the first.

The people affected did not form a single "Japanese public." Tenant families in the northeast who depended on one rice crop, farm households in Nagano and Gunma who raised silkworms for sale to the United States, women who reeled silk in mills, small manufacturers in Osaka, graduates of Tokyo universities searching for clerical posts, and the managers of Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, and Yasuda experienced the same years in very different ways. The chapter also keeps the empire in view. Rice from Korea and Taiwan affected the prices Japanese farmers received, the Bank of Taiwan stood at the center of the 1927 crisis, Okinawan sugar farmers faced their own collapse, and settlers and Ainu communities in Hokkaido confronted crop failures in a different environmental and legal setting. Korean workers who had migrated to Japanese cities were often among the first to lose their jobs.

The evidence available is rich but must be read with care. It includes Diet proceedings, Bank of Japan and Ministry of Finance statistics, National Archives of Japan documents on the 1927 panic, Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry surveys, cooperative and village records, and the business press, including the weekly Tōyō Keizai Shinpō. It also includes newspaper reports, social surveys, and the writings of economists and officials. Statistics of the period, such as price indexes, farm debt totals, and unemployment counts, were compiled by agencies with their own definitions, and several of the figures in this chapter are approximations drawn from standard scholarly summaries. Students should treat them as indicators of direction and scale rather than as exact measurements.

Chapter 73 depends on earlier chapters and leaves certain subjects to later ones. Chapter 56 explained the origins of the zaibatsu, and this chapter analyzes their interwar position. Chapter 68 covered the Rice Riots of 1918 and Chapter 69 the tenant and labor movements. Chapter 71 narrated the Great Kantō Earthquake, and this chapter asks only how the disaster affected finance. Chapter 74 will treat the Manchurian Incident, military autonomy, and the breakdown of parliamentary restraint in detail. The present chapter describes the economic conditions in which those events occurred, but it does not claim that the depression made them inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the wartime boom, speculation, and the downturn of 1920 left unresolved debts that shaped the banking system throughout the 1920s.
  2. Describe the structure of rural poverty, including tenancy, rents, credit, taxation, and dependence on rice and silk, and distinguish conditions among regions.
  3. Distinguish the Shōwa Financial Crisis of 1927 from the Shōwa Depression of 1930–1931 in terms of causes, mechanisms, and consequences.
  4. Trace the sequence from the March 1927 panic through the Suzuki Shōten and Bank of Taiwan crisis, the Privy Council's rejection of the rescue ordinance, the moratorium, and banking consolidation.
  5. Define liquidity and solvency and apply the distinction to the events of 1927.
  6. Analyze how Japan's dependence on raw silk exports to the United States transmitted the world depression to farm households.
  7. Evaluate competing arguments about the return to gold at the old parity and the policies of Inoue Junnosuke and Takahashi Korekiyo.
  8. Interpret statistics on prices, exports, banks, and rural debt critically, distinguishing nominal revenue from household income.
  9. Assess why the zaibatsu gained relative strength through the crises, and distinguish documented relationships between business and politics from allegations of corruption.
  10. Compare the experiences of tenant farmers, landlords, industrial workers, white-collar employees, small manufacturers, colonial producers, and major financial groups.
  11. Explain how economic grievances contributed to political instability without treating militarism or the Manchurian Incident as inevitable results of the depression.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits, and recognize whose perspectives are missing.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did problems created by the postwar downturn of 1920 remain unresolved for the rest of the decade, and what did that delay cost?
  2. What made a statement in the Diet capable of setting off a banking panic, and what does this suggest about the difference between a trigger and a cause?
  3. Why did the 1927 crisis damage many small banks while strengthening the largest ones?
  4. How did Japan's integration with world markets, especially through raw silk, expose rural households to events in New York?
  5. Was the return to gold in 1930 an avoidable error, a defensible gamble that coincided with bad luck, or something in between?
  6. Why did a record rice harvest in 1930 deepen rather than relieve rural distress?
  7. Why did the same crises produce such different outcomes for banks, large firms, small businesses, and farm households?
  8. How should historians connect economic hardship to political instability without reading the later 1930s backward into the early years of the depression?

Historical Context

By 1920 Japan was an industrial and imperial power, but a lopsided one. The First World War had brought an export boom. Shipbuilding, textiles, and heavy industry expanded, Japan moved from being a net debtor to a net creditor, and fortunes were made by shipowners and trading houses (the "narikin," or nouveaux riches, of the period). Chapters 56 and 63 described the earlier growth of industry and labor. Prices rose steadily and then sharply, reaching the rice price spike of 1918 that produced the riots examined in Chapter 68. Behind the boom lay a structure that had changed less than the headlines suggested. About half of Japan's working population still lived by agriculture and related occupations, most farms were tiny, and a large share of the cultivated land was rented.

The monetary setting also mattered. In September 1917, following the United States, Japan prohibited the export of gold, which in practice suspended the gold standard it had adopted in 1897. For the next twelve years the yen floated against gold-linked currencies. Governments and the Bank of Japan discussed a return to gold throughout the 1920s, and the official view held that the old parity was the proper goal. This meant that the economy's adjustment to the wartime price rise was repeatedly deferred: Japanese prices remained high relative to those of trading partners, imports exceeded exports in most years, and policymakers waited for a moment to "clean up" the economy before restoring gold.

The political setting, covered in Chapters 59, 67, and 69, was one of party cabinets operating within the Meiji constitutional order. The Seiyūkai and the Kenseikai (from 1927 the Rikken Minseitō) alternated in power, the franchise expanded in 1925, and the Privy Council, the House of Peers, and the military retained independent authority. Both major parties had ties to large business, and neither was a farmers' or workers' party. Economic policy was therefore made by finance ministers, Ministry of Finance bureaucrats, and the Bank of Japan, and contested in a Diet where landlords and business interests were heavily represented. The empire was part of the economy: Korea and Taiwan supplied rice and sugar, the Bank of Taiwan and the Bank of Chōsen served as colonial banks of issue, and colonial and Okinawan producers sold into Japanese markets they could not influence.

Finally, the Great Kantō Earthquake of September 1923 (Chapter 71) cast a long financial shadow. The emergency credit measures adopted after the disaster were meant to be temporary, but they became entangled with older bad debts and with political argument, as Section 73.6 explains. Chapter 69 described the movements that tenants, workers, and women formed in the same years. This chapter turns from organization and protest to the economic structures, financial institutions, and policy decisions that determined what those movements were up against.

Section 73.1 · Division I — Postwar Instability and the Foundations of Rural Crisis

The Post–World War I Economy and the Crisis of 1920

The First World War gave Japan an extraordinary opportunity. With European industry diverted to war production, Japanese firms supplied textiles, ships, chemicals, and machinery to Asian and Allied markets that had lost their usual sources. Exports surged, shipping rates soared, and the country accumulated foreign exchange. Entrepreneurs founded new companies, banks expanded their loans, and stock and commodity speculation flourished. Prices rose throughout the war and then faster, as the rice price rise of 1918 revealed. When the war ended, many expected a slump, but a short postwar boom in 1919 carried prices and speculation higher still, as European demand for raw materials and the restocking of depleted inventories fed expectations of continued growth.

The reversal came in the spring of 1920. In March the Tokyo stock market collapsed, and commodity prices, especially for raw silk, cotton yarn, and rice, fell sharply over the following months. Traders and manufacturers who had borrowed heavily to hold inventories or build capacity now found that the value of their holdings had dropped below what they owed. In April, runs on banks broke out in several cities, and the failure of the trading firm Masuda Shōten became a symbol of the collapse. The government and the Bank of Japan responded with emergency lending and attempts to support prices. In the raw silk market, for example, official and semi-official support was organized to prevent a total collapse. These measures calmed the immediate panic, but they did not clear away the underlying losses. The wholesale price index fell significantly within about a year, and some commodities lost a third or more of their peak value.

The important historical point is what happened next. Rather than forcing a wholesale writing-off of bad debts, the authorities and the banks largely rolled them over. Loans to troubled firms were extended, the Bank of Japan's support allowed lenders to avoid recognizing losses, and the borrowers lingered on with a heavy debt burden. Scholars have described the result as a chronic "frozen loan" problem: banks continued to hold credits whose real value was uncertain, and neither depositors nor, in many cases, the supervising authorities knew how large the problem was. This choice had a logic. Immediate liquidation might have caused a deeper panic and many bankruptcies, and officials hoped that growth would eventually restore the borrowers' ability to pay. But it also meant that the crisis of 1920 was not so much resolved as postponed, and the debts it left would resurface in 1927. The 1920s were not a decade of stagnation, since industrial output continued to grow, but they were a decade in which a particular set of financial problems accumulated beneath the surface.

The uneven liquidation of the wartime boom is crucial to the chronology. The boom had rewarded not merely efficient exporters but firms and speculators able to borrow against rapidly rising inventories. When selling prices fell, a merchant might still possess the same goods and factories but have assets worth less than the loans contracted to acquire them. A lender could seize collateral and crystallize a loss, or renew the loan and hope that prices and profits recovered. The second course avoided immediate bankruptcy but tied up money that could no longer readily finance new enterprise. This distinction between a loss already incurred and one formally recorded helps explain why bank balance sheets could appear healthier than the credit relationships beneath them. It also explains why an otherwise growing industrial economy could harbor a particularly fragile banking system. The period's apparent calm depended partly on postponing the recognition of failure.

The decision not to liquidate every troubled borrower also reflected political and social choices. Firm closures could throw workers out of employment, deprive merchants of payments, and endanger neighboring banks holding one another's bills. Officials faced the problem that a generalized rush to settle obligations might destroy businesses that would survive under normal conditions. Emergency finance therefore bought time, a valuable public service, but also reduced the pressure to distinguish viable firms from firms whose debts were permanently impaired. Subsequent failures cannot be attributed simply to negligence: administrators had no painless option. The analytical question is whether relief was paired with supervision and restructuring. As the Bank of Japan's historical research emphasizes, the incomplete treatment of nonperforming loans is the link between the post-1920 contraction and the more public reckoning of 1927.

Section 73.2 · Division I

Industrial Growth, Regional Inequality, and Japan's Uneven Economy

The interwar Japanese economy combined remarkable modern industry with enormous variety in firm size, regional wealth, and working conditions. Large cotton-spinning companies such as Dai Nippon Spinning, Kanebō, and Tōyō Spinning ran mechanized mills employing thousands of young women. Electric power companies expanded rapidly and, in the late 1920s, raised funds by selling bonds in New York and London. Shipbuilding, steel, machinery, chemicals, and electrical equipment grew in a belt of industrial regions: the Keihin area around Tokyo and Yokohama, the Hanshin area around Osaka and Kobe, the Chūkyō region around Nagoya, and northern Kyushu. Chapter 70 describes the urban consumer culture that these regions produced, and Chapter 63 described their labor history.

Yet the modern sector was only part of the picture. Most manufacturing establishments were small workshops and household enterprises making textiles, ceramics, lacquer, food products, and a wide range of consumer goods, often as subcontractors to larger firms or merchants. Wages in small enterprises were lower than in large firms, working conditions were less regulated, and employment was less secure. Historians sometimes describe this contrast as a "dual structure," though the term should be used with caution, since small firms and large firms were closely linked through subcontracting and credit rather than existing in separate worlds. What matters for this chapter is that a downturn in credit or demand reached small enterprises with fewer buffers than the large companies had, and the banks that served them were generally the small and weakly capitalized ones that failed in 1927.

Regional inequality reinforced the pattern. The industrial belts and the major cities offered wage employment, consumer markets, and access to finance. The northeast (Tōhoku), much of Hokkaido, and the more remote parts of the southwest had fewer factories and relied on agriculture, fishing, forestry, and local crafts. The empire added another layer. Korea and Taiwan were developed as suppliers of rice and sugar to the metropole, Korean and Okinawan workers migrated to Osaka and other industrial cities where they often took the lowest-paid jobs, and Manchuria was increasingly treated as an arena for investment. Industrial progress thus coexisted with widespread insecurity. A national average of growth concealed the fact that the benefits of expansion and the costs of downturns fell on different people and places, and the crises described below sharpened the contrast.

The industrial geography was linked by networks rather than divided into a prosperous city and a self-contained poor countryside. Cotton-spinning mills purchased imported raw cotton and employed young women recruited from farming districts; their remittances helped rural families pay taxes or acquire equipment. Iron, machinery, and electric power depended on capital-intensive enterprises whose continued operation required regular borrowing. Small workshops supplied parts, packaging, and finishing services to larger manufacturers, while wholesalers provided advances and purchased their products. When a bank stopped renewing credit, its borrowers could be forced to sell inventories at a discount, interrupt payments to subcontractors, and dismiss workers. The resulting contraction traveled along transactions as well as along geography. A village that seemed far from a stock exchange could still feel a crisis through the loss of a daughter's wages, a failed merchant, or the withdrawal of a local bank's credit.

Regional averages also conceal differences in household strategies. Some rural districts combined farming with weaving, fishing, seasonal construction, or home-based production, providing alternative income when one commodity price declined. Other areas had few such options, particularly where weather and transport limited crops and industrial employment. The central uplands depended heavily on sericulture but could also draw upon established trading routes and labor migration; the northeastern rice districts had a different combination of market exposure and harvest risk. These variations matter when historians compare later maps of hunger, indebtedness, and emigration. They discourage an interpretation in which every rural household was equally poor or every city worker enjoyed prosperity. Economic development created new channels for advancement, yet the available channels depended sharply on property, gender, region, and creditworthiness.

Section 73.3 · Division I

Landlords, Tenant Farmers, and the Structure of Rural Poverty

Chapter 69 introduced tenancy as the setting for the organized disputes of the 1920s. Here the emphasis falls on its economic structure. Land had been private property since the Meiji land-tax reform, and by the interwar years something like 45 to 47 percent of cultivated land was rented, with a higher proportion of rice paddy. Farm households fell into rough categories: owner-cultivators who worked only their own land, part-owners who rented additional plots, and full tenants. By common estimates only about three in ten households were pure owner-cultivators, with perhaps four in ten owning part of what they farmed and the remainder, a quarter or more, renting all of it. The average farm was about one hectare, small by any international standard, and many households farmed far less. Output per hectare was high thanks to intensive labor and rising use of purchased fertilizer, but the units were too small to generate much surplus.

Rent, as Chapter 69 noted, was commonly paid in kind and commonly amounted to about half of the main crop. The rent was fixed as a share of the harvest or a set quantity of rice, and it was due regardless of the market price. The tenant also had to pay for seed, fertilizer, and tools, to meet household needs, and often to repay debts. In good years a family might keep enough rice to feed itself and sell a small surplus. In bad years it borrowed. Landlords were not a uniform group. Large landlords in places such as Niigata and Yamagata held hundreds of hectares and lived on rents, while many small landlords held a few plots, worked part of the land themselves, or combined renting with teaching, village office, or small business. Some resident landlords were deeply embedded in village life and acted as local lenders, patrons, and officials, while absentee landlords who lived in towns dealt with tenants through agents. Landlord power was also political: landlords were prominent in prefectural assemblies, in the Seiyūkai in particular, and among the voters who elected members of the Diet.

Credit was the hinge of rural vulnerability. Farm households borrowed from landlords, local moneylenders and merchants, banks, and increasingly from the industrial cooperatives (sangyō kumiai), which had been created in 1900 and expanded their credit functions in the following decades. They borrowed to buy fertilizer, to pay taxes and school costs, to cover weddings and funerals, and to survive poor harvests. Rural households also paid a rising local tax burden, since schools and local administration were largely financed at the municipal level. The state tried to encourage owner-farming through the Owner-Farmer Establishment program of 1926, which provided low-interest loans for tenants to buy land, but its scale was modest compared to the number of tenant households. Regional differences were large: the commercialized Kinki region and western Honshu had more developed tenant organization and by-employment; the northeast had a higher dependence on a single crop in a harsher climate; Hokkaido had settler farms on newly opened land, often with larger holdings but with severe climatic risks; and Okinawa depended on sugar. The Ainu of Hokkaido, who had received small allotments of farmland under the 1899 Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act, generally held poor land with little capital, and their position in this system was weaker still.

An economic history of tenancy must distinguish rental arrangements from the broader allocation of risk. A rent set as a quantity of harvested rice meant the tenant's physical obligation did not fall when the selling price collapsed; a rent set as a share of output divided crop risk differently. Local custom, bargaining power, irrigation quality, and the size of the holding shaped the terms. Tenants who wished to improve drainage or apply costly fertilizer could be discouraged if they lacked secure possession of the land and feared that a landlord would appropriate part of the gain. At the same time, landlords sometimes advanced seed, helped arrange credit, or financed communal works. These functions help explain the persistence of the system, but should not be confused with equality of bargaining power. Disputes often arose precisely because both parties claimed that older understandings no longer fitted falling prices and mounting obligations.

Household budgets were not simply the proceeds from one harvest. A smallholder might cultivate several separate plots, keep livestock, produce vegetables for use or sale, and send family members into seasonal wage labor. Costs included fertilizer bought from merchants, interest on advances, school expenses, and local taxes whose amounts did not move automatically with the rice price. Households with little land could be net buyers of rice even while farming it; they might temporarily benefit from cheaper food yet lose more through collapsing wages and market sales. The balance therefore depended on what each household sold, what it purchased, and which obligations had fixed nominal values. A claim that falling agricultural prices uniformly impoverished every farmer would miss these distinctions. The more convincing account identifies why heavily indebted tenants and smallholders with narrow income sources were disproportionately exposed.

Section 73.4 · Division I

Rice, Sericulture, and Japan's Dependence on Agricultural Markets

Rice was both the staple food and the principal commercial crop, and its price was therefore a political as well as an economic matter. After the riots of 1918, the government sought to stabilize prices. The Rice Law of 1921 authorized state purchases and sales to smooth price swings. Imports from the colonies increased as the colonial governments promoted higher rice output, notably through Korea's rice production increase program, which began in 1920. Colonial rice kept urban prices low and was welcomed by consumers, but landlords and farmers in Japan complained that it depressed what they received. Rice policy thus set producers against consumers and metropolitan farmers against colonial exporters, and it placed Korean cultivators, many of whom ate millet while exporting rice, in a subordinate economic role within the empire.

Sericulture was the second great pillar of the rural economy and the one most exposed to international markets. At its peak in the late 1920s roughly two million farm households, about two in five, raised silkworms, selling cocoons to filatures that reeled them into raw silk. Japan was the world's leading exporter of raw silk, and raw silk accounted for something like a third of the value of the country's exports in the late 1920s. The overwhelming majority went to the United States, where it was woven into women's stockings and other fabrics. Silk was a cash crop that tied farm households in upland districts of Nagano, Gunma, Yamanashi, Fukushima, and elsewhere to the New York market. It supplemented rice income for many families and was the main source of cash for some. Competition from rayon (artificial silk) was already growing, but in the 1920s raw silk remained a mainstay of foreign exchange earnings, and its earnings paid for imported cotton, iron, and machinery.

Women were essential to both rice and silk. They worked in the fields, but silkworm rearing in particular was a form of labor performed largely by women and children, in rhythms of several weeks of intensive work in spring, summer, and autumn, feeding the worms mulberry leaves around the clock and then selling the cocoons. Young women from farm households also worked in the reeling mills, sending wages home, as Chapter 63 and Chapter 69 described. Household income in sericulture districts therefore depended on three linked flows: cocoon sales, the earnings of daughters in mills, and rice. All three were vulnerable to the same shock from abroad. Because tenancy, debt, and market dependence overlapped in the same households, a fall in cocoon prices could not be absorbed within the household but passed directly into rent arrears, borrowing, and the sale of assets.

The silk economy joined separate forms of labor across the Japanese countryside and the American consumer market. A household grew mulberry leaves and reared the worms, but the cocoons had to be marketed to a reeling establishment, where workers produced a standardized raw silk suitable for export. Dealers and exporters in Yokohama transmitted prices from foreign buyers back through filatures to cocoon suppliers. The timing of contracts mattered: a family might borrow before rearing its worms to purchase materials, while the selling price was determined weeks later. Even a family that produced an excellent crop could receive too little cash to cover its advance. A large share of raw silk shipments went to the United States, so the industry's vulnerability arose not merely from exporting, but from reliance on one principal overseas market and on consumer purchases that could be reduced during hard times.

Agrarian and colonial policy further complicated the market. Rising rice output in Korea and Taiwan was shaped by state investment, irrigation works, marketing institutions, and the demands of metropolitan consumers, not by a freely negotiated relationship among equally powerful producers. Increased shipments could help hold down food costs in Japanese cities while pressuring some farmers' selling prices; Korean cultivators did not necessarily retain the food they grew for shipment. Within Japan, policies aimed at consumer affordability could conflict with policies aimed at protecting rice-growing villages. Students should therefore avoid explaining the rural crisis by crop failures alone. The problem in 1930 was the opposite: an abundant harvest could coincide with poverty when prices collapsed, rents and debts remained due, and other household earnings fell.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Sericulture Household in the Late 1920s

A composite reconstruction of patterns documented in agricultural surveys and later oral histories. It describes no specific family.

In a farming household in the uplands of Nagano, the year was divided between the rice or dry-field cycle and the silkworm seasons. When the silkworms hatched, the main room of the house might be given over to trays of worms, and for several weeks the women and children of the family fed them mulberry leaves day and night, cleaned the trays, and watched for disease, while men carried leaves from the mulberry fields. A household might raise silkworms two or three times a year. When the cocoons were ready, they were sold to a reeling firm or through a cooperative, and the price per unit, set in a market connected to Yokohama and New York, determined much of the household's cash income for that season.

The cash paid rent, bought fertilizer, covered school fees, and repaid debts. A daughter might spend several years in a reeling mill, her earnings or advance wages returned to the household. When cocoon prices fell, the household lost the income at once, and the loan taken for mulberry planting, tools, or fertilizer remained. The experience illustrates why historians treat rural distress in the depression as a problem of linked risks rather than of one failed crop: the farm, the household's women, and the family's debts were bound into a single economy whose price was set abroad.

Section 73.5 · Division II — Banking Weakness and the Shōwa Financial Crisis of 1927

The Banking System and the Accumulation of Bad Loans

Japan's banking system in the 1920s had several tiers. At the top stood the Bank of Japan, founded in 1882, which issued banknotes, held the government's accounts, and acted as the lender of last resort to commercial banks. Alongside it were special banks serving particular purposes, including the Yokohama Specie Bank for foreign exchange, the Industrial Bank of Japan, the Hypothec Bank for agricultural and real-estate loans, and the colonial banks of issue such as the Bank of Taiwan and the Bank of Chōsen. The commercial or "ordinary" banks, which took deposits from the public, ranged from the great city banks affiliated with the zaibatsu (Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, Yasuda) and the Dai-Ichi Bank, to well over a thousand small provincial banks with modest capital. The Banking Ordinance of 1890 set few barriers to entry, and there was no deposit insurance. Savers in a small bank had to rely on the bank's reputation and the local standing of its owners.

The weakness of the system lay less in the number of banks than in the way they lent. Many banks had close relationships with a small number of borrowers, often firms owned by or connected to the bank's own directors. A bank might lend a very large share of its funds to one trading company or manufacturer, rolling over the loans as the borrower's condition deteriorated, and the borrower in turn used the credit to expand. When the postwar downturn of 1920 destroyed the value of many such firms' assets, the banks did not write the losses off. As Section 73.1 explained, the debts were extended, and the Bank of Japan's support helped lenders keep the problem quiet. Supervision by the Ministry of Finance was limited, and balance sheets were not transparent. Depositors, and sometimes bank managers themselves, did not know how much of a bank's portfolio was sound.

The consequences were gradual. Over the 1920s a series of local bank failures occurred, usually without a general panic, and larger institutions absorbed some of the weak ones. Meanwhile the stronger banks gained deposits, as savers moved their money toward institutions with greater reputations. The system therefore divided in a way that would deepen in 1927: a small group of large banks with strong ties to major industrial groups and the Bank of Japan, and a large population of weaker banks whose survival depended on the confidence of their local depositors and on the assumption that the authorities would not allow a general collapse.

A bank's legal category did not by itself indicate its security. The four great zaibatsu-related banks could draw upon diversified customers, professional managers, and institutional standing; a small local bank might combine a genuine public service with dangerous dependence on a single influential family or enterprise. Deposits were payable at relatively short notice, while loans to factories, property owners, and trading firms could not be recovered immediately without causing losses. If many depositors asked for currency at once, even a solvent lender required liquid reserves or help from another institution. An insolvent institution, by contrast, had assets worth less than its liabilities and could not be restored by temporary cash alone. The difference is central to interpreting emergency aid: a lender-of-last-resort loan may stop a needless run, but repeated loans to borrowers that cannot repay can transfer eventual losses to others.

Bank supervision was increasingly recognized as a public problem. The postwar crisis made clear that directors with commercial interests could use depositors' money to support affiliated companies and conceal deteriorating asset quality. When the Ministry of Finance and the central bank debated reform, they confronted the tension between protecting independent local lenders and reducing the instability caused by fragmented credit institutions. Regulations on capital and inspections were responses to the underlying governance of banking, not merely to frightened queues outside bank branches. The eventual consolidation program made the larger banks stronger in aggregate, but it did not automatically preserve the supply of small-business and farm credit once provided by local institutions. Understanding the different interests of depositors, borrowers, shareholders, and officials prevents the banking narrative from becoming a simple story of rescue followed by prosperity.

Historical Vocabulary
Liquidity and Solvency

Liquidity is the ability to meet demands for payment promptly, in cash. Solvency is the condition in which total assets are worth more than total liabilities. A bank can be solvent but illiquid: if its loans are sound but long-term, it may not be able to pay all depositors at once. It can also be insolvent but liquid: if its loans have lost value but depositors do not yet know it, it may keep paying for years. A central bank that acts as lender of last resort can lend cash to an illiquid but solvent bank to carry it through a run. The difficulty arises when authorities cannot tell which condition applies, and in Japan in the 1920s it was often impossible to tell.

The distinction matters for this chapter because the panic of March 1927 mixed both problems. Some banks that came under pressure were fundamentally sound, and runs threatened them only because depositors lost confidence. Others, such as Watanabe, had been insolvent in substance for years. A bank run does not discriminate between the two, which is why the same panic could ruin some institutions and strengthen others.

Section 73.6 · Division II

Earthquake Bills and the Financial Legacy of 1923

The Great Kantō Earthquake of September 1, 1923, destroyed much of Tokyo and Yokohama and with them the records, buildings, and cash flows of countless businesses (see Chapter 71 for the disaster itself). The government imposed a payment moratorium on September 7 to prevent immediate collapse. To keep credit flowing, it then authorized the Bank of Japan to rediscount "earthquake bills" (shinsai tegata): commercial bills issued before the disaster whose payment was affected by it, because the issuer or the place of payment lay in the stricken area. Banks that held such bills could obtain cash from the Bank of Japan, and the government undertook to compensate the central bank for losses up to a set limit, initially 100 million yen. The policy was associated with Inoue Junnosuke, then governor of the Bank of Japan, and it succeeded in preventing a financial collapse in the weeks after the disaster.

The difficulty was that the category was too elastic. A bill could be described as affected by the earthquake whether or not the underlying debtor had been ruined by the disaster or had already been in difficulty before it. Banks had incentives to present troubled credits as disaster-related, since the Bank of Japan would take them. By the mid-1920s a large amount, on the order of two hundred million yen according to commonly cited figures, remained outstanding and unlikely to be collected in full. The bills had thus become a repository for debts from the 1920 downturn as well as from the earthquake, and the effort to settle them required the government to ask the Diet to extend the guarantee and decide who would bear the loss.

The Diet debate over this legislation, held in the winter of 1926–1927, made the problem public. Opposition parties, including the Seiyūkai, attacked the Kenseikai cabinet for using public money to rescue private firms and banks, and discussion of which institutions and companies held unrecoverable bills circulated widely. Politically, the debate was about whether public guarantees should protect the banking system. Financially, it had a different effect: it told depositors that major institutions were in difficulty and that the authorities had not yet settled how losses would be allocated. This is the crucial link between 1923 and 1927. The earthquake did not cause the crisis, but the earthquake bills stood at the center of a controversy that turned latent weakness into public suspicion.

The rediscounting mechanism requires particular care because the name 'earthquake bills' is easily misleading. A commercial bill represented a promise that payment would be made at a future date; a bank holding it expected to receive funds when the obligation matured. Damage to the issuing firm, its records, or the transport and payment networks could prevent punctual settlement even when the underlying enterprise remained viable. By discounting such paper, the Bank of Japan gave the holding bank cash before maturity. The government's indemnity against central-bank losses enabled officials to accept more questionable paper than ordinary rules permitted. In a genuine emergency that extension of credit supported payments, employment, and commerce. Yet the very flexibility that made the facility useful also opened opportunities to classify older loans as disaster-linked and to postpone an honest appraisal of their value.

The numbers illustrate this unresolved problem but must not be treated as interchangeable. The original indemnity ceiling of 100 million yen was a limit on possible government compensation, not the total face value of paper presented. The value of bills discounted was larger, and an important stock remained unpaid years later. The question facing legislators in 1927 was thus how to settle obligations under a guarantee initially justified as a temporary disaster response. The controversy crossed ordinary party lines because it raised competing principles: who deserved assistance after a catastrophe, whether private management should bear losses, and whether abrupt withdrawal of support would impose still larger costs on the public. The Bank of Japan's retrospective analysis connects these competing objectives directly to the weaknesses exposed by the financial panic.

Section 73.7 · Division II

The March 1927 Panic: Kataoka Naoharu, Watanabe Bank, and Depositor Confidence

On March 14, 1927, in a session of the House of Representatives Budget Committee discussing the earthquake bills legislation, Finance Minister Kataoka Naoharu reported that the Tokyo Watanabe Bank had failed. Watanabe was a Tokyo bank connected with the Watanabe family's business interests, and it had long been in difficulty, with heavy exposure to loans to related firms and to earthquake bills. According to standard accounts, however, the bank had not actually suspended payments at the time of Kataoka's statement, and the minister appears to have spoken on the basis of incomplete information about a situation still developing. By the next day, the bank did close, and in the following days depositors besieged branches of Watanabe and of other banks in Tokyo, Yokohama, and elsewhere, withdrawing cash.

Historians have been careful to treat this as a trigger and not as the cause. The conditions for the panic had been present for years: frozen loans, doubtful earthquake bills, a long list of weak institutions, and a public debate that had made the weakness visible. What Kataoka's statement did was to convert a general anxiety into the specific knowledge that a named bank had failed, in the authoritative voice of the finance minister. Depositors who did not know whether their own bank was sound acted on the safest assumption, which was to withdraw. The runs spread to banks that were not in trouble, because depositors had no reliable means of distinguishing them. This is the dynamic that the distinction between liquidity and solvency describes. A sound bank facing a run is illiquid, not insolvent, but it can be destroyed all the same if no one provides cash.

The government and the Bank of Japan responded in the following days. The Diet passed the amended earthquake bills legislation, and, in the same month, enacted a new Banking Law that raised minimum capital requirements and gave the Ministry of Finance greater powers over bank mergers and supervision (it took effect in January 1928). The Bank of Japan lent to banks under pressure, and the larger banks, which were receiving inflows of deposits from those fleeing the weaker ones, paid out cash without difficulty. The panic subsided by the end of March, but a number of institutions had failed or suspended business, and the strain on the financial system had not been resolved. A second and more dangerous shock followed in April, when the failure of a major trading company brought down a bank that had a colonial charter.

The March events unfolded in stages rather than as one uninterrupted nationwide collapse. The finance minister's statement on March 14 reached a public already following the earthquake-bill debate. Tokyo Watanabe Bank and the associated Akaji Savings Bank suspended business the following day, and several other institutions faced withdrawals or stopped payments during the next week. On March 23 the Diet approved legislation dealing with earthquake bills, contributing to temporary calm. These dates help separate the minister's announcement from the institutional failures that preceded it and the remedial action that followed it. They also show why the press mattered: depositors learned of one bank's condition through reports that made them question their own bank's capacity to return savings. Panic was not a mysterious collective emotion. Under unequal access to information and the absence of deposit insurance, withdrawing early could appear individually prudent even when it was socially destructive.

The new Banking Law was intended to address that imbalance over a longer horizon. Minimum capital requirements were set at different levels depending on location, and smaller institutions faced pressure to merge rather than merely increase nominal capital on paper. Tighter inspection and a preference for better-capitalized lenders were supposed to reduce the exposure of depositors to locally connected borrowers. The reform did not immediately cure the panic; it would take effect in 1928 and shape years of consolidation. Conversely, the March law's existence should not make the ensuing April crisis appear inexplicable. A major colonial bank and a trading group had accumulated obligations on a scale that could not be settled simply by a general rule for small commercial banks. The second stage therefore demanded emergency government action, not only regulatory modernization.

Section 73.8 · Division II

The Bank of Taiwan, Suzuki Shōten, and the April Crisis

Suzuki Shōten was a Kobe trading firm that had grown during the First World War into one of Japan's largest and most diversified business groups. Under the direction of its manager Kaneko Naokichi, it expanded into sugar, camphor, flour, steel, shipbuilding, and numerous other fields, with dozens of affiliated companies. Its expansion was financed to an extraordinary degree by the Bank of Taiwan, the colonial bank of issue, which had branches in Japan, China, and Southeast Asia and which had lent to Suzuki since the 1910s. By 1927 the Bank of Taiwan's exposure to Suzuki was enormous, and the group's own earnings could not support the debt. In March and April, as the panic spread and the Bank of Taiwan's ability to keep funding the firm came into question, the bank halted new lending. Suzuki Shōten collapsed in early April.

The failure threatened the Bank of Taiwan itself, which now held a mass of loans to a bankrupt borrower and faced withdrawals as news spread. A failure of the colonial bank of issue would have had consequences beyond Japan: it would have hit the Taiwan economy and the Japanese banking system. The Wakatsuki cabinet therefore prepared an emergency imperial ordinance under which the Bank of Japan would be authorized to make special loans to the Bank of Taiwan, with the government guaranteeing the Bank of Japan against losses. Under the Meiji Constitution an emergency ordinance issued when the Diet was not in session required the consultation of the Privy Council. The Privy Council was an unelected body of senior statesmen, and its members included people hostile to the cabinet. On April 17, 1927, the Council rejected the ordinance. The Wakatsuki cabinet resigned the same day.

Historians have discussed the Privy Council's motives at length. Some members objected on constitutional and fiscal grounds, arguing that a guarantee of unlimited potential losses to rescue a private firm's creditor was improper. Others were influenced by political hostility to the cabinet, including dissatisfaction with Foreign Minister Shidehara Kijūrō's China policy. The precise weighting remains a subject of discussion. The result was that an institution outside the elected government could block a rescue the cabinet considered essential and force a change of government in the midst of a financial emergency. It also showed the vulnerability of party cabinets to non-elected bodies, a theme taken up in Chapter 74. The financial dimension of the episode also deserves attention: the crisis of the Bank of Taiwan was not only a Japanese banking story but a colonial one, since a bank created to manage the finances of a colony had become the main creditor of a metropolitan conglomerate.

Suzuki's dependence on one bank was not simply an accidental failure of diversification. During the war, access to cheap and abundant credit had allowed the trading concern to extend operations into manufacturing and overseas distribution faster than its own capital and earnings could support. The Bank of Taiwan, although chartered for a colonial financial purpose, developed extensive metropolitan and international business and became tied to Suzuki's fortunes. When business contracted and confidence faded, the distinction between the trading company's failure and the bank's own financial weakness blurred. The government could not treat the issue as the liquidation of an ordinary merchant: the bank issued notes in Taiwan and occupied a place in the empire's system of trade and payments. A collapse threatened other depositors and counterparties who had never dealt directly with Suzuki. This is the clearest interwar illustration of how concentrated lending made financial risk systemic.

The emergency ordinance also exposed the political architecture of the Meiji constitutional system. The Wakatsuki cabinet and its financial advisers believed that central-bank lending supported by a public loss guarantee could prevent further panic; opponents in the Privy Council insisted that the cabinet's proposal lacked an adequate fiscal and constitutional foundation or that its approval would reward reckless creditors. The proposed indemnity ceiling, widely recorded as 200 million yen, was a maximum loss guarantee rather than a transfer automatically made to Suzuki's shareholders. The Privy Council's rejection on April 17 therefore blocked a specific financial stabilization plan while making clear the vulnerability of a party cabinet to an unelected institution. The consequences were immediate: the cabinet resigned and confidence deteriorated further. The underlying debate—how much public risk should be accepted to avoid private banking collapse—outlasted the constitutional confrontation.

Section 73.9 · Division II

Emergency Intervention, the Bank Moratorium, and Financial Consolidation

On April 20 Tanaka Giichi formed a Seiyūkai cabinet, and Takahashi Korekiyo, the veteran financier who had been finance minister under earlier cabinets, returned to the post. Within two days the government acted. On April 22, an emergency imperial ordinance, this time approved by the Privy Council, imposed a payment moratorium of three weeks, running until May 12. Banks in Tokyo and elsewhere closed for two days and then reopened under the moratorium, which limited the amounts depositors could withdraw and allowed banks to defer payments. The Bank of Japan simultaneously supplied cash on a large scale. According to widely repeated accounts, it hastily printed large-denomination notes, including 200-yen notes printed on only one side, to meet the demand for currency. The moratorium bought time for the authorities to arrange the longer-term measures.

Those measures came in a special session of the Diet in early May. Legislation authorized the Bank of Japan to make special loans to banks under pressure, with the government compensating it for losses up to a ceiling of 500 million yen, and a separate arrangement provided for the relief and restructuring of the Bank of Taiwan. When the moratorium expired, the banks reopened and the panic did not resume. Takahashi's emergency work was acknowledged as effective, though it was a rescue of institutions rather than of borrowers. Suzuki Shōten was liquidated, its debts a burden on the Bank of Taiwan for years. Inoue Junnosuke, who had managed the 1923 emergency, became governor of the Bank of Japan again in mid-1927.

The longer-term consequence was consolidation. The Banking Law, which took effect in January 1928, required ordinary banks to meet minimum capital standards and encouraged mergers. The Ministry of Finance ordinary-bank series reports 1,420 institutions at the end of 1926, 782 at the end of 1930, and 538 at the end of 1932; this decline includes mergers and closures, not only failures. The largest banks (Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, Yasuda, and Dai-Ichi) increased their share of deposits, as savers moved their money toward institutions that had visibly weathered the panic. For the localities that lost their banks, the cost was real: deposits were frozen or lost, local credit to small firms and farmers dried up, and the loss of a locally owned bank removed a source of finance that the larger banks did not necessarily replace. The 1927 crisis thus established a pattern that would recur in the depression: stabilization came through support for the central institutions, while the burden fell on smaller and local participants.

The emergency measures must be read as a sequence. The Tanaka cabinet took office on April 20; the moratorium ordinance was promulgated on April 22; banks reopened after a short nationwide closure and resumed business under temporary payment restrictions. The moratorium ran for twenty-one days and was lifted in May. Its role was to interrupt a self-reinforcing cycle in which withdrawals forced banks to demand repayment from borrowers or to dispose of assets at distressed prices. Temporary limits on withdrawals relieved pressure at precisely the moment the Bank of Japan and the Ministry of Finance were arranging credit facilities. The blank-backed 200-yen notes issued during the emergency were a visible sign of the logistical urgency: the National Printing Bureau records their production and use, while the Bank of Japan's Currency Museum explains the wider rush for banknotes. They are useful historical evidence that financial panics concern currency availability as well as corporate balance sheets.

The consolidation that followed can be described more precisely than a rounded national estimate. A Ministry of Finance ordinary-bank series reproduced from the Bank of Japan's centennial history reports 1,420 institutions at the end of 1926, 782 at the end of 1930, and 538 at the end of 1932. These are counts of ordinary banks, not figures including every savings or specialized bank; the decline reflects mergers and closures as well as failures. Large city banks could gain deposits and customers, but some provincial regions saw local lenders disappear or become branches whose lending decisions were made elsewhere. Consolidation could also improve diversification and oversight, so the consequences should not be presented as wholly negative. The social history of the crisis lies in this tension: confidence was restored by building larger, more regulated institutions, yet the route to stability could reduce locally controlled credit and concentrate economic decision-making.

Section 73.10 · Division III — The Great Depression and the Crisis of Rural Society

The World Depression and Japan's Exposure to Foreign Markets

The Shōwa Depression had a different origin from the crisis of 1927. The earlier episode grew out of the domestic banking system. The later one began abroad. The American stock market crash of October 1929 was followed by a prolonged contraction in the United States, and the collapse of income and employment there reduced demand for imported goods. Other countries followed: world trade shrank sharply, commodity prices fell, and the international financial system came under strain as banks failed and governments struggled to keep their currencies convertible. Japan was not the center of this storm, but its economy was tightly linked to the markets that were hit hardest.

The most important link was raw silk. The United States took the overwhelming majority of Japanese raw silk exports, and American demand was concentrated in a luxury consumer product, women's silk stockings, whose purchase could be postponed in hard times. When American incomes fell, silk consumption dropped, and the price of raw silk in New York and Yokohama fell steeply, roughly by half between 1929 and 1931 according to commonly cited figures. Japanese exporters and filatures could not simply find other buyers, since there was no comparable market for such quantities. Rayon, a cheaper substitute, further weakened silk's position. Because the export price fell, so did the price the filatures could pay for cocoons, and thus what farm households received.

Other exports were also hit, and the overall value of Japan's exports fell dramatically, nearly halving in yen terms between 1929 and 1931. Part of this decline reflected falling prices, not only falling quantities, a point of importance for interpreting the statistics. Colonial producers were affected too: rice and sugar prices in Korea and Taiwan fell, and colonial farmers faced the same squeeze between fixed obligations and collapsing revenues, often without the buffers available to Japanese households. The mechanism that carried the world depression into Japan was therefore not a general "contagion" but a specific chain: American consumers, silk prices, cocoon prices, farm household income, rural debt. The next section explains how the Japanese government's own policy intensified this chain.

Silk exports show how a commodity economy transmits a fall in demand across many stages of production. American consumers and manufacturers reduced their purchases; importers in the United States pressed for lower prices; exporters in Yokohama received less for a unit of silk; reeling mills adjusted their purchases of cocoons; and rural households were paid less for weeks of work. Foreign demand and domestic household income were not connected instantaneously or at one uniform price. Contract dates, inventories, quality grades, exchange-rate movements, and the bargaining positions of merchants could alter the timing. Yet the overall direction was unmistakable. A family could not readily offset the collapse by producing twice as many cocoons, because mulberry land, labor time, weather, and market demand placed limits on production. This is why a depression beginning in the United States could become a household debt crisis in upland Japan.

The trade statistics also demand attention to units. A fall in export value expressed in yen can result from lower quantities shipped, lower foreign-currency selling prices, or movements in the yen exchange rate. The distinction became especially important after Japan abandoned gold at the end of 1931. A weaker yen could raise the yen value of sales or make Japanese goods cheaper to foreign customers without restoring the price received by a cocoon farmer to its old level. Raw silk, cotton textiles, machinery, and other exports followed different paths; raw silk faced both the depression and competition from artificial fibers. Japan's position in world trade was therefore uneven rather than uniformly declining or recovering. Students should use the chapter's export table as an account of nominal values, and should avoid using it alone to infer employment, physical output, or rural purchasing power.

Section 73.11 · Division III

Inoue Junnosuke, the Gold Standard, and Deflationary Policy

The Rikken Minseitō cabinet of Hamaguchi Osachi took office in July 1929 with a program of fiscal retrenchment, the reduction of government expenditure, and the restoration of the gold standard. Inoue Junnosuke, a former Bank of Japan governor, became finance minister. The government announced in November 1929 that gold exports would again be permitted, and the embargo was lifted on January 11, 1930, with the yen restored to its pre-1917 parity of roughly 49.85 American cents. This rate was higher than the yen's market value, meaning that the return required prices and costs in Japan to fall to compensate. Britain had returned to gold in 1925 at its old parity, and by 1930 the gold standard was regarded across much of the world as the foundation of a sound international financial order.

Inoue's reasoning combined several arguments. Stable exchange rates, he held, would restore confidence, encourage foreign lending, and promote trade. A return at the old parity, rather than a devalued rate, would honor the commitments of the state and keep faith with creditors. And the deflation required to restore the old rate would have a salutary effect on the economy by forcing out inefficient firms and banks, compelling a "rationalization" of industry and ending the postwar habit of propping up weak enterprises. This last argument had an echo of the choice made in 1920: where the authorities then had avoided a reckoning, Inoue intended to impose one. The government also launched a campaign urging households to economize, tightened the budget, and encouraged cartels and rationalization in industry.

The timing proved disastrous. Japan returned to gold just as the world depression was deepening, so deflationary policy was layered on top of a collapse in export demand. Domestic prices fell, as intended, but so did production, profits, and wages. Gold flowed out of the country, which required the Bank of Japan to restrict credit further, and the outflow accelerated after Britain left the gold standard in September 1931, when speculators began to wager that Japan would follow. Contemporary critics argued that the policy was making the depression worse. Supporters countered that the underlying causes were international and that a return to gold had been necessary. The debate has continued among historians.

The exchange-rate choice can be clarified through a simplified balance sheet. A firm exporting silk received foreign currency but paid many of its wages, loans, and taxes in yen. If the yen were restored at a high official value, each dollar of sales would be worth fewer yen than under a depreciated exchange rate; restoring profitability would require lower domestic costs, higher productivity, or both. Imported goods and foreign debts might become cheaper in yen, benefits that helped explain some supporters' preference for the old parity. The burdens and benefits were not equally distributed. Debtors with fixed yen obligations could suffer when wages and prices fell, while creditors benefiting from increased real purchasing power might favor monetary stability. The gold standard was consequently not a neutral technical rule. Its restoration was a choice about whose adjustment costs would be tolerated to recover international monetary credibility.

Inoue's policy should nevertheless be interpreted in the context of contemporary assumptions. Senior officials feared that a devalued return would signal financial weakness and jeopardize access to foreign capital; the prestige of stable money also reflected lessons that policymakers drew from earlier inflation. Critics such as Ishibashi Tanzan disputed whether Japan could bear the domestic deflation required by the old parity. These positions cannot be judged only with knowledge of the eventual outcome. The world economic collapse worsened unexpectedly, while the administration had already committed itself to discipline and rationalization. Once Britain left gold in September 1931, expectations of a Japanese exit increased, gold outflows continued, and defending the peg became more expensive. Contemporary arguments therefore concerned not just whether monetary rules were desirable but when to change course as international conditions shifted.

Historical Vocabulary
Gold Standard and Parity (kinhon'i, heika)

Under a gold standard, a country's currency is defined as a fixed weight of gold and can be exchanged for it, and gold can be freely imported and exported. This fixes exchange rates among countries on the standard. The parity is the official rate at which the currency exchanges for gold or other gold-standard currencies. Japan's prewar parity was set in 1897, and when the gold embargo of 1917 ended, the question was whether to restore that parity or to adopt a new, lower one reflecting the yen's reduced value. Restoring the old parity meant that the yen was worth more in foreign exchange than the market had been valuing it, so domestic prices had to fall.

Students should avoid assuming that the gold standard was simply a technical device. It was also a political and moral commitment, associated with financial orthodoxy, fiscal discipline, and international standing. Its defenders spoke of confidence and honor, and its critics of the costs imposed on those who were unable to adjust. A return to gold was thus a choice about who would bear the burden of adjustment.

Historical Debate

Was the Return to Gold at the Old Parity a Mistake?

The question: Did the decision to return to gold in January 1930 at the prewar rate cause, or significantly worsen, the Shōwa Depression, or was it a defensible policy overwhelmed by events outside Japan's control?

Interpretation A — A costly error: Contemporary critics such as the economic journalist Ishibashi Tanzan argued that the return should have been made at a lower rate, or postponed. Later scholars have emphasized that the policy imposed deflation at the worst possible moment, intensified the fall in farm prices, drained gold, and delayed recovery until the policy was abandoned in December 1931. They note that countries that left gold early recovered earlier.
Interpretation B — A defensible policy overtaken by the world depression: Other scholars stress that the decision was made in 1929 under assumptions common across the world, that the opportunity to return had been awaited for years, and that the sharpest declines originated in the collapse of American demand rather than in Japanese policy. They also point to Inoue's argument that the 1920s' avoidance of adjustment had left an economy needing reform, and they caution against judging the decision with hindsight about the severity of the depression.

Evidence: Both sides use the timing of price declines, gold outflows, export figures, comparisons with other countries' experience, contemporary economic journalism, and the records of the Ministry of Finance and the Bank of Japan. Work by scholars such as Nakamura Takafusa and Mark Metzler, among others, has examined how monetary policy interacted with the empire and the world economy. Most recent treatments see the two interpretations as overlapping: the world depression was the main shock, while the gold policy turned a severe recession into a more prolonged and socially damaging one.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians assign responsibility for the suffering of the early 1930s, and how they judge the credibility of party governments and their financial leaders.

Section 73.12 · Division III

The Collapse of Rice and Silk Prices

The combination of the world depression and deflationary policy produced a collapse in agricultural prices that was more severe than the decline in prices of manufactured goods. Between 1929 and 1931, rice prices fell by roughly 40 to 50 percent in nominal terms, according to commonly cited estimates, and the price of cocoons fell by a comparable or larger proportion. These figures are approximate, and they vary by region, grade, and season, but the direction and scale are not in doubt. Farm prices fell further than the prices of the goods farmers had to buy, so the terms of trade turned against the countryside. Economists sometimes call this a "scissors" effect: the price of what farmers sold dropped faster than the price of what they bought.

The year 1930 added a paradox. The rice harvest was a record, and the abundance pushed prices down further. Contemporaries described the situation with a phrase, hōsaku binbō, "poverty amid abundance." For a household that sold a share of its crop, a bigger harvest sold at a much lower price could yield less cash than a smaller harvest sold at a higher one. For a tenant, who owed a fixed rent in kind, a bigger harvest meant the same rent, but a lower price for the remainder. The landlord who received rent in kind and sold it in the market also lost revenue, so the fall in prices did not simply transfer money from landlords to tenants. Small landlords with debts of their own were hurt, and the large landlords' incomes fell as well, though they had greater reserves.

Students should distinguish carefully among three quantities. Nominal revenue is the quantity sold multiplied by its price in yen. Real income is what a household's revenue can buy. And net household income is what remains after costs, rent, taxes, and debt payments. Many obligations were fixed in yen, including taxes, loan repayments, and installments, so as prices fell, the real burden of those obligations rose. Farm households had also relied on wage income from daughters in mills and from sons in seasonal or urban work, and this income fell as factories laid off workers. The decline in actual household income therefore exceeded the decline in crop prices alone. This is why historians emphasize the interaction of falling prices, fixed obligations, and the loss of side income.

The phrase 'poverty amid abundance' becomes clearer when prices and quantities are analyzed together. Suppose that a farm harvested more rice but the market price of each unit fell by nearly half: the larger physical crop need not compensate for the collapse in selling price. Moreover, not every household sold the same proportion of its harvest, and a landlord receiving rice rent had different exposure from a tenant paying it. A family could be simultaneously a producer of rice, a purchaser of consumer goods, a wage earner, and a borrower. Fertilizer prices and interest charges might not fall at the same pace as rice and cocoons, so the amount left after essential payments could contract much more sharply than gross receipts. The 'scissors' effect was a historical relationship among differently moving prices, not proof that every item bought by farmers became more expensive in yen.

Silk added a distinct external shock to this domestic pricing problem. Once American demand for silk products fell, a cocoon-producing household had little control over the price offered by the buyer. Reeling establishments, facing reduced export receipts, pressed costs down and sometimes cut operations or employment. Families that formerly combined cocoon proceeds with a daughter's wages from a silk mill could lose both sources in the same season. The decline was especially severe where households had borrowed to expand mulberry cultivation or invested heavily in purchased inputs. These losses also reduced demand for village merchants, transport services, carpenters, and food sellers. A statement such as 'agricultural prices fell 40–50 percent' is a useful starting point only if the historian specifies the commodity, years, and location; it cannot substitute for explaining these linked adjustments in rural economic life.

Historical Vocabulary
Hōsaku binbō ("poverty amid abundance")

Hōsaku binbō was the contemporary description of the situation in which a bumper harvest brought hardship because prices fell by more than output rose. It captures the way market dependence changes the meaning of a good harvest: in a subsistence economy, abundance is a benefit, but in a market economy with fixed debts and rents, abundance can be a disaster. The phrase does not mean that the rural poor were starving in a year of plenty, though some did suffer hunger in 1931 and 1934 when crops failed. It refers to the economic mechanism by which volume and income moved in opposite directions.

The term is a reminder that Japanese farmers in the 1930s were not isolated peasants outside the modern economy. They were producers for markets, and their misery in 1930 resulted from the way markets and obligations linked them to forces they could not control.

Section 73.13 · Division III

Farm Debt, the Tōhoku Crisis, and Rural Family Survival

Rural households had borrowed before the depression, and the collapse of incomes meant that debts that had been manageable became unpayable. Official estimates around 1932 put total farm debt on the order of four to five billion yen, though counts varied and the burden was unevenly distributed. Households fell behind on loans to landlords, moneylenders, cooperatives, and banks, and some lost land through foreclosure or sale. Local taxes and school fees continued to fall due, and many villages struggled to pay teachers and meet local government costs. Cooperatives, which had lent to their own members, found themselves holding bad loans and, in some cases, failed.

The northeast bore a heavy additional burden from the weather. Tōhoku farming depended on a single rice crop in a cool climate, and cold summers, associated with the yamase wind from the Pacific, could devastate it. In 1931 cold weather produced serious crop failures across the northeast and Hokkaido, and a second severe failure followed in 1934. The weather disaster arrived in districts that were already deeply in debt and had no cocoon income to fall back on. Reports described farm families eating wild plants and roots, going without adequate clothing and fuel through the winter, and falling behind on school fees. Contemporary accounts were said to place the number of children unable to bring lunch at more than two hundred thousand nationwide; the original counting method and date have not been independently verified. The Ministry of Education did, however, introduce documented national support for meals for impoverished schoolchildren in September 1932.

Families adopted a range of strategies. They cut consumption, delayed repayments, sought rent reductions, sold livestock and land, and sent members to work elsewhere. Return migration reversed the usual flow: jobless workers came back from cities and mills to households that could ill afford extra mouths, which raised the burden on villages. Women and children were deeply affected. Daughters often left for work in mills or in urban service and entertainment trades, and reports in the early 1930s, particularly from the northeast in 1934, described families placing daughters in indentured service or, in some cases, selling them into licensed prostitution or similar work. Students should handle such accounts with care. Documented cases exist and were reported at the time, but newspaper stories and reformist writing sometimes generalized from striking instances, and the scale of the practice is hard to establish. The responsible conclusion is that child and daughter placement was a real survival strategy in the worst-affected districts, not that it describes all or most rural families.

Distress was not confined to the main islands. In Hokkaido, settler farmers on recently cleared land suffered from the cold-weather crop failures, and Ainu communities, whose small allotments and weak legal position limited their options, were among the most vulnerable. In Okinawa, the price of sugar had collapsed after 1920, and by the late 1920s and early 1930s the prefecture's economy was in such crisis that residents were reported to be eating the poisonous cycad palm when it was inadequately prepared, a situation known as the "cycad hell" (sotetsu jigoku). Many Okinawans emigrated to Osaka and Kawasaki, to the Pacific mandate territories, and overseas, where they entered the lowest rungs of the labor market. In Korea, the depression coincided with the continued extraction of rice, and Korean farmers migrated to Japan in growing numbers, reaching a population of several hundred thousand in the metropole by 1930, where they faced discrimination and were disproportionately likely to lose jobs.

Rural distress also changed the relation between households and public institutions. When taxes could not be collected promptly, village governments struggled to pay teachers and maintain schools, while families faced pressure to keep children at home to work. Japan's Ministry of Education later recorded that roughly one in ten municipalities delayed some or all elementary-school teachers' salaries in its December 1930 investigation. In September 1932, the ministry created a state-supported school-meal program for children from poor households, demonstrating that educational administration had become part of emergency welfare. A Tokyo municipal education history reports that contemporaries spoke of more than 200,000 children nationally who lacked a midday meal, but the underlying count and its precise survey basis have not been established here. It should be cited as a reported estimate, not as a verified 1932 Ministry survey. The official school-meal measure, in contrast, is clearly documented in education histories.

Villagers' responses varied. Some families sold timber, livestock, or stored possessions; some negotiated a delay with a creditor or relied on relatives in another prefecture; others joined cooperatives or sought government work. Seasonal migration could reduce the number of dependants at home while bringing cash into the village, but jobs were difficult to find during an industrial contraction. Stories of daughters being sold into service or sexual exploitation circulated widely and sometimes described documented coercion, but sensational reports cannot establish how typical any practice was. Gender and age shaped vulnerability: women undertook unpaid household work and textile-related labor even as the prices of their products fell, and children bore consequences in nutrition, attendance, and opportunities for continued schooling. A first-edition textbook should present these patterns without constructing an invented representative victim or claiming an unsupported national prevalence.

Section 73.14 · Division III

Urban Unemployment, Industrial Contraction, and the Social Geography of Depression

Cities did not escape. Exporting industries, especially textiles and silk reeling, cut output and laid off workers. Mills reduced wages and extended working hours, and strikes broke out in response, as in the large textile disputes of 1930. Rationalization campaigns in heavy industry reduced employment, and the government encouraged cartels, notably under the Important Industries Control Law of 1931. Small manufacturers and merchants who depended on bank credit failed in large numbers, and small shopkeepers faced falling sales. Official unemployment estimates in 1930 and 1931 ran to several hundred thousand, though most historians regard them as undercounts, since many jobless workers returned to their villages and were not recorded as unemployed. Korean workers, concentrated in low-paid manual jobs, were among those hardest hit.

Experience varied by industry and by occupation. Export-oriented industries, such as silk and cotton textiles, suffered most severely at first. Industries serving domestic demand, including food processing, utilities, and some branches of chemicals, were more resilient. Workers who kept their jobs in the early 1930s sometimes found that falling prices partly offset reduced wages, so that employed urban households could see their real incomes hold up or even rise slightly, while the unemployed and the rural households that depended on their remittances suffered. White-collar workers, whose numbers had grown in the 1920s, faced a labor market in which university graduates struggled to find positions, a situation reflected in popular films and literature of the period. The relief system was thin: the Relief and Protection Law (Kyūgo Hō), passed in 1929 and implemented in 1932, provided limited public assistance, and most support came from families, charities, and local government.

The geography of the depression was therefore irregular. The northeast and the silk districts suffered most intensely, along with colonial and Okinawan producers. Industrial regions experienced layoffs, but those with diversified or domestic-oriented industry recovered sooner. The reverse flow of migrants meant that urban unemployment was transferred partly to the villages, making rural distress worse. These variations explain why contemporaries did not agree on what the depression meant and why political reactions diverged, a theme developed in Division IV.

Official unemployment statistics are particularly hard to interpret because the modern category of a job seeker without work did not cover every form of labor insecurity. Factory employees could be dismissed outright, placed on shorter shifts, or lose access to overtime and bonuses; home workers and subcontractors could receive fewer orders while remaining formally self-employed. Young clerical aspirants faced fewer openings at firms and government offices, but their experience differed from that of migrant construction laborers or women working in textile mills. A declining wage rate and a fall in days worked could combine to reduce annual income even for someone never recorded as unemployed. These distinctions are indispensable when comparing the depth of the urban depression with the distress recorded in farm villages, where household employment and family production often blurred conventional categories.

Financial contraction also affected the geography of employment. When an urban firm dismissed workers, some returned to rural households that were themselves losing income from rice and silk. Others moved between factories or towns searching for short-term wages, increasing pressure on relatives and local charities. Industrial employers had uneven capacities to maintain production: large integrated firms could preserve access to bank credit, whereas suppliers dependent on orders from a few customers and on local bank discounting were vulnerable to cascading failures. Relief measures were limited and often mediated by municipalities, employers, charitable bodies, and family networks rather than by a universal insurance system. This helps explain why industrial output could begin to recover before many workers experienced stability. Measures of national production cannot by themselves describe unemployment, purchasing power, or the lived experience of insecure employment.

Section 73.15 · Division IV — Big Business, Economic Policy, and Unequal Recovery

Tenant Disputes, Rural Cooperatives, and Economic Responses from Below

Rural people did not simply endure the crisis. Official counts of tenancy disputes, which stood at well over two thousand in the late 1920s, rose to roughly 2,400 in 1930 and above 3,400 in 1931 and 1932, and continued to climb afterward, though, as Chapter 69 cautioned, such counts depend on how disputes were defined and recorded. The content of the disputes shifted. In the 1920s many involved demands for rent reductions and security of tenure. In the early 1930s they increasingly concerned survival: requests for relief from rent in years of price collapse, resistance to evictions and foreclosures, and opposition to the sale of mortgaged land. Tenants appealed to landlords, to conciliation committees established under the 1924 law, and, where organizations existed, to tenant unions that coordinated rent strikes and legal action.

Landlords were also under pressure. With rice prices low, many landlords' rental incomes fell, and small landlords with their own debts could be as hard pressed as the tenants. Some responded by tightening rents and evicting tenants, which intensified disputes, while others negotiated reductions. A comprehensive tenancy law, which would have strengthened tenants' legal security, was debated in the Diet but did not pass, defeated in the House of Peers in 1931. The failure meant that conciliation remained the main legal avenue, and its effect, as Chapter 69 explained, was to channel conflict rather than change the underlying legal relationship.

The countryside also organized economically. Industrial cooperatives (sangyō kumiai) provided credit, joint purchasing of fertilizer, and marketing, supported by the central cooperative bank established in the 1920s. Villages formed mutual aid arrangements and debt-adjustment committees. From 1932, the government, responding to the rural emergency, launched public relief works to provide wages in villages and a campaign of rural economic revitalization (keizai kōsei) that urged villages to plan, economize, and rely on their own efforts, and encouraged debt adjustment through cooperatives and legislation. Historians have viewed these measures with ambivalence. They did channel funds to the countryside and strengthen cooperatives, but they also emphasized self-help and moral exhortation over structural reform, leaving landlord rights and the burden of debt largely intact. They show how the state, having left the countryside to deflation, now sought to manage the political consequences.

Cooperative action did not mean that rural society became politically unified. Tenants, owners, landlords, merchants, and village officials could all favor cheaper credit while disagreeing over rents, land rights, and taxation. A purchasing cooperative might reduce the cost of fertilizer and help a farmer sell produce collectively; a credit cooperative could offer an alternative to a moneylender, but it still had to collect repayments and protect deposits. Programs encouraging individual households to buy land promised longer-term security, yet falling income made installment payments harder to sustain. Conciliation committees could settle a dispute over a specific rent reduction without changing the rules governing future tenancy. Economic organization from below thus mattered, although the benefits depended on which villagers had the resources and authority to participate.

The rural revitalization campaigns launched from 1932 provide a second level of interpretation. Administrators urged communities to improve their accounts, diversify production, organize savings, and undertake public works, often using moral language about thrift and self-reliance. The government did furnish some support, but practical capacity varied with the local tax base, the condition of cooperatives, and the availability of committed organizers. Kerry Smith's study of village revitalization illustrates why local responses cannot simply be described as passive recipients of policy: villagers debated and reshaped official schemes in pursuit of their own priorities. Nor did the programs erase longstanding differences in property or access to markets. Their significance lies partly in the growth of local administrative coordination, an institutional legacy that connected the immediate depression to the more interventionist economy of the later 1930s.

Section 73.16 · Division IV

Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, and Yasuda: The Economic Power of the Zaibatsu

Chapter 56 described the origins of the zaibatsu. By the 1920s, the largest were organized as family-controlled holding companies standing atop a network of subsidiaries: Mitsui Gōmei (1909), Yasuda Hozensha (1912), Mitsubishi Gōshi (1917), and the reorganized Sumitomo Gōshi (1921). Each combined a bank, trading, mining, manufacturing, shipping or insurance interests, and a range of affiliated firms. Mitsui's strength lay in Mitsui Bank, the trading firm Mitsui Bussan (which handled a substantial share of Japan's foreign trade, by some estimates around a fifth, though figures vary), and mining. Mitsubishi was prominent in shipbuilding, mining, shipping, and the Mitsubishi Bank. Sumitomo combined copper and metals with the Sumitomo Bank. Yasuda was primarily a financial group, built around the Yasuda Bank and insurance.

Their advantages in the crises were structural. They had access to internal capital, since the bank in the group could lend to affiliates and the group could shift resources among its firms. They had strong relationships with the Bank of Japan and with government officials, and they drew on professional managers recruited from the best universities. In 1927, they benefited from a flight of deposits to safety, as savers sought institutions that had visibly weathered the panic. They could borrow at lower cost than smaller competitors and had access to foreign credit. Their diversification also gave them room to absorb losses in one sector with gains in another, and, when rivals failed, the means to acquire distressed assets or customers.

Students should avoid two exaggerations. The zaibatsu did not own the entire economy: their share of national paid-up capital was smaller than their reputation suggested, though it grew during the 1930s, and many firms, especially small and medium enterprises, had no connection to them. Nor were the zaibatsu uniformly immune. They too faced risks and made misjudgments, and the groups competed among themselves. The point is relative strength: in a period when small banks, small firms, and farm households were being squeezed, the largest business groups were positioned to survive and, in some respects, to expand. This divergence helps explain the sharpness of public resentment toward big business, a theme developed in the next section.

The organizations of the major groups were not identical. Holding companies helped families retain voting control while employing professional managers and separating operations into banks, trading houses, mines, and factories. Mitsubishi's strengths in heavy industry and shipbuilding differed from Mitsui's trading and mining reach; Sumitomo's copper and finance had a different history from Yasuda's heavily financial portfolio. Comparing these structures helps explain why an economic disturbance did not affect the groups uniformly. Some affiliates faced shrinking export demand or declining asset values, while others could draw upon internal finance and established relationships with large customers. Their influence lay as much in access to information and the ability to shift resources as in any single share of national capital. A business-group label should therefore prompt investigation of subsidiaries, family ownership, managerial autonomy, and banks rather than substitute for those details.

Financial concentration also had a regional cost and a regional benefit. An expanded city bank branch network could offer depositors stronger reserves and more standardized accounting than a family-controlled village bank. But its lending officers might favor larger borrowers or securities judged safe by a central management committee rather than local craft firms whose reputations had been built over decades. Research on Yasuda Bank's integration of affiliates, for example, describes the reorganization of Higo Bank into its branch structure in 1923 and subsequent central supervision of lending. Such examples complicate the idea that strong banks simply absorbed weak ones after 1927: some consolidation preceded the panic, and branch policies could alter local credit relations over time. The critical historical question is how improvements in system-wide stability interacted with the concentration of discretion and bargaining power.

Section 73.17 · Division IV

Big Business, Political Influence, and the Debate over Economic Concentration

Relationships between major firms and parties were close and widely known. Scholars have long noted that the Seiyūkai had ties to Mitsui and the Kenseikai and its successor the Rikken Minseitō to Mitsubishi, which was connected by family links to Kenseikai leaders. Businessmen contributed to party funds, bureaucrats and politicians moved between public and private posts, and banks bought government bonds and financed public projects. Such relationships were not secret. They were also not in themselves evidence of corruption, since political donations and personal connections were normal features of party politics in many countries. Historians must therefore distinguish between documented relationships, such as party funding and bond underwriting, and specific allegations of bribery or policy purchased by money, many of which were made in the press and the Diet without being proved.

The most notorious episode of the period was the "dollar buying" of 1931. After Britain left the gold standard in September 1931, it became widely expected that Japan would follow, which would lower the yen's value. Major banks, including those of the zaibatsu groups, bought large amounts of dollars in anticipation, and when the yen fell after December, the purchases earned substantial profits. The facts of the purchases are documented, including in contemporary press accounts and later scholarship, though their exact scale and the intentions behind them were disputed. Defenders argued that banks were protecting themselves against a foreseeable fall in the yen and that the purchases were legitimate hedging. Critics saw speculation at the expense of the national economy, undertaken by institutions whose profits came while farmers suffered. The episode fixed an image of big business as selfish and unaccountable, an image that political commentators and radical groups exploited. Violence directed at business and political leaders in early 1932 belongs to the history of Chapter 74, and students should note that the connection between public resentment and such violence was indirect and has been debated.

The debate about concentration also had an institutional side. The government encouraged cartels in the early 1930s under the Important Industries Control Law of 1931, a measure that benefited established firms by allowing coordinated production limits and price agreements. Observers argued over whether such arrangements promoted efficiency or entrenched large firms against competitors and consumers. At the same time, new industrial groups were emerging, often associated with chemicals, electrical goods, and heavy industry, and with investment in Korea and Manchuria. Groups such as Nissan, associated with Ayukawa Yoshisuke, and Nichitsu (Japan Nitrogenous Fertilizer), associated with Noguchi Shitagau, grew in the 1930s and are sometimes called "new zaibatsu." They are important for later chapters on the wartime economy, and their growth signals that the structure of big business was not static.

The political economy of the zaibatsu extended beyond individual donations or personal friendship. Banks held government securities, trading houses depended on customs and foreign-exchange rules, and industrial firms sought contracts, licenses, or protective tariffs. Political leaders, in turn, needed credit, information about business conditions, and support for public borrowing. Such repeated exchanges created influence even where no one made an unlawful payment. The historian must therefore ask which policy was adopted, who argued for it, and how the costs and benefits were distributed rather than infer a corrupt bargain from the presence of a family relationship. The popular association of Mitsui with the Seiyūkai and Mitsubishi with the Kenseikai/Minseitō is an analytical shorthand, not a claim that each group always controlled or supported a single party. Corporate and party interests could also diverge.

The dollar-buying controversy of 1931 illustrates how an action could be privately defensible and publicly corrosive. Banks and corporations exposed to foreign-currency liabilities had reasons to protect themselves against the expected decline of the yen; speculative purchases could amplify pressure on the currency or appear to anticipate decisions unavailable to ordinary savers. After Japan left gold, widely reported gains gave critics a vivid symbol of an unequal economy: institutions with access to foreign exchange profited while indebted farmers and smaller borrowers had little scope to hedge. Contemporary newspapers and later historians differ in their estimates of transactions and in how they interpret motives. The same care applies to industrial cartels, which might prevent ruinous overproduction but could also sustain prices and exclude competitors. The main issue is the growing capacity of concentrated firms to shape market outcomes and public debate, not a blanket conclusion that every instance of commercial coordination was criminal.

Section 73.18 · Division IV

Takahashi Korekiyo and the Turn Away from Deflation

The Wakatsuki Minseitō cabinet, formed in April 1931 after Hamaguchi's resignation, struggled to maintain the gold standard in the face of continuing outflows, and it collapsed in December 1931 amid disagreements over policy. On December 13, the Seiyūkai cabinet of Inukai Tsuyoshi took office with Takahashi Korekiyo as finance minister. Takahashi immediately reinstated the embargo on gold exports, and within days convertibility of banknotes into gold was suspended. The yen, no longer tied to gold, fell rapidly against the dollar, dropping to about half or less of its former parity within a year. The decision ended the deflationary experiment of 1930–1931.

Takahashi's policies went beyond leaving gold. The Bank of Japan lowered interest rates, credit eased, and the government increased spending, financing the resulting deficits by issuing bonds. From November 1932, the Bank of Japan directly underwrote government bonds and sold them on to the market, a mechanism that provided funds without immediate pressure on interest rates. The government also introduced controls on capital flight in 1932 and on foreign exchange in 1933 to prevent speculators from undermining the yen. These measures were pragmatic and, in important respects, a break with the financial orthodoxy of Inoue. Takahashi, who had criticized the gold return, argued that the economy needed stimulus and that the depreciated yen would assist exporters.

Students should distinguish stages. The suspension of the gold standard and the fall of the yen in late 1931 and 1932 were the initial reversal. Deficit spending and, from late 1932, Bank of Japan underwriting developed over the following year. The composition of spending shifted as the military budget grew after the Manchurian Incident and the creation of Manchukuo, subjects of Chapters 74 and 75. Takahashi, who later tried to curb military spending, is a figure whose policies helped recovery but also opened a path toward larger military budgets. His story belongs partly to a later chapter, and the present chapter's task is to explain why the turn from deflation mattered for the economy.

The monetary turn can be traced through the exchange rate and the financing of government spending. At the old gold parity, one yen was worth almost fifty American cents; after leaving gold, it depreciated sharply during 1932. In yen terms, exporters receiving dollars or pounds could obtain more domestic currency for the same foreign sale, while imports priced in those currencies became more costly. Easier money and lower interest rates could support borrowing and production. The effect was not universal. Industries depending heavily on imported raw materials faced higher input costs, and a weaker yen did little to restore the long-term demand for silk stockings. Takahashi's achievement was to change the direction of policy during a contraction, not to eliminate the unequal economic structures inherited from the 1920s. Economic historians therefore distinguish the competitive advantage of depreciation from fiscal stimulus and from the monetary expansion that followed it.

Timing also matters for assessing the financing innovation. The decision to resume an embargo on gold exports came in December 1931, while direct Bank of Japan underwriting of newly issued deficit-financing government bonds began on November 25, 1932, according to the Bank's monetary-history research. The authorities initially expected the central bank to sell bonds onward rather than retain the full stock indefinitely. This helped the treasury obtain financing at low cost while the public and private financial system absorbed new debt. Such arrangements could revive demand in a depressed economy, but they also raised a longer-term question about how to restrain public borrowing once the external gold-standard discipline had been removed. The growth of military expenditure made that question increasingly urgent. The discussion here should stop at the early consequences of recovery and leave the later political contest over budgets to the relevant chapters.

Section 73.19 · Division IV

Recovery, Persistent Inequality, and the Political Consequences of Crisis

Japan's recovery from the depression began earlier than that of many industrial countries. Industrial production surpassed its 1929 level by about 1932–1933, driven by exports, which rebounded as the weak yen made Japanese goods cheap, and by growth in heavy industry, chemicals, and, increasingly, military-related production. Cotton textile exports expanded rapidly, and by 1933 Japan had become the world's leading exporter of cotton cloth, prompting accusations of "social dumping" and tariff responses from Britain, its colonies, and other countries. The government's easier credit and public spending supported domestic demand, and large firms, with their access to finance and their capacity to expand, were well positioned to profit.

The recovery was uneven. Raw silk never regained its former price, as rayon and the long-term decline of American demand weakened the market, and sericulture districts remained depressed. Rice prices improved somewhat with the weaker yen and with the Rice Control Law of 1933, which aimed to stabilize prices, but a further cold-weather crop failure in the northeast in 1934 renewed hardship there. Farm debt remained a heavy burden, and relief and revitalization programs eased pressure without resolving it. Wages in industry lagged behind output and profits, and small firms that had survived the crisis operated in an economy increasingly dominated by large enterprises. The zaibatsu and newer groups entered the 1930s with strengthened positions. In this sense the recovery confirmed the pattern of the crises: those with capital, credit, and access to the state were positioned to benefit, while those dependent on weak prices or weak institutions recovered slowly or not at all.

The political consequences are among the most debated aspects of the period. Surveys, newspapers, and the language of political groups all show deep disillusionment with the parties and with big business in the early 1930s. Parties were associated with corruption, with ties to the zaibatsu, and with the deflationary policy of the Minseitō, while the zaibatsu were associated with profiteering. Radical critics on the right and left argued that the political and economic system had failed the countryside. Some young officers and activists invoked rural distress to justify direct action, and nationalist agrarian thinkers condemned capitalism and the cities. Historians nevertheless caution against a simple causal chain. The relationship between economic hardship and political radicalism was indirect, many of the most severely affected rural households did not support radical movements, and military politics had roots in institutional developments, foreign policy, and ideology that the depression did not create. The Manchurian Incident, which occurred in September 1931 while deflationary policy was still in force, was planned by officers of the Kwantung Army and cannot be explained as a response to the depression. The most defensible statement is that economic crisis weakened the credibility of party government and provided arguments and audiences for those who wished to replace it, without making that outcome inevitable.

A recovery statistic answers a narrower question than is sometimes assumed. Rising industrial production measures activity in factories; it does not show that a tenant family's rents were affordable or that an unemployed worker had regained a stable wage. A weak yen helped competitive export industries, particularly cotton textiles, while continued structural substitution away from natural silk limited gains in cocoon-growing districts. Even if agricultural selling prices rose somewhat after 1931, outstanding debts incurred before the depression were not automatically forgiven. In regions exposed to another bad harvest, including Tōhoku in 1934, households remained vulnerable. This divergence is the chapter's central distributional finding: economic stimulus could produce national recovery without reversing long-standing inequalities of property, credit, and regional access to markets.

The political aftermath is equally complex. The parties' association with unpopular financial decisions and large corporations offered their opponents persuasive material, but public disillusionment did not take a single ideological form. Some rural reformers sought stronger cooperatives, protection against eviction, and local public works; others embraced agrarian moral renewal; some radicals opposed capitalism entirely. The international and military disputes surrounding Manchuria had their own chronology, institutional actors, and ambitions, and the 1931 intervention cannot be explained by a falling rice price alone. Economic crisis enlarged the audience for criticism of established institutions and affected the resources available to government. It did not decide which competing political project would prevail. The historian's task is to keep these overlapping causes in view, rather than convert the country's subsequent history into the predetermined outcome of a single economic shock.

Section 73.20 · Division V — Historical Interpretation and Chapter Review

Economic Crisis, Concentrated Power, and the Limits of Parliamentary Japan: Synthesis and Review

The chapter began with a question: why did repeated economic crises produce such different outcomes for major financial institutions, industrial firms, small businesses, and rural households? The evidence assembled in the preceding sections suggests an answer built on three layers. First, vulnerabilities were distributed unequally before the crises. Tenant farmers carried fixed rents and debts, small banks held concentrated and opaque loans, small manufacturers depended on credit from those banks, and colonial and peripheral regions sold commodities into markets they did not control. Second, the shocks transmitted through specific mechanisms. The 1920 downturn left debts that surfaced in 1927, and the world depression passed through silk prices, while deflationary policy intensified falls in farm incomes. Third, the responses protected institutions more than people. Emergency lending and guarantees stabilized the banking system in 1927, and Takahashi's reflation rescued industry and exporters, but rural debt, tenancy, and the precarity of small enterprises were addressed only partially.

Historians offer competing explanations of how these elements fit together. Some emphasize structural features of the economy, including the weight of agriculture, the dependence on silk, and the role of the banks. Others emphasize policy, stressing the postponement of adjustment in the 1920s and the timing of the gold return. Still others stress the international system, in which Japan, like other countries, was caught by the contraction of world trade and the rigidities of the gold standard. The positions are not mutually exclusive, and the strongest accounts integrate them. The 1920s have also been reassessed. The older image of a decade of stagnation has been qualified, since industrial output grew and living standards in the cities rose, but the same decade carried unresolved financial problems and rural inequalities that made the crises of 1927 and 1930 severe.

Students should be cautious with statistics. National aggregates, such as exports or industrial production, can show recovery while specific groups remain in crisis. Prices, debts, and unemployment figures were collected by agencies with their own definitions and purposes, and many are estimates. Nominal values must be distinguished from real ones, and averages must be disaggregated by region, class, and gender. The chapter has also attempted to keep the empire in view, since the Bank of Taiwan, colonial rice, Okinawan sugar, and Korean migration all formed part of the economy that experienced these crises. Finally, the chapter has resisted teleology. The crises weakened confidence in party government and in big business, but later events, including the Manchurian Incident and the political violence examined in Chapter 74, had their own causes and were not mechanical products of the depression.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan's industrializing and internationally connected economy became vulnerable between about 1920 and 1933, why rural households bore particularly severe hardship, and how repeated financial crises affected confidence in banks, business leaders, and parliamentary government. Its central argument is that the crises emerged from the interaction of structural rural inequality, fragile banking relationships, international market dependence, and disputed government policies, and that their effects were distributed unevenly, strengthening major corporations while deepening hardship among farmers, workers, and smaller enterprises.

The starting point was the First World War boom and its collapse in 1920. Exports, shipbuilding, and speculation had expanded rapidly, and in the spring of 1920 the stock market and commodity prices fell. The authorities and banks chose to roll over bad loans rather than write them off, and the resulting frozen debts remained in the banking system throughout the decade. The 1920s were not simply a period of stagnation, since industry grew, but they were a period in which financial problems accumulated beneath the surface.

The economy was uneven. Large modern firms and industrial regions coexisted with small workshops and weakly capitalized local banks, and the countryside combined small farms, high tenancy, rents commonly equal to about half of the main crop, and heavy dependence on credit. Rice and silk tied rural households to markets. Roughly two in five farm households raised silkworms, and raw silk, sold overwhelmingly to the United States, accounted for a large share of Japan's exports. Women's labor in rearing silkworms and reeling silk was essential to household income. Colonial rice from Korea and Taiwan, Okinawan sugar, and Hokkaido settler and Ainu farming completed a picture of peripheral regions exposed to shocks.

The Shōwa Financial Crisis of 1927 was a banking and credit crisis. Its underlying causes were the frozen loans of 1920, earthquake bills that had become entangled with older bad debts, and bank lending concentrated on related firms. The trigger was the finance minister's statement of March 14 about the Tokyo Watanabe Bank. The crisis deepened in April when the trading firm Suzuki Shōten collapsed, threatening the Bank of Taiwan, whose rescue the Privy Council refused to approve, bringing down the Wakatsuki cabinet. The Tanaka cabinet's moratorium, Bank of Japan lending, and government guarantees ended the panic, and the Banking Law of 1927 led to consolidation, strengthening the largest banks and eliminating hundreds of smaller ones.

The Shōwa Depression of 1930–1931 had a different origin. The world depression that followed the American crash of 1929 destroyed demand for silk, and Japan's return to the gold standard in January 1930 at the old parity added deflationary pressure. Rice and cocoon prices fell by roughly half, the record 1930 harvest deepened the paradox of "poverty amid abundance," and crop failures in the northeast in 1931 compounded the hardship. Farm debt mounted, families cut consumption and sent daughters to work, and cities experienced layoffs and unemployment that were partly exported back to the villages. Okinawa, Hokkaido, Korea, and migrant workers experienced the crisis in distinct and often harsher forms.

Rural people responded through tenancy disputes, cooperatives, and relief and revitalization programs, and the state offered conciliation and self-help rather than structural reform. The zaibatsu, meanwhile, gained relative strength. Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, and Yasuda combined banking, trade, and industry, benefited from flights of deposits to safety, and had privileged access to credit and government. Their relationships with parties were documented, while allegations of corruption must be distinguished from proof. The dollar buying of 1931 became a symbol of resentment, and newer industrial groups began to emerge.

Takahashi Korekiyo's suspension of the gold standard in December 1931, the resulting fall of the yen, the easing of credit, and deficit financing, including Bank of Japan underwriting from late 1932, produced an early recovery of industry and exports. Cotton textile exports surged, and Japan's recovery began earlier than that of many other economies. But the recovery was uneven. Raw silk did not regain its former price, rural debt remained heavy, the northeast suffered again in 1934, and large firms were strongest.

Historians interpret these developments through structural, policy, and international explanations, and they debate the gold return in particular. The strongest accounts combine them: the world depression was the principal shock, the gold policy intensified and prolonged its effects, and the structure of the economy determined who bore the cost. The crises weakened confidence in party government and big business, but the chapter has argued that they did not make the political breakdown examined in Chapter 74 inevitable. Chapter 74 takes up that breakdown, and later chapters return to the wartime economy that the recovery helped to shape.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a modern economy's crises are never only economic events. They are distributions of risk, loss, and advantage among people with unequal power. The bank runs of 1927 and the price collapses of 1930 were consequences of decisions about debt, currency, and rescue, and the answers to who would be protected, and who would bear the burden, were political answers. The pattern the chapter has traced, in which institutions were stabilized and households absorbed much of the cost, helps explain why contemporaries lost confidence in both the parties and the leaders of big business.

The chapter also connects regions that textbook narratives often separate. The silk uplands of Nagano, the cold-prone villages of Tōhoku, Hokkaido's settlers and Ainu, Okinawa's sugar fields, the Bank of Taiwan, and Korean rice and migrant workers all belonged to a single imperial economy, and their fortunes were linked through prices, credit, and policy. Seen from this angle, the depression was not just a metropolitan event with rural side effects, but a crisis whose costs were spread across an empire in ways that mirrored its hierarchies.

The developments examined here shaped later history. The consolidation of banks and the strengthening of the zaibatsu left an economy more concentrated, while Takahashi's reflation and the controls of 1932–1933 established precedents for state direction that later served wartime mobilization. Rural debt and tenancy, left largely unresolved, became part of the background to the land reform of the occupation. Above all, the loss of confidence in party government and in big business created conditions in which radical alternatives could find audiences. Chapter 74 takes up that political breakdown, and students who approach it with the evidence of this chapter in mind should resist the temptation to treat it as the automatic outcome of economic hardship. Crisis narrowed the paths open to Japan, but the choices that followed were made by identifiable people and institutions, and they could have been otherwise.
