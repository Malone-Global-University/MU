Mingei, Literature, New Religions, and Cultural Experimentation

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1914, according to the standard accounts of the Mingei movement, a young teacher and painter named Asakawa Noritaka, who lived in colonial Korea, visited the writer and philosopher Yanagi Sōetsu at his home in Abiko, east of Tokyo, and brought him a porcelain jar made in the Joseon kingdom. It was not a famous treasure. It was a made object of the kind that had been used in Korean households and sold, often cheaply, to collectors. Yet Yanagi's encounter with such objects began a long argument about beauty, labor, religion, and power that would shape Japanese cultural life for decades.

The jar is a useful way into this chapter because it joins several questions that run through the cultural history of Taishō and early Shōwa Japan. How should beauty be judged: by the fame of an artist, by a skilled maker's training, or by use in ordinary life? Who has the authority to say what counts as art, and to say what counts as "tradition"? What happens when a Japanese intellectual admires the work of a people who are governed by Japan? And how should creative people respond to a society being reorganized by factories, cities, newspapers, mass migration, and imperial expansion? Chapter 70 asked how people consumed the new culture of the modern city. This chapter asks how writers, artists, craftspeople, religious leaders, and their followers created, contested, preserved, and redefined culture, and why those efforts mattered.

The responses were not unified. Some artists wanted to destroy established conventions and borrowed the vocabulary of European Dada, Futurism, and Constructivism. Some craftspeople and collectors wanted to recover what they believed was lost beauty in anonymous, handmade, regional objects. Novelists explored psychological uncertainty, desire, and the unreliability of testimony; others wrote about factory floors, cannery ships, and the dormitories of the poor. Poets experimented with colloquial language. Women writers claimed literary authority in a market that often treated them as curiosities. New religious communities, some of them founded by women with little formal education, offered healing, ancestral rites, and visions of a renewed world to people disoriented by migration and insecurity. These movements sometimes cooperated, often clashed, and occasionally overlapped in membership.

The people involved were not limited to educated men in Tokyo. The Ainu young woman Chiri Yukie, who died at nineteen in 1922, left a collection of oral epics that was published the following year and still holds a place in modern Japanese literature. Korean and Taiwanese writers worked under colonial censorship and chose among languages that carried different political meanings. Okinawan scholars debated the place of Ryukyu in Japan's past. Women such as Hayashi Fumiko, Miyamoto Yuriko, Sata Ineko, Kotani Kimi, and Deguchi Nao helped shape literature and religion. Potters, weavers, and woodworkers whose names were seldom recorded produced the objects that Mingei advocates celebrated, and their own views are difficult to recover. Keeping these varied people in view prevents the chapter from becoming a gallery of famous male authors.

The chapter's central argument is that modern Japanese cultural life developed through competing efforts to redefine the relationship between tradition and innovation, individual creativity and collective life, spiritual meaning and material progress, and cultural independence and international exchange. Neither "tradition" nor "modernity" was a fixed category. Mingei advocates, who seemed to defend tradition, were deeply modern: they wrote in the language of Western aesthetics, relied on publishing and museums, and drew inspiration from the English Arts and Crafts movement and from Korean objects. Avant-garde artists who seemed to reject tradition drew on Japanese printmaking and typography. New religions that seemed to revive old beliefs used magazines, lecture circuits, and corporate-style organization. Students should resist the idea that Japanese culture alternated between "Western" and "Japanese" impulses.

The evidence is rich and uneven. It includes objects held in museums, especially the Japan Folk Crafts Museum; magazines such as Shirakaba, Bungei Jidai, Akai Tori, Kōgei, and Nyonin Geijutsu; novels and stories that have been translated into English and many other languages; manifestos and exhibition records; religious scriptures, periodicals, and police and court files; and memoirs. Each category has limits. Surviving craft objects have been selected by collectors; magazines record the views of editors and contributors; police files record the concerns of the state; and religious publications record leaders' teachings more fully than followers' experiences. Translations of literary and religious texts can alter meaning, and this chapter therefore paraphrases sources and identifies where students should consult originals.

The chronology concentrates on roughly 1910 to 1933, with selective earlier background and later consequences. The founding of the Shirakaba circle in 1910, the 1914 jar, and the 1918 launch of the children's magazine Akai Tori open the story. The year 1923 brings the publication of Ainu Shin'yōshū, the formation of MAVO, and the Great Kantō Earthquake (Chapter 71). The year 1924 brings the New Sensation School's Bungei Jidai and the Korean Folk Art Museum; 1925 brings the coinage of the word mingei; 1926 brings the museum prospectus; 1927 brings Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's death; and 1930 brings the first publications associated with Seichō no Ie and the Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai. The Japan Folk Crafts Museum opened in 1936. The chapter builds on Chapters 63 to 71, especially Chapter 64 (new religions), Chapter 65 (mass culture), Chapter 69 (labor and reform), and Chapter 71 (the earthquake), and it prepares for the economic crisis of Chapter 73 and the political collapse of Chapter 74.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how industrialization, urbanization, and imperial expansion shaped Japanese debates about authenticity, craft, creativity, and spiritual life between about 1910 and 1933.
  2. Trace the development of the Mingei movement from Yanagi Sōetsu's encounter with Korean ceramics to the opening of the Japan Folk Crafts Museum in 1936.
  3. Analyze the aesthetic philosophy of "beauty in use," anonymous craftsmanship, and regional craft, and evaluate its contradictions involving labor, commerce, and colonial power.
  4. Distinguish among Naturalism and the I-novel, the Shirakaba circle, aestheticism, the New Sensation School, and proletarian literature, and explain how each responded to modern society.
  5. Interpret literary works by Akutagawa, Tanizaki, Kawabata, Yokomitsu, and proletarian writers as historical evidence about psychology, class, and urban life.
  6. Assess the contributions of women writers, Ainu, Korean, Okinawan, and Taiwanese authors, and the constraints imposed by gender, colonial rule, and censorship.
  7. Compare MAVO and other avant-garde art, the creative print movement, and experimental theater and design with Mingei's approach to tradition.
  8. Explain the social conditions that supported new religious movements and compare Ōmoto, Reiyūkai, Seichō no Ie, and the Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai.
  9. Evaluate the First Ōmoto Incident of 1921 as evidence of the limits of religious freedom under the Meiji constitutional order.
  10. Evaluate primary sources, including manifestos, prospectuses, prefaces, and religious writings, for creator, audience, purpose, and limits.
  11. Assess competing interpretations of cultural change: opposition to modernization, alternative modernities, or different expressions of the same transformations.
  12. Avoid treating Japanese culture as timeless, uniformly traditional, or simply Westernized, and avoid reading the political collapse of the 1930s backward into the creativity of the 1920s.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did industrial modernity make handmade, anonymous, and regional objects newly valuable to intellectuals, and who decided which objects deserved that value?
  2. How did Japan's imperial position shape the Mingei movement's admiration for Korean, Okinawan, and Ainu culture, and can admiration coexist with unequal power?
  3. In what ways did literary experimentation respond to urban life, psychological uncertainty, and class conflict, and why did writers disagree about literature's social purpose?
  4. How did women, Ainu, Korean, Okinawan, and Taiwanese writers gain or fail to gain cultural authority within a Japanese-language literary market?
  5. How did avant-garde artists borrow from international modernism while developing forms of their own, and how did their conception of tradition differ from Mingei's?
  6. Why did new religious movements attract followers in the 1910s and 1920s, and how did the state decide which groups to tolerate, regulate, or suppress?
  7. To what extent did commerce, patronage, and publishing enable or compromise cultural independence?
  8. Were Mingei, avant-garde art, proletarian literature, and new religions opposed to modernization, alternative versions of modernity, or products of the same changes, and how should historians avoid reading the later authoritarian turn backward into these movements?

Historical Context

By 1910 Japan had been rebuilding its cultural institutions for four decades. In the 1870s officials adopted the word bijutsu ("fine art") to translate a European category, and they sorted objects into fine arts, crafts, and industrial products as they prepared for international expositions. Government schools taught Western-style painting and design, and exports of ceramics, lacquer, and textiles earned foreign currency. Intellectuals debated whether Japan should imitate European art or defend a national tradition, and by the early twentieth century most of them accepted that the two could not be cleanly separated. Chapter 55 and Chapter 58 described the educational and cultural reforms of the Meiji period, and Chapter 59 and Chapter 61 described the constitutional state and the war with Russia that shaped the generation now entering adulthood.

The economy and society in which these debates took place changed rapidly. Factory employment, discussed in Chapters 63 and 69, drew young women and men from rural villages into cities and mills. Newspapers and magazines, Chapter 65 explained, reached larger audiences. Higher education expanded, especially after a 1918 reform recognized private universities, producing a larger class of students, teachers, critics, and aspiring writers. The First World War boom (Chapter 66) enriched some industrialists, who became patrons of museums and research institutes, and the postwar recession and inflation created unease. Chapter 70 described the department stores, cafés, cinemas, and radio of the modern city, and Chapter 71 described the 1923 earthquake that destroyed much of Tokyo and Yokohama and disrupted its publishers and workshops.

Politics set boundaries. Chapter 69 described the Public Peace Police Law, the Peace Preservation Law of 1925, and the 1925 suffrage reform. Censorship under publication and newspaper laws allowed the Home Ministry to ban or edit works judged harmful to public order or morals, and writers often substituted blanks or symbols for banned words. Colonial rule in Korea, Taiwan, and Karafuto, and the incorporation of Okinawa and Hokkaido, meant that cultural debates took place inside an empire in which Japanese-language institutions held the greatest power. Chapter 62 described the colonization of Korea and the 1919 March First Movement, which prompted the colonial government to relax some restrictions on Korean-language publishing even as it maintained police control.

Religious life had also been reorganized. The Meiji state separated Shinto and Buddhism and treated shrine Shinto as a national rite rather than a religion (Chapter 55), while recognizing certain "sect Shinto" groups and Buddhist denominations. Groups outside these categories existed in an uncertain legal position, and Chapter 64 introduced two of the earliest, Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō. By the Taishō period the number of new movements was growing, and their members included urban migrants, small shopkeepers, and rural families who sought healing, moral guidance, and community. Throughout the chapter, students should keep in view that these developments were taking place together, in the same cities, magazines, and households.

Section 72.1 · Division I — Cultural and Intellectual Foundations of Modern Japan

Cultural Japan between Taishō and Early Shōwa

The cultural world examined here took shape between the death of the Meiji emperor in 1912 and the early 1930s. Many writers saw the end of Meiji as the end of an era. The novelist Natsume Sōseki, whose novel Kokoro (1914) was set against the Meiji emperor's death and the suicide of General Nogi, died in 1916, and Mori Ōgai died in 1922. The younger writers and artists who followed had grown up in a society that already had universities, a national press, railways, and a recognized literary establishment (bundan). They did not need to argue that Japan should have a modern culture; they argued about what kind.

Several features distinguished the intellectual environment. Education expanded: the University Ordinance of 1918 recognized private universities, and student societies, reading groups, and coterie magazines multiplied. Many intellectuals embraced "culturalism" (kyōyōshugi), the cultivation of the self through reading philosophy, literature, and art, a current that overlapped with Taishō-era humanism and with the language of democracy described in Chapter 67. Vitalist and idealist philosophy, Tolstoy, Nietzsche, Bergson, and the Russian novelists circulated widely in translation. The generation of the 1920s was also marked by disillusionment: the war boom had enriched some and the recession of 1920 and the Kantō earthquake of 1923 shook confidence in progress. Marxism, discussed in Chapter 69, provided an alternative analytical vocabulary after about 1920.

Taishō culture also contained assumptions of empire. Japanese intellectuals traveled to Korea, China, and Taiwan; they collected, studied, and wrote about colonial peoples; and they often treated colonized communities as sources of authenticity or as objects of knowledge. Some, such as Yanagi Sōetsu, criticized colonial violence, while sharing assumptions about Japanese cultural leadership. The cultural experimentation of the period should therefore be understood as a debate within a stratified imperial society, and the voices of colonized people, women, and workers must be sought deliberately.

A final feature was a pervasive unease about modernity. Writers described neurasthenia, alienation, and loss of community. Some sought a cure in individual self-expression, some in art that served the working class, some in rural simplicity, and some in religion. These responses did not map neatly on the political spectrum; the same person might embrace avant-garde art and spiritual practice, or support labor movements and admire folk craft. The remainder of Division I explains the specific environments in which these responses developed.

The chronology of these cultural arguments matters. In the 1910s, journals rooted in elite secondary schools and universities helped make individual character, artistic personality, and ethical cultivation central themes. After the First World War, the readership and occupational range of the participants widened: salaried employees, teachers, printers, and students encountered arguments about culture in mass-market books as well as private literary circles. During the later 1920s, Marxist critics questioned whether an individual's sincerity could overcome inequalities of class and empire. The resulting disputes did not divide neatly between old and young: the same publisher might circulate romantic poems, experiments in prose, and fiction exposing industrial hardship. The historian should therefore reconstruct cultural movements as overlapping associations rather than assign each decade an exclusive intellectual style.

Political change also affected the public meaning of culture. The rise of party cabinets, the expansion of male suffrage in 1925, and the existence of restrictive press and policing laws provided contradictory incentives. An author could address a larger reading public while facing the removal of politically suspect passages. A critic might admire European individualism yet accept assumptions about colonial hierarchies. When examining a work, its date of composition, magazine of first publication, intended readership, and later republication should all be established before assigning it a place in the history of democratic or authoritarian ideas. That method will guide the discussion of Mingei, literary experimentation, and religious association.

Section 72.2 · Division I

Industrial Modernity and the Search for Cultural Meaning

Industrialization changed what people made, who made it, and how it was valued. Factory-made ceramics, textiles, glass, and household goods became cheaper and more uniform, and rural artisans who had produced for local use faced competition from mass-produced goods transported by railways. At the same time, the modern city created new consumers of objects and images. Chapter 63 described the labor system, and Chapter 70 described the commercial culture of department stores and advertising. This section asks how these changes affected ideas of authenticity and creativity.

The Meiji state had encouraged craft production for export, and it had separated "fine art" (bijutsu), judged by individual genius, from "craft" (kōgei) and "industrial arts," judged by skill and market. The hierarchy was borrowed from European academies but also reflected Japanese practices of connoisseurship, including the elite tea culture described in Chapter 38. By the early twentieth century, critics worried that export crafts had become ornamental and mechanical, and they looked to foreign models. The English Arts and Crafts movement, associated with John Ruskin and William Morris, offered an influential argument that industrial production degraded both workers and objects. Yanagi and others read these writers, but adapted them rather than copying them.

Intellectual unease about industrial modernity took several forms. Some intellectuals idealized preindustrial village life, an idealization that Chapter 69 shows was far from the experience of tenant farmers. Some argued that art should serve working people and that class conflict was the central problem. Some turned inward and explored the instability of perception and identity. Some sought spiritual renewal in religious movements. All, however, relied on modern institutions: magazines, publishers, exhibitions, universities, railways, and an urban audience. The movements studied in this chapter were not retreats from modernity but internal critiques of it.

The effects of industrial production were neither uniform nor instantaneous. Village potteries often combined household agriculture, seasonal employment, inherited kiln knowledge, and sales to merchants; machine-made substitutes competed with some wares while railway links opened new markets for others. The ability to make a vessel cheaply did not explain its form by itself. Clay composition, fuel costs, glazing methods, and the expectations of customers determined what could be produced. Nor was a handmade pot necessarily a relic: it might be manufactured in quantity for urban distribution. By treating every workshop as preindustrial, cultural critics risked obscuring how craftspeople actually made their living.

The opposition between autonomous fine art and practical craft also depended on institutions. Art schools, government-sponsored exhibitions, department-store galleries, and collectors rewarded recognizable artistic names. Objects without an attributed maker were less likely to enter art histories, yet they were present in ordinary houses and markets. Mingei critics reversed the hierarchy of aesthetic value, but often used the same modern infrastructure—catalogs, photographic reproductions, exhibitions, and affluent patrons—that had privileged signed artworks. Meanwhile the proletarian writer asked a different question: not whether a commodity was beautiful, but who controlled its production and received the return from its sale. These contrasting approaches should be compared as historically situated responses to changing work.

Cause and Effect

Industrialization and the Revival of Craft Values

Long-term causes

The Meiji division between fine art and craft; export-oriented industrial arts; the arrival of Arts and Crafts ideas; the growth of factory production and rail transport; and an educated urban public interested in aesthetics.

Short-term causes

Wartime growth and postwar recession; rapid urban growth; the circulation of Korean, Chinese, and European craft objects; the Shirakaba circle's interest in individual expression; and the personal encounters of Yanagi with Asakawa Noritaka and Bernard Leach.

Event or transformation

Intellectuals redefined handmade everyday objects as sources of beauty, and a named movement, Mingei, emerged in 1925 to 1926.

Immediate effects

Collecting trips, exhibitions, a journal, workshops and a museum; craft production in Mashiko, Kyoto, Tottori, and elsewhere acquired new audiences.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The movement shaped postwar craft and design, but it also turned inexpensive objects into prestige goods and entangled craft with colonial and commercial relationships (Section 72.13). Industrialization did not "cause" craft revival mechanically: the movement depended on individuals, patrons, and contingent encounters. It is also misleading to treat the movement as simply anti-industrial, since its supporters relied on modern transportation, publishing, and wealth.
Section 72.3 · Division I

Shirakaba, Humanism, and the New Artistic Individualism

The Shirakaba ("White Birch") circle began in 1910 around a magazine founded by young men who had studied at the Gakushūin, the school for the aristocracy. Its leading figures included Mushanokōji Saneatsu (1885–1976), Shiga Naoya (1883–1971), Arishima Takeo (1878–1923), Arishima Ikuma, Satomi Ton, and Yanagi Sōetsu. The magazine continued until 1923. Its members were privileged, and their privilege gave them leisure and access to European books, but it also made them targets of criticism from writers who regarded them as insulated from social conflict.

Shirakaba was important for its enthusiasm for individuality and for European art. The magazine published special issues and reproductions on Rodin, Cézanne, Van Gogh, and other European artists, and in 1912 the sculptor Rodin sent a few works to the group in appreciation, a gift that members treated as a major event. The group admired Tolstoy and Whitman and held an ethical view of art: authentic expression, they believed, was an affirmation of human dignity. Mushanokōji's fiction, such as Omedetaki hito (1911), celebrated naive self-confidence. Shiga's short, precise stories, including "Kinosaki ni te" (1917), became models for later autobiographical prose, and his novel An'ya kōro ("A Dark Night's Passing"), serialized over many years from 1921, is regarded as a major work of the period.

Humanism was not only an aesthetic position. Arishima Takeo, who had studied in the United States and been influenced by Christianity and socialism, wrote novels such as Aru onna (1911–1913), about a woman who defies convention, and in 1922 he gave up his inherited Hokkaido farm to its tenants. He died in 1923. Mushanokōji founded the community "Atarashiki mura" (New Village) in Hyūga in 1918, a communal experiment in which members worked and pursued creative activity. These efforts reveal the difficulty of reconciling personal freedom with social obligation, a theme that recurs in later divisions. Yanagi's own work for the magazine on William Blake and religion, and his interest in craft, grew out of the same ethical aesthetics.

Historians treat Shirakaba as both a liberating and a limited movement. It opened Japanese readers to European modernism and defended individual feeling against social conformity. It also assumed that cultivated individuals could define beauty. Later critics, including proletarian writers, argued that its humanism ignored class, while Mingei would replace the individual genius with the anonymous craftsman. The group is thus a starting point for the debates examined in the rest of the chapter.

Shirakaba was not a school with a single manifesto. The members' literary judgments diverged, and the meaning of self-cultivation was repeatedly debated. Mushanokōji's experiment at Atarashiki-mura, the "New Village" established in 1918, attempted to create a community in which artistic work and cooperative living could be pursued together. Its practical difficulties exposed the distance between moral aspiration and the economic conditions of farming. Shiga's concentration on observation and self-examination had a different character: in his short fiction, what is withheld, noticed, or quietly acknowledged may be as important as overt action. Arishima's work explored conscience, privilege, and the position of a person attempting to act ethically within a stratified society.

The magazine's printed reproductions of European painting were themselves a cultural technology. A reader who might never visit a museum in Europe could encounter images and arguments concerning Rodin, Cézanne, or van Gogh through a Japanese-language journal. Yet reproduction also stripped artworks from their original settings and made them part of a new intellectual mythology of personal genius. Yanagi later made a striking turn: the importance he once assigned to the exceptional individual would be qualified by his admiration for makers whose names had disappeared. The contrast does not mean he abandoned the movement's concern with ethical beauty; it shows how a shared concern could lead to different theories of authorship.

Section 72.4 · Division I

Literary Societies, Artistic Circles, and the Culture of Debate

Modern Japanese literary and artistic life was organized through small groups. Writers formed coterie magazines (dōjin zasshi) funded by members, such as Shirakaba, Mita Bungaku, and later Bungei Jidai. Commercial magazines such as Chūō Kōron, Kaizō, and Shinchō offered payment and wider audiences, and Kikuchi Kan's Bungei Shunjū, founded in 1923, became an influential monthly. Between these two levels lay the bundan, the informal network of established writers, editors, and critics that conferred reputation. Chapter 70 explained the mass market for newspapers and magazines; here the emphasis is on how ideas were argued and circulated inside it.

Translation provided material for argument. Writers read Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Chekhov, Baudelaire, Rimbaud, Proust, Joyce, and the German Expressionists, sometimes in editions published within a few years of their appearance abroad. Translators such as Mori Ōgai and Ueda Bin had established a tradition in the Meiji period, and many young writers, including Akutagawa and Kawabata, studied foreign literature at the university. Reception was selective: Japanese authors adapted European techniques to local subjects. Art circles worked similarly, importing reproductions, exhibitions, and books, and sending students to Paris, Berlin, and London.

Criticism was itself a creative field. Critics and writers disputed realism, naturalism, formalism, and Marxist criteria, and the debates sometimes took the form of exchanges across magazines. Patronage mattered: wealthy supporters funded salons and exhibitions, and publishers subsidized experimental journals. Women's participation was expanding but restricted, as Division IV explains. This environment gave cultural movements a social structure. Mingei grew through a network of collectors and potters; MAVO through exhibition and publication; new religions through magazines and lecture meetings. The map study below helps students visualize these networks.

The dōjin zasshi, or coterie magazine, joined sociability to publication. Contributors shared editing costs, selected material, reviewed one another's work, and often used the printed issue to establish a collective identity before commercial publishers took notice. The material conditions were precarious: paper prices, distribution networks, printing contracts, and the availability of advertising could determine whether a journal lasted beyond a few issues. Editors also made consequential choices about whom to invite. A first publication in a circle associated with influential men could establish a career; exclusion could leave women, colonial writers, and working-class authors dependent on alternative networks.

The circulation of a literary text did not end with its appearance in print. A story might first be debated within a small circle, then reprinted in a wider magazine, reviewed by critics, and finally included in a collected edition with revisions. Translation followed a similar sequence, often through an intermediary language and under the conventions of the Japanese literary market. A rigorous account of cultural experimentation must therefore ask who introduced a foreign work, how it was translated, which passages or ideas attracted attention, and how readers adapted them to local disputes. These networks explain how a controversial technique or theory could travel rapidly without every participant meeting its original author.

Section 72.5 · Division I

Cultural Experimentation after the Great Kantō Earthquake

Chapter 71 described the earthquake of September 1, 1923, the fires, the violence against Koreans and others, and the reconstruction of Tokyo. This section considers only its effects on cultural life. The disaster destroyed publishers, printing works, bookshops, and studios in Tokyo and Yokohama, and many writers and artists relocated, at least temporarily. Shirakaba, which had been in decline, ceased publication in 1923. Tanizaki Jun'ichirō moved to the Kansai region, and his move coincided with a shift toward Kansai settings and traditional aesthetics in his work, though he had been exploring such themes earlier.

The disaster also stimulated experiments. Artists associated with MAVO took part in decorating temporary barracks and shops, a project that blurred the line between art and everyday life, and the architect and ethnographer Kon Wajirō and his colleagues documented the improvised shelters and developed "modernology" (kōgengaku), a method of recording contemporary urban life. Reconstruction projects favored modern reinforced-concrete architecture and new housing forms, and Chapter 70 described the commercial reorientation of the city. Writers responded with reportage, essays, and fiction, and some, including Akutagawa, wrote about the experience with a mixture of detachment and unease.

Historians debate how much the earthquake transformed culture. Some argue that it accelerated the shift from Tokyo's older commercial districts toward a more modern, American-inflected metropolis and promoted the avant-garde. Others emphasize continuity: the artistic movements studied here began before 1923, and many of their characteristic forms had already appeared. The sensible position is that the earthquake provided a catalyst, a rupture in institutions, and a shared experience that writers and artists interpreted in very different ways, including the killing of the anarchist Ōsugi Sakae and Itō Noe in its aftermath (Chapter 69), which signaled the danger facing radicals. Students should avoid treating the earthquake as the single cause of the cultural change of the 1920s.

The earthquake disrupted the practical institutions of culture: printing presses and stocks of paper were destroyed, manuscripts and artworks lost, and publishers had to find substitute premises. A bookstore's reopening could depend on the restoration of transport and credit just as much as on readers' interest. Writers and artists whose homes were ruined encountered displacement alongside clerks, artisans, and factory workers. In that sense cultural history cannot isolate aesthetic reactions from the physical destruction of workplaces and neighborhoods. Nor was the transition from disaster to reconstruction politically neutral: the emergency repression and massacres examined in Chapter 71 exposed the vulnerability of Koreans, Chinese residents, and political dissidents.

Artists did not respond with one common style. Some emphasized grotesque or fractured images, others the rebuilding of everyday surroundings; some wrote accounts of uncertainty rather than confident reports. The turn toward Kansai settings in Tanizaki's writing illustrates why a simple earthquake-caused explanation is inadequate: relocation changed the environment in which he worked, while preexisting interests in language and Japanese aesthetics continued to develop. For MAVO, the temporary city offered materials and sites for experimentation, but the group's international avant-garde influences predated the disaster. Students should distinguish a change in opportunity or location from a change in artistic philosophy.

Section 72.6 · Division II — The Mingei Movement: Folk Craft and the Reinvention of Tradition

Yanagi Sōetsu, the Asakawa Brothers, and the Discovery of Korean Ceramics

Yanagi Sōetsu was born in 1889 into an elite family and studied at the Gakushūin, where he came under the influence of the Buddhist scholar Suzuki Daisetsu (D. T. Suzuki). He became a member of the Shirakaba circle, writing on religion, on William Blake, and on Western art, and he married the contralto Kaneko, who supported his work. Before about 1914 his interests were largely in European art and religious philosophy. The shift toward craft came through a series of encounters. He met the English potter Bernard Leach in 1911, in the Shirakaba milieu, and in 1914, according to standard accounts, Asakawa Noritaka visited him with a Joseon ceramic piece. Yanagi was struck by the object's character, and he began to ask why a utilitarian piece made by an unidentified craftsman should give so much aesthetic satisfaction.

Asakawa Noritaka (1884–1964) and his younger brother Asakawa Takumi (1891–1931) lived in Korea as members of the Japanese colonial community, and they had deep knowledge of Korean craft. Takumi worked for the Government-General's forestry administration, learned Korean, and studied Joseon pottery and everyday objects directly in markets and villages. He wrote books on Korean tableware and ceramics that remain important, and his collaboration with Yanagi was crucial: he provided knowledge of Korean language, regional production, and the social uses of objects. Takumi died young, in 1931, and Yanagi mourned him publicly, a sign of how seriously Yanagi regarded the partnership. Noritaka also collected and later wrote about Korean ceramics. Historians now give the Asakawas a larger place in the story than earlier accounts that treated Yanagi as the sole discoverer.

Yanagi visited Korea in 1916 and returned repeatedly. He studied Joseon white porcelain, stoneware, and furniture, and he came to believe that Korean craft embodied qualities of simplicity, naturalness, and unselfconscious beauty. His first major works on the subject appeared in the early 1920s. In retrospect, the encounter with Korean ceramics preceded and shaped his later interest in Japanese craft: he applied to Japanese rural objects the criteria he had developed with Korean ones. Section 72.7 and Section 72.13 examine the colonial context in which that happened, which included Yanagi's admiration, the Asakawas' expertise, and the unequal power of the Japanese state.

The 1914 encounter has often been narrated as a moment of revelation, but its retrospective symbolism should be kept separate from what can be documented about the network. The Asakawa brothers' sustained study of ceramics, kilns, and regional customs provided knowledge that a single visit or gift could not supply. Noritaka's engagement with Korean ceramics and Takumi's practical work in forestry and craft helped bring Yanagi into contact with locally made things that elite Japanese collectors had rarely classified as fine art. They were not simply anonymous intermediaries in another man's discovery. Later scholarship has recovered their own collecting, writing, and institutional contributions.

Joseon white porcelain presents a useful historical problem. Plain jars and bowls came from a long tradition marked by the resources and social hierarchies of Korean kingdoms; their apparent simplicity did not make them timeless expressions of a national temperament. Different kilns served courtly and common markets, and distinctions of glaze, firing, form, and clay matter. Yanagi's readings responded to the shape of these objects, but he also endowed them with ethical and emotional meanings. Students can compare a dated museum catalog with Yanagi's aesthetic description: the former asks who made and used a vessel and how it was produced; the latter asks what qualities of beauty it seemed to embody. Both are evidence, but for different questions.

Section 72.7 · Division II

Korean Folk Art, Colonialism, and the Seoul Museum

Japan had made Korea a protectorate in 1905 and annexed it in 1910 (Chapter 62). In 1919 a mass nonviolent movement for independence, the March First Movement, was suppressed with force. In May 1919 Yanagi published a newspaper essay addressed to the Korean people, written in sympathy, which criticized Japanese militarism and the suppression of Korean aspirations. Few Japanese intellectuals spoke so openly. In 1922 he published an essay protesting the planned demolition of Gwanghwamun, the main gate of Gyeongbokgung palace, to make room for the Government-General building, and the essay was also circulated in Korean translation. The gate was eventually moved rather than destroyed, and historians commonly credit Yanagi's protest, along with Korean opposition, as one factor, though the causes are debated.

Yanagi and the Asakawas also created an institution. The Korean Folk Art Museum (Chōsen Minzoku Bijutsukan) opened in 1924 in a building within Gyeongbokgung palace in Seoul, displaying Joseon ceramics, furniture, and crafts collected by Yanagi and the Asakawas. Funds came from donations and from benefit concerts that Kaneko performed. The museum served as a model for Yanagi's later plans for a Japanese museum. It also raised an uncomfortable question: whose heritage was being preserved, and by whom? A Japanese-run museum in the Korean royal palace, under a colonial government, collected and interpreted Korean objects at the moment when Korean culture was subject to political suppression.

Yanagi's writing about Korean art also invited criticism. He characterized Korean art, especially its line, as expressing a "beauty of sorrow," a view that later Korean and Japanese scholars have analyzed as reflecting a colonial gaze that equated Korean culture with suffering and passivity. He understood himself as defending Korean culture, and Korean intellectuals of the time reacted in varied ways, some welcoming his protest and some resisting his interpretations. Scholars such as Kim Brandt and Yuko Kikuchi have shown that the movement's roots in Korea cannot be separated from Japan's imperial position. The primary source window below introduces a paraphrase of Yanagi's 1919 essay; students should read translated texts and critical studies.

The museum's location within the grounds of Gyeongbokgung Palace made its cultural position especially complex. A palace associated with the Korean monarchy had been transformed under Japanese colonial administration, and a museum authorized within that setting did not exist outside the structures of colonial power. The museum could make Korean objects visible and preserve examples that might otherwise be dispersed; at the same time, display decisions, acquisition channels, and the language of interpretation depended heavily on Japanese collectors and officials. Korean artists and scholars were not merely passive subjects of those choices. Their reactions, including criticism of how Korean culture was represented, are indispensable to assessing the institution.

Yanagi's interventions on behalf of Korean culture should be evaluated as specific acts rather than used to pronounce a single verdict on his politics. In the aftermath of the March First Movement, criticism of repression carried real significance in metropolitan Japanese discourse. Yet a defense of aesthetic achievement could coexist with an assumption that Korea's cultural character was principally sorrowful or that a Japanese interpreter was especially qualified to define it. The distinction is analytical: an argument against violent colonial policy is not automatically an argument for Korean political independence. Read the 1919 essays together with contemporary Korean responses and the later texts on Korean artistic form before concluding where Yanagi's criticism began and ended.

Section 72.8 · Division II

The Birth of Mingei: Defining the Beauty of Ordinary Things

The word mingei, a contraction of minshūteki kōgei ("craft of the people"), was coined in 1925. According to the standard account, Yanagi, with Hamada Shōji and Kawai Kanjirō, formulated the term while traveling to examine Buddhist wooden images by the itinerant monk Mokujiki Gogyō (1718–1810), whose work Yanagi had been studying since 1924. Accounts of the precise itinerary should not be treated as settled without the contemporary travel records. The term gave a name to an interest that had been developing for years. In 1926 Yanagi, Hamada, Kawai, and the potter-scholar Tomimoto Kenkichi's circle prepared a prospectus for the establishment of a Japan Folk Crafts Museum, and this document, issued under Yanagi's name with the others, defined the movement's program.

The definition rested on a series of contrasts. Mingei objects were useful, made for ordinary people, plentiful and inexpensive, made by hand, and made in regional traditions by artisans who did not sign their work. They were distinguished from bijutsu (fine art) created by individual artists for display, from aristocratic craft such as the elaborately decorated objects made for elite patrons, and from the standardized products of factories. The Mingei vocabulary also distinguished the objects' beauty from the maker's intention: the craftsman did not aim at beauty, and the beauty emerged from honest use of materials and inherited technique.

This formulation was a historical construction. It selected a category of objects, organized them under a new name, and gave the movement a mission of discovery, collection, and education. The category's boundaries were chosen by educated Tokyo intellectuals, and many objects that fit its description had been regarded as unremarkable. Equally, the term implied that "folk" craft was disappearing and needed protection. Whether it was disappearing or changing is a historical question: rural craft had always adapted to markets and materials. The prospectus therefore reveals both the movement's aims and the assumptions of its authors.

The naming of a movement involved more than a pleasing abbreviation. In classical connoisseurship and modern exhibition systems, objects were arranged by workshop lineage, artist, material, school, or imperial cultural prestige. Calling them the "craft of the people" reorganized the archive by social use and the presumed status of makers. It implied that commonplace containers, baskets, and work textiles could be historically and aesthetically important without being rare masterpieces. The founders' 1926 prospectus translated this claim into a program of acquisition, comparison, and public education. The eventual museum belonged to the age of modern collecting, not to an uninterrupted institution inherited from village society.

The supposed oppositions had practical exceptions. Some acclaimed Mingei objects were luxury purchases for elite households; others were unsigned because their makers' names had not been recorded by dealers, not because the workers lacked identity. Handmade manufacture and market-oriented production could coexist. Similarly the founders themselves were educated, named practitioners whose artistic reputation helped make the unsigned objects visible. The most productive historical question is therefore not whether a vessel perfectly satisfies a fixed set of criteria, but how the criteria reshaped taste, influenced production, and changed the economic value of objects after they entered museum collections and urban markets.

Section 72.9 · Division II

The Philosophy of Beauty in Use and Anonymous Craftsmanship

Mingei theory rested on a few central ideas. The most important was yō no bi, "the beauty of use." Objects made for daily use, Yanagi argued, achieved beauty because their form followed function, because makers repeated proven forms, and because use polished them over time. The corollary was that beauty did not require decoration or the expression of individual genius. Another central idea was anonymity. The craftsman who made a bowl in a rural kiln did not seek fame, and the lack of self-consciousness, Yanagi believed, allowed beauty to emerge. Yanagi's book Kōgei no michi ("The Way of Craft," 1928) elaborated these views.

The theory drew on religious sources. Yanagi had studied Buddhism with Suzuki Daisetsu and was drawn to the Pure Land tradition's emphasis on reliance on "other power" (tariki), the idea that salvation comes through the grace of Amida rather than through individual effort. He extended this to craft: the anonymous artisan relied on tradition and materials rather than personal ingenuity, and beauty came as a gift rather than an achievement. He also admired the Lotus and Zen traditions and the carvings of Mokujiki. Later in his life his writings emphasized the religious dimension of beauty more directly. These ideas combined with Western sources, including Ruskin and Morris, and with Yanagi's study of Blake.

The theory has been criticized for several reasons. Artisans were not anonymous to those who knew them; workshops had masters, apprentices, and family networks. The belief that craftspeople worked without self-awareness may reflect the viewer's assumptions rather than the maker's experience. And the movement's own practice contradicted the doctrine of anonymity, because its celebrated potters, Hamada, Kawai, and Leach, were individual artists who signed their work. Yanagi argued that studio potters could work in the spirit of the folk tradition, but the tension remained. The theory, nevertheless, changed how many people looked at objects.

A close examination of "beauty in use" should begin with a particular object. A kitchen bowl has to be stable, easy to handle, suited to its purpose, and capable of surviving repeated cleaning; those requirements constrain its dimensions and surface treatment. Its final form nevertheless depends on choices made by workers, merchants, and users. The aesthetic interpretation that function produces beauty is thus a proposition about a relationship between craft practice and appearance, not a laboratory law. It may be persuasive when examining a robust jar, yet less useful for explaining ceremonial objects or textiles prized for decoration. The category can be tested by objects that resist its rules.

Yanagi's religious language added another dimension. Later formulations drew analogies between the craftsperson's apparently unselfconscious labor and Buddhist ideals of freedom from egotism, especially Pure Land concepts. Those analogies had their own intellectual history, and their full elaboration should not be projected back unchanged onto every statement of the 1920s. The theory also created an ethical difficulty: anonymous excellence was praised by writers whose names were preserved in every museum catalog, while women and workshop assistants might disappear from the record. A history of the movement must therefore document kiln labor, attribution, and remuneration as carefully as it discusses aesthetics.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Mingei and Related Terms

Mingei is a contraction of minshūteki kōgei, "popular" or "folk" craft, coined in 1925. It does not mean all traditional Japanese craft, and it does not mean tourist souvenirs. It refers to a movement and a canon defined by Yanagi and his associates. Yō no bi means "beauty of use." Kōgei means "craft" or "industrial art" and was also the title of Yanagi's journal (1931 onward). Bijutsu is the Meiji-era term for "fine art." Mumei means "nameless" or "unsigned" and describes the anonymous maker in Mingei theory. Tariki ("other power") is the Pure Land idea that Yanagi applied to craft.

The English phrase "folk art" is a partial translation: the Japanese movement was concerned with crafted objects of daily life, not necessarily with art made by amateurs, and its "folk" (min) was a choice made by intellectuals, not a self-description by craftspeople.

Hamada Shōji, Kawai Kanjirō, and Bernard Leach

Hamada Shōji (1894–1978) studied ceramics at the Tokyo Higher Technical School and worked at the Kyoto Municipal Ceramics Laboratory, where he met Kawai Kanjirō. After meeting Leach and Yanagi he traveled with Leach to England in 1920 and helped build the Leach Pottery at St Ives in Cornwall, where they constructed a climbing kiln of Japanese type. Hamada returned to Japan in 1923 and in 1924 settled in Mashiko, a town in Tochigi Prefecture with a local ceramic tradition. There he developed a workshop using local clay, stones, and ash glazes, and he combined local and Korean, Chinese, and English influences. He visited Okinawa's Tsuboya pottery district in the years around 1918 and later, and he admired the Okinawan yachimun tradition. Mashiko became the center of folk pottery associated with Mingei.

Kawai Kanjirō (1890–1966), born in Shimane, trained in ceramic chemistry and worked in Kyoto. He achieved fame in the early 1920s with technically brilliant works inspired by Chinese ceramics, and after coming under Yanagi's influence he turned away from showy technique toward simpler forms and bold glazes, and later to sculpture. His workshop in the Gojōzaka district of Kyoto, which he operated for decades, trained assistants and became a place for visitors. Kawai's career illustrates the movement's paradox: a highly trained individual, praised for technique, who adopted the vocabulary of anonymity.

Bernard Leach (1887–1979) was born in Hong Kong, studied art in London, and came to Japan in 1909. He learned raku pottery in Tokyo and became a friend of Yanagi and the Shirakaba circle. At St Ives he combined English slipware traditions with Japanese and Korean influences, and his book A Potter's Book (1940) shaped studio pottery in Britain and America. The exchanges among Leach, Hamada, and Yanagi were reciprocal: Leach introduced Western craft ideas and techniques, and Japanese potters offered an aesthetic and technical vocabulary that Leach adapted. Historians treat this as an example of international movement of craft ideas rather than a one-way transfer.

The workshop was a site of technical experimentation rather than merely an expression of philosophy. Hamada's training gave him detailed knowledge of glaze chemistry and kiln practice. In St Ives, he and Leach faced the problem of adapting Japanese firing techniques to different fuels, clay bodies, equipment, and customers. Early firings were inconsistent; learning through failed batches was central to the exchange. Leach's later accounts helped translate Japanese craft ideals for British readers, but the exchange was collaborative and depended on practical help from other potters. It should not be narrated as a single teacher bringing an unchanged Japanese tradition to Europe.

Kawai's Kyoto studio makes a complementary case. His earlier, highly polished decorated ware had already shown formidable technical skill before he joined Yanagi's intellectual project. Retaining that skill while adopting simplified shapes and vigorous glaze effects complicates the notion that desirable craft must be made without artistic self-consciousness. The named studio artist could cultivate an appearance of spontaneity that was itself technically demanding. Comparison of the early and later pieces therefore reveals artistic choices, not a simple decline from fine art into anonymous folk production. The movement's actual members worked at the boundary between professional artistry and the idealized life of the unnamed craftsman.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Craft Workshop and Its Labor

A composite reconstruction of patterns documented in histories of Mashiko and in studio-pottery scholarship. It describes no specific workshop.

A pottery workshop such as Hamada's in Mashiko depended on more than a master. Clay had to be dug, weathered, and prepared; wood or other fuel had to be gathered; the kiln, which might be fired for several days, required teams of workers; and apprentices learned techniques by working beside experienced potters. Household members helped with preparation and sales. A workshop's output included both the pieces associated with the master and many ordinary vessels made by assistants and local potters. Visitors came to buy and to learn, and the town's potters adapted to new customers who wanted "mingei" pieces.

This picture complicates the theory of anonymous craft. The labor of assistants, local potters, and household members was essential but seldom credited, and the market created by Mingei collectors changed what some potters made. Historians therefore read craft workshops as places where inherited technique, personal artistry, family labor, and commerce were intertwined.

Section 72.11 · Division II

Regional Crafts, Textiles, Ceramics, Woodwork, and Everyday Objects

Mingei's collecting trips covered much of Japan. Members traveled to rural kilns, textile districts, and villages and wrote about what they found. The objects included ceramics from regional kilns such as Tamba and Onta, indigo-dyed and woven textiles, baskets, lacquerware, woodwork, folk paintings such as Ōtsu-e, and carvings such as Mokujiki's. Local production systems varied: some were household crafts practiced by farm families in the off-season, some were village kilns shared by several families, and some were commercial workshops supplying regional markets. The movement's attention could bring new customers, new designs, and sometimes revival of declining crafts, as in Tottori, where Yoshida Shōya opened a shop and supported local production in the early 1930s.

Different regions and peoples should not be treated as interchangeable. Korean craft was produced in a society under colonial rule, with its own kilns, markets, and furniture traditions. Okinawan crafts, including Tsuboya pottery, bingata stencil-dyed cloth, and banana-fiber textiles, arose from Ryukyu's history as a kingdom with extensive trade, and the Mingei group's interest in them deepened in the late 1930s, when Yanagi and his associates visited Okinawa. Ainu crafts, including elm-bark-fiber garments and carved objects, were made within Ainu communities for ritual and household use and were also collected by outsiders. Mingei advocates tended to place such regional traditions under the general idea of folk beauty, which risked obscuring their different histories, and the later chapters on Okinawa and Hokkaido return to these questions.

The relationship between craft and household was gendered. Textile production, in many regions, was women's work, and women's skill produced some of the most admired Mingei objects. Yet commentary by Mingei writers seldom named the weavers. Similarly, the male potters who became famous worked alongside family members and assistants. Where evidence permits, historians now reconstruct the labor of women, apprentices, and regional communities, using local records, oral histories, and the objects themselves.

Regional objects can be used to reconstruct economic geography. A kiln's production depended on local clay and timber supplies; a cloth depended on fibers, dye plants, household tools, and the organization of women's labor. Transport determined how far finished goods could travel, and wholesalers frequently mediated the relationship between maker and consumer. A curator's label that identifies only a region and an attractive pattern can hide these networks. When comparing a ceramic bottle from Mashiko with cloth woven in Okinawa, students should first examine differences in materials, labor, users, and distribution. Their presence in a single Mingei display does not imply a single social or political history.

The wider empire further complicates the movement's regional map. Objects from colonial Korea were acquired under conditions different from those of ceramics purchased in a Japanese market town. Ainu handiwork came out of communities subjected to assimilation and dispossession, not simply peripheral branches of national Japanese folk art. Okinawan vessels and textiles likewise had histories shaped by Ryukyuan institutions, annexation, and commercial ties. The lesson is methodological: write labels that make makers and conditions visible, distinguish the date of manufacture from the date of collection, and record where an object's history cannot be established. Recovering regional craft requires work beyond celebrating its appearance.

Section 72.12 · Division II

Collecting, Exhibitions, Publishing, and the Japan Folk Crafts Museum

Mingei became an institution through collecting, exhibition, publication, and patronage. Collecting began informally: Yanagi and associates bought objects on their travels and displayed them in homes and temporary exhibitions. In 1927 and afterward the group organized exhibitions in Tokyo and elsewhere, and they sold craft through shops, a practice that gave artisans income and the movement revenue. In 1931 Yanagi began the journal Kōgei, which published essays on craft, regional reports, and photographs and shaped a community of readers and contributors. In 1934 the Japan Mingei Association (Nihon Mingei Kyōkai) was formed.

The museum required money and land. The Kurashiki industrialist Ōhara Magosaburō, owner of the textile company Kurashiki Boseki and founder of an art museum and of the Ōhara Institute for Social Research that Chapter 69 introduced, provided major funds. The Japan Folk Crafts Museum (Nihon Mingeikan) opened in 1936 in the Komaba district of Tokyo, in a building whose design Yanagi supervised, and it displayed objects chosen according to his criteria. Other figures also joined: the printmaker Munakata Shikō and the textile dyer Serizawa Keisuke, whose stencil work developed within the movement, became prominent. The museum thus completed a transition from informal aesthetic judgment to a formal movement with a publication, a membership, a patron, and a building.

Patronage and commerce shaped what the movement could do. Wealthy patrons made the museum possible, and sales to collectors supported workshops. At the same time, the movement's prestige could raise prices, and the goods that Mingei described as ordinary and inexpensive became desirable for urban buyers. Section 72.13 considers this transformation more critically, and Section 72.40 places it within the wider relationship between culture and commerce.

A collection became a public argument when its objects were selected and arranged for viewers. The Japan Folk Crafts Museum did not display an exhaustive inventory of all crafts in the archipelago. It exhibited examples assembled through the collecting judgments of Yanagi and his colleagues, creating patterns of similarity that could influence how visitors understood "the people." The photographic and written culture around the journal Kōgei, first issued in 1931, was equally important: essays and images carried the movement beyond those who could travel to exhibitions. By 1936 its institutional setting made a particular aesthetic vocabulary far more durable than the private taste of an isolated collector.

Institutionalization also entailed administration: fundraising, purchasing, conservation, cataloging, space for exhibitions, and decisions about educational audiences. Patronage by business figures linked the movement to the industrial economy it sometimes criticized. Named makers could obtain commissions or higher prices from urban customers, while other artisans remained outside the museum's favored categories. Later celebration of Mingei as a national style should not conceal these early choices. The historian should distinguish contemporary statements of purpose from the retrospective narrative of an institution that survived and came to symbolize the movement.

Section 72.13 · Division II

The Contradictions of Mingei: Anonymous Labor, Colonial Power, and Commercial Value

Mingei was an influential and a contradictory movement. Its celebration of ordinary objects redirected attention from elite art and helped preserve crafts that might have disappeared. Yet it also raised four problems. First, the doctrine of anonymity concealed the labor of particular people, including women, apprentices, and regional communities. Second, the idealization of rural craft overlooked rural poverty: the tenant households described in Chapter 69 produced crafts as supplements to income under pressure, not out of unselfconscious serenity. Third, collecting and exhibition transformed inexpensive utilitarian objects into valued commodities. Fourth, the movement's roots in colonial Korea and its later interest in Okinawa and Ainu craft depended on Japanese intellectuals' authority to define the value of other peoples' culture.

Scholars have analyzed these problems in detail. Studies of Yanagi's writing on Korea examine how the "beauty of sorrow" interpretation may have reinforced colonial stereotypes while also resisting colonial destruction of Korean heritage. Studies of the movement's relationship to the Japanese state note that Mingei intellectuals later participated in cultural projects of the 1930s and 1940s, as the state promoted regional crafts and national culture, and that the movement's emphasis on Japanese tradition could be adapted to nationalist purposes. At the same time, the movement's founders were not simple apologists for the state, and they resisted some of its policies. A balanced account must keep both facts in view.

The language of anonymous craftsmanship may conceal conflicts within production. A kiln often depended on apprentices, wage laborers, family members, and dealers; the fact that finished wares carried no signature did not mean that all contributors occupied equal positions. Urban collectors could raise the selling price of goods without ensuring that women spinning thread, apprentices preparing clay, or workers hauling fuel received a comparable benefit. Where account books or workshop testimony survive, they are more direct evidence about compensation than an idealizing essay. An object's beauty and the fairness of its production are distinct questions, even when cultural reformers connected them.

The colonial tension is equally concrete. Japanese collectors who defended Korean craft could still speak for Korean makers in exhibitions and publications, sometimes portraying a society in the language of timeless sorrow or aesthetic purity. Korean commentators could value preservation while rejecting such descriptions. Nor was Mingei simply an anticommercial movement: catalogues, elite collections, shop sales, and later museum status created new demand. Evaluating the movement requires keeping several judgments separate: what was preserved, who selected it, how that selection altered markets, and which communities gained authority to interpret their own past. The same evidence may support appreciation of preservation and criticism of its inequalities.

Historical Debate

Admiration or Appropriation? Colonial Assumptions in Mingei

The question: Was Yanagi's engagement with Korean (and later Okinawan and Ainu) craft primarily a respectful defense of cultures threatened by assimilation, or primarily an expression of Japanese colonial authority over the definition of beauty?

Interpretation A — A principled, if limited, defense: Some scholars emphasize that Yanagi criticized colonial violence, defended Korean heritage when many Japanese did not, worked with Korean collaborators, and helped preserve objects and traditions that might otherwise have been neglected. They argue that holding him to modern standards of decolonial thought overlooks the risks he took.
Interpretation B — Aesthetic paternalism within empire: Others argue that Yanagi's interpretation of Korean art as sorrowful and passive, his role as an arbiter of Korean culture, and the removal of Korean objects into Japanese-controlled collections reproduced colonial hierarchies, even when intended as respect. They argue that the movement's categories belonged to the imperial knowledge system.

Evidence: Both sides rely on Yanagi's essays, the records of the Korean Folk Art Museum, Korean responses, and the later history of the objects. Work by Kim Brandt, Yuko Kikuchi, and Korean and Japanese scholars suggests that the interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians understand cultural exchange within empire and whether admiration can be separated from power.

Section 72.14 · Division III — Literary Innovation, Psychological Exploration, and Social Criticism

Naturalism, the I-Novel, and the Literature of Personal Experience

Writers of the 1910s and 1920s inherited a literary establishment shaped by Naturalism. In the first decade of the twentieth century, writers influenced by Zola, Maupassant, and Ibsen advocated unadorned depiction of reality, and Tayama Katai's Futon (1907), in which a middle-aged writer confesses feelings for a young female student, became a landmark. Japanese Naturalism came to emphasize the author's own experience more than the scientific observation of society, and from it developed the shishōsetsu or "I-novel," fiction that closely follows the author's life, with the narrator's perspective and the author's identity blurred. Critics debated whether such writing was an authentic form of Japanese modern literature or a limiting convention.

The I-novel and related confessional writing expressed values that were powerful in the bundan: sincerity, authenticity of feeling, and the artist's attention to the self. Shiga Naoya's controlled, unsentimental prose gave the form a model. At the same time, other writers had misgivings about it. Some thought it narrowed literature to private life, and some thought it encouraged complacency about moral questions. Later critics, including those in the proletarian movement, argued that it neglected social structures. Scholars today emphasize that the I-novel was not a single formula; authors varied in how directly they used personal experience, and many works blurred fact and fiction deliberately.

The writers examined in the following sections can be understood in relation to this inheritance. Akutagawa, Tanizaki, and the New Sensation writers experimented with narrative form and artifice; proletarian writers shifted attention from private to collective experience; women writers used the confessional mode to claim authority over subjects that male writers had represented for them; Ainu, Korean, and Taiwanese writers confronted the problem of which language and form to use. The literary scene should be understood as a field of argument rather than a procession of schools.

The shishōsetsu was not the same thing as an unedited diary. Authors selected scenes, rearranged time, employed fictional personae, and developed conventions for making readers interpret apparent candor as literary authenticity. Confessional narrative could create an intense sense of intimacy while concealing choices about what was omitted. Critics sometimes equated the quality of writing with the moral seriousness of the author's life, which encouraged arguments over whether a beautiful novel required a truthful self. Such assumptions should not be accepted as neutral descriptions. They were part of the historical culture of literary judgment.

Naturalism had emerged earlier than the 1920s, notably in debates around Shimazaki Tōson and Tayama Katai. For the writers now entering the field, it offered both models and constraints. Akutagawa's historical transformations, Tanizaki's constructed worlds of desire, and the New Sensation School's unexpected metaphors each challenged the expectation that a serious work would transparently reveal its author's experience. Conversely, proletarian critics challenged the social exclusiveness of a literary establishment focused on personal troubles. Comparing these responses shows why formal innovation was not a matter of novelty alone: it involved competing answers to the question of whose experience deserved representation and what counted as truthful writing.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Literary Terms

Shishōsetsu (I-novel) is fiction based closely on the author's life. Bundan is the literary establishment, the network of writers, editors, and critics that conferred reputation. Jun bungaku ("pure literature") referred to serious literary writing, in contrast with taishū bungaku ("popular literature") aimed at a mass readership; the distinction was contested and commercially significant. Shinkankakuha is the New Sensation School, whose writers sought to renew literary perception. Puroretaria bungaku is proletarian literature, written from or on behalf of the working class, and often linked to political movements. Fuseji are the blanks or symbols used to replace passages removed or self-censored.

These terms are partly the vocabulary of critics who wished to defend or attack particular groups. Students should treat them as historical categories whose boundaries were argued over at the time rather than as neutral descriptions.

Section 72.15 · Division III

Akutagawa Ryūnosuke: History, Psychology, and Moral Uncertainty

Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (1892–1927) studied English literature at Tokyo Imperial University and began publishing in 1914. His story "Rashōmon" (1915) and "Hana" ("The Nose," 1916), which Natsume Sōseki praised, brought him fame. He wrote prolifically in the short-story form, drawing on medieval Japanese tale collections such as the Konjaku monogatari, on Christian materials from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and on Chinese and European sources, and retelling them with psychological and ironic emphasis. He published "Kumo no ito" ("The Spider's Thread") in the children's magazine Akai Tori in 1918.

"Yabu no naka" ("In a Grove," 1922) is perhaps the best known example of his technique. It presents conflicting testimonies about a murder, none of which can be verified, and the story's refusal to resolve the contradictions has made it a model for discussions of subjective truth. The 1950 film Rashōmon by Kurosawa Akira, which draws on both "In a Grove" and the earlier story, popularized the idea abroad. Students should distinguish the historical author from later adaptations: the film's structure and setting differ from the story, and the "Rashomon effect" is a modern concept rather than Akutagawa's own phrase.

Akutagawa traveled to China in 1921 as a correspondent for the Osaka Mainichi shinbun, and his travel writing registers both interest and the assumptions of a Japanese observer. In his last years he wrote more directly personal works, "Kappa" (1927), a satire set among water imps, and posthumously published autobiographical pieces such as "Haguruma" ("Cogwheels"). He died in July 1927, leaving a note that referred to a vague sense of unease about his future. His death was widely discussed as marking the end of an era of literary individualism. In 1929 the young Marxist critic Miyamoto Kenji won a major essay prize with a study of Akutagawa as a literature of defeat, an example of how the next generation read him. The Akutagawa Prize, established in 1935 by Kikuchi Kan, perpetuated his name.

Akutagawa's "Rashōmon" (1915) situates an ordinary person's moral choice amid the collapse of established expectations, while "In a Grove" (1922) presents incompatible accounts of the same violent event. Rather than supplying a dependable final version, the latter story draws attention to the motives and limitations of different witnesses. Its structure is useful for historians because it dramatizes a problem common to archives: several people may claim knowledge, but each account is shaped by position, interest, memory, or self-defense. The story is not a report from a historical court, and its philosophical power depends on that fictional construction. It nonetheless makes the modern anxiety about reliable evidence vivid.

Akutagawa's literary practice involved painstaking attention to older Japanese and Chinese narratives as well as Western fiction. "The Nose" (1916) transformed inherited material through irony and psychological detail; "Hell Screen" (1918) investigated artistic obsession, power, and cruelty. Later writing expressed mounting unease about intellectual life and the conditions of modern authorship, but the suicide in 1927 should not be used as a retrospective key that turns all earlier stories into symptoms. Critics debated whether his death marked the exhaustion of a certain literary individualism; that judgment is evidence of the contemporary literary field, not proof that experimentation had ended. Students should track how characterization, narrative framing, and competing viewpoints actually function in each work before explaining its historical importance.

Section 72.16 · Division III

Tanizaki Jun'ichirō: Desire, Beauty, and Cultural Ambivalence

Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (1886–1965) emerged in 1910 with "Shisei" ("The Tattooer"), a story of erotic obsession and artistic power that Nagai Kafū praised. His early work was marked by aestheticism, sensuality, and fascination with the grotesque and with women as objects and agents of desire. Through the 1910s he wrote stories, plays, and essays, and in the early 1920s he worked for the film company Taikatsu, writing scripts and exploring cinema, an experience that fed his fiction (see Chapter 65).

His novel Chijin no ai ("Naomi," serialized 1924–1925) portrays a salaryman who raises a young café waitress, Naomi, as a modern woman, and who becomes her slave. The book was a bestseller and has been read as a satire of the craze for Western fashion and the "modern girl" (Chapter 70), as a study of male masochism, and as evidence of the instability of gender roles in the city. After the 1923 earthquake Tanizaki settled in the Kansai region, and his writing turned toward older Japanese aesthetics. Tade kuu mushi ("Some Prefer Nettles," 1928–1929) depicts a couple whose marriage is failing and the husband's growing attraction to traditional Osaka puppet theater and to a culture that he associates with the old Kansai. His essay "In Praise of Shadows" (1933–1934), usually regarded as later, expresses reflections on Japanese aesthetics that are best understood as the culmination of tendencies developing in the 1920s.

Tanizaki's ambivalence toward tradition and the West illustrates the chapter's theme. He enjoyed Western fashion and cinema and also explored what he regarded as the beauty of Japan's past. Students should avoid reading this simply as a "return to Japan." Tanizaki's "tradition" was selective and aesthetic, constructed in part through contact with Western ideas, and his portrayal of women has been analyzed critically for its fetishization as well as for its insight into gender and desire.

Tanizaki's interest in erotic power and self-fashioning complicated any simple account of Western influence. In "The Tattooer" (1910), the making of a bodily image becomes an encounter between art, desire, and domination. The story is neither a straightforward endorsement of artistic freedom nor merely an illustration of moral decadence: it asks what happens when the creator's fantasy collides with the agency of the person represented. His earlier urban settings, fascination with film and spectacle, and willingness to depict unsettling desires aligned with a literary culture eager for new sensations, but he did not belong neatly to a single school.

After the earthquake, moving to the Kansai region enlarged Tanizaki's engagement with local speech, performance, architecture, and older literary conventions. "Naomi," serialized in the 1920s, stages the contradictions of aspiration toward a fashionable Westernized life through the protagonist's obsessive relationship. Its representation of a young woman's apparent independence is filtered through a male narrator whose judgments are unreliable. Reading it historically therefore requires separating the author's crafted narrative voice from the attitudes the novel examines. The later essay "In Praise of Shadows" (1933) can illuminate a developing concern with light, materials, and domestic aesthetics, but should not be imported uncritically into his earlier fiction as proof of a sudden post-1923 rejection of modern culture.

Section 72.17 · Division III

Kawabata Yasunari and the Experimentation of Early Shōwa Literature

Kawabata Yasunari (1899–1972) was orphaned young, studied literature at Tokyo Imperial University, and began publishing in the early 1920s. He was a founding contributor to Bungei Jidai and a theorist of the New Sensation School, writing essays that defended the group's renewal of perception. His novella "Izu no odoriko" ("The Izu Dancer," 1926) tells of a student traveling in the Izu peninsula who becomes attached to a young traveling entertainer. Its restraint and lyricism made it a popular success, and it shows Kawabata's attention to the transient and to the emotional associations of landscape.

In the late 1920s and early 1930s he moved between modes. Asakusa Kurenaidan ("The Scarlet Gang of Asakusa," serialized 1929–1930) evokes the entertainment district of Asakusa with a collage of episodes, an experiment in urban literature that engages the amusements and underworld discussed in Chapter 70. "Suishō genshō" ("Crystal Fantasies," 1931) used stream-of-consciousness techniques influenced by European modernism. Later in the 1930s he turned toward Yukiguni ("Snow Country"), whose first sections appeared in 1935, with a more spare and traditionally inflected style, and he won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1968. Historians caution against seeing a sharp division between "modernist" and "traditional" phases: Kawabata's engagement with classical Japanese aesthetics and with European modernism existed together from the beginning.

Kawabata's early career illustrates how the language of literary movements can conceal a writer's range. As a student and young magazine contributor, he participated in literary networks that promoted innovation in the sensory organization of prose. In "The Izu Dancer" (1926), a student's travel with itinerant performers supplies occasions for delicate observation of social distance, emotional misunderstanding, and fleeting intimacy. The work is neither simply a travelogue nor a transparent confession. Choices of viewpoint determine what the reader learns about the dancer, whose experience cannot be reduced to the educated young man's feelings. The story thus opens questions about class and mobility even when critics emphasize its lyrical quality.

Participation in the New Sensation School did not entail a permanent allegiance to a formal program. Kawabata continued to experiment with compressed imagery, discontinuous perceptions, and subtle arrangement of scenes while also pursuing older Japanese literary themes. His subsequent international reputation should be kept distinct from what his works meant to readers in the 1920s. At the time, publishing in coterie and commercial journals, exchanging criticism with Yokomitsu and others, and establishing a distinctive prose voice mattered more immediately than later awards. Close reading should compare a passage's sensory framing with its social setting: a sophisticated aesthetic technique may both disclose and partially obscure the inequalities of the world represented.

Section 72.18 · Division III

Yokomitsu Riichi and the New Sensation School

Bungei Jidai ("The Literary Age") was founded in October 1924 by younger writers, including Yokomitsu Riichi (1898–1947), Kawabata Yasunari, Kataoka Teppei, and others, with the support of Kikuchi Kan, who sponsored the magazine as a rival to proletarian and older literary movements. The critic Chiba Kameo applied the label shinkankakuha ("New Sensation School") to the group in a review of that year. The writers aimed to renew language by capturing perception directly, using unexpected metaphors, personification of objects, and rapid shifts of viewpoint. They drew on European Expressionism, Futurism, Dada, and French writers such as Paul Morand.

Yokomitsu's story "Atama narabi ni hara" ("Heads and Bellies," 1924) is famous for its opening sentences about a speeding train that depict the ordinary scene with startling metaphor. His novel Shanghai, serialized from 1928 and published in book form in 1931, set against the 1925 May Thirtieth Movement in the International Settlement, depicts a cosmopolitan colonial city and Japanese residents within it; scholars discuss it as an example of Japanese modernism's engagement with empire. "Kikai" ("Machine," 1930) is a short experimental work narrated by a worker in a nameplate workshop whose perception is dominated by suspicion and by the logic of the mechanism, and it is often cited as a landmark of Japanese psychological modernism.

The New Sensation School defended the autonomy of art against proletarian demands, but it was also a response to urban modernity: the speed, noise, and commodification of the city altered perception, and a new literature should register that. It lasted as a group only a few years. Its members moved in different directions, and some, such as Yokomitsu, later turned to national themes. The primary source window below treats the group's manifesto-like statements.

The formal experiments associated with the New Sensation School deserve attention at the level of sentences, not just movement labels. In Yokomitsu's "Heads and Bellies," transportation and bodily imagery disorient ordinary perspective; in "Machine," the workshop becomes a system of pressures and suspicions within which the narrator cannot reliably order his experience. Such writing makes perception an event rather than a neutral lens. The reader has to infer relationships from sharp images and shifts in attention, an effect different from the familiar sequence of exposition, conflict, and resolution. The technique also reveals a historical fascination with machinery, concentrated workplaces, nervous stimulation, and fractured metropolitan experience.

The internationalism of the school was selective. European modernist devices reached the Japanese literary field through translation, criticism, and other local mediators, but their use responded to conditions in Tokyo and beyond. Yokomitsu's "Shanghai" extends the problem outward: a city organized by foreign concessions, commercial movement, and revolutionary mobilization becomes a setting through which Japanese protagonists encounter the inequalities of empire. Students should not treat the novel as an objective account of China; they should ask how narrative viewpoint organizes relations among Japanese, Chinese, and other residents. The school's distinctive contribution was to make form itself an argument about modern experience, even as its members disagreed about what politics, if any, should follow.

Section 72.19 · Division III

Proletarian Literature: Work, Poverty, and Revolutionary Experience

Proletarian literature (puroretaria bungaku) arose from the labor and socialist movements described in Chapter 69. Early expressions included the magazine Tane maku hito ("The Sower," 1921), and in the mid-1920s organizations such as the Japan Proletarian Literary Federation and later the Nippon Artists' Proletarian Federation (NAPF, formed in 1928) united writers, critics, and artists. Theoretical disputes were intense: Aono Suekichi's 1926 essay argued that proletarian literature should develop a consciousness of its aims, and Kurahara Korehito advocated "proletarian realism" in 1928. The movement had to work under censorship and police surveillance, and many works circulated with blanks where the censor had intervened.

Hayama Yoshiki (1894–1945), who had worked as a sailor, wrote Umi ni ikuru hitobito ("Those Who Live on the Sea," 1926) about the harsh life of seamen. Tokunaga Sunao (1899–1958), who worked in a printing company, wrote Taiyō no nai machi ("The Street Without Sun," 1929), based on the Kyōdō Printing strike of 1926 that Chapter 69 described; the novel portrays the poverty of the workers' neighborhood and the course of the strike. Kobayashi Takiji (1903–1933) wrote Kanikōsen ("The Crab Cannery Ship," 1929), in which workers on a fishing and canning ship in the northern waters are exploited by an overseer and eventually organize resistance, and Tōseikatsusha ("The Life of a Party Member," 1933). Kobayashi was arrested and died in police custody on February 20, 1933, after torture, a fact that makes him a symbol of the repression discussed in Chapter 74.

Literary scholars have emphasized that proletarian fiction was not simply propaganda. Authors experimented with collective protagonists, documentary material, and montage, and they extended the range of literary language to include the speech and experience of workers. Critics also note limitations: the movement's writers were often intellectuals, the politicized program could constrain form, and the party-line disputes within the movement were severe. After 1933 a wave of "conversions" (tenkō) of leftists under pressure ended the organized movement, but its influence persisted in postwar literature and in the 2008 revival of interest in Kanikōsen, which is a later development.

The proletarian literary movement created institutions as well as texts. Publishing outlets such as Senki, writers' groups, and the Nippona Artista Proleta Federacio (NAPF), organized in 1928, joined literature to theater, visual art, and political activism. The names and alignments of organizations changed frequently under ideological disputes and police pressure. A novel could therefore serve several audiences at once: workers recognizing their conditions, activists seeking solidarity, and literary critics arguing about whether social commitment improved or constrained artistic form. The presence of a worker protagonist alone does not make a work a documentary record; authors used selection, fictionalization, and dramatic structure to produce an interpretation of labor.

Tokunaga Sunao's "The Street without Sun" was grounded in the 1926 Kyōdō Printing strike and connects the factory gate, union organizing, police action, and household hardship. Kobayashi Takiji's "The Crab Cannery Ship" (1929) places laborers on a vessel where geographical isolation intensifies exploitation and collective resistance. The ship, work schedule, and chain of command create a setting whose physical organization is central to the story. Comparing these works helps students distinguish the experience of an urban printing worker from that of a maritime laborer. The authors' political commitments matter, but their technical decisions—collective protagonists, shifts in viewpoint, compressed confrontations—also explain why these texts became important literary interventions.

Section 72.20 · Division III

Children's Literature and the Cultural Discovery of Childhood

In July 1918 the writer Suzuki Miekichi (1882–1936) launched the monthly Akai Tori ("Red Bird"), explicitly intended to provide children with literature of artistic quality and to counter the commercial juvenile fiction that he considered vulgar. Prominent authors contributed: Akutagawa's "Kumo no ito" appeared in its first year, and Arishima Takeo, Kitahara Hakushū, and others contributed stories or poems. Ogawa Mimei wrote fairy tales for children and for the new magazines, and Niimi Nankichi's fable "Gon gitsune" ("Gon, the Little Fox") appeared in the magazine in 1932, a later example.

The magazine also launched the dōyō movement, children's songs composed with literary lyrics by poets such as Kitahara Hakushū and Saijō Yaso and set to music by composers including Yamada Kōsaku. Rival magazines emerged, and the market for children's publishing grew along with the new middle class and expanded schooling. The movement reflected an ideal of childhood as innocent and imaginative, a conception that historians connect to urban middle-class family life and to educational reformers' emphasis on the child's own perspective, discussed in Chapter 64.

Scholars have also noted the movement's limits. The "child" in these stories was typically an urban, middle-class child; commercial publishers such as Kōdansha's Shōnen kurabu (1914) reached far larger audiences with adventure stories; and some poets, such as Saijō Yaso, later wrote patriotic songs for wartime. Children's literature thus illustrates the difficulty of separating cultural experimentation from commercial and political forces, and the importance of asking who the intended child reader was.

The cultural revaluation of childhood was visible in writing practice. Instead of treating young readers solely as recipients of moral instruction, reformers associated with Akai Tori encouraged stories, poems, and children's songs that respected the child's emotional life. The movement attracted notable writers and poets, including Kitahara Hakushū and Noguchi Ujō, and it helped establish the dōyō as a recognizable field of modern children's culture. Yet the ideal of innocent childhood was itself shaped by schooling, adult editorial choices, middle-class expectations, and the ability of families to buy magazines. The voices of rural and working children are less consistently represented.

Periodicals were not only containers for literature. Editors commissioned illustrations, selected readers' submissions, advertised publications, and shaped standards of acceptable language and feeling. A tale or song appearing in a children's magazine may later be collected and remembered as part of national culture, but its first readers encountered it in an issue alongside advertisements, pictures, editorial comment, and other works. The historian should examine that original context. This approach prevents children's literature from appearing as a spontaneous discovery of natural childhood detached from its place in educational reform and the expansion of cultural markets.

Section 72.21 · Division III

Modern Poetry, Free Verse, Symbolism, and Literary Experimentation

Modern poetry was transformed in the early twentieth century by the shift from classical forms (tanka and haiku) and early imitations of European verse toward colloquial free verse. Hagiwara Sakutarō (1886–1942) is the central figure. His collection Tsuki ni hoeru ("Howling at the Moon," 1917) used colloquial speech and sound to express anxiety, solitude, and bodily sensation, and his Aoneko ("Blue Cat," 1923) continued with a more formal, musical approach. Critics praised him as having established a modern poetic language. Takamura Kōtarō's Dōtei ("Journey," 1914), influenced by Rodin and Whitman, celebrated personal strength and love.

Other poets also stand out. Miyazawa Kenji (1896–1933), from Iwate in the northeast, privately published the collection Haru to shura ("Spring and Asura") in 1924, with poetry that drew on science, agricultural experience, and Nichiren Buddhist belief, and he gained wide recognition only after his death. Takahashi Shinkichi wrote Dada poetry in the early 1920s. In the late 1920s the journal Shi to shiron ("Poetry and Poetics," 1928) promoted modernist poetry by Haruyama Yukio, Kitagawa Fuyuhiko, and Nishiwaki Junzaburō, who drew on Surrealism and the English poets. Nakano Shigeharu and others wrote proletarian verse.

Poetry thus shows the same lines of division as prose: confessional lyricism, urban modernism, proletarian commitment, and religious or regional vision. It also shows the circulation of ideas through small magazines and private publication. Regional writers such as Miyazawa, who lived outside Tokyo's literary center, remind students that experimentation was not confined to the capital.

Hagiwara Sakutarō's Howling at the Moon (1917) challenged the expectation that modern poetry should reproduce older patterns of rhythm and diction. Colloquial language and disturbing urban or bodily imagery could make emotional estrangement into the subject of verse rather than merely adorn an established theme. Other poets adapted symbolism, modernist montage, or politically engaged free verse. Students should compare how line breaks, repeated sounds, changes of register, and unusual metaphors affect the speaking voice. A poem's formal difficulty is not just a decorative effect; it may require the reader to inhabit uncertainty or unease rather than receive an immediately paraphrasable lesson.

Poetic experimentation also circulated through performance, music, journals, and translations. In a poem published in a small journal, visual arrangement and typography may matter differently than they do in an anthology many decades later. The terms "symbolist," "proletarian," and "modernist" identify tendencies rather than mutually exclusive memberships. A poet might imitate French verse, write song lyrics, and address social inequality at different stages of a career. The chapter should therefore distinguish a historically identifiable publication community from a critical label applied afterward. This distinction is especially important when evaluating links between modern poetry and the experimental graphics of the avant-garde.

Section 72.22 · Division III

The Literary Field in Conflict: Aesthetic Freedom, Social Purpose, and Censorship

By the late 1920s literary debate centered on whether literature should serve a social purpose. Proletarian critics argued that writers must align with the working-class movement, while modernists and others defended aesthetic autonomy. The disputes were not purely abstract. Funding, publication venues, and political danger depended on the answer. In 1929 the magazine Kaizō held an essay contest in which Miyamoto Kenji's Marxist reading of Akutagawa took first place and Kobayashi Hideo's essay on "Various Designs" (Samazama naru ishō), which defended a more subjective and aesthetic criticism, took second, illustrating the range of the field. Critics sometimes crossed these lines; Kobayashi Hideo later became one of the most influential modern critics.

Censorship shaped all positions. The Home Ministry could ban publications for obscenity or for endangering public order, and the Peace Preservation Law (Chapter 69) criminalized organized activity against the national polity. Writers coped by self-censorship, by using blanks (fuseji) for risky words, by publishing in private editions, or by writing allegorically. Proletarian works were more vulnerable, but modernist and erotic works were also censored. The tightening after 1928, the mass arrests of communists, and the "conversions" of the early 1930s changed the field. The Akutagawa Prize of 1935 and the growth of mass popular fiction represented a different direction.

The arguments were sharpened by concrete disputes over labor and political organization. Proletarian critics urged literature to expose economic structures and contribute to social transformation; writers who defended aesthetic autonomy warned that political prescriptions could reduce characters to illustrations of doctrine. Neither position guaranteed a superior work. In practice, writers associated with proletarian organizations experimented with point of view and visual montage, while modernists without explicit revolutionary affiliations could depict social alienation and coercion. The debate is most productive when applied to particular stories rather than summarized as propaganda versus art.

State censorship intervened unevenly. Publishers could lose an issue, be forced to remove passages, or anticipate official objections and edit a manuscript before submission. The Peace Preservation Law targeted political associations, while publication laws and administrative powers also constrained expression. A text printed with deletion marks is evidence not only of what an author wanted to say, but of the relationship among editors, censors, and readers. The growth of a commercial readership made suppression potentially costly for businesses that relied on circulation. The debate over literary autonomy was thus embedded in a system where the market created opportunities and the state constrained the kinds of association and language that could be publicly sustained.

Historical Debate

Artistic Autonomy or Social Obligation?

The question: Should literature and art be judged by their independence from political purposes, or by their contribution to social change?

Interpretation A — Autonomy as creative freedom: Writers such as the New Sensation group argued that art discovers new forms of perception and that subordinating it to a political program destroys its purpose. Historians sympathetic to this view note the formal achievements of modernist fiction and the dangers of party discipline.
Interpretation B — Art as part of social struggle: Proletarian critics argued that literature could not be separate from class relations, that writing for the privileged avoided social reality, and that art should help workers understand and change their conditions. Historians sympathetic to this view emphasize the new subjects and audiences the movement opened.

Evidence: Debaters cite manifestos, critical essays, and works from both camps, along with censorship records. Many scholars now argue that the camps shared concerns, such as the effects of the machine and the city, and that individual works often crossed the boundary.

Why it matters: The debate shaped how Japanese writers responded to the repression of the 1930s and how historians evaluate the political responsibilities of artists.

Section 72.23 · Division IV — Women, Minorities, and Colonial Cultural Voices

Women Writers and the Struggle for Literary Authority

Women writers had a recognized place in Japanese literature since the Heian court (Chapter 19), but the modern literary market posed new barriers. The bundan was male-dominated; critics often read women's writing as confession rather than art; and women's access to higher education, publishing contacts, and unchaperoned travel was restricted. The early twentieth century saw significant change. Yosano Akiko's poetry collection Midaregami (1901) had asserted women's passion, and the journal Seitō (1911–1916), founded by Hiratsuka Raichō, had provided a forum for women's writing and for debates on the "new woman" (Chapter 64). Nogami Yaeko, Tamura Toshiko, Uno Chiyo, and others published fiction, and Yoshiya Nobuko's sentimental stories for girls, collected as Hana monogatari ("Flower Tales," 1916–1924), depicted intense relationships among girls and attracted a large readership.

Institutions for women's writing grew. The Bunka Gakuin, a school founded in 1921 by Nishimura Isaku with Yosano Akiko and others, admitted women and men, and the monthly Nyonin Geijutsu ("Women's Arts," 1928–1932), edited by Hasegawa Shigure, offered women a venue for essays, fiction, and criticism and published Hayashi Fumiko's serialized diary. Women's writing treated marriage, sexuality, independence, motherhood, and work with new frankness, though the themes were framed by the market, which often favored sensational depictions and by censorship. Scholars now stress that women writers did not form a single school: they ranged from conservative to socialist, from aesthetes to labor activists.

The struggle for literary authority was also a struggle over interpretation. Critics who described women's writing as "feminine" and emotional limited its reception, and women writers sometimes accepted, adapted, or challenged those labels. The following section examines two writers whose work centered on women's labor and mobility. Students should keep in mind that the women discussed here were mostly literate, urban women with access to publishing; the literary voices of rural and factory women, with exceptions such as accounts produced by reformers, are much harder to find.

A close study of the publication record reveals different paths to authority. Yoshiya Nobuko's fiction addressed girls and young women through forms critics once dismissed as merely sentimental; its circulation demonstrates that an audience can be historically important even when elite literary histories discount its favored genres. Nogami Yaeko developed a long career of fiction and criticism, while Uno Chiyo used relationships, urban settings, and style to challenge conventional expectations of women's narrative roles. These writers had different class backgrounds, readerships, and political beliefs. Grouping them together can illuminate shared barriers, but should not erase distinctions among their chosen literary strategies.

Editors and periodicals were decisive. Nyonin Geijutsu, launched in 1928 under Hasegawa Shigure, offered a forum where literary experiment, debates over sexuality, labor questions, and arguments about women's social responsibilities could appear alongside one another. Magazines also documented disagreement: political writers did not necessarily share an agenda with advocates of art for its own sake. A published autobiographical voice might be praised as "authentic" precisely because critics treated the author as an object of curiosity, a double standard not always imposed on male novelists. Students should ask how reviews described women's writing, who controlled payment and editing, and how the magazine's social networks helped some writers while excluding others.

Section 72.24 · Division IV

Hayashi Fumiko, Miyamoto Yuriko, and the Literature of Women's Work

Hayashi Fumiko (1903–1951) grew up in poverty, moving with her family among towns in Kyushu and the Inland Sea, worked in a succession of jobs including as a café waitress and factory worker, and moved to Tokyo. Her Hōrōki ("Diary of a Vagabond"), serialized in Nyonin Geijutsu from 1928 and published as a book in 1930, presented a fragmentary, vivid account of a poor woman's life with a frankness about hunger, work, and love that readers found startling. It became a major bestseller, and its royalties made Hayashi financially secure. She later wrote novels and stories of ordinary women's lives; her postwar fiction is outside the scope of this chapter.

Miyamoto Yuriko (1899–1951), born Chūjō Yuriko into a well-off family, published her first work, "Mazushiki hitobito no mure" ("A Flock of Poor People"), as a teenager in 1916–1917, a story based on a visit to a Fukushima village in which she saw rural poverty. She studied briefly in the United States, married and divorced, and lived with the Russian-literature scholar Yuasa Yoshiko, with whom she traveled to the Soviet Union from 1927 to 1930. Her novel Nobuko (serialized 1924–1926) fictionalizes her own marriage and search for independence. After returning to Japan she joined the proletarian movement and the Communist Party, and she married the party leader Miyamoto Kenji in 1932. She was arrested more than once in the 1930s.

Sata Ineko (1904–1998) began working as a child in a caramel factory and in restaurants, and published "Kyarameru kōjō kara" ("From the Caramel Factory") in 1928, drawing directly on her labor experience, later associating with proletarian writers. These authors differed ideologically: Hayashi was not a party member and her work is closer to personal narrative; Miyamoto and Sata moved toward Marxism. What united them was attention to women's work, poverty, mobility, and the conflicts between personal relationships and political commitment. Their writing complements the labor history of Chapter 69, where women workers appear mainly in reformers' accounts and statistics.

Hayashi Fumiko's writing about precarious employment and movement across cities should be read in relation to the actual instability of housing and work, not as a romantic myth of the suffering artist. Her serialized Hōrōki ("Diary of a Vagabond") gave literary form to experiences of poorly paid jobs, unsettled relationships, travel, and insecurity. Its episodic construction allowed changes in voice and perspective; the literary persona cannot simply be equated with every event in the author's biography. The commercial reception of the work also demonstrates that depictions of hardship circulated through a market hungry for recognizable female personalities.

Miyamoto Yuriko's trajectory was different. In works such as Nobuko, she examined relationships, female self-determination, and the constraints of family life before becoming more closely engaged with proletarian politics. Sata Ineko's accounts of women's employment brought the office, workplace, and economic dependence into fiction at a time when those experiences were often treated as marginal to "serious" literature. Comparing the three reveals how gender and class interacted without producing an identical narrative voice. Marriage and motherhood, for instance, could appear as sources of affection, economic vulnerability, social discipline, or conflict. Historical analysis should identify the situation depicted and the techniques employed instead of assuming that any single writer spoke for all working women.

Section 72.25 · Division IV

Chiri Yukie, Ainu Oral Literature, and the Politics of Cultural Preservation

Chiri Yukie (1903–1922) was born in Noboribetsu, Hokkaido, and raised in Horobetsu by her grandmother Monashinouku and her aunt Kannari Matsu (Imekanu), a Christian Ainu woman, both of whom carried knowledge of Ainu oral literature. In 1918 the linguist Kindaichi Kyōsuke, visiting Hokkaido to record Ainu language, met her and encouraged her to write down the oral epics she knew. She wrote them in romanized Ainu with Japanese translations and, in 1922, moved to Tokyo to continue the work in Kindaichi's household. She died on September 18, 1922, of heart failure at the age of nineteen. Ainu Shin'yōshū ("Ainu Songs of the Gods"), thirteen kamui yukar (songs in which gods narrate their stories), was published in 1923, with a preface by Chiri.

The work's significance is multiple. It preserved the sung performances of Ainu oral literature in a form that could be read, and its Japanese translations are often praised as literary achievements. It gave Japanese readers access to a world in which animals and other beings speak as gods. It also bears the traces of its circumstances. The Ainu were subject to Japanese assimilation policy, formalized in the Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899, which promoted agriculture and Japanese-language schooling while taking land and restricting traditions. Chiri's work was produced within that colonial situation, with support from a Japanese scholar, and its publication depended on a Japanese publishing house. Scholars ask how much of the book reflects Chiri's choices and how much Kindaichi's editorial role, and they emphasize her agency as translator and author. English-language scholarship, including translation by Kyōko Selden, has made her work known beyond Japan. Chiri's younger brother Chiri Mashiho became an important Ainu linguist.

The 1920s also saw other Ainu writing and organizing. Yamabe Yasunosuke published Ainu tales in the 1910s, and in 1930 Ainu people formed the Hokkaido Ainu Association to advocate for welfare and rights, though its history extends into later chapters. The preface of Chiri's book, the subject of the next window, is one of the best-known Ainu statements of the period.

The manuscript of Ainu Shin'yōshū was the result of multilingual intellectual labor. Chiri transcribed thirteen kamui yukar from the Horobetsu Ainu oral tradition in Latin letters, prepared parallel Japanese translations, and supplied a preface aimed at readers who did not share her linguistic and cultural background. In these performances, a nonhuman spiritual being may speak in the first person; the narratives carry ecological, social, and ceremonial knowledge whose implications cannot be captured by presenting them as quaint animal fables. Sarah M. Strong's study emphasizes the intimate family and regional settings in which Chiri learned this material, as well as the agency involved in transforming an oral tradition into a bilingual book.

The preface is itself a historical intervention. Chiri addressed an Ainu world under pressure from settlement, schooling, and assimilation and asserted the cultural worth of a tradition often dismissed by outsiders. The subsequent editorial history cannot be reduced to the statement that Kindaichi "discovered" her. His encouragement and involvement were consequential, but the linguistic choices and translations were hers. To evaluate a translated passage, students should distinguish the Ainu oral performer, Chiri as transcriber and translator, Kindaichi as scholarly mediator, and the modern translator of the Japanese or Ainu text into English. The chain of transmission is part of the evidence; it does not erase the authority of the Ainu author.

Section 72.26 · Division IV

Korean Writers, Cultural Nationalism, and Colonial Expression

Korean literary life in the 1910s and 1920s developed under colonial conditions. After the March First Movement the Government-General allowed a limited relaxation of controls, and Korean-language newspapers such as Dong-a Ilbo and Chosun Ilbo began in 1920, along with literary magazines. Censorship remained severe: issues were suppressed, and the colonial police monitored writers. Many Korean writers studied in Tokyo and absorbed Japanese literary trends. Yi Kwang-su's novel Mujeong ("The Heartless," serialized 1917) is often described as the first modern Korean novel; Yŏm Sang-sŏp and Kim Tong-in wrote naturalist and aesthetic fiction in the early 1920s; and in 1925 the Korea Artista Proleta Federatio (KAPF) formed, linked to the proletarian movement in Japan and in conflict with nationalist writers.

Questions of language were central. Writing in Korean meant using a language that the colonial state marginalized in education, and writing in Japanese could reach a larger audience but meant entering the colonizer's literary field. In the 1920s most Korean literature was written in Korean. Later developments, including Chang Hyŏk-chu's Japanese-language story that won a Japanese literary prize in 1932 and wartime pressure to write in Japanese, belong to subsequent chapters. Korean writers debated modernism and national culture, asking how to build a modern literature that was both contemporary and distinct from Japan's.

Historians caution against two simplifications: that Korean literature merely imitated Japanese models, and that it was purely a nationalist resistance. Korean writers drew on Japanese, Russian, Chinese, and European sources, and they pursued varied political and aesthetic aims. The existence of Korean-language modern literature also reminds students that Japanese colonial rule did not produce a single "colonial culture"; it produced a contested cultural field in which colonizers and colonized interacted unequally.

Literary developments under Japanese colonial rule should be anchored in identifiable genres and institutions. Korean-language newspapers and journals became important forums for education, social criticism, and literary innovation after the March First Movement, although they operated under continued censorship and surveillance. Yi Kwang-su's earlier novel Mujŏng ("The Heartless," 1917) helped establish the modern Korean novel; the prose of Kim Tong-in and poems by Kim So-wŏl show the variety of forms that developed during the following decade. Their cultural agendas differed: modern literature was not automatically nationalist, and a work could explore individual desire while also responding to colonial institutions.

Translation and travel created connections, but power was unequal. Korean students in Tokyo encountered Japanese journals and modernist thought while organizing their own associations and publishing projects. Writers could negotiate Korean, Japanese, and other languages, each with different audiences and political risks. The use of Japanese should not be treated as proof of assent to colonial rule, any more than Korean-language writing should always be categorized as direct resistance. Specific works need to be examined for author, intended audience, censorship history, and narrative perspective. Such differentiation also prevents the treatment of colonial literature as a mere footnote to metropolitan Japanese experimentation: it had its own institutions, chronologies, and intellectual controversies.

Section 72.27 · Division IV

Okinawan and Taiwanese Cultural Voices within the Japanese Empire

Okinawa had been annexed in 1879 as Okinawa Prefecture (Chapter 54, and Chapter 32 on the earlier kingdom). In the early twentieth century Okinawan scholars began to study their own history and culture. Iha Fuyū (1876–1947), whose Ko Ryūkyū ("Old Ryukyu," 1911) is often seen as founding modern Okinawan studies, argued that Ryukyuan culture was a branch of Japanese culture while valuing its distinct heritage, a position that scholars discuss as both a defense of Okinawan dignity and an accommodation to assimilation. Visitors from the mainland such as Yanagita Kunio and Orikuchi Shinobu traveled to the islands in the 1920s and used Okinawan songs and practices as evidence for theories of ancient Japanese religion, a use that again raised questions about who defined the culture. Okinawan writers and artists, including the poet Yamanokuchi Baku, who moved to Tokyo in the 1920s, wrote in Japanese while engaging their regional background. Economic hardship in Okinawa in the later 1920s, treated in Chapter 73, shaped the cultural scene.

In Taiwan, annexed in 1895 (Chapter 60), intellectuals formed the Taiwan Cultural Association in 1921 and petitioned the Diet for a Taiwanese legislature, as Chapter 69 noted. The magazine Taiwan Minpao and other publications carried literary and political writing, and Chinese-language vernacular literature developed, with Lai He (1894–1943) publishing stories in the 1920s that depicted colonial injustice, among them "Yi gan 'Cheng-zai'" ("A Scale," 1926). Writers in Taiwan faced multiple linguistic choices: Hokkien, Mandarin written vernacular, and Japanese, taught in colonial schools. Taiwanese students in Tokyo also published magazines. Taiwan's Indigenous peoples, subject to a separate colonial administration, appear in the literature mainly as objects of Japanese ethnography in this period.

These histories should not be treated as a single "minority experience." Ainu in Hokkaido, Okinawans in a prefecture of the metropole, Koreans in a colony annexed in 1910, and Taiwanese in a colony acquired in 1895 each faced different legal statuses, languages, and relationships to Japanese institutions. What they shared was the challenge of maintaining cultural agency within an unequal political order, a challenge explored in the map study below.

Okinawan scholarship developed within a complex history of annexation, language policy, and regional differentiation. Iha Fuyū's studies of Ryukyuan language and culture challenged contemptuous ideas about Okinawan history while sometimes framing its relationship to the Japanese archipelago in ways that could be recruited into assimilationist narratives. The historian must therefore attend to both his scholarship and the political uses made of it. Okinawan songs, performance, and oral narratives had value within local communities before they were collected as ethnographic specimens or recategorized as parts of a national cultural inheritance.

In Taiwan, writers associated with the emerging New Literature movement confronted education in Japanese, the availability of Chinese writing, and censorship under colonial rule. Lai He wrote fiction and poems concerned with rural society, social injustice, and the predicament of ordinary Taiwanese subjects. His work cannot be treated as simply the Taiwanese equivalent of Japanese proletarian writing; his linguistic and institutional context differed. Comparison of an Okinawan scholar, a Taiwanese writer, and an Ainu collector reveals three distinct relationships to Japanese rule. A responsible history should ask what readers could access the work, how its language functioned politically, and how later national narratives may have claimed it. These questions establish cultural agency without presenting all imperial subjects as a homogeneous minority.

Section 72.28 · Division V — Avant-Garde Art, Design, and Experimental Expression

MAVO and the Assault on Established Artistic Conventions

MAVO was formed in June 1923, with a first exhibition in July, by a group of young artists led by Murayama Tomoyoshi (1901–1977) and including Ōura Shūzō, Yanase Masamu, Kadowaki Shinrō, and Ogata Kamenosuke. Murayama had visited Berlin in 1922, where he encountered Dada, Constructivism, and Expressionism, and he brought back ideas and materials. The group's name, MAVO, was an invented word that Murayama explained in various ways, and it signaled their rejection of rational labels. They created works from found objects, wire, and scraps of material, including collages and assemblages, and they presented them with provocative performance and display. Their magazine Mavo appeared in seven issues in 1924 and 1925, and they used typography, posters, and handbills to disrupt conventional presentation.

MAVO attacked the established art world, particularly the official exhibitions and academic painting. Its members issued manifestos in a deliberately aggressive style and staged events that mixed visual art, performance, and architecture. After the earthquake they joined the decoration of improvised barracks and shops, taking art into everyday space. Their activities extended to architecture, stage design, and design for publications. The group dissolved in 1925, and its members moved on to other work, including political art for the proletarian movement. Historians such as Gennifer Weisenfeld have documented the group's wide-ranging influence.

MAVO should be examined as a set of actions, works, and publications, not simply as a Japanese variation on Dada. After forming in 1923, its members showed objects assembled from unconventional materials and used the manifesto, exhibition, street performance, and printed page to provoke audiences accustomed to the boundaries of painting and sculpture. Gennifer Weisenfeld's study emphasizes the links among artistic experimentation, consumer society, architecture, and the experience of the modern city. The group's magazine was itself an artistic object in which typography, layout, and language confronted established expectations of reading.

The movement's activism included interventions in the built environment, especially attempts to link aesthetic experiment to temporary structures and public places after the earthquake. Such activities were uneven, and a planned intervention should not be described as completed without documentation. MAVO did not speak with a single ideological voice: individual members' politics and artistic interests shifted, and the group's short life reflected the instability of avant-garde associations. Comparing dated exhibition records with later scholarly reconstructions helps distinguish the publicity surrounding deliberately provocative acts from what audiences actually saw. The historical point is that boundaries among art, advertising, architecture, and performance were being negotiated in public.

Section 72.29 · Division V

Dada, Futurism, Expressionism, and the Experimental Image

Japanese artists engaged with international modernism early. Futurist ideas circulated by 1912, the Futurist Art Association (Miraiha Bijutsu Kyōkai) formed in 1920, and the Dada movement had followers, such as the poet Takahashi Shinkichi, in the early 1920s. Artists such as Kanbara Tai, Kinoshita Shūichirō, and Yorozu Tetsugorō had already experimented with Fauvism, Cubism, and Expressionism in the 1910s, and groups such as the Nika-kai (1914) and Sōdosha provided exhibition venues. Murayama's trip to Berlin gave direct contact with Constructivism, and magazines brought images from abroad.

Japanese avant-garde artists did not merely import. They used materials, typography, and printing practices that drew on Japanese graphic traditions, and they connected the image to politics: after 1925 many turned toward proletarian art. The experimental image also moved into commercial design, including posters and book covers, blurring the boundary between art and design that Mingei defended in another way. The comparison below contrasts the two orientations.

Dada challenged the assumption that an artwork must be harmonious, carefully finished, and separated from ordinary things. Futurist arguments about speed, machinery, and the modern city offered a different vocabulary, while Expressionism privileged the distortion of appearances to communicate intense psychological or social experience. These were not identical European movements, and Japanese artists did not adopt them in complete doctrinal packages. Imported journals, reproductions, theater, and translated statements supplied materials that artists reworked in their own circumstances. A pasted fragment of newspaper, for example, could simultaneously refer to the commercial press and challenge the expectation that art was made with traditional painting materials.

Experimental imagery also depended on how works were encountered. A collage reproduced in a small magazine, an installation in an exhibition room, and a performance in the street involved different audiences and different degrees of risk. Artists made modern materials—machine parts, printed lettering, photographs, commercial wrapping—visible as cultural forms rather than neutral backgrounds. Critics interpreted these practices as liberation, foolish provocation, or evidence of social dislocation. Their conflicting responses are part of the source record. The strongest analysis identifies individual objects, publication dates, and exhibition settings before interpreting the general "avant-garde" as a coherent program.

An additional problem concerns the survival of evidence. Ephemeral constructions were dismantled, fragile paper deteriorated, and performances left only descriptions and photographs. Later museum reconstructions can help recover the movement, but they are not the original experience. Students should compare catalogs and contemporary criticism and specify whether an object survives, is known through a photograph, or is reconstructed from testimony. This is especially important when a movement deliberately blurred the distinction between a work of art and an event.

Section 72.30 · Division V

Creative Prints, Graphic Art, and the Redefinition of Artistic Production

The woodblock print had been the commercial medium of the Edo period (Chapter 46), produced by teams of designers, carvers, and printers under a publisher. In the early twentieth century two movements redefined it. The shin-hanga ("new prints") movement, associated with the publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō, revived the division of labor and made landscapes and figure prints for domestic and foreign buyers. The sōsaku-hanga ("creative prints") movement, in contrast, held that the artist should draw, carve, and print the work himself or herself, expressing personal vision. Yamamoto Kanae (1882–1946), whose 1904 print of a fisherman is often cited as an early example, and who founded the Japan Creative Print Association in 1918, was a leader. Onchi Kōshirō (1891–1955) produced abstract prints, and Hiratsuka Un'ichi (1895–1997) became a major printmaker.

Sōsaku-hanga thus transferred the ideals of the individual artist, as promoted by Shirakaba, to printmaking, and it competed for the status of fine art. This position is the opposite of the Mingei ideal of anonymity, though Munakata Shikō, a printmaker associated with Mingei, combined individual vision with folk inspiration. The debates about print, craft, and fine art show that the boundaries among these categories were being renegotiated, as Division II argued from the craft side.

The sōsaku-hanga, or creative-print movement, challenged a division of labor characteristic of much commercial woodblock production. In traditional publishing, designer, block carver, printer, and publisher might be separate specialists. Advocates of creative prints argued that an artist should shape the design, carve the block, and carry out printing, thereby bringing the process under a more unified conception of personal expression. This principle was articulated well before the 1920s, with Yamamoto Kanae often associated with its early development, but the interwar period saw wider experimentation with the medium. The distinction was an ideological claim about authorship, not proof that every creative print was produced without assistance.

Onchi Kōshirō and other practitioners expanded the visual possibilities of the print through abstraction, unusual color combinations, and attention to the grain and texture of wood. The same medium could serve landscape representation, modern psychological expression, or everyday graphic design. Prints were also reproducible: unlike a singular painting, an edition could circulate through exhibitions and collectors' networks. This complicates the contrast between originality and mechanical multiplication. To interpret a surviving print, students should examine the block, paper, registration of colors, inscriptions, number of impressions, and the relation between a creator's intention and the actual effects of printing. The technique's material constraints were a source of invention rather than an obstacle to serious artistic expression.

Section 72.31 · Division V

Experimental Theater, Dance, and Women's Artistic Participation

Modern theater (shingeki, "new drama") sought to stage translated and original plays in a realistic or experimental style, in contrast to Kabuki. After early groups such as the Literary Society and the Free Theater, the Tsukiji Little Theatre opened in June 1924 under Osanai Kaoru and Hijikata Yoshi, a small, modern playhouse in Tokyo that staged European plays and trained actors. It was funded in part by Hijikata's own resources, and after Osanai's death in 1928 the company divided. Women actors, such as Tamura Akiko, gained prominence, though the earlier example of Matsui Sumako, who had starred in Ibsen and Tolstoy in the 1910s, showed the personal costs of fame for a woman performer.

Modern dance developed in parallel. Ishii Baku (1886–1962), who trained in Europe in the early 1920s, taught modern dance to Japanese students and choreographed "dance poems," and his students included Eguchi Takaya and Miya Misako. Women artists were active as dancers, teachers, and performers, but they also faced constraints of social expectation and financial dependence. Revue theaters such as the Takarazuka Revue, which Chapter 70 considers in the context of mass entertainment, offered another context for women's performance. Experimental theater and dance thus show bodily expression as a site of artistic modernism and of debate over gender, though much of the more radical postwar experimentation, such as Butoh, belongs to a later chapter.

The Tsukiji Little Theatre, opened in 1924 under the leadership of Osanai Kaoru and Hijikata Yoshi, became a major institutional experiment in shingeki, or new theater. Its small purpose-built performance space, coordinated stage design, and interest in modern European drama promoted an alternative to conventional entertainment and older theatrical forms. Direction, lighting, sound, and the organization of performance were understood as artistic problems in their own right. The company presented translated plays and developed Japanese theatrical practice through rehearsal methods and technical experimentation. The repertoire and personnel changed, so the theater's career should be reconstructed through programs and contemporaneous reviews, not from a generic list of famous names.

Modern dance and movement also challenged assumptions about the trained body. Ishii Baku and other performers explored expressive dance outside the forms of classical Japanese theater and Western ballet, while women performers negotiated opportunities that remained constrained by gender conventions. The avant-garde often aspired to liberate performers from old rules, but artistic institutions still depended on access to rehearsal space, training, money, and an audience. Experiments in stage design could share visual strategies with MAVO or modern graphic arts without implying identical politics. Contemporary reviews reveal that novelty was contested: some viewers saw emotional intensity and freedom, others indiscipline or imported spectacle. The historian should preserve those differences instead of describing all experimental theater as progressive by definition.

Section 72.32 · Division V

Architecture, Applied Arts, and the Experimentation of Everyday Design

Ideas from art entered everyday environments through architecture, interiors, furniture, and graphic design. In 1920 the Secessionist Architecture Group (Bunri-ha Kenchikukai), including Horiguchi Sutemi and Yamada Mamoru, announced a break from historical styles. The Tokyo Peace Exposition of 1922 displayed model "culture houses" (bunka jūtaku) combining Western-style rooms with Japanese features, reflecting the movement for daily-life improvement that Chapter 70 discussed. Frank Lloyd Wright's Imperial Hotel in Tokyo opened in 1923, on the day of the earthquake. The Bauhaus's influence reached Japan through students such as Yamawaki Iwao and Michiko, who studied there around 1930, and the German architect Bruno Taut, who came in 1933 and worked in Takasaki.

The state also promoted design. The Industrial Arts Institute (Kōgei Shidōsho), founded in 1928 in Sendai by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, researched design for export and domestic markets and later hosted Taut. Its modern, functional approach overlapped with Mingei's emphasis on utility, though it embraced industrial production. Chapter 71 covers reconstruction engineering; the point here is the circulation of artistic ideas into the built environment and everyday objects. Graphic designers also adapted avant-garde typography and photography to posters and magazines, shaping the visual culture of department stores and newspapers.

The result was a wide field of experimentation in which craft and design, handmade and machine-made, Japanese and international, were combined in changing ways. The Mingei movement drew one line in this field, but it was not the only way to bring beauty into daily life.

The relationship between cultural experiment and material manufacture was especially visible in architectural education and design workshops. The Bunriha architectural group, formed around 1920 by architects including Horiguchi Sutemi, criticized established compositional assumptions and explored modern expressive possibilities. Later, the Industrial Arts Institute, founded in Sendai in 1928, sought to improve the design and marketability of Japanese products through research and demonstration. These were different institutional projects: one arose from professional architectural debates, the other from state-sponsored industrial design. Both treated form as something that could be deliberately studied rather than merely inherited.

The Bauhaus and other European schools supplied influential ideas about integrating art, craft, and functional objects, but their reception in Japan involved individual teachers, translated writings, exhibitions, and local commercial needs. Modern chairs, lamps, posters, and book designs could be developed for consumers with different budgets and expectations. An industrially produced household object might embody serious formal experimentation even though it failed the stricter Mingei preference for handmade craft. The contrast shows that "beauty in everyday life" had multiple meanings. Students should ask which materials and methods a design required, who could afford it, and how the maker expected it to be used. Such questions join artistic history to changing labor and the distribution of urban goods.

Section 72.33 · Division VI — New Religions, Spiritual Experimentation, and Religious Reform

Religious Change in an Industrializing Society

Religious life in Taishō and early Shōwa Japan cannot be described as a contest between "Shinto and Buddhism." The Meiji state had classified shrine Shinto as a national rite outside the category of religion, recognized thirteen sect-Shinto bodies and several Buddhist denominations, and left other groups in an uncertain position. Scholars use the postwar term shinkō shūkyō ("new religions") for movements founded since the nineteenth century, while prewar officials often called unrecognized groups ruiji shūkyō ("quasi-religions"), a label that implied suspicion. Tenrikyō (founded by Nakayama Miki in 1838) and Konkōkyō (Konkō Daijin, 1859), introduced in Chapter 64, gained legal recognition as sect Shinto; newer movements had no such protection.

Migration, insecurity, illness, and family strain supplied followers. Workers who had left villages for factories and cities lost the support of village shrines and household temples; women faced the stresses of marriage, childbirth, and work; epidemic disease, including the influenza pandemic of 1918 to 1920, and the economic downturn increased anxiety. New movements offered healing, explanations of misfortune, ritual practices that could be performed at home, and communities of mutual support. They also offered leadership roles to people, including women with little formal education, who had been excluded from established religious hierarchies. Established Buddhist temples, funded by funerary and memorial services, were often seen as unresponsive.

The state response varied. Officials could tolerate groups that stressed loyalty and morality, regulate those that seemed to threaten public order, and suppress those that appeared to challenge imperial authority. A Religions Bill proposed in 1927 to establish a general framework failed to pass in the Diet, and further efforts culminated in the Religious Organizations Law of 1939, a later development. The following sections examine four movements with different origins and trajectories.

The category "new religion" is a later scholarly way of grouping organizations whose founders and followers often understood themselves differently. Some taught devotion to particular kami; others drew from Lotus Sutra practice, ancestor rites, healing, millenarian expectation, or combinations of spiritual traditions. Organizational differences mattered as much as doctrine. A movement with a charismatic founder and headquarters did not operate like a network of household meetings, and a publishing-centered teaching differed again. The legal distinction between approved sects and unrecognized groups influenced their ability to own property, hold gatherings, and defend themselves against intervention.

New religions did not simply fill a vacuum left by Buddhism and shrine worship. Many participants continued relationships with family temples, neighborhood rituals, and local shrines while also attending newer gatherings. Seeking a cure or guidance about family conflict need not have meant abandoning an older religious identity. Practices could be individualized, but movements often provided community, reciprocal assistance, and routes for women to exercise leadership outside conventional institutions. Researchers therefore attend to the social geography of members: village and city, occupations, household structures, and transport links. State police records document anxiety about alleged deviance but may reveal much less about the ordinary appeal of the meetings they monitored.

Shinkō shūkyō is the scholarly term for "new religions." It is applied retrospectively to movements of different periods and should not suggest that they share one doctrine. Ruiji shūkyō, "quasi-religion," was an official label for groups that the state did not recognize as religions. Section 72.34 · Division VI

Ōmoto: Deguchi Nao, Deguchi Onisaburō, and Spiritual Renewal

Ōmoto began with Deguchi Nao (1837–1918), a poor widow in the town of Ayabe in present-day Kyoto Prefecture who, in 1892, experienced a possession by a deity and began to issue revelations. Her writings, produced in a simple hand and known as the Ofudesaki ("Tip of the Brush"), condemned the world's injustice, predicted a coming renewal, and called for a reordering of society. She lived in poverty and supported herself through odd work. Her message attracted a following in the Ayabe region.

Deguchi Onisaburō (1871–1948), born Ueda Kisaburō in a farming family near Kameoka, studied Shinto and spiritual practices, joined Nao in 1898, and married her youngest daughter Sumi in 1900. He reorganized the group, systematized its teachings, and made it a national movement. Under his leadership Ōmoto purchased a daily newspaper in 1919, published a magazine and books, built facilities in Ayabe and Kameoka, and attracted intellectuals, military officers, and journalists. From 1921 he dictated the large work Reikai monogatari ("Tale of the Spirit World"), a vast account of spiritual realms. He also promoted Esperanto, undertook a journey to Mongolia in 1924, and in 1925 helped establish the Universal Love and Brotherhood Association, expressing an ecumenical, internationalist spirit. Taniguchi Masaharu, later founder of Seichō no Ie, was among those who worked for Ōmoto as a journalist.

Ōmoto was a movement of paradoxes. It taught world renewal and criticized existing authority, yet used the language of emperor-centered nationalism. It was led by a woman prophet and an organizer-priest, and women held significant roles. Its healing and spiritual practices drew ordinary followers while its publications drew intellectuals. Historians such as Nancy Stalker have emphasized its creativity and its ambiguous relationship with the state. The next section considers the state's response.

Deguchi Nao's writings must be placed at the center of the movement's history rather than treated merely as an origin story preceding a famous male organizer. Her automatic writings, known as the Ofudesaki, expressed a vision of divine intervention and a thorough overturning of a corrupt world. Their religious vocabulary emerged from local belief and personal suffering as well as broader traditions. For followers, the promise of renewal could turn the burdens of poverty, illness, and exclusion into signs of a spiritually meaningful crisis. The history of the text also raises questions of authorship and transmission: manuscripts, copies, editorial intervention, and later presentation by the institution are not necessarily identical documents.

Onisaburō brought new organizational methods and interpretive claims. Publications, lectures, art, ritual, and the group's developing centers at Ayabe and Kameoka offered followers a shared cultural world. He also encouraged ambitious accounts of international and spiritual transformation. The fact that the movement used modern media did not make it secular, any more than the use of older revelation made it isolated from modern politics. Its size and visibility heightened official fears that a charismatic religious leader might challenge the symbolic authority reserved for the emperor. Students should compare the founder's original revelatory language with later organized teachings and distinguish what participants claimed from what state prosecutors alleged.

Section 72.35 · Division VI

The First Ōmoto Incident and the Limits of Religious Freedom

In February 1921 police arrested Deguchi Onisaburō and several leaders, and authorities searched and began to demolish Ōmoto facilities. The charges included lèse-majesté (fukei) and violation of the newspaper law. Officials were alarmed by Ōmoto's rapid growth, its prophecies of world renewal, its newspaper's criticism of the existing order, and rumors that its teachings challenged the imperial house, at a time when the authorities feared radicalism after the Russian Revolution and the Rice Riots. The press, much of it hostile to Ōmoto, played a role in building public suspicion.

Onisaburō spent 126 days in detention before his release on bail in 1921. Although he was convicted in the subsequent proceedings, appeals and retrials prolonged the case; the proceedings ended through an amnesty on May 17, 1927, following Emperor Taishō’s death. Release on bail and the legal termination of the case were separate events. Ōmoto reorganized and, in the late 1920s and early 1930s, grew again, attracting military and political figures and expanding its international work. It was suppressed far more severely in 1935, when authorities arrested leaders, destroyed buildings, and dissolved the organization, a later event that Chapter 77 and following chapters place in the context of wartime ideology.

The first incident shows the boundaries of religious freedom under the Meiji constitution. Article 28 guaranteed freedom of belief "within limits not prejudicial to peace and order" and compatible with subjects' duties, language that gave officials discretion. Groups that appeared compatible with the kokutai could be tolerated; those that seemed to threaten it could be suppressed. The incident also illustrates the importance of public opinion, the press, and the relationship between new religions and the emperor system.

The First Ōmoto Incident began with coordinated arrests on February 12, 1921. Onisaburō and other leaders were accused under provisions concerning lèse-majesté and the press, as officials examined whether the movement's teachings threatened imperial authority. The legal course is important because a dramatic arrest did not end the affair. After 126 days in detention, Onisaburō was released on bail. The subsequent proceedings included conviction, appeal, and further litigation, until amnesty following Emperor Taishō's death brought the case to a close in May 1927. A textbook must distinguish release from custody, the outcome of individual judgments, and termination of the case; these are not three descriptions of one event.

The case shows how constitutional guarantees were conditioned by the state's interpretation of public order. A follower might understand prophetic images of renewal as spiritual transformation, while police regarded similar language as an implied political claim over the emperor or the state. Official suspicion was amplified by the period's anxieties about radicalism, social disorder, and widely publicized religious enthusiasm. That interpretive distance deserves analysis rather than an automatic assumption that the state accurately described the movement's intentions. Yet the group's own teachings were not politically neutral, and its leaders could employ national and imperial language. The second suppression in 1935 was far more destructive, but bringing it into this section should clarify the later trajectory without erasing the distinctive legal history of 1921–1927.

Section 72.36 · Division VI

Reiyūkai, Lay Buddhism, and the Reinvention of Ancestor Veneration

Reiyūkai ("Association of Friends of the Spirits") developed from lay Nichiren Buddhist circles. Its origins are traced to Kubo Kakutarō (1892–1944), a man from the Tokyo area who had experience in business and religious study, and to the influence of a lay teacher, Wakasa Tokuji, in the late 1910s. The association developed through organizing initiatives in the 1920s; 1925 appears as a milestone in some histories, but a formal inaugural ceremony took place on July 13, 1930, with 104 attendees, including children. Kubo worked with Kotani Kimi (1901–1971), his sister-in-law, who became a principal religious leader. No separate date of legal incorporation is established by the evidence presented here. Its practice centered on the Lotus Sutra, recited in homes, and on rites for ancestors, including the belief that unresolved spiritual problems arising from ancestors' spirits could cause misfortune in the family and could be addressed by memorial rites and good conduct.

The movement spread through small group meetings (hōza), in which members shared problems and received counsel, and through publications. It attracted members from the urban working and lower-middle classes and rural migrants, offering an alternative to temple-centered funerary practice and a role for laypeople, men and women, as religious teachers. Helen Hardacre's study of Reiyūkai emphasizes the movement's blending of ancestor veneration with a lay Buddhist ethic of repaying debts to ancestors. In the 1930s Reiyūkai grew substantially, and after 1945 it became a source of further movements, though those developments lie beyond this chapter.

Reiyūkai shows a reinvention of ancestor veneration, an ancient practice, for an urbanizing society in which households were separated from ancestral villages and graves. It also shows women's leadership in a religious movement with Nichiren Buddhist roots.

The development of Reiyūkai was gradual. Kubo Kakutarō's work in lay Buddhist practice and memorial rites provided an important foundation, while the participation and leadership of Kotani Kimi gave the emerging association both an organizational center and a form of religious authority not restricted to ordained men. Sources that speak of an early-1920s origin, a 1925 organizing milestone, and the 1930 foundation do not necessarily contradict one another: they may refer to the growth of practices, the consolidation of a circle, and the public inauguration. The organization's own historical account dates its formal inaugural ceremony to July 13, 1930, with 104 participants, including children. This should not be restated as a date of legal incorporation without separate evidence.

Reiyūkai practice was organized around ancestor memorialization and Lotus Sutra devotion but took distinctive forms in small lay gatherings. Members could speak about illness, debt, family tensions, and uncertainty in a setting where counsel and mutual attention formed part of religious participation. The emphasis on ancestors linked an innovation in organization to a familiar dimension of household ritual, which was particularly relevant as migration separated people from their natal communities. Kotani's position illustrates both opportunities and constraints: a woman could hold recognized spiritual authority while operating within moral assumptions about family duties. The later expansion of Reiyūkai should be treated as a consequence, not projected back onto a modest early gathering.

Section 72.37 · Division VI

Seichō no Ie, Value-Creating Education, and New Spiritual Communities

Taniguchi Masaharu (1893–1985) was a writer and editor who had worked for Ōmoto and was interested in spiritual and psychological currents, including Western "New Thought." In 1930 he began publishing a magazine, Seichō no Ie ("House of Growth"), through which he developed a teaching that the true nature of human beings is perfect and that illness and misfortune are illusions of the mind. The movement spread through the magazine, lectures, and study groups. It combined spiritual practice with a modern language of self-improvement and, in later decades, came to emphasize reverence for the emperor and conservative political positions, developments that lie beyond this chapter.

The Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai ("Value-Creating Education Society") has a different origin. Makiguchi Tsunesaburō (1871–1944), an educator who had written Jinsei chirigaku ("The Geography of Human Life," 1903), developed a theory of education aimed at creating value through the child's interaction with the environment. With Toda Jōsei (1900–1958), who financed publication, he issued the first volume of Sōka kyōikugaku taikei ("System of Value-Creating Pedagogy") on November 18, 1930, the date later commemorated as the society's founding. In the early 1930s it was a small circle of educators, and its formal inaugural ceremony was held only in 1937; Makiguchi had begun associating with Nichiren Buddhism in 1928, and the religious mass movement developed later, especially in the postwar decades. Makiguchi died in prison in 1944 after his arrest under the Peace Preservation Law for refusing wartime religious policy, a later consequence.

These two movements illustrate the diversity of new spiritual communities. One began as a publishing venture and teaching of mind and spirit; the other as an educational reform society with Buddhist foundations. Students should avoid reading back later size or political character into 1930.

Seichō no Ie's early history is inseparable from print. Taniguchi Masaharu circulated his ideas through a magazine launched in 1930 and developed a vocabulary of spiritual perfection, positive thinking, and healing. His prior association with Ōmoto did not make the new movement a straightforward branch of it. Religious borrowing and personal reinterpretation interacted with the marketing possibilities of magazines and lectures. A reader could encounter the teaching before attending a meeting, and the printed text itself became an instrument of spiritual persuasion. Later institutional doctrines and political activities need to be distinguished from the movement's initial public form.

The origins of Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai were different. Makiguchi Tsunesaburō developed a theory of value-creating education that criticized rote instruction and emphasized the life experience of the learner; Toda Jōsei helped prepare and disseminate his work. Publication of the first volume of Sōka kyōikugaku taikei on November 18, 1930, is commemorated by the later organization as its founding, but the formal inaugural ceremony occurred in 1937. At the earlier stage it was chiefly a circle interested in educational reform with developing Nichiren Buddhist commitments, not a postwar political-religious mass organization. Keeping the dates distinct prevents institutional retrospection from flattening a complicated development. These two cases show how a movement's later name and fame can obscure the modest, different ways it first took shape.

Section 72.38 · Division VI

Women, Healing, Communication, and New Religious Networks

Women's roles in new religions were prominent across the movements. Nakayama Miki of Tenrikyō and Deguchi Nao of Ōmoto were founders; Kotani Kimi was the leading figure of Reiyūkai; and women served as healers, circle leaders, and members. These roles contrasted with the male clerical hierarchies of Buddhist and Shinto institutions. Healing, often by prayer, ritual, or spiritual teaching, was a major draw, and it met needs unfulfilled by medicine that many could not afford. Authorities and physicians sometimes criticized such practices.

Communication networks were crucial. Magazines such as Ōmoto's publications and Seichō no Ie, lecture tours, small meetings, and the railways that carried teachers made it possible for movements to spread regionally. Organization differed: Ōmoto had a charismatic headquarters, Reiyūkai a system of branches and circles, and Seichō no Ie a publishing center and study groups. Tensions arose between religious autonomy and administrative surveillance: police monitored meetings, and leaders had to decide how much to stress loyalty to the state.

The participation of women in new religions cannot be reduced to a symbolic association with nurturing or healing. Deguchi Nao authored revelations that became the foundation of an organized movement; Kotani Kimi helped build a lay Buddhist community and exercised religious authority within its leadership. Women also acted as organizers of meetings, hosts, religious advisers, fundraisers, and transmitters of teachings across kinship networks. Such labor was often essential to the movement even when institutional histories emphasized charismatic founders. Comparison of official biographies with women's memoirs and local branch documents can reveal how authority was recognized, negotiated, and limited.

Claims about healing require the same source criticism as political claims. A leader's publication may describe a cure as evidence of divine efficacy; a doctor or police officer may dismiss it as superstition; a follower may remember the experience as relief from fear or isolation. These records answer different questions and should not be collapsed into a medical conclusion. Small circles could provide companionship and practical advice while also imposing expectations about morality, household roles, or loyalty to the organization. Rail travel and inexpensive print enlarged their reach, but local leaders, personal contacts, and word of mouth often remained decisive. The social history of religion therefore belongs alongside doctrine and state surveillance.

Section 72.39 · Division VII — Cultural Modernity, Tradition, and the Limits of Experimentation

Japan and the International Circulation of Cultural Ideas

The movements of this chapter were shaped by exchanges that crossed borders in several directions. European literature reached Japan through translation, and Japanese writers adapted realism, symbolism, Expressionism, and psychological techniques. European art arrived through reproductions, exhibitions, and study abroad: Rodin's gift to the Shirakaba group, Murayama's stay in Berlin, and the Bauhaus students of 1930 are examples. British studio pottery and Japanese craft influenced each other through Leach and Hamada. Within Asia, Korean ceramics, Chinese pottery, and Ryukyuan textiles shaped Japanese collectors' tastes, and Korean and Taiwanese students in Tokyo carried Japanese literary models home, transforming them.

Historians stress adaptation rather than imitation. Japanese writers did not simply copy European modernism; they combined it with local subjects and with classical aesthetics, as in Kawabata and Tanizaki. Mingei took ideas from Ruskin and Morris but grounded them in Buddhist philosophy and East Asian craft. Proletarian writers adapted Soviet theory to Japanese conditions. Even where borrowings were direct, the context differed: a Dada gesture in Tokyo after an earthquake meant something different from one in Zurich. At the same time, the circulation of ideas was unequal. Cultural capital flowed through imperial and colonial hierarchies, and Japanese intellectuals could travel more freely, publish more widely, and define more categories than their Korean or Taiwanese counterparts.

The international dimension also included the other direction, as Japanese art and literature became known abroad. Leach's pottery in England and translations of Japanese literature, which were limited before the 1920s but growing, created new audiences. Cultural exchange thus had its own politics, which the next section examines in relation to commerce and patronage.

The movement of ideas was mediated by unequal institutions. Japanese students with access to elite education and travel could study European painting, philosophy, and literature, while many Korean and Taiwanese intellectuals encountered Japanese universities through a colonial educational structure that restricted opportunities at home. Their subsequent use of literary modernism or social criticism was not simply a return shipment of Japanese influence; it involved debates about language, social reform, and national culture that developed on their own terms. Similarly the translation of Russian fiction or European manifestos involved selection: publishers decided what to make available, and reviewers told readers how to interpret it.

The craft exchange between Hamada, Leach, and their collaborators illustrates how technical knowledge moved through hands as well as books. Kiln construction, clay preparation, glaze testing, and practical apprenticeship could not be transported intact as abstract theories. The resulting pottery belonged neither wholly to an unchanged Japanese tradition nor simply to British reform. Students should distinguish evidence of direct contact—letters, documented visits, and surviving objects—from a general resemblance of style. Such discipline also matters for claims about influence between avant-garde movements: similar experimental images in different countries do not by themselves prove a chain of borrowing. International culture was shaped by correspondence, translation, materials, finance, and the uneven mobility of people.

Section 72.40 · Division VII

Art, Commerce, Patronage, and Cultural Institutions

Experimental culture depended on money. Coterie magazines were funded by members or patrons, commercial magazines paid contributors and set audiences' expectations, and galleries and department stores sold works and hosted exhibitions. Kikuchi Kan's support enabled Bungei Jidai; Ōhara Magosaburō's money made the Japan Folk Crafts Museum; Hijikata Yoshi's wealth supported the Tsukiji Little Theatre; religious movements depended on members' donations. The expansion of the book market, including inexpensive one-yen series in the late 1920s, increased writers' earnings and the importance of marketing.

Did commerce contradict independence? The evidence is mixed. Commercial success could free writers from patrons, as Hayashi Fumiko's bestseller did, and publishers took risks on new work. But markets also standardized what sold, encouraged sensational content, and made writers dependent on editors. The Mingei movement's sale of craft to urban buyers supported workshops but changed the meaning of the objects. Patrons' interests shaped agendas: industrial wealth sometimes underwrote social-science research, craft museums, and avant-garde theater simultaneously, which complicates any simple opposition between capitalism and culture.

Cultural institutions were also sites of exclusion. Museums collected objects from Korea and Okinawa without those communities' control. Publishers selected which women or colonial writers to promote. Voluntary associations gave opportunities to those who could join. Historians therefore examine institutions as enabling and constraining forces. Chapter 73 will take up the larger economic structure that affected these patrons, including the financial instability of the late 1920s.

An artistic field involves several linked markets. Writers needed paper, printing, editorial support, payment, distribution, and reviews; potters needed raw materials, kilns, dealers, and buyers; theaters needed buildings and admission revenue. A rich patron might support experimentation that commercial publishers rejected, but patronage could create new dependencies. The cheap-book publishing initiatives of the 1920s broadened readership while encouraging branding and competition for recognizable authors. A small magazine could proclaim hostility to bourgeois values yet depend on purchases by students and salaried employees. The supposed boundary between market culture and artistic independence was therefore a recurring source of conflict rather than an easily maintained line.

Cultural gatekeeping also took material form. Museums selected certain objects, publishing houses promoted some writers, and critics decided whose work merited serious attention. A regional craft maker might gain a market through an influential collector but lose control over how the work was categorized. Women writers could attract a large public while being denied the prestige accorded to men. Censorship added another gatekeeper with legal power. These institutions left different kinds of evidence: catalogues, circulation figures, reviews, correspondence, account books, and official notices. A history built only from manifestos would describe what artists hoped to achieve; the additional records help establish who could participate and what actually happened.

Section 72.41 · Division VII

Inventing Tradition within a Modern Imperial Society

"Tradition" in these movements was selected, defined, and sometimes created. Mingei's folk craft was assembled from objects chosen by educated collectors; Tanizaki's classical Osaka was imagined from a modern vantage; proletarian writers constructed an inherited tradition of working-class struggle; new religions reinterpreted ancestors, scriptures, and kami. Even official "tradition" was modern: State Shinto, as Chapter 55 explained, was a Meiji creation. The point is not that these traditions were false but that they were made, and that different groups made different ones.

The making occurred in an empire. Korean, Okinawan, and Ainu cultures were preserved, collected, studied, and represented by Japanese intellectuals, often with sympathy but within unequal power relations. Yanagi's museum in Gyeongbokgung, Yanagita's use of Okinawan songs, and Kindaichi's editing of Ainu epics all involved Japanese scholars defining other peoples' heritage. At the same time, Chiri Yukie, Iha Fuyū, Lai He, and Korean writers represented their own communities, sometimes using the colonizer's language and institutions. A history that includes only Japanese tradition-makers would omit these voices, and a history that treats them only as victims would ignore their agency.

The interpretive stance recommended here is attention to who spoke, for whom, and under what conditions. Terms like "appropriation," "preservation," and "representation" describe different relationships, and historians should use them with evidence rather than as slogans.

The idea of "invented tradition" is best used as a question about selection and institutionalization, not as an accusation that all heritage is fraudulent. A practice may have an old history while the modern designation of its essence, the boundaries of its community, and the institutions preserving it are new. Mingei gathered particular objects under a classificatory label established in 1925; museums and publications then encouraged readers to imagine a coherent craft past. The collection could preserve actual skills and objects while presenting them through a framework its makers had never used. Similar processes shaped modern editions of folklore, the standardization of performance, and the selection of regional practices for display.

The colonial context made authority over classification especially important. A Japanese collector, a Korean potter, a Taiwanese writer, and an Ainu translator could participate in cultural preservation from very different political positions. Ainu oral narratives were not awaiting literary value until a Japanese scholar encountered them, and Korean ceramics possessed histories independent of Yanagi's discovery narrative. Yet it would also be misleading to erase the contributions of Japanese intermediaries to the circulation of particular texts and objects. The historical task is to reconstruct the chain of production, recording, translation, display, and interpretation. Only then can terms such as preservation, collaboration, exoticization, and appropriation be applied with warranted specificity.

Section 72.42 · Division VII

Creative Freedom, Political Pressure, and the Approach of the 1930s

The late 1920s and early 1930s brought increasing constraints. The Peace Preservation Law of 1925 and the mass arrests of March 1928 (Chapter 69), the extension of the law's penalties, and the creation of political police structures narrowed the room for leftist writers and artists. Censorship tightened for publications, and the Manchurian Incident of 1931 (Chapter 74) began a new phase of nationalist mobilization. Kobayashi Takiji's death in custody in 1933 and the wave of "conversions" of leftists the same year ended the organized proletarian movement. Religious movements faced scrutiny as well, culminating in the 1935 Ōmoto suppression.

Yet responses were varied, and many forms of creativity continued. Mingei expanded, culminating in the museum of 1936, and later aligned in complicated ways with state interest in regional craft. Modernist writers produced major works, and popular fiction and radio flourished. Women's writing continued. Religious movements grew. Some artists and writers adapted to pressure, some resisted, and some turned to private or traditional subjects. Chapter 73 will address the economic crisis that deepened social tension, and Chapter 74 the political collapse. It would be wrong to treat the cultural experimentation of the 1920s as doomed to repression; contemporaries did not know the future, and the diversity of responses was itself historical evidence.

The tightening of political conditions affected cultural forms unevenly. The police violence that killed Kobayashi Takiji in 1933 was a brutal warning to organized proletarian writers, but it did not render the writings of every modernist or craft advocate illegal. Some publications continued; others changed subject, rhetoric, publisher, or associations. The term tenkō, often translated as ideological conversion, described pressures and decisions within the communist and wider leftist milieu that could include public recantation, genuine change, or pragmatic adaptation. Accounts of conversion should therefore identify the individual and the circumstances instead of treating it as a single collective experience.

The later growth of Mingei and the 1935 suppression of Ōmoto show why parallel cultural histories cannot be reduced to a common trajectory. Officials might welcome rural handicraft promotion while fearing an independent religious organization or radical literary association. Writers, potters, and religious participants also differed in how they understood loyalty and public duty. A historical interpretation that explains 1920s creativity only as an inevitable prelude to war deprives contemporaries of agency and neglects alternatives they actually proposed. The chapter should end by carrying forward these unresolved conflicts—economic insecurity, political coercion, imperial inequality, and genuine creativity—rather than announcing that modern culture had reached a predetermined end.

Section 72.43 · Division VIII — Historical Interpretation and Chapter Review

Tradition, Modernism, and Spiritual Renewal: Interpreting Cultural Change

Historians have interpreted these movements in three main ways. One approach sees them as reactions against modernization: Mingei's rejection of industrial production, new religions' response to disorientation, and literature's expression of alienation are read as protests against rapid change. A second approach emphasizes "alternative modernities": the movements are seen as creating distinct paths of modern culture, not as retreats from modernity. A third stresses that the movements were different expressions of the same transformations, sharing institutions, media, and international sources, and that the distinction between "traditional" and "modern" is itself a product of the period's debates.

Each interpretation has strengths. The reaction thesis explains the language of loss that pervades the sources; the alternative-modernity thesis highlights creativity and the plurality of modernity; the shared-transformation thesis explains why Mingei potters, avant-garde artists, and religious leaders used the same magazines and railways. Interpretations also vary by movement: scholars debate whether Mingei was fundamentally nationalist, anti-colonial, or both; whether proletarian literature should be judged by its politics or its form; whether minority writers should be read as resistance, accommodation, or both; and whether new religions were liberating or controlling. This chapter's position is that the movements were modern projects that defined tradition and modernity in competing ways, within an imperial society whose inequalities shaped who could speak.

Consider what each interpretation can and cannot explain through a specific comparison. A handmade vessel and an experimental collage may look like opposites, yet both could be selected for exhibition by the same urban elite and promoted through modern photography and print. A revival of ancestor rites and a program of educational reform drew on older language while organizing themselves through new associations. A proletarian novel and a modernist story might differ in political commitments while sharing a concern with how modern labor altered experience. These comparisons favor a relational account of cultural change, but the relational approach can become vague unless it identifies specific institutions, texts, and social conflicts.

Historians also disagree about the meaning of agency within empire. A Japanese scholar's sympathy for Korean objects can be documented without using it to deny colonial inequality; an Ainu writer's dependence on a Japanese publisher does not cancel her authorship. A religious follower's experience of solidarity does not eliminate the possibility of internal pressure, and a named studio potter's admiration for anonymous labor does not equal the social position of a workshop assistant. The most defensible synthesis asks what the sources allow us to say and whose records have survived. Students should defend an interpretation with at least two distinct types of evidence—such as a published manifesto and a material object, or a founder's text and an official record—and explain what each leaves unresolved.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the cultural and intellectual responses of Japanese society to industrial, urban, and imperial modernity between about 1910 and 1936. Its central argument is that modern Japanese cultural life developed through competing efforts to redefine the relationship between tradition and innovation, individual creativity and collective life, spiritual meaning and material progress, and cultural independence and international exchange. The movements studied were not retreats from modernity; they depended on magazines, museums, railways, patrons, and international ideas, and they defined "tradition" in new ways.

The foundations lay in the expansion of education, publishing, and urban life, in the legacy of the Meiji separation of fine art and craft, and in widespread unease about mechanization and social change. The Shirakaba circle introduced European art and a humanist individualism, and small magazines and coteries organized debate. The 1923 earthquake disrupted institutions and stimulated experiments, though many developments had begun earlier.

Mingei grew from Yanagi Sōetsu's encounter with Joseon ceramics, mediated by the Asakawa brothers, and developed into a movement that named "beauty of use" and anonymous craftsmanship as sources of value. The Korean Folk Art Museum of 1924, the term mingei of 1925, the 1926 prospectus, the journal Kōgei, and the Japan Folk Crafts Museum of 1936 mark its institutionalization, with patronage from Ōhara Magosaburō. The potters Hamada Shōji, Kawai Kanjirō, and Bernard Leach exemplify its international and individual dimensions. The movement's contradictions include the concealment of craft labor, the idealization of rural life, the commercial transformation of humble objects, and the colonial setting of its Korean origins.

Literary innovation proceeded along several lines. The I-novel and Naturalism provided the background. Akutagawa explored ambiguity and testimony, Tanizaki desire and cultural ambivalence, and Kawabata and Yokomitsu, with the New Sensation School of 1924, sought a new perception adequate to the modern city. Proletarian writers such as Hayama, Tokunaga, and Kobayashi depicted labor and collective struggle, and the field divided over aesthetic autonomy and social obligation under increasing censorship. Children's literature, modern poetry, and women's writing extended the field.

Women writers claimed literary authority through venues such as Nyonin Geijutsu, and Hayashi Fumiko, Miyamoto Yuriko, and Sata Ineko wrote about poverty, work, and political commitment. Chiri Yukie's Ainu Shin'yōshū of 1923 preserved Ainu oral literature within a colonial context of assimilation. Korean, Okinawan, and Taiwanese writers faced different legal statuses and language choices, and these histories must be distinguished rather than merged.

In the visual and performing arts, MAVO attacked established conventions with materials and manifestos drawn from international modernism, while sōsaku-hanga redefined printmaking around the individual artist. Shingeki, modern dance, and architectural and design experiments carried artistic ideas into theaters and daily environments. Comparing these with Mingei shows two modern responses: one valuing rupture and the individual, the other continuity and anonymity.

New religions responded to migration, insecurity, and illness. Ōmoto, founded on Deguchi Nao's revelations and expanded by Deguchi Onisaburō, combined world-renewal teaching with publishing and internationalism and was suppressed in 1921, illustrating the limits of religious freedom under the Meiji constitution. Reiyūkai reinvented ancestor veneration through lay Lotus Sutra devotion and small circles, and Seichō no Ie and the Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai emerged in 1930 as a spiritual teaching and an educational reform society respectively, with later histories that must not be read back. Women's leadership and communication networks were important across these movements.

The wider interpretation emphasizes adaptation of international ideas, the dependence of culture on commerce and patronage, the construction of tradition within an unequal empire, and the narrowing room for creative freedom in the early 1930s, as the Peace Preservation Law, censorship, and the death of Kobayashi Takiji in custody indicate. Historians read the movements as reactions against modernization, as alternative modernities, or as products of shared transformations; the chapter's position is that they were competing modern projects.

These developments shaped later history. Mingei and modernist design influenced postwar craft and design; proletarian and women's literature provided models for postwar writing; minority writers' work became foundations for later Ainu, Korean, Okinawan, and Taiwanese cultural movements; and the new religions, some suppressed and some tolerated in the 1930s, became large organizations after 1945. The chapter prepares for Chapter 73, which examines the economic crisis that deepened social strain, and Chapter 74, which examines the collapse of parliamentary restraint.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that modern Japanese culture was argued over, not simply received or inherited. Craftspeople, collectors, novelists, poets, artists, and religious leaders offered competing answers to the same questions about what machines, cities, and empire were doing to human life. Their answers were creative, and they were also constrained by money, patronage, censorship, gender, and imperial hierarchy.

The chapter also shows how unevenly cultural authority was distributed. Those who defined Japanese tradition, collected Korean and Okinawan objects, or edited Ainu epics were mostly educated Japanese men with access to publishers and museums. Chiri Yukie, Hayashi Fumiko, Kotani Kimi, Lai He, and the unnamed craftspeople whose work was admired remind students that cultural history includes those who spoke from less powerful positions, and whose words and labor survive in fragmentary form.

The movements shaped later history. Mingei's ideas influenced postwar craft and design, and its museum remains. Modernist and proletarian literature provided resources for postwar writers, and the repression of the left in the early 1930s became part of postwar memory. Minority and colonial writers laid foundations for later cultural movements in Hokkaido, Okinawa, Korea, and Taiwan. New religions, which the state suppressed or tolerated according to its own calculations, became large organizations after 1945, and their prewar experience informed debates about religious freedom.

Finally, the chapter connects Japan to the wider world and prepares for what follows. Ideas moved among Tokyo, Seoul, Berlin, Cornwall, and Moscow, and were transformed on the way. Chapter 73 turns to the economic crisis that strained patrons and communities, and Chapter 74 to the collapse of parliamentary restraint. Students who understand the cultural experimentation of the 1920s as diverse, creative, and unequal will be prepared to see that the later narrowing of cultural life was a historical process, not a foregone conclusion.
