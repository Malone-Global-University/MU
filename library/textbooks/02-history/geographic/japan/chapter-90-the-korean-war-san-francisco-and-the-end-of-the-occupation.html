The Korean War, San Francisco, and the End of the Occupation

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On September 8, 1951, Japan's prime minister signed two treaties in San Francisco on the same day, in two different buildings. The first, signed in the city's opera house before delegates of many nations, would legally end the state of war when it came into force. The second, signed afterward at a military post, arranged for American forces to stay. Together they defined what “independence” would mean for Japan.

The two signatures are a useful way into this chapter because they capture its central problem. When the treaties entered into force on April 28, 1952, the Allied occupation of Japan's main islands ended, the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers (SCAP) and his headquarters ceased to govern, and Japan again conducted its own diplomacy. Yet American troops remained on Japanese soil under a treaty Japan had not negotiated as an equal, and Okinawa and other islands remained under American administration. The chapter asks what kind of sovereignty Japan recovered in 1952, and how military dependence, economic recovery, the Cold War, and the unresolved legacies of empire shaped its limits.

The road from occupation to the San Francisco settlement ran through a war in Korea. When North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel on June 25, 1950, Japan was still governed by SCAP, still suffering from the deflationary Dodge Line (Chapter 89), and still debating how far the “Reverse Course” would go. Within days American troops stationed in Japan were fighting on the Korean peninsula, and Japan became a rear base: a source of ports, airfields, hospitals, repair yards, trucks, textiles, and labor. American purchases, known in Japan as tokuju (special procurement), supplied dollars and demand that helped lift Japanese industry out of the slump. Chapter 91 will examine the high-growth economy that followed. This chapter examines the immediate effects and their limits, and insists on a point that Japanese memory sometimes blurs: Korea was not a neutral stage for Japanese recovery but a country devastated by a war whose human costs vastly exceeded Japan's economic gains.

The war also changed American strategy and Japanese institutions. As occupation divisions left for Korea, General Douglas MacArthur directed the Japanese government in July 1950 to create a National Police Reserve of 75,000 men and to enlarge the Maritime Safety Agency. The new force was described as a police body, but it carried infantry weapons and was trained by American officers, and it set off a durable argument about Article 9 of the 1947 Constitution (Chapter 84). In the autumn of 1950, Japanese minesweepers and their crews, operating under American command, cleared mines off the Korean coast, an episode kept quiet at the time and rarely recalled in later textbooks. The same months saw Washington decide that a peace treaty with Japan should be concluded quickly, on terms that tied Japan to the Western camp.

The people involved were not a single “Japan” facing a single “America.” Prime Minister Yoshida Shigeru sought independence at the lowest military and financial cost; John Foster Dulles sought a durable anti-communist alignment; British, Soviet, Chinese, Indian, Korean, Philippine, and Australian governments each pursued different aims and fears; Japanese socialists split over whether to accept a peace that left the country in an American security system; Okinawans, whose islands were excluded from the restored state, petitioned for return to Japan; and Koreans and Taiwanese living in Japan, who had been imperial subjects for decades, lost Japanese nationality by an administrative decision announced days before the treaty took effect. Each experienced “the end of the occupation” differently, and the chapter keeps those differences in view.

Two legal distinctions govern the discussion. Signing the two San Francisco treaties in September 1951 did not itself end the occupation: that occurred when the treaties took effect in April 1952. Nor did Article 3 of the peace treaty place Okinawa into an actual United Nations trusteeship. It granted the United States extensive administrative powers while leaving sovereignty and future disposition open to continuing diplomatic argument. The text therefore separates a government’s juridical authority from the practical distribution of land, military power, and citizenship rights.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually uneven. Treaties registered with the United Nations and published in diplomatic collections give exact legal wording. Japanese government reports, such as the Economic Stabilization Board's September 1950 report on special procurement preserved by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records (JACAR), document the economic stakes. The National Diet Library's modern-history materials reproduce MacArthur's July 1950 directive, and the Okinawa Prefectural Archives preserve the records of the Government of the Ryukyu Islands. The Foreign Relations of the United States series publishes American diplomatic reporting. Against these official sources stand memoirs, newspapers, and later research on those who left few documents: base-town workers, Korean residents of Japan, and Okinawans. Official statistics, especially, were compiled by agencies with their own definitions and purposes, and figures in this chapter are approximations unless the text says otherwise.

The relationship between Korea and Japan also requires care. Wartime procurement changed production and foreign-exchange earnings in Japan, but the destruction, displacement, and death inflicted on the Korean peninsula cannot be evaluated as a Japanese business opportunity alone. Japanese ports and workers, Korean civilians, American troops, Okinawan base communities, and former colonial subjects in Japan belonged to the same regional history but experienced radically different costs and benefits. Comparing their positions is part of the chapter’s method, not a supplement to its diplomatic narrative.

Chapter 90 closes Part XII. It builds on Chapter 82's account of SCAP, Chapter 84's constitutional reforms, Chapter 87's treatment of black markets and crime, and Chapter 89's account of the Reverse Course. It looks ahead to Chapter 91's economic miracle, Chapter 92's 1960 security-treaty crisis, and Chapter 99's Okinawan base politics. It avoids two simple stories: one in which the end of the occupation is a clean return to national independence, and one in which Japan simply became an American dependency. The evidence supports a more difficult conclusion, in which both descriptions are partly true.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the outbreak of the Korean War in June 1950 changed Japan's strategic position and the calculations of American and Japanese policymakers.
  2. Describe the roles Japan played as a rear base, including ports, airfields, repair facilities, hospitals, and supply, without treating Korea merely as a source of Japanese prosperity.
  3. Distinguish special procurement contracts from commercial exports and from completed earnings, and interpret the statistical evidence for the Korean War boom.
  4. Analyze who benefited and who did not from the wartime procurement economy, including workers, contractors, base communities, and Korean residents of Japan.
  5. Reconstruct the creation of the National Police Reserve in July–August 1950 and explain why its status was constitutionally contested.
  6. Evaluate positions on Article 9 and rearmament taken by Yoshida Shigeru, Ashida Hitoshi, the opposition parties, and American officials.
  7. Trace the negotiation of the San Francisco Peace Treaty, including the roles of Yoshida and Dulles and the disputes over China, Korea, and the Soviet Union.
  8. Interpret the territorial, reparations, and security provisions of the 1951 treaties and distinguish the Security Treaty of 1951 from its 1960 revision.
  9. Explain what ended and what continued on April 28, 1952, including the persistence of American bases.
  10. Assess Okinawa's status under Article 3, the difference between administration and sovereignty, and the political institutions of the Ryukyu Islands.
  11. Analyze how the peace settlement altered the nationality and legal status of Koreans and Taiwanese in Japan.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of the 1952 settlement and primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits.

Historical Context

By early 1950 the occupation was in its fifth year and its character had changed. The early program of demilitarization and democratization described in Chapters 82–84, which dissolved the armed forces, removed wartime leaders, drafted a new constitution, and reformed land, labor, and education, had given way to what historians call the Reverse Course (Chapter 89). American planners, alarmed by the Chinese Communist victory in 1949 and the Soviet atomic test of the same year, increasingly treated Japan as an industrial asset to be stabilized rather than a defeated enemy to be reformed. The Dodge Line of 1949, named for the Detroit banker Joseph Dodge, imposed a balanced budget, ended subsidies, and fixed the yen at 360 to the dollar. It broke inflation but produced bankruptcies, layoffs, and a deep sense of insecurity, and by spring 1950 many Japanese business leaders feared prolonged depression.

The occupation's political posture was also shifting. The Red Purge of 1950 removed suspected communists from newspapers, public bodies, and industry. The labor movement was being reorganized, and the General Council of Trade Unions of Japan (Sōhyō) was founded in July 1950. Yoshida Shigeru, who led the Liberal Party government, had worked for an early peace treaty since 1947 and had come to accept that Japan would have to pay for it in some form of security arrangement with the United States. Meanwhile many Japanese, including socialists and intellectuals, hoped for an “overall peace” with all former enemies, including the Soviet Union and China, and for a neutral Japan. These hopes were soon tested by the Cold War.

Korea itself had been a Japanese colony from 1910 until 1945. Its liberation was followed by division at the 38th parallel into Soviet and American zones and, in 1948, into two rival governments: the Republic of Korea in the south and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea in the north. Both claimed to be the legitimate government of the whole peninsula, and the border saw repeated clashes before June 1950. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans lived in Japan as a legacy of colonial migration and wartime mobilization (Chapters 62 and 79), and their political loyalties were divided between organizations aligned with each Korean government. The Korean War therefore touched Japanese society directly, not only through trade and bases.

Okinawa and the Ryukyus lay outside this narrative of occupation reform. After the Battle of Okinawa (Chapter 80), the islands were governed directly by the American military, and during the Korean War they became a major air and supply base. Their future status was one of the issues that Japanese and American negotiators would have to settle. Finally, the surrounding region was in flux: China's new communist government, a British government that had recognized it, a Soviet Union that had occupied the Kurils, and Southeast Asian states recently independent from colonial rule and fearful of a resurgent Japan all had stakes in the treaty.

This chapter draws on sources of several kinds, and students should keep their different strengths in mind. Treaties give precise language but say nothing about how they were experienced. Diplomatic papers reveal what negotiators said to one another, often with the aim of recording a particular position. Japanese economic reports describe the aggregates that officials chose to measure. The voices of workers, Koreans in Japan, and Okinawans appear mainly in petitions, local newspapers, memoirs, and later oral histories, and they require deliberate effort to recover.

Section 90.1 · Division I — War Next Door: Procurement, Logistics, and Economic Recovery

June 25, 1950: The Korean War and Japan's Strategic Position

In the early hours of June 25, 1950, North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel in a coordinated attack on the Republic of Korea. The invasion followed years of border clashes and of competing claims by two governments, each of which asserted that it alone represented Korea. Historians no longer treat the war as a mere outgrowth of great-power maneuver. Archival research in Moscow, Beijing, and Seoul has shown that Kim Il Sung pressed Stalin and Mao Zedong for approval, that Stalin gave a qualified assent, and that the conflict grew out of a civil struggle over the political future of a divided country as well as out of the Cold War. Korean political agency, in both the north and the south, drove events. So did earlier decisions of the great powers: the 1945 division of the peninsula had been a military convenience by the American and Soviet occupiers, not a Korean choice, and it had been made in the aftermath of thirty-five years of Japanese colonial rule.

The United States responded within days. President Harry Truman authorized air and naval support for South Korea, and the United Nations Security Council, whose Soviet delegate was boycotting its meetings over the question of China's seat, passed resolutions condemning the attack and recommending assistance to South Korea. A July 7 resolution asked for a unified command under the United States. General MacArthur, already the SCAP in Tokyo, became commander of the United Nations Command as well. The forces fighting under that flag were overwhelmingly American and South Korean, with contingents from Britain, the Commonwealth, Turkey, and other states. The war then passed through several phases that students should keep distinct: a North Korean advance to the Pusan perimeter in the summer of 1950; the American-led landing at Inchon on September 15, which turned the tide; a United Nations advance north of the parallel in October; the intervention of Chinese forces in late October and November, which forced a long retreat; and a bloody stalemate along roughly the line of the original border from 1951. Armistice negotiations began in July 1951, and an armistice was signed on July 27, 1953.

The human toll on the peninsula was catastrophic. Estimates vary and remain debated, but total deaths among Korean soldiers and civilians, north and south, are usually placed in the millions, and the destruction of cities, towns, and infrastructure, aided by massive aerial bombardment, left a generation of survivors who had lost homes and family members. Families were separated by the new line of control. Refugees moved in great numbers. This must be placed at the center of any account of Japan's relation to the war, because Japanese prosperity in these years drew on a conflict whose victims were overwhelmingly Korean. A chapter about Japan's recovery that treated Korea only as a market would reproduce the logic of the economic benefit rather than explain it.

Why did the war matter so much for Japan? The answer begins with geography. The Japanese islands lie only a few hundred kilometers from the Korean peninsula across the Korea Strait. Kyushu's ports and airfields were within easy reach of Pusan. At the beginning of the war the American Eighth Army had four divisions stationed in Japan as occupation forces, and they were among the closest sizable American units to Korea. Within days of the invasion, elements of the 24th Infantry Division were airlifted and shipped to the peninsula, where they met strong North Korean forces in July 1950; poorly equipped and thinly trained for combat, they suffered heavy losses in their first engagements. The other divisions followed. In a few weeks, the occupation army that had governed a defeated country was fighting a war a short distance away, and the American commander in Tokyo had become the commander of a coalition.

Japan's importance was also industrial. Despite wartime destruction and postwar shortages, it retained a large stock of factories, railways, shipyards, ports, and skilled labor. These capacities lay close to the fighting, and they could be tapped far more rapidly and cheaply than by shipping equipment across the Pacific. American planners had by 1949 already begun to regard a stable, industrial Japan as a strategic asset in Asia; the war confirmed that judgment and gave it immediate practical meaning. In economic terms the effect was a sudden injection of demand. In strategic terms it was a reminder that Japan, though still under occupation and formally without armed forces, was already a participant in the Cold War.

Japanese society experienced the war's outbreak with mixed feelings. Newspapers reported on it after the end of systematic CCD censorship in October 1949, but under continuing occupation political influence and pressures, and many Japanese feared being drawn into a new conflict. Memories of 1945 were fresh, and the idea that Japan might again be a target or a belligerent was alarming. Others, especially in business and government, quickly recognized the possibility of an economic revival. Korean residents of Japan watched events with particular intensity, since the war divided their communities and threatened their relatives. Behind both reactions stood the fact that Japan's decisions about the war had been made elsewhere: Japanese officials were asked to cooperate with American requirements rather than to determine them. That asymmetry, between a Japan that provided bases and labor and a United States that set strategy, anticipated the structure of the alliance defined in 1951.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Rear Base, Logistics, and the United Nations Command

Logistics in military usage means the movement, supply, maintenance, and support of forces: ports and shipping, railways and trucks, fuel, ammunition, repair shops, hospitals, communications, and the labor that runs them. A rear base (in Japanese contexts sometimes kōhō kichi) is an area behind the fighting that provides such support. Japan's role in 1950–1953 was that of a rear base, not a combatant: its territory and economy supported the war while its government was formally subordinate to SCAP, and later, to a treaty-based American presence.

The United Nations Command was the unified military command authorized by the Security Council in July 1950 and led by the United States. Despite its name, it was in practice an American-dominated command, though many states contributed forces. Its headquarters were in Tokyo, which is why Japanese ports, airfields, and rear-area facilities served a United Nations war. Students should note that Japan was not a United Nations member at this time, and that the “UN” label did not make the Japanese government a party to the Command. Later chapters on Japan's relations with the world will return to this ambiguity.

Section 90.2 · Division I

Japan as a Rear Base: Troop Movements, Ports, Aircraft, and Repair Services

The practical business of turning Japan into a rear base began almost at once. American forces had occupied large numbers of buildings, ports, and airfields since 1945, and these facilities required little adaptation. Soldiers and supplies moved by rail and road to embarkation ports such as Yokohama, Kobe, Sasebo, Moji, and Hakata, and from there by ship across the Korea Strait. Sasebo and Yokosuka served as major naval bases, and Kure in Hiroshima Prefecture hosted British Commonwealth forces who staged through Japan on their way to Korea. Airfields at Itazuke near Fukuoka, Ashiya, Yokota near Tokyo, and others supported fighters, bombers, and transports, while Kadena on Okinawa became a major base for strategic bombers. For months, the air war over Korea was directed largely from Japanese and Okinawan airfields.

Equipment repair was an early and important industrial function. The war consumed vehicles, weapons, and aircraft at a high rate, and returning them to service quickly required shops and skilled workers. American units used Japanese factories, arsenals, and former military depots for rebuilding trucks, tanks, aircraft engines, and artillery, and they hired Japanese firms and workers to do much of the work under American supervision. Army depots near Tokyo and Yokohama and the Kokura area of northern Kyushu became centers of this activity. The existence of such repair capacity within a few days' shipping time of Korea gave Japan a distinct advantage over alternative suppliers, and it allowed the American military to sustain the campaign without waiting for deliveries from the United States.

Transportation and shipping likewise relied on Japanese labor. Stevedores loaded and unloaded cargo at the ports. Japanese railway workers carried supplies and troops. Japanese seamen crewed vessels chartered by the American military, including some that took part in landings and resupply operations on the Korean coast, and thousands of Japanese civilians worked directly for American units in various capacities. Hospitals in Japan, including American military hospitals in the Tokyo area and elsewhere, received the wounded who were evacuated from Korea, and Japanese doctors, nurses, and staff worked in support roles. The involvement of Japanese personnel in activities close to or in the war zone was not publicized at the time. As Section 90.8 explains, it raised legal and constitutional questions that the government preferred not to address in public.

Logistical capacity also had to be managed rather than simply switched on. A port with surviving cranes could still be immobilized by shortages of rail wagons, spare parts, or skilled stevedores; an airfield with a usable runway still needed fuel delivery, maintenance crews, and weather services. American officers could set wartime priorities, but Japanese ministries, railway administrators, private shipping agents, port employers, and local governments had to organize much of the everyday movement. This divided administration is visible in contemporary American supply reporting, which describes Japanese contributions to repair, transport, and communications rather than only factory production. In practical terms, a military order might pass through several public and private institutions before a truck was rebuilt or a shipment reached the docks. Such a process could restore civilian work while also transferring scarce materials and labor toward military users. The question for historians is therefore not simply how much material Japan provided, but how wartime priorities changed access to transport, wages, maintenance facilities, and usable industrial space for people outside the procurement system.

Communications, construction, and services extended the pattern. American forces needed telephone and telegraph links, barracks, warehouses, laundry and food services, and recreational facilities for large numbers of soldiers. Japanese contractors built or repaired these facilities and provided supplies. These activities were less dramatic than a tank factory, but they involved many firms and workers and flowed into the wider economy. Section 90.3 turns to the way these demands were organized into contracts.

The military's presence also shaped the social geography of Japan, as Section 90.5 explains. Base towns near Tokyo, Yokosuka, Sasebo, and Kokura filled with soldiers on leave, and their economies reorganized around the needs and spending of American personnel. The soldiers' presence involved bars, clubs, and sex work, as well as legitimate trades, and it generated friction and violence. Chapter 86 has examined the relations between American servicemen and Japanese women; the Korean War intensified them by sending large numbers of troops through Japan on rest and recuperation.

Cause and Effect

The Korean War and the Transformation of Japan's Security and Industrial Role

Long-term causes

The Cold War division of Asia after 1945; the Chinese Communist victory of 1949; Japan's location and industrial base; the American shift toward the Reverse Course; the division of Korea at the 38th parallel after Japanese colonial rule; the deflationary Dodge Line of 1949.

Short-term causes

The North Korean invasion of June 25, 1950; the presence of American occupation divisions in Japan; the Security Council resolutions placing the war under a United Nations Command led by the United States; the need for rapid repair, shipping, and supply.

Event or transformation

Japan became a rear base: its ports, airfields, factories, and labor supported the war, and the American occupation army left for the fighting.

Immediate effects

Special procurement orders and export demand; creation of the National Police Reserve to fill the security gap; Japanese minesweepers in Korean waters; acceleration of peace-treaty planning; the social impact of large numbers of soldiers on leave.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The war helped shape the economic recovery examined in Chapter 91 and the security arrangement defined in the 1951 treaties. But it did not “cause” the economic miracle: Japan's recovery drew on prewar industrial capacity, occupation reforms, American aid, and domestic decisions, and the boom of 1950–1951 was followed by a downturn. Nor did it simply impose rearmament on Japan: Japanese officials resisted, adapted, and interpreted American demands. The war's ethical significance also cannot be reduced to its economic effects, because Korea bore its human costs.
Section 90.3 · Division I

The Special Procurement Economy: Contracts, Dollars, Businesses, and the State

The Japanese term tokuju, short for “special demand” or “special procurement,” refers to purchases of goods and services in Japan by American military and related agencies. It was not new in 1950. The occupation had always bought local goods and services, and a share of the occupation's costs had been paid by the Japanese government. What changed after June 1950 was the scale, urgency, and composition of the orders. Officials in the Quartermaster Corps, the engineers, the ordnance services, and the Navy placed orders for the items a large campaign needed, and the Japanese government and its agencies acted as intermediaries between the military and the firms that would fill them. The early orders were for relatively simple items: sandbags, blankets, tents, uniforms, cloth, and barbed wire; repair and construction work; trucks; and other equipment. Later orders included more specialized machinery and munitions-related goods.

The mechanisms deserve explanation, because they shaped who benefited. American units generally paid in dollars, which Japanese authorities collected and credited to the central bank's foreign-exchange accounts, so that the receipts counted as foreign exchange earnings. Japanese firms received yen at the official rate. Contracts were let by American officers but frequently filtered through Japanese ministries, trade associations, and procurement offices, giving established firms and those with connections to the occupation an advantage. Large trading companies, which had been broken up by the occupation's reforms and were reforming in new forms, acted as middlemen. Textile firms, steelmakers, machine builders, construction companies, shipbuilders, and truck manufacturers all sought contracts. Toyota, which had faced near-collapse and a bitter labor dispute in spring 1950, is commonly cited as a firm that was rescued by American military truck orders, and other automobile makers received similar help. Such cases should be understood as examples within a larger pattern rather than as a story of one industry.

The Japanese government actively promoted the system. Officials of the Economic Stabilization Board, the Ministry of International Trade and Industry, and the Bank of Japan monitored orders, allocated materials, and sought to ensure that firms could meet requirements. Because the orders came in dollars and because Japan was short of foreign exchange, officials welcomed them as a means of financing imports of raw materials and food. They also saw an opportunity to use the demand to restore industrial capacity that the Dodge Line had left idle. This was not a free market in the textbook sense. The occupation retained wide authority, and Japanese policy shaped how orders were filled, how scarce materials were allocated, and how foreign exchange was managed.

Historians draw an important distinction among three quantities that popular accounts often merge. The first is procurement contracts: the value of orders placed, which indicates demand but not completed delivery. The second is procurement receipts: the dollars actually paid, which enter the balance of payments as earnings from services and goods supplied to American forces. The third is commercial exports: the sales of Japanese goods to private foreign buyers, which also surged during the war because of the global rearmament boom and rising prices. These measures overlap in their causes but not in their definitions, and adding them together can double-count or obscure the sources of growth. The Source Window below uses one of the first, and the Data Study in Section 90.4 sets the series side by side.

Finally, the tokuju system raised questions about dependence. The dollars helped Japan pay for imports and eased the foreign-exchange constraint that had held back recovery, but they also tied part of the economy to American military decisions. When orders fell after the armistice, as the later chapters of this book note, Japanese officials and business leaders had to consider how to sustain growth without them. The Korean War boom was real, but its meaning for the long-run economy is an interpretive question, not a fact that the statistics settle by themselves.

Section 90.4 · Division I

Recovery with Qualifications: Export Growth, Production, Foreign Exchange, and Prices

The economic effects of the war on Japan can be described in several dimensions, each with qualifications. The first is output. Industrial production, which had fallen far below prewar levels at the end of the war and recovered unevenly in 1946–1949, rose strongly in 1950 and 1951. Japanese industrial output regained or surpassed its prewar peak, usually measured against the average of 1934–1936, during 1951, and gross national product recovered to prewar levels in the same period. Textiles, steel, machinery, chemicals, and construction all expanded. Observers in the early 1950s spoke of a “string boom” (ito-hen keiki) in cotton and synthetic textiles and a “metal boom” (kinzoku-hen keiki) in steel and nonferrous metals, reflecting the commodities in demand. Capacity that had stood idle during the Dodge slump was brought back into use.

The second dimension is foreign exchange. Before the war Japan had struggled to earn the dollars it needed for imports of cotton, oil, coal, iron ore, and food. Special procurement receipts supplemented exports and allowed imports to grow, and that eased the balance-of-payments constraint on recovery. In some years, procurement receipts equaled a substantial fraction of the value of commercial exports. At the same time, rising raw-material prices raised the cost of imports, and Japan's trade deficit with the dollar area remained a concern. The Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Control Law and related controls gave the government tools to manage the allocation of scarce foreign exchange, and the creation of institutions such as the Export-Import Bank of Japan and the Japan Development Bank gave it ways to channel credit toward preferred industries. These measures were not caused by the war, but the war made them more effective by increasing the foreign exchange they could allocate.

The third dimension is prices and distribution. The war triggered a global commodities boom, as governments stockpiled raw materials, and prices of imported materials rose sharply in 1950 and early 1951. Japanese wholesale prices climbed rapidly, and consumer prices rose more slowly but noticeably. The boom was followed, once the early panic of stockpiling faded and armistice talks began in mid-1951, by a downturn in some commodity markets and a slowdown in parts of Japanese industry, including textiles. In other words, the war did not produce a straight line of growth. Firms that had expanded on the assumption of continued high prices faced losses, and the benefits were unevenly distributed, as Section 90.5 explains.

The relationship between the war boom and the Dodge Line deserves care. The Dodge Line had stabilized prices and balanced the budget by cutting demand, and it had produced a recession whose severity is debated by economists. The war arrived while the Japanese economy was in the trough of that recession. It thus provided demand at precisely the point at which Japanese policymakers needed it, and it allowed the stabilization program to be judged a success rather than a failure. Some historians therefore argue that the war “rescued” the Dodge Line, while others emphasize that recovery was already under way in early 1950 and that the war accelerated rather than created it. The evidence, including monthly production and export figures, supports a view in which both elements mattered: the war was a powerful accelerant of a recovery already in progress, and it changed the political meaning of that recovery.

The statistical evidence is best read as several overlapping but nonidentical series. An increase in nominal commercial exports may reflect higher world prices as well as greater physical quantities; a contract signed for military supplies may never yield a payment of equal amount in the same year. A contemporary U.S. assessment reported Japanese commercial exports averaging roughly $70 million per month in July–September 1950 and $125 million in April–June 1951. That comparison suggests a marked expansion but cannot isolate the Korean War's independent contribution without commodity-level values, prices, destinations, and physical volumes. The same assessment gave Korean War procurement of approximately $25 million per month in its reporting context. Researchers should not add this rate to the export averages as though they formed a single homogeneous series. A more convincing interpretation must combine the official balances with Japanese firm records, port traffic, employment patterns, and changes in imported material costs. Statistics are indispensable here, but the categories used to compile them are historical decisions in their own right.

It is important not to equate economic recovery with prosperity for all or with the war's justification. Living standards recovered slowly, and real wages lagged behind output. Housing remained scarce, and the country was still marked by wartime destruction, as Chapter 85 described. The war's contribution to growth was real, but it was also one input among several: occupation land reform had increased rural purchasing power, the labor reforms had changed industrial relations, American aid had financed imports through 1951, and the Japanese state continued to guide investment. Section 90.5 · Division I

Who Benefited? Workers, Port Cities, Contractors, and Korean Residents in Japan

The benefits of the war economy were distributed unevenly. The greatest gains went to firms that held or obtained procurement contracts and to those in industries with surging demand, and to the banks, trading companies, and officials who connected them. Large manufacturers recovered capacity and profits. Small and medium-sized subcontractors benefited from orders passed down the chain, though often on thin margins and with the risk that orders would vanish. Entrepreneurs with connections to the occupation, and former military men with technical skills, found new opportunities. In some cases, the reappearance of familiar names from the prewar industrial world in the contract lists was a sign of the broader continuity that the Reverse Course had allowed, a continuity that Chapter 89 examined in the context of the zaibatsu's partial reconstitution.

For workers, the picture was mixed. The boom reduced unemployment in many industries and created new jobs in repair shops, shipyards, ports, and construction. It also encouraged migration to industrial regions. But the political climate was unfavorable to labor. The Red Purge of 1950 had removed activists from many workplaces, and the formation of the Sōhyō federation in July 1950 reflected a labor movement that was reorganizing under pressure and was divided over the war and the peace treaty. Real wages lagged behind rising prices, and many workers in procurement industries labored under American supervision with little bargaining power. Workers employed directly by American forces were governed by special arrangements and could be dismissed or reassigned without the protections that Japanese labor law afforded. Strikes and disputes in the following years reflected the tension between an economy that was growing and workers who felt that they had not shared equally in its gains.

Port cities and base towns changed in particular ways. Yokohama, Kobe, Sasebo, Moji, and the ports of Kyushu saw their harbors fill with ships, their warehouses with cargo, and their streets with soldiers. Merchants, landlords, and entertainment businesses in base towns prospered, while local residents bore the costs of crowding, noise, and conflict. The relationship between a base and its town combined economic dependence with resentment, a pattern that would recur in later decades and in Okinawa with particular intensity. Chapter 86 described the networks of bars, clubs, and sex work that surrounded the American military; the Korean War expanded them. Women who worked in these settings faced stigma and danger as well as income, and their experiences were seldom recorded in official reports.

Korean residents of Japan occupied an especially complicated position. Many were poor, concentrated in cities such as Osaka and Kobe, and employed in low-paid work, in scrap-metal trades, in small factories, and in black-market activity that the postwar crackdown had made precarious. Some benefited from the demand for scrap metal and manufactured goods, but they were also subject to discrimination in employment and housing. Their communities were divided between organizations aligned with the southern government, such as Mindan, and organizations aligned with the northern government and with the left. When war began, activists in some leftist groups opposed the war and the use of Japan as a base, and they faced police surveillance and repression. A smaller number of Korean residents volunteered to fight for South Korea. The Japanese government, meanwhile, was preparing legislation that would change the legal status of Koreans as part of the peace settlement, as Section 90.18 explains. For this population, the war was not a source of opportunity but a source of danger, division, and uncertainty about the future.

Some Japanese also paid directly for their government's cooperation. As Section 90.8 describes, Japanese seamen and minesweeper crews served in Korean waters, and casualties occurred. Their stories were long kept out of public discussion because of the political sensitivity of Japanese participation in a foreign war. The benefits and risks of the war economy were thus distributed among different people: owners and contractors, workers and townspeople, and those who were exposed to the danger of the war's front. Understanding the war's economic stimulus requires asking about all of these groups, not only about the macroeconomic aggregates.

Workers and Military-Base Communities, 1950–1952

A man in his thirties who had been demobilized in 1945 and spent the next years in irregular work might in 1950 find a job at a vehicle-repair depot or on the docks of a port city. His family might depend on his earnings and on the wages of a wife who worked in a laundry, a canteen, or a shop serving soldiers.

These ordinary situations show that the economic stimulus was not an abstraction: it altered the daily lives of people in particular places, with advantages and costs that were distributed unequally.

Section 90.6 · Division II — Security Institutions and the Article 9 Dilemma

July–August 1950: MacArthur Orders a National Police Reserve

When the American divisions left Japan for Korea in the summer of 1950, they left a gap. The occupation army that had been responsible for the security of the main islands was now committed elsewhere, and Japan had no armed forces of its own: the Imperial Army and Navy had been dissolved in 1945, the 1947 Constitution renounced war and the maintenance of war potential in Article 9, and the civil police, decentralized under occupation reforms, were organized for local order rather than national defense. American officials worried about internal disorder, about the possibility of infiltration, and, more broadly, about whether Japan could defend itself if the Soviet Union or China attacked. These concerns were partly exaggerated, but they were sincerely felt.

On July 8, 1950, MacArthur sent a letter to Prime Minister Yoshida Shigeru, which the National Diet Library's modern-history materials document. It authorized the Japanese government to establish a National Police Reserve (Keisatsu Yobitai) of 75,000 men and to increase the Maritime Safety Agency by 8,000 personnel. The directive was framed as a measure to strengthen Japan's ability to maintain internal order and to guard its coasts, not as a restoration of armed forces. It came as an order from the Supreme Commander, whom the Japanese government could not refuse, though Yoshida and some of his advisers had already considered ways to build a limited force. The letter did not specify in detail the force's equipment or organization, which the Japanese government and the American advisers then worked out. The Japanese government formally established the Police Reserve by ordinance on August 10, 1950.

The institutional design reflected the sensitivities of the moment. The reserve was placed under the National Rural Police and the civil-police system rather than under a defense ministry, and its members were called police officers rather than soldiers. Yet its organization soon resembled that of a light infantry force. Recruits were organized into units, trained in infantry tactics, and equipped with American-supplied weapons, including rifles, machine guns, and, as the force grew, heavier equipment. American officers, most prominently Colonel Frank Kowalski, played a major role in organizing and advising the new body. Recruits came from a society that had suffered terribly from war and was marked by unemployment, and many joined because of the regular pay and the chance of steady work, not out of enthusiasm for military service. The first units began training in autumn 1950.

A question of personnel also arose. The occupation had purged many former professional officers from public life as part of demilitarization, and the new force could not easily draw on their experience. American planners initially preferred a force led by officers without a record of prewar military leadership, and the early officers were largely drawn from police and civil backgrounds. Over the next two years, as the purge was eased and finally lifted, former officers entered the organization in growing numbers, and advocates of a stronger force pressed for greater continuity with the prewar military. The relation between the new force and the old army, and the role of former officers in shaping it, became a subject of continuing controversy.

The decision to create the Police Reserve raised an obvious contradiction with the occupation's earlier policy. In 1946 and 1947 the same occupation authorities had imposed, and had publicly praised, the renunciation of war in the new Constitution. Now they were authorizing a force that would, in practice, be trained and equipped as a military. MacArthur himself had said, in earlier years, that Japan's defense rested on the Constitution and on American protection, and he continued to describe the new measure in terms of police functions. The contradiction was one that both American and Japanese officials preferred to manage through careful language rather than to resolve in public. Section 90.7 explains how that management worked and why it provoked opposition.

Section 90.7 · Division II

Article 9, Yoshida, Ashida, and the Meaning of Rearmament

Article 9 of the 1947 Constitution renounces war as a sovereign right and the threat or use of force as a means of settling international disputes, and its second paragraph declares that land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will not be maintained. Its drafting, described in Chapter 84, involved both American and Japanese contributions. During the Diet's debate in 1946, a revision introduced by Ashida Hitoshi added a phrase at the beginning of the second paragraph linking it to the aim of the first. Ashida and others later argued that this addition was meant to leave room for self-defense, though at the time Yoshida had stated that the article denied even the right of self-defensive war, and the government's early interpretation was strict. The ambiguity of the text and the changing positions of its interpreters made Article 9 a contested document from the beginning.

The creation of the Police Reserve forced the government to explain how a force of tens of thousands, equipped with infantry weapons, could be reconciled with a constitution that forbade war potential. The official answer, developed in 1950–1952, was that the Police Reserve was not an army because its purpose was to maintain domestic order and because it lacked the capabilities of a modern military: it did not possess the heavy weapons, aircraft, and naval vessels that would constitute “war potential” in the sense of the Constitution. This definition was tied to the force's equipment and function rather than to its organization and training, and it left room for a gradual increase in capability that governments in later years would exploit. Critics regarded the explanation as a legal fiction, and some officials privately agreed that the line between a police reserve and an army was a matter of degree.

Yoshida's own position combined acceptance and resistance. He accepted the necessity of the Police Reserve as a matter of practical politics, since it came from MacArthur. He also resisted pressure for larger rearmament, arguing that Japan's economy could not bear the cost, that rapid rearmament would provoke domestic opposition and alarm neighbors who remembered the war, and that the country's resources should go first to reconstruction. This position is often associated with what later commentators called the “Yoshida line” or “Yoshida Doctrine,” a label applied afterward to a strategy of minimizing military spending, relying on American security guarantees, and concentrating on economic growth. Students should treat the term with caution. Yoshida did not announce a doctrine in 1950, and historians debate how consistently he or his successors followed such a strategy. What the evidence shows is a prime minister making pragmatic decisions in response to American demands and domestic constraints.

American officials, for their part, wanted more. In January 1951 John Foster Dulles came to Tokyo and urged the Japanese government to commit to a substantial rearmament. Reports of American discussions of a much larger force than the Police Reserve circulated, and Yoshida resisted, citing economic limits and political risk. The outcome was a compromise: Japan would increase its security forces gradually, and the details would be left to future negotiation, while the peace treaty and security treaty would affirm the principle that Japan could participate in collective security arrangements and that American forces would remain. The Japanese government thereby avoided committing itself to a large army while accepting the principle of increased responsibility.

The domestic opposition was serious. The Japan Socialist Party, whose left wing was especially hostile, opposed rearmament, and the Japanese Communist Party opposed both the Police Reserve and the American alliance. Intellectuals, unions, and peace organizations argued that Japan should remain neutral and demilitarized, and that the Constitution represented a national commitment to peace. The Sōhyō federation, which had initially been created with the support of the occupation as an anti-communist alternative, moved in the early 1950s toward a more militant position that included opposition to rearmament and to the security treaty. At the same time, conservative politicians, including some in Yoshida's own party and in opposition parties, argued that Japan needed real armed forces and that Article 9 should be revised. This disagreement produced a long argument, which continues in the present century, about whether to interpret Article 9 as permitting a self-defense force or to amend it.

Ashida's role illustrates how positions evolved. In 1947, as foreign minister, he had written a memorandum, often called the Ashida Memorandum, suggesting that the Japanese government might request the continued presence of American forces for defense as a voluntary arrangement; this concept anticipated the bases-for-security bargain of 1951. After the war began in Korea, Ashida, no longer in office, argued publicly that Article 9 did not prohibit forces for self-defense and that Japan should build them. The use of his amendment in such arguments shows how interpretation of the Constitution changed with circumstances: the phrase inserted in 1946 was later read as the key to a right of self-defense, though the original intention remains a matter of historical debate. The meaning of Article 9 in 1950 was therefore not settled by the text; it was made by political decisions about how to apply it.

Section 90.8 · Division II

Japanese Minesweepers in Korean Waters: An Exceptional Military-Adjacent Mission

One of the most revealing episodes of the war involved Japanese participation in mine-clearing off the Korean coast. Mines had been laid in large numbers in Korean waters, many of them supplied by the Soviet Union and laid by North Korean forces, and they threatened the landings and naval operations that the United Nations Command planned. The American Navy had few minesweepers in the region and urgently needed more. Japan, by contrast, had a large number of minesweeping craft and trained crews, because the Japanese Navy had retained its minesweepers after 1945 to clear the thousands of mines laid in Japanese waters during the war. By 1948 this work had been placed under the new Maritime Safety Agency, an organization intended to function as a coast guard.

In October 1950, the American Navy requested Japanese minesweepers for operations off the Korean coast, and vessels of the Maritime Safety Agency, along with their crews, sailed for Korea. The deployment took place under American command and in great secrecy. The Japanese government did not announce the mission, and the crews were told little. They worked first near Wonsan on the east coast, where mines delayed the landing of United Nations forces, and in other areas. The work was dangerous. One of the vessels struck a mine and was lost, and at least one crew member was killed, according to commonly cited accounts. Other personnel were injured. The service ended in December 1950, as the Chinese intervention reshaped the war, and the ships returned to Japan.

The number of Japanese vessels involved is a good example of why historians must pay attention to scope. JACAR's research describes a Japanese minesweeping force of about twenty minesweepers in the broader operation, including vessels used in different areas and tasks over the period. American naval histories, by contrast, identify eight Japanese minesweepers participating at Wonsan. The difference does not necessarily mean that one account is wrong. The figures refer to different scopes: one to the entire Japanese contribution over the whole period and across the different operations, the other to the specific operation at one port. When historians compare numbers, they must ask what each source was counting, over what period, and for what purpose.

The dangers were not hypothetical. During minesweeping operations off Wonsan, the Japanese minesweeper MS-14 struck a mine on October 17, 1950. United States naval historical reporting records one Japanese crew member killed and eighteen injured. These casualties belong to the Japanese operation and must not be confused with the separate American minesweeper losses suffered in the same Korean coastal campaign. The distinctions matter beyond numerical accuracy: the names of vessels, dates of accidents, and command relationships can be established more securely than the precise experience of every individual aboard. For crew members and their families, a mission that remained obscure in public discussion involved risk comparable to other hazardous naval duties. The event complicates retrospective descriptions of Japan as wholly outside the Korean War. Yet a minesweeping detachment operating under occupation direction did not make the Japanese government a formal belligerent or resolve the constitutional question of military activity. The mission demonstrates the gap between the legal label placed on an institution and the work its personnel could be required to perform.

The legal questions raised by the mission were serious, and they explain the secrecy. Sending government vessels with armed crews, even if they were minesweepers rather than warships, into a war zone appeared to conflict with Article 9 and with the occupation's own regime. It also placed Japanese personnel at risk in a conflict in which Japan was not formally a belligerent. Japanese officials justified the mission as a measure under SCAP's authority and as a continuation of the Maritime Safety Agency's mine-clearing duties, and they emphasized that the crews were civilians employed by a civil agency. The American command, for its part, treated the Japanese vessels as part of the forces available to it. Neither description fit comfortably within the postwar constitutional order, which is why the episode remained little known to the Japanese public for decades.

The minesweeping mission illustrates several broader themes. It shows the extent to which Japanese government agencies operated under American direction in 1950, with limited ability to refuse. It shows that Japanese personnel were exposed to the war's dangers, which complicates the image of Japan as a beneficiary at a safe distance. It shows how the language of civil administration, such as “safety agency” and “police reserve,” could conceal military functions. And it shows the importance of sources: much of what is known about the mission comes from later research and from the recollections of participants, which require careful use because they were recorded long after the events and often in the context of debates about Japan's security policy. Section 90.9 · Division II

From Police Reserve toward National Safety Institutions

The Police Reserve did not remain a fixed institution. Over 1951 and early 1952 its size and character changed, and the transition to a new organizational form was planned before the peace treaty took effect. Understanding the sequence requires distinguishing events that occurred under the occupation from those that came after April 28, 1952.

Under the occupation, the Police Reserve was enlarged beyond its initial authorization and its training became more military. American advisers arranged the delivery of weapons and vehicles, and units were organized into larger formations. At the same time, the Maritime Safety Agency created a small maritime force, often called the Maritime Guard (Kaijō Keibitai), in April 1952, a few days before the treaty took effect. This body was organized within the Maritime Safety Agency and was formed in anticipation of the transition to independence. The timing demonstrates that planners treated the new security institutions as part of the same process that produced the peace settlement, and that they wished to have organizational arrangements in place before American forces reduced their direct role.

The dates of the transition are especially revealing. The Maritime Guard was established within the Maritime Safety Agency on April 26, 1952, two days before the treaties entered into force. The National Safety Agency began functioning on August 1, 1952, after the July 31 promulgation of its enabling legislation. On that August date the maritime organization was reorganized as the Coastal Safety Force; the land National Safety Force did not begin until October 15, 1952, when the Police Reserve was reorganized. These events should not be collapsed into a single August formation. Japan's Ministry of Defense preserves the separate steps in its institutional chronology. The staggered schedule reflected decisions about personnel, administration, and service organization as well as broader constitutional debate. By identifying which measures preceded sovereignty and which followed it, historians can distinguish occupation instructions from legislation and administration adopted by the restored Japanese state. Institutional continuity was strong, but the legal source of authority had changed.

After April 28, 1952, the question of Japan's military institutions became one of Japanese law and policy within the treaty framework. The Security Treaty's preamble expressed the American expectation that Japan would increasingly assume responsibility for its own defense, and the Japanese government, facing both American pressure and domestic opposition, moved cautiously. Legislation promulgated on July 31, 1952 established the National Safety Agency (Hoanchō), which opened on August 1; the maritime organization entered the new agency as the Coastal Safety Force. The Police Reserve was reorganized as the land-based National Safety Force (Hoantai) only on October 15, 1952, not in August. The change of names reflected a shift in function from internal order toward a broader security role, though the government continued to insist that these bodies were not “war potential” within the meaning of Article 9. The Safety Force was larger than the Police Reserve and began to receive heavier equipment.

The final step in this institutional sequence lies beyond this chapter's chronological scope. In 1954, after a further American mutual-security agreement and legislative action, the National Safety Agency was reorganized as the Defense Agency and the forces became the Ground, Maritime, and Air Self-Defense Forces. Chapters 92 and 99 will return to the later development of these forces and to the continuing constitutional controversy about them. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that the roots of the Self-Defense Forces were laid in 1950–1952, within a framework that combined American pressure, Japanese caution, and constitutional ambiguity.

A final distinction concerns command and accountability. Police Reserve recruits and officers were subject to rules that emphasized civilian oversight, yet their instructors, weapons, organization, and emerging missions increasingly resembled those of a defense force. Officials who preferred the designation 'police' stressed domestic security and argued that a limited, defensive organization did not amount to prohibited war potential. Critics countered that its equipment and increasingly coordinated formations made the label evasive. Their disagreement was not merely rhetorical: under the new constitution the Diet controlled appropriations and legislation, and citizens were entitled to debate the purposes for which armed public personnel might be deployed. The institutions created in 1950–1952 therefore became a recurring test of whether constitutional restraint could shape security policy when the alliance partner expected expanding military capability. That tension endured after the formal occupation and became central to Japan's later defense politics.

The institutional story also contains questions of civil-military relations. Many Japanese feared that a new military would revive the prewar pattern in which the armed forces had dominated politics. The decision to place the Police Reserve and its successors under civilian agencies, rather than under an independent ministry, and the continued presence of American advisers, were partly attempts to prevent such a revival. At the same time, the gradual return of former officers and the creation of professional institutions gave the new forces continuity with the past. Historians continue to debate how much the postwar forces departed from the prewar military and how much they inherited its personnel, practices, and values. The question belongs to the larger problem, addressed throughout Part XII, of how much changed and how much persisted in the transition from empire to postwar state. Section 90.10 · Division III — Negotiating Peace and the Security Settlement

Who Wanted Peace, and on What Conditions? Yoshida, Dulles, and U.S. Strategy

By 1950 almost everyone involved agreed that the occupation could not last indefinitely, but they disagreed about what should replace it. For the Japanese government, an early treaty restored sovereignty, ended the humiliations of foreign control, and allowed Japan to rejoin the international community. For American officials, a treaty offered a way to secure Japan's alignment with the West and to relieve the United States of the costs and political burdens of governing a foreign country. The question was on what terms. Earlier American plans for a punitive or demilitarizing peace, discussed in the first years of the occupation, had given way to a plan for a lenient settlement that preserved a continuing American military role.

Yoshida Shigeru had worked for an early peace since the late 1940s, and his thinking about its terms evolved. He understood that the United States would insist on military arrangements, and by 1950 he was willing to accept American bases as the price of independence, preferring that outcome to a prolonged occupation or to a peace that depended on Soviet and Chinese consent. In 1950 he sent Finance Minister Ikeda Hayato to Washington, with a message that Japanese officials might offer American bases if Washington wished to propose them, so that the initiative would appear to come from the United States. The approach reflected a long-standing Japanese diplomatic technique of letting the other side make a request that could then be accepted as a concession. Yoshida's calculation was that the bases would be tolerable, that economic recovery would be better served by limited armaments, and that the alternative, an overall peace that included the Soviet Union and China, was unlikely to be achievable.

John Foster Dulles, a Republican lawyer and diplomat appointed by Truman's Democratic administration as a bipartisan consultant on the treaty, led the American effort. He was committed to anti-communism, to a Pacific security system that would link Japan to the United States, and to a treaty that was not so harsh as to breed resentment, as he believed the Treaty of Versailles had done. In the spring of 1950, even before the war began, Dulles had been working on a treaty plan, and the outbreak of war gave him added leverage and urgency. In September 1950 Truman authorized negotiations. In November the American government circulated a statement of principles for the treaty, which included a limited program of territorial changes, no limits on Japanese rearmament, no reparations in the traditional sense, and the continued stationing of American forces at Japan's request.

Dulles traveled to Tokyo in January 1951, and his meetings with Yoshida were the decisive stage in the bilateral bargain. He pressed for a substantial Japanese rearmament, and Yoshida resisted, arguing that the economy could not support a large force, that Japanese public opinion was not ready, and that Asian neighbors would be alarmed. The talks produced an understanding that Japan would expand its forces gradually and that American forces would remain. Dulles also visited Seoul, Manila, Canberra, and other capitals to address the concerns of states that feared Japanese revival, and the United States concluded security pacts with the Philippines (August 30, 1951) and with Australia and New Zealand (the ANZUS Treaty, signed September 1, 1951) to reassure them. These arrangements, together with the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty, are often described by scholars as forming the “San Francisco System”: a set of bilateral and small multilateral alliances centered on the United States, which linked Japan to the West while giving its former victims some protection against it.

The British government was a key partner and a source of difficulty. Britain had recognized the People's Republic of China in January 1950, while the United States continued to recognize the Republic of China on Taiwan, and the two governments disagreed over which Chinese government should participate in the treaty. Britain also had different views about Japanese trade competition, a sensitive question for British industry, and about the treatment of its own prisoners of war. The Anglo-American talks, culminating in a joint draft of June 1951, produced compromises. Neither Chinese government would be invited, and Japan would be free to decide after regaining sovereignty which to deal with. The formula settled the immediate problem while leaving the larger question for later.

The domestic American politics of the treaty also mattered. Republican senators, many of whom attacked the Truman administration for “losing China,” would not approve a treaty that seemed to favor Beijing, and Dulles's bipartisan role was designed to secure their support. The dismissal of MacArthur in April 1951, after a dispute over the conduct of the war in Korea, removed the commander who had governed Japan and replaced him with General Matthew Ridgway, who would oversee the transition. The change symbolized the end of an era but did not alter the treaty's course. By mid-1951, the main outline of the settlement was clear: a lenient peace, American bases, a limited Japanese military, and exclusion of the communist powers.

Section 90.11 · Division III

A Separate Peace: China, the Soviet Union, Korea, India, and Asian Criticism

The treaty that emerged is often called a “separate” peace (tandoku kōwa) because it was concluded with the states of the Western bloc and without a Soviet signature or representation by either Chinese government. The Japanese public debated the alternative, an “overall” peace (zenmen kōwa), which would have included all former enemies. Advocates of an overall peace, among them many socialists, intellectuals, and some conservatives, argued that a separate peace would lock Japan into the Western camp and make future reconciliation with the Soviet Union and China impossible. Yoshida and the government replied that an overall peace was unrealistic under Cold War conditions and that further delay would prolong the occupation. This debate, conducted in newspapers and public meetings in 1950–1951, was one of the major public discussions of the postwar period.

The exclusion of China was the most consequential omission. The People's Republic had been founded in October 1949 and had fought American forces in Korea since late 1950. The Republic of China on Taiwan claimed to be China's legitimate government but had no effective control of the mainland. The Chinese Communist government denounced the proposed treaty, and Premier and Foreign Minister Zhou Enlai issued a statement in August 1951 condemning the draft as illegal in the absence of Chinese participation. Neither government attended San Francisco. The compromise left Japan to decide its relation with China later, and under American pressure, Yoshida wrote a letter to Dulles in December 1951 stating that Japan would conclude a treaty with the Nationalist government. That agreement, the Treaty of Taipei, was signed on April 28, 1952, the same day the San Francisco treaties entered into force, and it entered into force in August 1952. Japan's relations with the People's Republic would not be normalized until 1972 (Chapter 96 and later).

The Soviet Union attended the conference but refused to sign. Its delegation, led by Deputy Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko, proposed amendments that included recognition of Chinese sovereignty over Taiwan and nearby islands, Soviet sovereignty over southern Sakhalin and the Kurils, limits on Japanese armaments, and the withdrawal of foreign troops within ninety days. These were rejected, and the Soviet delegation, along with those of Czechoslovakia and Poland, did not sign. Japan and the Soviet Union technically remained in a state of war until the Joint Declaration of 1956, and the dispute over the southern Kuril Islands and the territorial question that the treaty left unresolved became a long-term source of tension. The Ainu and other indigenous communities of Sakhalin and the Kurils, already displaced by the war, were not consulted about these arrangements.

Korea's position was ambiguous. The Republic of Korea, led by Syngman Rhee, wanted to participate as a signatory and to receive compensation as a victim of Japanese colonial rule. The United States and Britain refused, on the ground that Korea had not been at war with Japan, since it had been a Japanese colony, and because of questions about the recognition of the two Korean governments. Korea was thus excluded as a signatory, though the treaty included provisions regarding Korean interests: Japan recognized Korean independence and renounced its claims, and it agreed to separate negotiations on property and claims. These negotiations began in late 1951 and lasted until the normalization treaty of 1965. Korean frustration at exclusion contributed to the persistent mistrust between the two countries, and the question of how to understand colonial rule, as an act of war or as a legal annexation, remained disputed.

Other Asian states responded differently. India declined to attend the conference, with Prime Minister Nehru objecting that the treaty limited Japanese sovereignty, left American forces in Japan, and failed to return Taiwan to China or satisfy Indian views on the Ryukyus and Bonins; India made a separate peace treaty with Japan in 1952. Burma and Yugoslavia also declined to attend. The Philippines and Indonesia, which had suffered heavily under Japanese occupation, attended and signed, but were uneasy about the provisions on reparations, as Section 90.14 explains. Australia and New Zealand, worried about a resurgent Japan, accepted the treaty on the condition of the ANZUS security pact. For all these governments, the treaty required a calculation between fear of Japan, fear of communism, and dependence on the United States.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Sovereignty, Residual Sovereignty, Administration, and Entry into Force

Sovereignty in international law means a state's supreme authority within its territory and independence from external control. In 1945–1952 Japan's sovereignty was limited by the authority of the Allied occupation, exercised by SCAP. Residual sovereignty was the term American officials, notably Dulles at the 1951 conference, used to describe Japan's continuing underlying claim to territories placed under American administration: Japan retained a latent sovereignty, though the United States held the right to exercise governmental powers there. The term is not defined in the treaty itself, and its meaning was disputed.

Administering authority means the state that exercises governmental power over a territory without necessarily holding sovereignty over it. Trusteeship refers to the United Nations system of supervising territories under an administering state. Signature and entry into force are different legal moments: a treaty signed in September 1951 had no legal effect until it was ratified and entered into force, which for the San Francisco Peace Treaty happened on April 28, 1952. Students must keep these distinctions in view, because Japan's sovereignty was restored by entry into force, not by the signing.

Section 90.12 · Division III

San Francisco, September 4–8, 1951: Conference and Signing

The conference opened on September 4, 1951, in the War Memorial Opera House in San Francisco, with representatives of about fifty states in attendance. Secretary of State Dean Acheson presided, and the American delegation, which included Dulles, had arranged the proceedings so that a draft treaty, already negotiated among the principal powers, could be adopted with limited amendment. The American organizers expected the conference to ratify an agreed text rather than to negotiate a new one. This procedure drew protests from the Soviet delegation, which argued that the conference was being denied a real debate, and the rules were adopted over their objection.

The Japanese delegation was led by Yoshida and included Ikeda Hayato, Tomabechi Gizō, Hoshijima Niro, Tokugawa Muneyoshi, and Ichimada Hisato, among others, and it was composed of members from different political and business circles to demonstrate national unity. Yoshida spoke on September 7. He accepted the treaty and expressed gratitude for the generous terms, while using his speech to register Japan's view that certain islands belonged to Japan, notably the southern Kurils (which he described as Japanese) and the Ryukyus and Bonins, whose return he hoped for. He read the speech in Japanese from a long scroll of paper, a presentational detail that was widely reported. The speech illustrates the limited room for maneuver that Japan had: it could state its hopes, but it could not alter the text.

Gromyko's attempts to amend the draft failed, and the Soviet delegation did not sign. On September 8, in the opera house, representatives of Japan and forty-eight Allied powers signed the Treaty of Peace with Japan. The treaty's formal title and registration with the United Nations, and the fact that its authentic text exists in English, French, and Spanish, with a Japanese translation, are documented in the United Nations Treaty Collection. The ceremony was a major diplomatic event, and it appeared to mark the end of Japan's status as a defeated enemy. Many Japanese watched it with relief and hope, others with unease.

Later that day, in a different place, a second treaty was signed. At the Presidio, a military post in San Francisco, Yoshida and American representatives signed the Security Treaty between the United States and Japan. Yoshida signed alone for Japan, having decided that the responsibility should rest with him as prime minister and not with other delegates, who might face domestic criticism. The choice of a military base for the signing, rather than the opera house, has often been interpreted as symbolic: the security treaty was an arrangement that the United States had concluded with Japan and not a multilateral accord. The separation of the two ceremonies also reflected the political calculation that the American government wished to present the peace treaty as a multilateral settlement and the security treaty as a bilateral matter. Section 90.15 analyzes the two instruments together.

The choice of separate venues made a complicated diplomatic compromise visible. The peace treaty was a multilateral instrument, with numerous Allied signatories and a public conference whose ceremonies emphasized Japan's return to international standing. The security agreement, by contrast, was negotiated between two governments whose bargaining positions were unequal. Yoshida's signature at the Presidio represented an active decision by a Japanese political leader, but the decision was made within narrow strategic possibilities. American planners feared a Japanese neutralist position and required usable bases; Japan's leadership sought the end of direct occupation without the fiscal and political cost of rapid large-scale rearmament. Domestic Japanese critics could therefore affirm the value of peace while opposing the conditions on which that peace was offered. Those positions must be reconstructed from parliamentary debates and party documents rather than flattened into a single pro-American or anti-American divide. The diplomatic achievement of September 8 was real, but the same sequence exposed the unresolved question of who would control the armed forces stationed on Japanese territory.

The signing was not the restoration of sovereignty. Each treaty required ratification, and the peace treaty would enter into force when Japan and a majority of designated signatories, including the United States, deposited their instruments. The Japanese Diet approved both treaties in the autumn of 1951, in a debate that split the Japan Socialist Party: its left wing opposed both treaties, while its right wing supported the peace treaty and opposed the security treaty, and the party divided into two in October. The American Senate approved the peace treaty in March 1952 by a wide margin. These steps took months, which is why the occupation continued through the winter, and why April 28, 1952, rather than September 8, 1951, is the date on which Japan regained sovereignty. Section 90.13 · Division III

Sovereignty and the Territorial Articles of the Peace Treaty

The treaty opened by ending the state of war between Japan and the signatory powers and by recognizing the full sovereignty of the Japanese people over Japan and its territorial waters. Article 1 provided that the state of war would end when the treaty entered into force and that the Allied Powers would recognize that sovereignty. This was the legal foundation for the end of the occupation: Article 6 required occupation forces to withdraw within ninety days of entry into force, with the important proviso that the provision did not prevent the stationing or retention of foreign forces under bilateral or multilateral agreements. That proviso made possible the Security Treaty, which allowed American forces to remain. The treaty also affirmed, in Article 5, that Japan possessed the inherent right of individual or collective self-defense recognized in the United Nations Charter, and that it could enter voluntarily into collective security arrangements. In legal terms, this was a recognition that Japan was a sovereign state with the rights of one.

Article 2 addressed the territorial settlement of the empire. Japan renounced all right, title, and claim to Korea, including the islands of Quelpart (Jeju), Port Hamilton, and Dagelet; to Formosa (Taiwan) and the Pescadores (Penghu); to the Kuril Islands and the portion of Sakhalin acquired under the Treaty of Portsmouth in 1905; to its former Pacific mandated islands; and to certain other claims, including in Antarctica and the Spratly and Paracel Islands. The article is notable for what it did not do. It renounced Japanese claims but did not state to whom the territories belonged. Taiwan was not transferred to either Chinese government, and the Kurils were not assigned to the Soviet Union, which had not signed. The treaty's silence left a series of unresolved questions: the sovereignty of Taiwan, the southern Kuril Islands, and the status of small islands in the East China Sea and the Sea of Japan, including those now disputed between Japan and Korea. Scholars, among them Kimie Hara, have argued that these ambiguities, deliberately left by American drafters in the context of the Cold War, produced territorial disputes that persist in the twenty-first century.

Article 3 dealt with the Ryukyu and Bonin islands. It provided that Japan would concur in any proposal by the United States to the United Nations to place under its trusteeship system, with the United States as the sole administering authority, the Nansei Shotō (the Ryukyu Islands) south of 29 degrees north latitude, together with the Nanpō Shotō south of 29 degrees north (including the Bonin and Volcano Islands), and the Parece Vela and Marcus Island. It further provided that, pending such a proposal and its affirmative action, the United States would have the right to exercise all and any powers of administration, legislation, and jurisdiction over these territories and their inhabitants, including their territorial waters. The United States never submitted a trusteeship proposal, so the temporary arrangement became the lasting one. The articles' wording matters: the treaty did not transfer sovereignty to the United States, and it did not establish a trusteeship. It authorized American administration for an indefinite period. Dulles, in a statement at the conference, said that Japan retained “residual sovereignty” over the islands, a term that gave Japanese officials a basis for later requests for return but did not alter the American rights of control.

The consequence was a division of the Japanese archipelago in 1952. Okinawa, the Amami Islands, the Miyako and Yaeyama groups, and the Bonins remained under American control, while the rest of Japan became sovereign. The line of 29 degrees north cut through the Tokara chain, and some islands north of it, as well as the southern Tokara Islands, were returned to Japanese administration on February 10, 1952. The Amami Islands were returned in 1953, the Bonins in 1968, and Okinawa in 1972. The result was a long period during which hundreds of thousands of Japanese nationals lived under foreign administration and outside Japanese constitutional protections. Sections 90.17 and 90.19 return to Okinawa in detail.

Article 3 and the other territorial provisions were also the product of a set of strategic judgments. American military planners saw Okinawa as a key base in the western Pacific, valuable for air power and for maintaining a presence near Taiwan, Korea, and China. They preferred to retain control of the islands without annexing them, which would have contradicted American statements opposing territorial aggrandizement, and the formula of administration with residual Japanese sovereignty offered a compromise. Japanese officials, including Yoshida, accepted it as the price of settlement and in the expectation, which events partly confirmed, that the islands would eventually be returned. The Okinawans themselves were not consulted. Their reactions are discussed in Section 90.17, and they demonstrate that a territorial settlement made by states can have very different meanings for the people who live there.

Section 90.14

Reparations, Claims, War Responsibility, and an Uneven Settlement

The treatment of reparations was among the most contested aspects of the treaty. The occupation-era debate, in which some officials had proposed removing Japanese industrial plant as compensation for the victims of Japanese aggression, had ended by 1949, when the Americans abandoned the program in favor of recovery. The 1951 treaty reflected that shift. Article 14 recognized in principle that Japan should pay reparations for the damage and suffering it had caused, but it also stated that Japan's resources were not sufficient to maintain a viable economy and at the same time to make complete reparation. It therefore provided that Japan would enter into negotiations with Allied powers whose territories had been occupied by Japanese forces and damaged by them, in order to compensate them by supplying the services of the Japanese people in production, salvaging, and other work, rather than by transfers of cash or goods in large amounts. In effect, reparations would take the form of services and goods produced in Japan, financed within Japanese capacity, and limited to states that had been occupied.

The article also contained a broad waiver. Except as the treaty otherwise provided, the Allied powers waived their reparations claims and other claims arising from the conduct of the war, and claims arising from the occupation. Article 19 contained the reverse provision: Japan waived all claims of Japan and its nationals against the Allied powers and their nationals arising out of the war. These provisions were intended to close the accounts of the war between governments. They were therefore treaty-level settlements that left open, in the eyes of many victims, the question of whether individuals could claim compensation. The distinction between state-to-state settlement and individual redress would become a major issue in the decades that followed, including in disputes over forced labor and military sexual slavery examined in Chapter 79 and in later chapters.

The reparations negotiations that followed took years. Japan concluded agreements with Burma in 1954, the Philippines in 1956, Indonesia in 1958, and South Vietnam in 1959, in each case combining payments of goods and services with development credits. These agreements provided some compensation to the states concerned, and they also served Japanese commercial interests by promoting exports and establishing relations. Critics at the time and historians since have noted that the arrangements often benefited Japanese firms as much as the recipient countries, and that the payments went to governments rather than to the individuals who had suffered. The Philippine government, which had suffered enormous losses during the war, delayed ratification of the peace treaty until a reparations agreement had been reached. Chinese claims were handled differently: the Nationalist government on Taiwan, in the Treaty of Taipei, waived reparations, and the People's Republic did the same in 1972, as part of the normalization of relations.

The wording and architecture of Article 14 demand separate attention from later bilateral payments. The peace treaty acknowledged damage and suffering, but it also recognized the severe limits on Japan's resources and envisaged negotiations over reparations from Japanese services and productive capacity. For states accepting the settlement, this was a framework for future claims handling, not a complete catalogue of victims or a universal schedule of compensation. Some former territories and combatant countries had different legal positions at San Francisco; many people who endured occupation, forced labor, dispossession, or military violence had no direct seat in the conference. The treaty's language concerning state claims therefore cannot be read as an empirical statement that all injuries had been remedied. It requires students to distinguish a government's waiver or settlement of claims from the historical record of harm and from the subsequent campaigns of individuals and communities seeking recognition. This tension between diplomatic closure and public memory would remain after 1952.

The treaty addressed war responsibility in other ways. Article 11 required Japan to accept the judgments of the International Military Tribunal for the Far East and of other Allied war crimes tribunals, and it provided that clemency and parole for those sentenced could be exercised only on the decision of the governments concerned. Japanese politicians and the public soon began pressing for the release of convicted war criminals, and in the following years most were freed through parole and sentence reductions, a subject discussed in Chapter 83. Article 16 addressed Allied prisoners of war by providing for the transfer of certain Japanese assets in neutral and enemy countries to the International Committee of the Red Cross for distribution to former prisoners. The treaty therefore did address some categories of victims, but it left others, particularly Asian civilians, forced laborers, and women forced into military brothels, without any specific provision.

From the perspective of Asian wartime victims, the settlement was thus uneven. The treaty was written by the victorious Western powers, and several states that had suffered greatly, including China and Korea, were absent as signatories. The emphasis on a lenient peace reflected an American determination not to repeat what Dulles saw as the mistakes of Versailles, but it meant that the burdens of the settlement fell heavily on those with the least influence over it. This does not mean that the treaty achieved nothing. It ended the war for most of Japan's former enemies and set the framework within which Japan would rebuild relations in Asia. But it left a legacy of unresolved claims and memory that would shape the region's politics for generations, and any account of the peace settlement must include these costs along with its diplomatic achievements.

Section 90.15 · Division III

The Other San Francisco Treaty: U.S. Bases, Security, and Legal Asymmetry

The Security Treaty between the United States and Japan, signed on September 8, 1951, was a short document of five articles. Its preamble stated that Japan, having been disarmed, had no effective means to exercise its inherent right of self-defense, and that irresponsible militarism had not yet been driven from the world, creating dangers to Japan; that Japan therefore desired American forces to remain, and that the United States was willing to maintain forces in and about Japan, in the expectation that Japan would itself increasingly assume responsibility for its own defense. Article 1 granted the United States the right to dispose land, air, and sea forces in and about Japan. It stated that these forces might be used to contribute to the maintenance of international peace and security in the Far East and to the security of Japan against armed attack from without, including assistance, at the express request of the Japanese government, to put down large-scale internal riots and disturbances caused through the instigation or intervention of an outside power. Article 2 barred Japan from granting bases or rights of transit to any third power without American consent. Article 3 left the conditions governing the stationing of forces to administrative agreements, and Article 4 provided that the treaty would expire when other arrangements for Japan's security were in place. The treaty contained no fixed term.

The legal asymmetry of the 1951 treaty is its most important feature. The treaty gave the United States a right to station forces in Japan but did not impose an explicit obligation on the United States to defend Japan. The language said that American forces “may” be used for Japan's security, which is not a commitment to do so. Nor did the treaty require Japan to assist in the defense of the United States. It did not contain a clause for consultation, or a provision for Japanese approval of the use of bases for operations elsewhere in Asia. The internal-disturbance clause allowed American forces to be used inside Japan at Japan's request, a provision that many Japanese found particularly troubling. These features distinguish the 1951 treaty from the revised Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security of 1960, which added a mutual-defense commitment, a consultation system, and a fixed term and removed the internal-disturbance clause. The conflicts over that revision, discussed in Chapter 92, can be understood only against the background of the 1951 treaty's perceived inequality.

The practical arrangements were set out in the Administrative Agreement, signed in Tokyo on February 28, 1952, by Dean Rusk and Earl D. Johnson for the United States and Minister of State Okazaki Katsuo for Japan. Okazaki did not become foreign minister until April 30, 1952. The agreement provided that Japan would supply American forces with facilities and areas, with a joint committee to determine which ones, and that Japan would make a contribution to the costs of the forces. It dealt with the entry and legal status of American personnel, with customs, taxation, and claims, and with criminal jurisdiction over servicemen. The facilities were, in practice, those already used by American forces during the occupation, and several thousand sites, including bases, camps, depots, and training areas, passed into treaty status without a comprehensive review. The agreement's jurisdiction provisions, which favored American authorities in practice, became a continuing source of grievance, and the burden of bases on local communities, in the form of noise, land use, accidents, and crime, shaped Japanese politics for decades.

The American interests served by the arrangement were clear. The United States wanted bases in Japan to support operations in Korea and to project power across the Western Pacific, and it wanted assurance that Japan would not drift toward neutrality or toward the Soviet bloc. The Japanese government wanted security without the costs of heavy rearmament, and it believed it could limit the arrangement's impact by negotiation. Yoshida's calculation was that the treaty gave Japan the benefit of American protection at a bargain price and allowed the economy to grow. His critics replied that it made Japan a dependent of the United States and a potential target in American conflicts. The debate over this trade-off, between security and autonomy, is one of the persistent themes of postwar Japanese politics.

The two treaties must be analyzed together but not confused. The peace treaty was multilateral, concerned the ending of the war, and addressed territory, reparations, and sovereignty. The security treaty was bilateral, concerned military arrangements, and was not open to the other signatories. Yet they were politically linked: American officials made clear that the peace settlement depended on the security arrangement, and Japanese officials understood that independence was available only on those terms. Article 6 of the peace treaty, with its proviso, provided the legal bridge between them. Considering them as a single package helps explain why Japan regained sovereignty and, at the same time, accepted American bases. Documentary cross-reference: Administrative Agreement signatories

Section 90.16 · Division IV — April 1952: Uneven Sovereignty and Continuing U.S. Power

April 28, 1952: What Ended and What Remained?

The Treaty of Peace with Japan entered into force on April 28, 1952, after the United States and enough other signatories had deposited their instruments of ratification. On that day, the legal state of war between Japan and the treaty parties ended, the occupation of the main islands ended, and the Japanese government regained the formal powers of a sovereign state. SCAP, the General Headquarters in Tokyo, and the Allied Council for Japan and Far Eastern Commission, which had supervised the occupation, ceased to exist. General Ridgway, the last Supreme Commander, left his post, and the American government opened an embassy in Tokyo under an ambassador, Robert Murphy. Japan resumed diplomatic relations with the treaty states and began to rejoin international institutions; in 1952 it became a member of the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, though its entry into the United Nations would wait until 1956, after a Soviet veto was lifted by the Joint Declaration of that year.

For many Japanese, the day was a moment of national significance. The restoration of independence was marked by public ceremonies, including a ceremony attended by the emperor on May 3, 1952, at which the government celebrated the recovery of sovereignty. Newspapers noted the end of the occupation and the departure of the Allied administration, and many ordinary people felt relief at the end of foreign rule. The practical effects of the change were less dramatic in daily life, since Japanese officials had been running the administration under SCAP supervision for years. But the symbolic change mattered: Japanese laws, orders, and decisions no longer required the approval of an occupation authority, and the government could, within the limits of the Constitution and the treaties, decide matters for itself.

Restored sovereignty also changed mundane lines of administrative authority. Japanese ministries no longer required SCAP approval for the ordinary exercise of many governmental functions, and diplomatic channels could operate between recognized governments without military headquarters acting as the final supervising authority. Yet the change was not equivalent to an overnight replacement of personnel, offices, or legislation. Many ministries had worked through Japanese civil servants throughout occupation; the same officials now had to interpret directives that had been issued under a different constitutional and legal relationship. Courts, the Diet, prefectural governments, and national agencies faced questions about which occupation-era rules should remain, be amended, or expire. For local communities near military installations, legal independence could therefore feel abstract: the foreign soldiers, employment opportunities, land restrictions, and traffic routes that affected daily life often remained. Evaluating April 28 requires attention to both a decisive change in formal authority and the slower, uneven transformation of institutions on the ground.

One immediate consequence was a reassessment of the occupation's reforms. The Japanese government, free of SCAP, could review the laws and institutions that the occupation had introduced. It did not repeal the Constitution, which remained in force, but it moved to revise or reverse measures it considered excessive, including some of the decentralization of the police, aspects of the education and labor systems, and the purge directives. The purge was formally ended, allowing the return to public life of many politicians and officials, among them figures who would play major roles in later conservative politics. The occupation's reforms were therefore not wholly erased, and many of them, such as women's suffrage, land reform, and the constitutional structure, proved durable. But the end of the occupation opened a period of adjustment in which the Japanese government and public negotiated which reforms to keep, modify, or abandon.

The new freedom was also quickly tested. On May 1, 1952, only three days after the treaty took effect, a May Day demonstration in the plaza before the Imperial Palace in Tokyo turned into a violent confrontation with police, who fired on demonstrators. Deaths and many injuries resulted, and the “Bloody May Day” incident became a symbol for those who argued that the new independence was incomplete and that the state remained repressive. The government responded with new legislation. In July 1952 the Diet passed the Subversive Activities Prevention Law, which gave the state powers to investigate and restrict organizations that it judged to be engaged in violent subversive activity, and which provoked protests from unions, scholars, and opposition parties. These events show that the restoration of sovereignty did not end conflict over the meaning of the postwar order; it moved the conflict from the relationship between Japan and its occupiers to a struggle within Japanese politics.

What remained were the structures that tied Japan to the United States. The Security Treaty and the Administrative Agreement kept American forces in the country, and the facilities used by the occupation continued to be used under the new arrangements. American bases occupied large areas of land, including land near major cities, and the Japanese government was obliged to provide land and to compensate owners. The Safety Force and Maritime Guard were developing under American advice. The economic relationship was strongly oriented toward the American market and American military procurement. And the territories of Article 3, including Okinawa, remained under American control. In short, the occupation of the main islands ended, but American power did not leave Japan. It continued, with a different legal basis and a different degree of visibility.

Section 90.17 · Division IV

Okinawa and the Ryukyus: Article 3, U.S. Rule, and Okinawan Political Agency

Okinawa entered 1952 in a very different position from the main islands. It had been devastated in the spring of 1945 by the Battle of Okinawa, described in Chapter 80, in which a large share of the civilian population died or was displaced. American forces occupied the islands, built bases, and governed through a military government. In the late 1940s, as the Cold War deepened, American planners decided that Okinawa should be a permanent strategic base, and the Korean War strengthened that decision. Kadena airfield and other installations supported air operations against Korea, and construction of bases expanded the American footprint. For Okinawans, this meant that the end of the war had not brought an end to military control; it brought instead a different form of it.

The Okinawan governing structure evolved under American direction. After the military government era, the United States established in December 1950 the United States Civil Administration of the Ryukyu Islands (USCAR), which placed the islands' government under an American civil administrator, under the authority of the Far East Command. Local governments, including those of the island groups, existed within this framework, and Okinawans held elections for local offices and, in 1952, for a legislature. On April 1, 1952, as the Okinawa Prefectural Archives documents, the Government of the Ryukyu Islands (Ryūkyū Seifu) was established. It had a chief executive, a legislature, and courts, and it was a genuine institution with Okinawan officials and staff. But its powers were limited: the American civil administrator could veto legislation, remove officials, and issue ordinances that had the force of law, and the chief executive was appointed by the American authorities, not elected. The Government of the Ryukyu Islands was therefore a local administration inside an American system of rule, not a sovereign or fully autonomous government.

Article 3 of the peace treaty, which took effect in April 1952, gave the arrangement a legal basis. It authorized the United States to exercise powers of administration, legislation, and jurisdiction in the islands without defining the islands' sovereignty or setting a date for their return. The result was that the people of Okinawa were Japanese nationals who lived outside the jurisdiction of the Japanese state. They could not vote in Japanese Diet elections, and the Japanese Constitution did not apply in the islands. Japanese government protection of their rights was limited. Their passports and travel to the main islands were controlled by American authorities. The contrast with the main islands, where sovereignty returned in April 1952, was a source of resentment, and many Okinawans came to describe April 28, the day of Japanese independence, as a day of separation, sometimes called a “day of humiliation.”

The new Government of the Ryukyu Islands illustrates that autonomy cannot be inferred simply from the presence of elections. Okinawa Prefectural Archives records show that the first legislature election occurred on March 2, 1952, and that the government was established on April 1 by USCAR proclamation. The legislature was elected, but the chief executive was appointed rather than chosen in a popular election; USCAR retained authority to issue controlling proclamations and orders. Civil courts similarly operated within a system in which American administrators retained powers of intervention. These arrangements created a space for local politics but limited the consequences of democratic participation. Okinawans could debate taxes, schools, and public welfare through their own institutions while major issues of military land use and ultimate administrative authority remained outside their control. A proper account must therefore make the archives of local elections and administrative orders as important as the treaty's abstract language about the islands.

Okinawan political agency took several forms. In 1951 a movement for reversion to Japan collected signatures on petitions, and a January 1952 American policy memorandum cited estimates of about 74 percent of Okinawa’s adults, 80 percent of those in the Southern Ryukyus, and 99 percent in Amami signing; these are contemporary U.S. estimates, not independently audited totals. Political parties, among them the Okinawa People's Party led by Senaga Kamejirō and various conservative and socialist groups, took different positions on the relationship with Japan and with the United States, ranging from demands for immediate reversion to proposals for continued autonomy or, among a small number, independence. The Ryukyu legislature, elected in 1952, passed resolutions asking for return to Japan, which the American authorities generally disregarded. Okinawans also organized around economic and social questions: the use of land, the conditions of base labor, wages, and the treatment of civilians by American soldiers. Land became the central issue. The American authorities used the legal authority of the military to take land for bases, and after 1953, when the government introduced new rules for land acquisition, the seizure of farmland by “bulldozers and bayonets” provoked a broad movement of protest. That struggle, which falls just after this chapter's chronological limit, grew out of conditions established in 1952.

The Okinawan economy depended heavily on the bases. Construction of military facilities, employment of Okinawans as workers, and spending by American personnel provided income in a society that had been devastated by war and whose agricultural base had been disrupted. At the same time, the bases brought problems, including crimes against civilians, accidents, and the occupation of land. This combination of dependence and grievance defined Okinawan politics for decades. Chapter 99 returns to these issues in the context of contemporary Japan. It is important, in the context of the present chapter, to recognize that Okinawans were not passive objects of an arrangement made elsewhere. They petitioned, organized, voted, and protested, and their experience shows that the “end of the occupation” in 1952 depended on where one stood. Section 90.18 · Division IV

Former Colonial Subjects, Nationality, and the Borders of the New State

The legal separation of Korea and Taiwan from Japan in Article 2 had direct consequences for the people from those territories who lived in Japan. At the end of the war, perhaps two million Koreans lived in Japan, many of them brought as laborers or migrants during the colonial period. Most returned to Korea in 1945–1946, but a substantial population stayed for reasons that included family ties, economic position, the instability in Korea, and restrictions on the property that returnees could take. Japan’s official registered-resident series records 535,065 persons under the Korean/Chōsen category in 1952; this is a dated administrative count rather than a census of everyone with Korean ancestry. Smaller numbers of Taiwanese also remained. These residents had been Japanese nationals, in the legal terms of the empire, and they were treated, in the immediate postwar period, in contradictory ways: the occupation regarded them as liberated people but also, for some purposes, as Japanese, and the Japanese government regarded them as nationals when it suited its purposes, such as requiring them to obey Japanese law, and as foreigners when it came to rights and protections.

In 1947, as one of its last acts under the Imperial constitution, the government issued the Alien Registration Order, which treated Koreans and Taiwanese in Japan as foreigners for purposes of registration, even though they had not formally lost Japanese nationality. This ambiguity was resolved by the peace treaty. On April 19, 1952, in the days before the treaty came into force, the Civil Affairs Bureau of the Ministry of Justice issued a circular to local officials declaring that, upon the treaty's entry into force, Koreans and Taiwanese would lose Japanese nationality, including those who lived in Japan. No Diet vote and no choice was offered to the individuals affected. Japanese nationality was removed by an administrative interpretation of the treaty's territorial provisions, even though the treaty itself does not specify nationality questions. The courts later upheld the government's position.

The wording of the April 19 nationality circular also reveals that administrative classification was more complicated than a single categorical sentence. It generally instructed that persons categorized as Korean or Taiwanese, including those resident in the home islands, would lose Japanese nationality when the treaty took effect. But it made distinctions for people whose family-register status had changed before that date through marriage, adoption, or related acts. A former colonial subject entered on a home-island register through such a relationship could be treated differently from someone whose registry status had not changed; the reverse adjustment could affect a person formerly recorded as a home-island Japanese. These exceptions did not constitute an individual choice offered to the wider population. Instead they show how the legal state sorted families through rules inherited from the empire. Students should compare the actual text of the circular with the experience of individuals, because neither a national aggregate nor a statutory label captures the consequences of a forced change in citizenship status.

The consequences were significant. Koreans and Taiwanese in Japan became foreigners, without a clear legal status. Legislation passed at the time of the treaty, including the Alien Registration Law, provided that those who had been in Japan before the end of the war could remain, pending the settlement of their status, but it did not grant permanent residence, and for years they lived under uncertain conditions. Many forms of social security and public benefits were limited to Japanese nationals, and later legislation providing pensions and relief to wounded veterans and bereaved families of the war excluded former colonial subjects who had served in the Japanese forces, even though they had been conscripted or recruited under the empire's rules. Employment in public service and many professions was closed to them, and discrimination in private employment and housing was widespread. The requirement of fingerprinting and registration, which became a source of resentment, emerged from this framework. Their legal status was the subject of negotiations between Japan and South Korea, which led to an agreement on the status of Korean residents in 1965, and the question of nationality and naturalization remained a source of contention for decades.

For Koreans in Japan, the legal change interacted with the division of the peninsula. Those who identified with the south often registered under the nationality of the Republic of Korea, while many others retained the designation “Chōsen,” which had been used in registration, and which was not a nationality of the northern state but became associated with it. Organizations such as Mindan, aligned with the south, and the organizations of the pro-northern side, which later formed Chongryon, competed for the allegiance of the community. The designation of someone's status thus became entangled with Cold War politics. It is important to distinguish these administrative categories from the question of personal identity and belonging. Many Zainichi Koreans were born in Japan, spoke Japanese as their first language, and regarded Japan as their home, even as they were classified as foreigners. The legal definitions, imposed from above, did not capture their lived identity, which continued to evolve and which Chapter 94 treats in the context of minority rights movements.

The Taiwanese case had its own features. The Treaty of Taipei, signed between Japan and the Republic of China on April 28, 1952, and entering into force in August of that year, settled the relations between Japan and the Nationalist government, and it affected the status of Taiwanese residents of Japan, who were treated as nationals of the Republic of China. This arrangement reflected the Cold War politics of the treaty: Japan recognized the government on Taiwan as the government of China, which excluded the People's Republic, and the legal categories of nationality followed. The history of Taiwan itself, which had been a Japanese colony since 1895, was not resolved by the treaty, because Article 2 did not say to whom Taiwan belonged. These ambiguities continued to shape the status of Taiwanese in Japan and the broader politics of the region. Continuity and Change Over Time

Imperial Nationality Categories and Their Postwar Transformation

What Changed

  • Koreans and Taiwanese in Japan, who had been imperial subjects with Japanese nationality, became foreigners on April 28, 1952, by administrative interpretation.
  • The empire's territorial boundaries were legally dissolved by Article 2 of the peace treaty.
  • Registration, residence, and benefits came to be organized around foreign-national status rather than imperial subjecthood.
  • Koreans in Japan were divided into categories linked to the two Korean states.

What Remained Continuous

  • The legal and social marginality of Koreans and Taiwanese, which had existed within the empire, persisted in new forms.
  • The Japanese state's practice of defining nationality administratively, with limited participation by those affected.
  • Discrimination in employment and housing, and exclusion from benefits for which they had served the empire.
  • The tie between family registers and national membership, which defined who counted as Japanese.

Who experienced change most directly? Korean and Taiwanese residents of Japan, whose legal status was redefined without their consent. Who experienced greater continuity? The majority population, for whom the change seemed administrative, and the state, whose categories remained intact. Why the pattern matters: The 1952 change shows how the end of empire was handled through legal reclassification, not through an accounting with the colonial past, and it explains why questions of nationality, residence, and belonging became central to Zainichi history.

Section 90.19 · Division IV

Independence or Strategic Dependence? Japanese and Regional Evaluations

How should historians evaluate the settlement of 1951–1952? The question was contested at the time and has remained so. One position, associated with Yoshida and with many conservative politicians and later historians, holds that the treaties achieved independence on acceptable terms. According to this view, Japan regained sovereignty, avoided punitive provisions, retained its territory in the main islands, escaped heavy reparations, and gained American protection that allowed it to devote resources to economic growth. The alternatives, in this reading, were worse: a prolonged occupation, a peace conditioned on larger rearmament, or an overall peace that the Cold War made impossible. The restraint with which Japan approached rearmament, on this view, preserved the pacifist character of the Constitution and kept military affairs from dominating politics.

A second position emphasizes dependence. Critics, including many socialists and intellectuals at the time and numerous scholars since, argue that the settlement made Japan a subordinate partner in an American-led Cold War system. The Security Treaty gave the United States bases without a reciprocal obligation, the Administrative Agreement limited Japanese jurisdiction over American personnel, and the exclusion of China and the Soviet Union tied Japan to one side of the Cold War. Scholars such as John Dower have emphasized the asymmetry of power in the relationship and the degree to which Japanese policy was shaped by American requirements, even as Japanese leaders made their own choices within the constraints. From this perspective, the end of the occupation was a transformation of control, not its end.

A third position focuses on the pacifist constitution. Some argue that Japan's decision to accept American bases while limiting its own forces preserved Article 9 as a restraint on militarization, and that this was a significant achievement that distinguished postwar Japan from earlier periods. Others reply that the Constitution was preserved at the price of its practical meaning, since the Police Reserve and its successors created forces that most observers considered military, and the Constitution's pacifism depended on the American military presence. The relationship between the Constitution and the alliance, which is sometimes described as a contradiction and sometimes as a division of labor, was thus established in 1951–1952.

The disagreement over dependence is especially clear when one separates the ability to make a decision from the ability to choose among credible alternatives. Yoshida and his cabinet were not passive recipients of American commands; they negotiated the pace of rearmament, defended Japanese fiscal priorities, and accepted a treaty that permitted more resources to remain in civilian economic recovery. At the same time, officials could scarcely ignore an American military presence already established during the occupation, a war continuing in Korea, and the enormous dependence on the United States for security and hard-currency access. An alliance agreement signed under such conditions may be legally valid and politically consequential while still resting on highly unequal bargaining power. The historical challenge is to document the margins of Japanese choice, rather than merely attach the labels 'sovereign' or 'dependent' to the whole relationship. That approach also prevents retrospective assumptions that later economic success proves the settlement was optimal, or that later base controversy proves all Japanese diplomacy was meaningless.

The regional perspective changes the scale of judgment. A treaty that looked generous to a defeated Japanese government could appear incomplete to Koreans denied a signatory role, to Chinese governments excluded from the settlement, and to residents of Okinawa left under a different administration. State-to-state peace, citizenship, reparations, territorial renunciation, and security basing were related problems but did not share a single timetable. The Soviet refusal to sign further exposed the Cold War boundaries of the conference. One reason the San Francisco settlement remains historically contentious is precisely that it resolved enough legal questions to terminate occupation without creating a single inclusive regional peace. This was a diplomatic settlement with uneven constituencies, not simply a ceremony marking the restoration of one country's rights. By treating those limits as part of the agreement's design, rather than as problems discovered only decades afterward, the chapter closes with an explanation of why the postwar order was both durable and disputed.

A fourth position addresses the unresolved legacies of empire. Scholars such as Kimie Hara argue that the San Francisco settlement, by leaving territorial questions open and by excluding the states most affected by Japanese aggression, produced a regional order that deferred the settlement of colonial and wartime issues. On this view, the territorial disputes, the questions of compensation and historical memory, and the divisions in East Asia were not accidents but consequences of a settlement designed for Cold War purposes. The experiences of Okinawans, Koreans in Japan, and the victims of Japanese war crimes, whose claims were not addressed or were handled at the level of states, illustrate the settlement's costs.

Historical Debate

Did Japan Recover Independence in 1952, or Enter a New Dependence?

The question: Historians and contemporaries have asked whether the San Francisco settlement should be understood primarily as the restoration of Japanese independence or as the creation of a new relationship of strategic dependence on the United States.

Interpretation A — Independence restored on pragmatic terms: The treaty ended the occupation, recognized Japanese sovereignty over the main islands, and allowed Japan to conduct its own foreign and domestic policy. The security arrangement was a choice by Japanese leaders who judged it the best available bargain, and it allowed them to limit military spending and pursue economic growth. Dependence was real but chosen and limited.
Interpretation B — Strategic dependence accepted: The terms of the settlement were set by American strategy, and Japan accepted bases and an unequal security treaty because the alternative was continued occupation. The treaty's separate peace, the Administrative Agreement, and the continued American control of Okinawa show that sovereignty was qualified from the beginning, and the structure of dependence shaped Japanese politics for decades.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the treaty texts, diplomatic records of the Yoshida–Dulles negotiations, Japanese government documents, the Administrative Agreement, and the political debates of 1951–1952. They differ in the weight they give to Japanese agency and to structural constraint. Many scholars now treat the two as complementary: Japanese leaders exercised real agency, and they did so within a framework set by American power. The perspectives of Okinawans, Koreans in Japan, and Asian neighbors complicate both accounts.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes their understanding of the origins of the U.S.–Japan alliance, the debates over revision in 1960, the persistence of base politics, and the relation between prewar and postwar Japan. It also affects how Okinawa and the former colonial subjects are placed in the narrative: as exceptions to a story of recovered independence or as evidence that the story was never complete.

Section 90.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis

Restored Sovereignty and the Uneven End of Occupation

The history of 1950–1952 shows a country whose circumstances changed profoundly within a short time. The Korean War turned Japan into a rear base and a supplier, and special procurement provided dollars and orders that accelerated a recovery already beginning. The same war led MacArthur to order a National Police Reserve, which began the rearmament that Article 9 seemed to forbid, and it drew Japanese minesweepers into Korean waters in secret. Washington concluded that a rapid, lenient peace would bind Japan to the West, and Yoshida accepted bases in exchange for independence. The treaties signed in San Francisco in September 1951 entered into force in April 1952, ending the occupation of the main islands.

Yet the settlement was uneven. Okinawa and other islands remained under American administration, while Koreans and Taiwanese in Japan lost their nationality by administrative decision. Neither Chinese government was represented, the Soviet Union attended but did not sign, Korea was excluded as a signatory, and reparations and claims were settled at the level of states and deferred. The Security Treaty gave the United States rights without matching duties, and the Administrative Agreement preserved the base system. Japan's government, which regained control over its domestic affairs, remained tied to American strategy in foreign and security matters.

So did Japan recover independence, enter a new dependence, or both? The evidence supports a combination. Japan regained legal sovereignty over its main islands, and its leaders exercised real agency in negotiating, resisting rearmament, and choosing the terms of the alliance. But that sovereignty was limited by a security relationship that Japan could not easily alter, by the exclusion of Okinawa, and by the exclusion of former colonial subjects from full membership. The later prosperity that Chapter 91 describes was not inevitable. It depended on decisions and circumstances that remained open in 1952, and the arrangements made then shaped the questions that would dominate the following decades: the future of Article 9, the alliance, the bases, and the memory of empire.

The question of autonomy can be answered more precisely by distinguishing domains. In domestic politics, Japanese governments after April 1952 acted largely on their own authority, revising occupation-era measures, passing the Subversive Activities Prevention Law, and setting economic policy through ministries and the Bank of Japan. In diplomacy, Japan could choose its relations only within limits: Yoshida's pledge to conclude a treaty with the Nationalist government on Taiwan, made under American pressure, shaped relations with China for two decades. In security, Japan's room for choice was narrowest, since the Security Treaty gave the United States rights of basing without reciprocal obligation, and the Administrative Agreement limited Japanese jurisdiction. Autonomy was therefore real but uneven, and it varied by domain and by the people concerned.

The legal record also reminds us that temporal boundaries matter. The Korean War continued after April 1952, and its armistice came only in July 1953. Japan's new security bodies continued to be reorganized after sovereignty returned, while political conflict over the presence of United States forces did not vanish. In Okinawa, the establishment of representative bodies did not grant effective control over the American administration or military land. For Korean and Taiwanese residents, the state categories associated with nationality loss were lived realities, not merely archival legal terms. The end of the occupation must therefore be understood as an institutional threshold rather than a completed social reconciliation. The issue that connects this chapter with the following account of high-growth Japan is not whether 1952 supplied an inevitable economic future, but how the choices and constraints created before that date shaped the possibilities of the decades that followed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan passed from foreign military occupation to restored sovereignty between 1950 and 1952, and how that passage was shaped by the Korean War, the Cold War, and the unresolved legacies of empire. Its central argument is that the settlement combined a real recovery of independence with a new strategic dependence on the United States, and that its limits fell most heavily on those with the least voice in it: Okinawans, former colonial subjects, and the victims of Japanese aggression.

The Korean War began on June 25, 1950, as a conflict between two rival Korean governments within the context of the Cold War. It devastated Korea, with deaths in the millions, and it transformed Japan's position. American occupation divisions in Japan were committed to the fighting, and Japan became a rear base: its ports, airfields, hospitals, repair depots, and factories supported the United Nations Command, and Japanese workers and seamen took part in activities close to the war zone.

The economic effects came through special procurement (tokuju), American purchases of goods and services in Japan, and through a surge in commercial exports. These measures must be distinguished: contracts, receipts, and exports are different quantities. Industrial output regained prewar levels, foreign exchange constraints eased, and the Dodge Line recession ended. But the boom was uneven, prices rose, a downturn followed in 1951–1952, and the benefits were distributed unequally among firms, workers, base communities, and Korean residents of Japan.

The war also produced new security institutions. On July 8, 1950, MacArthur directed the creation of a 75,000-man National Police Reserve and an enlargement of the Maritime Safety Agency, and the Reserve was established on August 10. Though described as a police body, it was equipped and trained as a light infantry force, and its creation set off a durable debate about Article 9. Yoshida accepted the force but resisted large rearmament, while American officials pressed for more and Japanese socialists and communists opposed rearmament altogether.

In the autumn of 1950, Japanese minesweepers operated under American command off the Korean coast in a secret mission that exposed their crews to danger and raised legal questions. Differing counts of the vessels involved, about twenty in the broader operation and eight at Wonsan, reflect differing scopes, and illustrate the care required in comparing figures. In 1952 the Police Reserve and Maritime Guard were reorganized into National Safety institutions, which anticipated the Self-Defense Forces of 1954.

The peace settlement was negotiated by Yoshida and John Foster Dulles in the context of American Cold War strategy. It was a separate peace: the People's Republic of China and the Republic of China were not invited, the Soviet Union refused to sign, India and Burma did not attend, and Korea was excluded as a signatory. The Treaty of Peace with Japan, signed on September 8, 1951, ended the war, recognized Japanese sovereignty, renounced Japan's empire in Article 2, authorized American administration of the Ryukyu and Bonin Islands in Article 3, and addressed reparations through services and waivers of claims in Articles 14 and 19.

The Security Treaty, signed the same day at the Presidio, allowed American forces to remain in and about Japan without imposing an explicit obligation to defend it, and the Administrative Agreement of February 28, 1952, set the conditions of the military presence. These instruments, distinct from the revised treaty of 1960, created the legal asymmetry that later generated political conflict. Together with the American security pacts with the Philippines, Australia, and New Zealand, they formed what scholars call the San Francisco System.

The treaties entered into force on April 28, 1952, which ended the occupation of the main islands and dissolved SCAP, but signing and entry into force are different legal moments. Events of the following days, including the May Day clash and the Subversive Activities Prevention Law, showed that the new independence did not end internal conflict over the postwar order. American bases continued, and the restoration of sovereignty opened a period of negotiation over which occupation reforms to keep.

Okinawa and the other Article 3 territories remained under American administration, a fact distinct from sovereignty. The Government of the Ryukyu Islands, established on April 1, 1952, was an Okinawan institution within the American system, with limited powers. Okinawans petitioned for reversion, organized politically, and protested land seizures, and they experienced April 28 as a day of separation. Meanwhile Koreans and Taiwanese in Japan lost Japanese nationality by an administrative circular on April 19, 1952, without choice, and entered a long period of legal uncertainty.

Historians interpret the settlement differently: as independence achieved on pragmatic terms, as strategic dependence, as a preservation of constitutional pacifism, or as a deferral of imperial legacies. The evidence supports a combination, and no interpretation can be adequate that omits Okinawa, the former colonial subjects, and the states and peoples excluded from the treaty. The settlement also set the stage for Chapter 91's account of the economic miracle and Chapter 92's account of the 1960 security-treaty crisis, but those developments were not inevitable consequences of 1952.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Korean War
War that began June 25, 1950, between North and South Korea, with a United Nations Command led by the United States and Chinese intervention from late 1950; armistice July 27, 1953.
United Nations Command
The unified command authorized by the Security Council in July 1950, led by the United States and headquartered in Tokyo.
Rear base
A territory behind the fighting that supplies, repairs, and supports forces; Japan's role in the Korean War.
Tokuju (special procurement)
American military purchases of goods and services in Japan, paid largely in dollars, which surged after June 1950.
Dodge Line
The 1949 stabilization program, named for Joseph Dodge, that balanced the budget and ended subsidies, producing a deflationary slump.
Ito-hen and kinzoku-hen keiki
The “string” (textile) and “metal” booms of 1950–1951 associated with wartime demand.
Procurement contracts, receipts, and exports
Three distinct measures: value of orders placed, dollars actually received, and private export sales; they must not be added together.
Economic Stabilization Board
Japanese agency (Keizai Antei Honbu) whose September 1950 report documents early procurement.
Red Purge
The 1950 removal of suspected communists from jobs in media, public bodies, and industry.
Sōhyō
General Council of Trade Unions of Japan, founded July 1950, which moved toward opposition to rearmament and the security treaty.
National Police Reserve (Keisatsu Yobitai)
The 75,000-man force authorized July 8 and established August 10, 1950; the ancestor of the Self-Defense Forces.
Maritime Safety Agency
Japan's coast-guard agency, established 1948, whose vessels cleared mines in Korean waters in 1950.
Article 9
The constitutional clause renouncing war and the maintenance of war potential; the focus of controversy over rearmament.
War potential
The constitutional term whose meaning the government defined narrowly to exclude the Police Reserve.
Ashida Memorandum
A 1947 proposal associated with Ashida Hitoshi suggesting a voluntary arrangement for American forces to remain for Japan's defense.
National Safety Agency and Safety Force
Organizations created in August 1952 from the Police Reserve and maritime force, preceding the 1954 Self-Defense Forces.
Separate peace (tandoku kōwa)
A peace concluded with the Western bloc and without the Soviet Union and China, as opposed to an “overall peace.”
Overall peace (zenmen kōwa)
The alternative of a treaty including all former enemies, favored by many Japanese socialists and intellectuals.
San Francisco Peace Treaty
The Treaty of Peace with Japan, signed September 8, 1951 and in force April 28, 1952.
Security Treaty (1951)
The five-article agreement permitting American forces in and about Japan, without an explicit duty to defend Japan; replaced by the revised treaty of 1960.
Administrative Agreement
The February 28, 1952 agreement setting conditions for American facilities, costs, and legal status of forces.
San Francisco System
Scholarly term for the network of U.S. bilateral and small multilateral alliances and settlements in Asia after 1951.
Entry into force
The legal moment a ratified treaty takes effect; April 28, 1952 for the peace treaty, distinct from signature.
Article 2 (Peace Treaty)
Provision renouncing Japanese claims to Korea, Taiwan and the Pescadores, the Kurils, southern Sakhalin, and other territories without naming new owners.
Article 3 (Peace Treaty)
Provision authorizing U.S. administration of the Ryukyu and Bonin Islands pending a never-submitted trusteeship proposal.
Residual sovereignty
The term used by Dulles for Japan's continuing underlying claim to the Article 3 islands, without American recognition of Japanese governing powers there.
Reparations (Article 14)
Provision for compensating occupied states through Japanese services and production, with waivers of other claims.
USCAR
The U.S. Civil Administration of the Ryukyu Islands, created in December 1950, above the Government of the Ryukyu Islands.
Government of the Ryukyu Islands
The local Okinawan administration established April 1, 1952, with limited powers under American authority.
Treaty of Taipei
Japan–Republic of China treaty signed April 28, 1952, in force August 1952.
Nationality circular of April 19, 1952
Administrative instruction that Koreans and Taiwanese lost Japanese nationality upon the treaty's entry into force.
Zainichi
Residents of Japan of Korean (and Taiwanese) descent whose legal status was redefined in 1952.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the end of a foreign occupation can coexist with the continuation of foreign power. The Japan that regained sovereignty in 1952 was an independent state with its own government, and also a partner in an unequal alliance whose terms it had accepted under pressure and with calculation. Understanding that combination is essential for reading the rest of the postwar period.

The chapter also reveals how war can transform an economy and a society while inflicting devastation elsewhere. Japan's recovery drew on a conflict that destroyed much of Korea, and honest history must keep both facts in view. The same applies to the settlement's treatment of empire: legal arrangements renounced Japan's colonies without addressing the consequences for those who had lived under colonial rule, and the treatment of Okinawans and Koreans in Japan shows how the costs of settlement were distributed.

The arrangements of 1951–1952 shaped later history. The Security Treaty's inequalities led to the crisis of 1960 discussed in Chapter 92, the Police Reserve led to the Self-Defense Forces and decades of constitutional debate, the territorial ambiguities of Article 2 produced disputes that continue, and Okinawa's bases remain a central political question, as Chapter 99 explains. The economic stimulus of the war was a prelude to the high growth of Chapter 91, but not its cause alone.

Finally, the chapter connects the archipelago to a wider world: to Korea and China, to the Soviet Union and the Cold War, to Southeast Asian states fearful of Japan, and to the United States. It reminds students that Japanese history cannot be written within the boundaries of the main islands, and that the end of the occupation was experienced differently in Tokyo, Naha, Osaka's Korean neighborhoods, and the ports and base towns that supplied a war next door.
