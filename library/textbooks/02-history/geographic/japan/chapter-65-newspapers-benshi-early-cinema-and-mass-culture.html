Part IX — Constitution, Industry, and Empire

Newspapers, Benshi, Early Cinema, and Mass Culture

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the space of about forty years, a country whose public news had circulated on single woodblock-printed sheets hawked by street vendors came to possess daily newspapers, photographic studios, illustrated magazines, modern theaters, permanent cinemas, and performers whose faces were known to strangers. The technologies were largely imported. The ways people used them were not.

On the twenty-eighth of January 1871 in the Gregorian calendar, which corresponds to the eighth day of the twelfth month of Meiji 3 in the lunisolar calendar then still in use, a printing company in the treaty port of Yokohama issued the first number of the Yokohama Mainichi Shinbun. It is generally recognized as the first Japanese-language daily newspaper. Its pages carried the kinds of material a port city's merchants needed: shipping news, commercial notices, foreign reports, and advertisements. Forty-one years later, in 1912, a merger of four film companies created Nippon Katsudō Shashin Kabushiki Kaisha, known as Nikkatsu, and the largest early Japanese film enterprise began to organize production, distribution, and exhibition under a single corporate roof. Between those two events lies the subject of this chapter: the formation of a commercial culture in which information, images, performances, and personalities could circulate among large and varied audiences, and in which the circulation itself had become a business.

This chapter is concerned principally with the period from the 1870s through approximately 1916, though earlier developments in Tokugawa publishing, performance, and visual culture are introduced where they explain how the new media took the forms they did. It follows several distinct but interconnected histories: the newspaper and the print industry behind it; photography, from the first commercial studios to the use of photographs in news, tourism, and imperial representation; the theater, in which kabuki, the political stage plays called shinpa, and the realist movement called shingeki developed in competition and partial cooperation; motion pictures and the live narrators called benshi who became inseparable from Japanese film exhibition; and the magazines, cartoons, recordings, advertisements, and celebrity culture that bound these forms into a recognizable commercial public sphere.

The central argument is that this transformation was not simply the importation of Western media. Japanese publishers, photographers, performers, exhibitors, and audiences adapted unfamiliar technologies to practices they already understood: storytelling and oral performance, kabuki spectacle, woodblock printing, commercial entertainment districts, and the sale of images of famous people. The newspaper had a precursor in the kawaraban news sheet, the first film shown was often a kabuki scene, and the benshi drew on a long tradition of professional narration. At the same time, the new media created things that had not existed before. They produced a daily rhythm of shared information, a reading public segmented by age, gender, and income, businesses that could mass-produce images, and a form of fame that depended on reproduction rather than on presence in a theater.

That transformation was neither neutral nor evenly shared. New media created opportunities for expression and for profit, and they widened audiences, but they also generated sensationalism, fostered patriotic spectacle during Japan's wars, and drew the attention of the state, which responded with press ordinances, police supervision of theaters, and censorship. Access to media was unequal across class, gender, region, and literacy. And the power to consume images and stories was very different from the power to produce them: Ainu, Okinawan, Korean, Taiwanese, and Chinese people appear in the images and entertainments examined here far more often as subjects displayed to others than as authors of their own representation. Three relationships therefore drive the chapter's narrative: between technology and business; between performers and audiences; and between commercial media and political authority.

Historians approach this subject through an unusual combination of evidence. The newspapers and magazines themselves survive in large runs, though with the usual caution that they record what editors chose to print. Photographs survive in albums and archives, but each is a made object, shaped by pose, costume, captioning, and market. Film is scarcer: most early Japanese films, like early films everywhere, have been lost, in part because nitrate film stock was fragile and flammable, and what remains is often fragmentary. Performances themselves vanished the moment they ended and must be reconstructed from playbills, reviews, memoirs, scripts, and the accounts of those who attended. This chapter therefore treats source evaluation as part of its subject, and its photographs, prints, and film fragments are discussed as constructed evidence rather than transparent windows onto the past.

The chapter has a defined place in the larger book. Earlier chapters established the Tokugawa culture of kabuki, ukiyo-e, and publishing (Chapters 46 and 50), the Westernizing currents of the early Meiji years (Chapter 58), and the political movements that used newspapers and the stage as tools (Chapter 57). The chapters just before this one treated the constitutional order, the wars, and the transformation of women's education and religion (Chapters 59–64). Here the emphasis falls on the emergence of the media institutions themselves, up to the mid-1910s. The mature urban consumer culture of the 1920s, with its department stores, radio, jazz, and fully developed film-star system, belongs to Chapter 70, and the democratic civil society of Taishō belongs to Chapter 67. This chapter supplies the foundations on which both rested.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the Tokugawa kawaraban news sheet from the modern daily newspaper, and trace the technological and commercial changes that produced the latter.
  2. Explain how print capitalism worked in Meiji Japan, including typesetting, advertising, postal distribution, subscriptions, and street sales.
  3. Compare political newspapers (ōshinbun) with popular newspapers (koshinbun), and assess the relationship between the press, public opinion, and government regulation.
  4. Analyze photographs as constructed historical evidence, taking into account who made them, who bought them, who appears in them, and how they were staged and captioned.
  5. Differentiate kabuki, shinpa, and shingeki, and explain why they should be understood as coexisting and competing forms rather than a sequence in which each replaced the last.
  6. Distinguish the milestones of early Japanese cinema: the arrival of apparatus, the first public screenings, the first domestically made films, the first permanent cinema, and the first production studios.
  7. Interpret the benshi as active interpreters and performers rather than as mere readers of silent films, while avoiding projecting the 1920s benshi culture back onto the 1890s.
  8. Trace the growth of magazines, cartoons, recordings, and advertising as parts of a segmented commercial entertainment market.
  9. Assess how celebrity was constructed through photographs, newspapers, postcards, and film, and how it differed from established theatrical fame.
  10. Evaluate how mass media represented Japan's wars, colonies, and the peoples of Ainu Mosir, the Ryukyu Islands, Korea, Taiwan, and China, and who had the power to shape those representations.
  11. Examine the legal and police mechanisms by which the state regulated newspapers, publications, theaters, and film exhibitions.
  12. Connect the media developments of c. 1870–1916 to the political, social, and consumer transformations examined in Chapters 67 and 70.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what senses was the Meiji newspaper a continuation of earlier practices of news and publishing, and in what senses was it a new kind of institution?
  2. Why did the new media so often take the form of adaptations of existing performance and publishing traditions rather than clean breaks from them?
  3. How did commercial incentives, rather than political or educational purposes alone, shape what newspapers, theaters, and film exhibitors offered to audiences?
  4. What can a photograph, a woodblock newspaper print, or a fragment of early film tell historians, and what can it not establish on its own?
  5. How should historians describe the relationships among kabuki, shinpa, and shingeki without imposing a simple narrative of progress?
  6. Why were early Japanese film screenings never silent, and what does the benshi reveal about how audiences understood the new medium?
  7. Who had access to mass media, who produced it, and who appeared in it as a subject, and how did these differ by class, gender, region, and ethnicity?
  8. Did the commercial press and entertainment industries broaden public debate, reinforce official nationalism, or do both at once?
  9. How did the state attempt to regulate print, the stage, and the screen, and how effective and consistent was that regulation?

Historical Context

The media developments of Meiji Japan rested on foundations laid during the Tokugawa period. Chapters 46 and 50 examined an urban culture in which commercial publishers in Edo, Kyoto, and Osaka produced illustrated books, guidebooks, popular fiction, and woodblock prints for a literate and increasingly consumer-minded public, and in which kabuki, bunraku, storytelling halls, and the pleasure quarters formed an entertainment economy of considerable scale. Literacy was widespread by premodern standards, supported by temple schools (terakoya) and domain schools, and the rental libraries that circulated books by the thousands were part of everyday urban life. A trained workforce of carvers, printers, illustrators, and distributors already existed. The new industries did not begin from nothing; they began from a mature commercial culture and a skilled labor force.

The Meiji state then created conditions that both enabled and constrained the new media. Chapter 55 and Chapter 58 described the construction of national schooling and the vogue for Western knowledge, and by the early 1900s a large majority of school-age children were enrolled in elementary school, producing a mass of readers. The state built a modern postal service beginning in 1871, a telegraph network, and railways from the Shimbashi–Yokohama line of 1872 onward, and these infrastructures determined how fast news and printed matter could travel. A telegraph cable linking Nagasaki to Shanghai and Vladivostok, laid in 1871 by a foreign company, tied the archipelago to the international news system. The government also, from the 1870s, treated the press as something to be both used and policed. Officials supported some newspapers, subsidized others indirectly, and issued ordinances that sent critical editors to prison, a pattern that Chapter 57 traced in the context of the Freedom and People's Rights Movement.

The wider East Asian context also matters. Newspapers and illustrated periodicals were being established in treaty ports such as Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Yokohama, and foreign residents ran English-language papers and photography studios that Japanese practitioners learned from. Foreign tourism, the international exhibitions at which Japan displayed itself, and the commercial export of Japanese images created an overseas market that shaped what photographers photographed and what performers performed. The wars with Qing China (1894–95) and Russia (1904–05), treated in Chapters 60 and 61, provided the most powerful single stimuli to press circulation and visual reporting, and the colonies that resulted created new audiences, new subjects, and new questions about whose voices appeared in the media.

Students should keep several cautions in mind. First, the word "mass" in "mass culture" should not be understood as a claim that everyone had equal access. Newspaper readers, theatergoers, and film audiences were concentrated in cities and treaty ports, and they were divided by price, literacy, and leisure. Second, new media did not abolish older ones: kawaraban sellers, kabuki, storytelling halls, and woodblock printers continued, adapted, or declined at different rates. Third, the sources for the history of the media are themselves media products, which means that historians must always ask who produced a source, for whom, and under what constraints. The chapters that follow in Part X, especially Chapters 67 and 70, will show how the foundations examined here matured in the democratic and consumer society of the 1910s and 1920s.

Division I — Newspapers, Printing, and the Creation of a National Reading Public · Section 65.1

From Kawaraban to Modern Newspapers: The Transformation of Japanese News

Long before the first daily newspaper, people in Japanese cities bought printed news. The kawaraban ("tile-block prints," a name whose origin is debated) were single sheets, usually printed from carved woodblocks and sometimes with crude illustrations, that reported fires, floods, earthquakes, vendettas, love suicides, scandals, and curiosities. Sellers cried the contents aloud on the street, and examples survive from the seventeenth century through the final decades of the Tokugawa period. Kawaraban were irregular, often anonymous, and sometimes produced to be sold only once; they did not report on shogunal politics, which the authorities forbade. They show that a market for printed news existed, but they were not periodicals in the modern sense: they had no subscription base, no continuous editorial staff, and no regular schedule.

The step toward periodic news came through translation. In 1862 the shogunate's Institute for the Investigation of Barbarian Books (Bansho Shirabesho) issued translated extracts from a Dutch-language newspaper published in the Dutch East Indies, a publication usually known as the Kanpan Batavia Shinbun, for official readers. In the treaty port of Yokohama, foreign-run English-language papers appeared in the 1860s, and Japanese residents who had lived abroad, among them the castaway-turned-interpreter Hamada Hikozō (Joseph Heco), produced Japanese-language digests of foreign news. The turmoil of 1868 then produced dozens of short-lived papers supporting one side or another in the Boshin War, many of which the new government quickly suppressed. These experiments established the genre, but they did not establish a stable business.

The stable business arrived in 1871 with the Yokohama Mainichi Shinbun, discussed in Section 65.3. Its significance lies less in being the first paper to appear on a given day than in being the first Japanese-language paper to appear every day with a continuing editorial and commercial operation. A further form, the nishiki-e shinbun (color-print newspapers), appeared in the early 1870s in Tokyo, in which woodblock artists such as Ochiai Yoshiiku and Tsukioka Yoshitoshi produced illustrated reports of crimes and curiosities in the visual language of ukiyo-e. These prints, which carried the Tokugawa tradition of illustrated news into the Meiji era, lasted only a few years before photographic and halftone reproduction and the move to text-driven small papers displaced them.

Historians therefore resist the temptation to treat kawaraban as a direct ancestor of the modern daily. They were related in function, as street-sold printed news, and the name of the Yomiuri Shinbun ("read-and-sell") recalls the vendors who read kawaraban aloud to attract customers. But the modern newspaper was a different kind of institution: a regular publication with employed writers, translators, and printers; revenue from both sales and advertising; and a standing relationship to the state through ordinances and, often, political patrons.

Historical Vocabulary
Kawaraban and the Daily Newspaper

Kawaraban were irregularly issued, single-sheet printed news broadsides, sold on the streets of Tokugawa cities. Nishiki-e shinbun were early Meiji illustrated news prints made in the ukiyo-e tradition. A daily newspaper (nikkan shinbun) in the sense used in this chapter is a regularly published periodical with continuing staff, a distribution system, and both subscription and advertising income.

The distinction matters because it would be misleading to say that Japan "already had newspapers" in the Edo period, and equally misleading to say that print news was a Western import with no domestic roots. The modern newspaper combined an existing public appetite for news and a skilled printing trade with new technologies, a new schedule of publication, and a new business model. The term "mass circulation" should also be used carefully: in this period it describes papers that reached many thousands of readers in cities, not that every household subscribed.

Section 65.2

Movable Type, Printing Businesses, Postal Networks, and Mass Circulation

A daily newspaper is a manufacturing operation as much as an intellectual one, and the technical foundations of the Meiji press were laid in the 1860s and 1870s. Japan had a long tradition of woodblock printing, in which a text and its illustrations were carved into a single plank, and the tradition remained economical for illustrated books and prints well into the twentieth century. But a daily paper, with new text every morning, needed movable type. The difficulty was the Japanese writing system. A printer of English could assemble text from a few dozen characters; a printer of Japanese needed thousands of kanji, plus two syllabaries, in several sizes, and had to store and retrieve them quickly.

The solution drew on foreign experience and domestic craft. In the late 1860s a printer named Motoki Shōzō, working in Nagasaki, learned Western methods of casting metal type from the American missionary printer William Gamble, and Motoki and his associates then established type foundries that supplied the growing industry. By the 1870s Tokyo and Yokohama had type foundries and printing companies capable of producing a daily paper. Compositors worked from cases holding thousands of characters, and the physical labor of typesetting a Japanese paper, with its multiple scripts and its use of reading aids called furigana alongside kanji, was heavy. High-speed rotary presses imported from Europe from the 1890s allowed the largest papers to print on a scale that earlier flat-bed presses had not permitted, and the larger papers were the ones that could afford them.

Distribution mattered as much as manufacture. The modern postal service, begun in 1871 under Maejima Hisoka and extended nationwide in the following years, made it possible to send a newspaper to a subscriber in another city, and newspapers and periodicals received preferential postal treatment. Railways, beginning with the Shimbashi–Yokohama line in 1872 and extending along the Tōkaidō and northward through the 1880s and 1890s, shortened delivery times, and the telegraph carried news itself faster than any printed object could travel. In cities, papers were sold by subscription, delivered by carriers to homes, and sold by street vendors and at stalls. Newspaper readership also required readers: elementary schooling, which became compulsory and widely attended, produced a literate population that could follow a paper that used both kanji and furigana reading aids, and this literacy gave the newspaper market its foundation.

Evidence for circulation must be handled with care. Newspapers often claimed figures that served advertising purposes, and records of reliable audited circulation are scarce for the early decades. Historians generally speak of the relative scale of papers and of broad growth, particularly after the wars of 1894–95 and 1904–05, rather than treating any single figure as firm. The more secure conclusion is that print capitalism, the profit-driven commercial production and distribution of printed matter for a market, was in place in the major cities by the 1880s and extended to the provinces through local newspapers and postal subscription.

Section 65.3

The Rise of Modern Newspapers: Yokohama Mainichi, Tokyo Nichinichi, Yomiuri, and Asahi

The Yokohama Mainichi Shinbun appeared in January 1871 in a city that was a laboratory for the new media. Yokohama's merchants needed commercial information about shipping, exchange rates, and prices, and the paper provided it, alongside foreign news and official notices. It used movable type and was published as a regular daily. It is a landmark because it showed that a Japanese-language daily could be sustained by a market, though it did not remain the dominant paper for long. The National Diet Library has digitized early runs, and the National Diet Library's documentation of newspaper history is a useful starting point for students seeking verified dates and publishers.

In Tokyo, the Tokyo Nichinichi Shinbun began in 1872 and, under the editor Fukuchi Gen'ichirō from 1874, became a leading paper that supported the government's gradualist outlook and offered informed commentary on politics. The Yūbin Hōchi Shinbun, founded in 1872, became a prominent organ of reformist opinion and was associated with the Ōkuma Shigenobu circle and, from 1879, with the editor Yano Ryūkei. Other papers, such as the Chōya Shinbun founded in 1874 and associated with the writers Narushima Ryūhoku and Suehiro Tetchō, became known for pointed political criticism. These were ōshinbun, large-format political papers addressed to educated men who followed national affairs.

Alongside them grew a different kind of paper. The Yomiuri Shinbun, which began on 2 November 1874, was a small-format paper that used a colloquial style and relied heavily on reading aids so that people without extensive training in classical Chinese could read it. It offered local news, crime reports, serialized stories, and human-interest items, and it reached ordinary townspeople, shopkeepers, and women readers more readily than the political papers did. This was the koshinbun ("small paper") tradition. In Osaka, the Asahi Shinbun was launched on 25 January 1879 by the financier Murayama Ryōhei and his associates; it soon became a major commercial paper, expanded to Tokyo in 1888 through its acquisition of an existing paper, and developed a reputation for combining reliable reporting with a broad readership. Other important papers of the era included Fukuzawa Yukichi's Jiji Shinpō (1882), Tokutomi Sohō's Kokumin Shinbun (1890), and the scandal-oriented Yorozu Chōhō (1892).

The distinction between elite political papers and popular papers should not be overdrawn. Over time the two forms converged: the successful political papers adopted features of the popular press such as serialized fiction, crime coverage, and advertising, and the popular papers added political reporting and editorials. By the 1890s the major dailies, especially the Osaka and Tokyo Asahi and the Osaka and Tokyo Mainichi, were large commercial enterprises that aimed to be both informative and entertaining. The persistence of regional papers, such as those in provincial cities, and the establishment of papers in the periphery, including the Ryūkyū Shinpō, launched in Okinawa on 15 September 1893 (initially issued on alternate days and becoming a daily in 1906), show that the press was never a purely Tokyo-Osaka phenomenon.

Section 65.4

Newspaper Enterprises, Editors, Advertisers, and the Commercial Press

By the 1880s the newspaper was not only a vehicle for opinion but a business, and its commercial logic shaped what it printed. Revenue came from three sources: subscriptions, retail sales by street vendors and at stands, and advertising. Subscription gave a paper predictable income and, through the postal system, a national reach; retail sales rewarded sensational headlines and the daily news-gathering that kept the paper fresh; advertising, which grew with the expansion of consumer goods, patent medicines, books, department-store and retail goods, and theatrical announcements, became the single most important source of profit for the leading papers. A paper that attracted advertisers needed a large and desirable readership, which in turn encouraged editors to broaden their appeal.

Staffing also changed. Early papers were often the work of a small group of literati, translators, and printers. By the 1890s major papers employed reporters (kisha), editorial writers, illustrators, correspondents in other cities and abroad, and a growing business staff, and journalism emerged as an identifiable occupation. Many early journalists came from former samurai families whose education in Chinese learning and political opinion prepared them for editorial work; others, like Fukuzawa Yukichi, were intellectuals who used newspapers as vehicles for their ideas. The reporters who gathered news were mostly men, and the profession remained overwhelmingly male, although women appeared as readers, subjects of coverage, and, in smaller numbers, as writers for women's pages and magazines. The commercial press also created related industries: news agencies, such as the Dentsū company founded in 1901, which combined advertising and news distribution, and advertising agencies that matched advertisers with newspaper space.

Competition drove innovation. Newspapers adopted new printing technology, installed telegraph connections, ran serialized novels that kept readers buying each day, offered prizes and promotional events, and sponsored public activities. Writers whose fiction appeared in newspaper serials reached readers in far larger numbers than book sales alone could have supplied; the novels of Ozaki Kōyō, serialized in the Yomiuri, and later those of Natsume Sōseki, who joined the Asahi in 1907, show how the commercial newspaper became a patron of literature. The commercial press thus pulled several forces together: a profit motive, a public that wanted to be informed and entertained, and writers who needed an audience.

Section 65.5

Political Journalism, Public Opinion, and Government Regulation

Newspapers quickly became instruments of political mobilization. During the 1870s and 1880s, as Chapter 57 described, advocates of constitutional government and popular rights used papers to publish petitions, report meetings, debate the shape of a future constitution, and criticize the oligarchs who dominated the government. The editorial column became a recognized form of political speech, and the debates among papers allowed readers across the country to follow the same arguments at roughly the same time. In this sense the press helped create the "public opinion" (yoron) that politicians increasingly invoked. Newspapers also reported the proceedings of political parties and, after 1890, the Imperial Diet, and they supported or opposed candidates in elections.

The government responded with both patronage and prohibition. In June 1875 the authorities issued a revised Newspaper Ordinance and a Libel Law that restricted criticism of officials and of the government and gave the Home Ministry power to suspend or ban publications. Over the next several years numerous editors and writers, including the Chōya Shinbun's Narushima Ryūhoku and Suehiro Tetchō, were fined or imprisoned, and some papers closed. Further ordinances in the 1880s tightened or adjusted these controls, and the Peace Preservation Ordinance of 1887 gave the state additional authority to deal with political agitators. The 1893 Publication Law and the Newspaper Law of 1909 formalized the regime: the 1909 law retained the Home Minister's power to ban the sale or distribution of papers and to suspend publication, and it subjected papers to rules about registration, deposit, and editorial responsibility. Prosecutions fell more heavily on opposition and socialist papers; the case of the Heimin Shinbun, founded in 1903 by Kōtoku Shūsui and Sakai Toshihiko, whose antiwar stance during the Russo-Japanese War ended in suppression, shows how this worked in practice.

Government regulation did not simply suppress the press; it also shaped what kinds of press could succeed. Heavy fines and the risk of closure rewarded caution, and some papers learned to publish critical commentary through satire, allusion, and the use of "nominal editors" (migawari), men who took the legal responsibility and the imprisonment in place of the real writers. Officials, meanwhile, cultivated friendly papers, supplied them with news, and used them to publish official views. Historians therefore describe the Meiji press as both a space for criticism and an object of state management, and they generally avoid the simple picture of a heroic free press battling a repressive state or of a docile press serving the government.

Section 65.6

War Reporting, Illustrated News, and the Making of National Spectacle

The First Sino-Japanese War of 1894–95 and the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05, whose military and diplomatic course is treated in Chapters 60 and 61, were also media events. Newspapers sent correspondents with the armies and navies, published battle reports and casualty lists, and issued extra editions, sometimes several in a day, whenever news arrived. Circulation rose sharply, and the major papers extended their national reach. Writers such as Kunikida Doppo, who reported on the 1894 war for a Tokyo paper, found in war correspondence an audience for their prose. The war also strengthened the commercial case for the telegraph, the rotary press, and the dedicated war correspondent.

The war was visualized in several forms at once. During the Sino-Japanese War woodblock artists such as Kobayashi Kiyochika and Mizuno Toshikata produced large numbers of colored triptych prints depicting land and naval battles, using the conventions of earlier battle prints, often with dramatic composition and imagined detail, and these were sold in great numbers. The publisher Hakubunkan issued illustrated serial publications on the war, including its Nisshin Sensō Jikki, and repeated the model for the war with Russia, increasingly making use of photographs reproduced through halftone processes. Picture postcards, legalized for private production in 1900, became a collecting craze during and after the Russo-Japanese War, and commemorative issues of postcards and lantern slides carried images of battles, generals, and sailors. Plays and storytelling performances staged the war, as Section 65.12 will show, and war footage was exhibited by early film exhibitors.

These representations were shaped by official control and by commercial interest. Military authorities restricted what correspondents could see and report, especially in 1904–05, when rules governed accreditation and the censoring of dispatches, and casualty information was managed. Many images, including woodblock prints, were composed rather than observed, and even photographs were selected, captioned, and sometimes staged. Readers received a vivid, emotionally engaging, and heavily curated picture of the war. This helped produce the patriotic enthusiasm that marked both wars; it also helped create the expectations that the Treaty of Portsmouth in 1905 seemed to betray, which fueled the Hibiya riot in September 1905 and in which some newspapers took part in whipping up public anger. The press was thus both a channel for information and a participant in political mobilization.

The wars also deepened the connection between the media and the empire. Reports from Taiwan, Korea, Manchuria, and the Liaodong Peninsula, accompanied by photographs and maps, taught domestic readers to see these places through Japanese eyes, and Japanese-language newspapers were founded in the new colonies and in Korea, with governmental support, to serve settlers and to convey official views. Historical Debate

Did the Commercial Press Broaden Political Debate or Strengthen Official Nationalism?

The question: By the early twentieth century the commercial daily press reached very large readerships. Historians disagree about whether the commercial success of newspapers widened and diversified public political discussion, or whether it tended to channel readers toward patriotic consensus, especially during the wars.

Interpretation A — The press as an engine of public debate: Some historians emphasize that papers brought politics to a wide audience, reported the Diet, criticized the government, and gave voice to opposition parties, labor, and socialists. They note episodes such as the antiwar writing of the Heimin Shinbun, the press's role in criticizing cabinet policies, and the growing practice of treating government action as subject to public judgment.
Interpretation B — The press as an engine of nationalist mobilization: Others stress that commercial competition rewarded stirring war coverage, that the biggest papers supported the wars enthusiastically, that official information and military censorship shaped what readers saw, and that popular indignation over the terms of the 1905 peace was stoked by newspapers that stood to profit from the excitement.

Evidence: Both positions draw on editorials, circulation patterns, press prosecutions, accounts of the Hibiya riots, and the memoirs of journalists, and on comparisons between papers. The positions are not mutually exclusive: the same papers could be critical of ministers and enthusiastic about the nation's wars.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question affects their understanding of whether mass media and mass politics in Japan tended toward pluralism or toward conformity, which shapes how they interpret the democratic movements of Chapter 67 and the militarism of later chapters.

Division II — Photography, Portraiture, and the New Visual Representation of Japan · Section 65.7

The Arrival of Photography and the First Japanese Commercial Studios

Photography reached Japan in the decades before the Meiji Restoration through several channels, and historians distinguish among them rather than crediting a single "first." Dutch traders at Nagasaki are known to have brought daguerreotype equipment in the 1840s, and some Japanese scholars of Dutch learning experimented with the process. The American expedition led by Commodore Perry in 1853–54 included a daguerreotypist, and its images of Japan and its people were made on Japanese soil. Domain lords with an interest in Western science, notably the Satsuma domain, also sponsored experiments, and a portrait of the Satsuma lord Shimazu Nariakira made in 1857 is often cited as an early Japanese-made photograph. All of these were experimental or isolated; none established a photographic trade.

The trade began with the arrival of the wet collodion process, in which a glass negative was coated, exposed, and developed while the coating was still wet, producing sharp negatives from which many albumen prints could be made. The process required chemical knowledge, equipment, and skill, but it allowed reproducible images at a price that a commercial studio could sustain. In the early 1860s, two Japanese practitioners established studios that are conventionally cited as among the first commercial photographic businesses run by Japanese: Ueno Hikoma in Nagasaki, who learned his craft through contacts with Dutch and other foreign residents and opened his studio around 1862, and Shimooka Renjō in Yokohama, who learned from foreign photographers in the treaty port and also established a studio in about 1862. Shimooka later moved his business to Tokyo and trained others. Claims about who was "first" depend on definitions: the first to experiment, the first to make a photograph in Japan, the first Japanese to run a studio for paying customers, and the first to earn a living from photography are different questions.

Foreign photographers played a major role. Felice Beato, who arrived in Yokohama in 1863 after photographing in India and China, worked there for roughly two decades, producing views, portraits, and, in collaboration with the artist Charles Wirgman for a time, a sophisticated line of albums for foreign buyers. He photographed scenes of the bombardment of Shimonoseki in 1864 and made the hand-colored images that defined the "Yokohama photograph" (Yokohama shashin) genre. His methods, his staff of Japanese assistants and colorists, and the market he served illustrate that early photography was a collaborative enterprise across national lines: foreign entrepreneurs often supplied capital, equipment, and access to the export market, while Japanese assistants supplied labor, local knowledge, and, increasingly, skill.

For historians, the commercial studio matters because it turned photography into a business and, therefore, into a source with a particular logic. A studio photograph was made for a paying customer who chose how to appear; a souvenir photograph was made for a purchaser who wanted a particular image of Japan; a news photograph was made for a publisher. Each of these purposes shaped what appears in the image. The Smithsonian's National Museum of Asian Art and the National Diet Library are among the institutions that preserve early photographs and biographical documentation of these photographers, and students can consult their online collections for specific images.

Section 65.8

Portrait Photography, Families, Celebrities, and Social Identity

Portraiture was photography's staple business and the one that touched the largest number of Japanese people. In the late Tokugawa and early Meiji years, former samurai, officials, and merchants had portraits made in Yokohama, Nagasaki, Tokyo, and other cities. A portrait was a formal event. The sitter chose clothing, posed against a backdrop, sometimes held props such as swords, books, or fans, and sat still for the exposure time. Early studios kept a supply of costumes and furnishings, and the visual conventions drew both on earlier portrait painting and on European studio photography. After the abolition of the samurai order, photographs of men in uniform, in Western-style suits, and in cropped hair recorded changes in appearance that the state encouraged, and the choice between traditional and Western dress became a statement of identity.

The state also discovered the political uses of photography. In 1872 the photographer Uchida Kuichi produced portraits of Emperor Meiji, who had until then been largely invisible to ordinary subjects, and the emperor appeared in Western military dress. In 1888 an engraver at the Printing Bureau, Edoardo Chiossone, an Italian working for the Japanese government, produced a drawn portrait of the emperor in military uniform, which was then photographed and distributed. Photographic reproductions of the emperor and empress (goshin'ei) were supplied to schools and public offices, and ceremonial practices around them developed, especially from the 1890s. The imperial portrait is thus an example of photography used to create a visual relationship between the emperor and his subjects, a topic taken up again in Chapter 77.

Ordinary families began to commission portraits too. Weddings, graduations, military service, and departures for study or overseas work became occasions for photographs, and the family portrait became a keepsake that migrants, soldiers, and students carried with them. Entertainers and public figures were also photographed, and their images were sold as cards and, from 1900, as picture postcards. A kabuki actor's photograph in costume extended his fame beyond the audience that had seen him on stage; the same logic would later apply to film stars. In these portraits, the sitter's participation mattered: they were not passive subjects, but they worked within the conventions the studio supplied and within what they could afford.

Women's portraits illustrate the tension between self-presentation and market. Studios photographed women as customers, but they also produced pictures of women, notably geisha and courtesans, for sale to foreign and domestic buyers. The same woman could appear as a subject of her own portrait and as an anonymous type in a commercial series. The historian's task is to ask who commissioned an image, who bought it, and what its caption and arrangement reveal.

Section 65.9

Travel Photography, Hand-Colored Images, and the Commercial Representation of Japan

A large part of the photographic trade served foreign tourists and residents, and it produced a distinctive product: albums of albumen prints, often hand-colored, that presented Japan as a country of picturesque landscapes, shrines and temples, craftspeople, festivals, and women in kimono. Beato's studio in Yokohama set the model. After he left Japan in 1884, the business continued under Baron Raimund von Stillfried and later others, and the photographer Kusakabe Kimbei, who had begun as a colorist for Beato and Stillfried, opened his own studio in Yokohama in about 1881 and became one of the most prolific and skilled producers. Albums were made to order: customers could choose among stock views and portraits and have an album bound with lacquer covers.

Hand-coloring was a Japanese craft transferred to a new medium. Studios employed painters and printmakers trained in the woodblock and brush traditions to color each print, and the delicate palettes of the best work reflect this training. The production was organized in a workshop system with specialization of tasks: a photographer or studio owner made the negatives, assistants produced the prints, and colorists applied pigments. The finished product circulated in the treaty ports and overseas, and was a form of what is sometimes called export art.

The images are not neutral documents of everyday life. Many were staged in studios with painted backdrops and props to produce representations of "types" such as the samurai, the geisha, the rickshaw man, or the farmer, and some presented customs that were already disappearing or that never had been as depicted. By the 1880s the samurai class had been abolished, but swordsmen continued to appear in photographs sold to foreigners. The curated Japan was a product of what buyers wished to see, an exoticized and timeless country that the surrounding modernization of the 1880s made no appearance in. This does not mean the images are worthless as evidence: they show clothing, tools, hairstyles, and the settings of work, and they document the commercial relationship between foreign buyers and Japanese producers. But it means that students must read them for what they were made to be.

Section 65.10

The Photograph as Historical Evidence: News, Empire, Documentation, and Manipulation

By the 1890s photographs were used for a wider set of purposes, and they raised the question of evidence in a sharper form. Newspapers and magazines began to publish photographs, first as engravings made from photographs and then, as halftone technology spread in the 1890s and 1900s, as direct reproductions. Illustrated war publications and the picture postcards of the Russo-Japanese War relied on photographs of troops, ships, and officers. The state and the military used photography to document campaigns, administration, and construction projects; colonial authorities used it to document territories and peoples; and anthropologists such as Torii Ryūzō, who photographed in Taiwan from the late 1890s and among the Ainu of Hokkaido and the Kuril Islands, used the camera as a tool of scientific description.

Photographs shaped how Japanese audiences saw the peoples of the new empire. The Ainu had been photographed in the 1870s by photographers associated with the Hokkaidō Colonization Commission, who documented both construction and the Ainu communities affected by colonization, and later photographers produced portraits as "types." Photographs of Taiwanese indigenous peoples, of Koreans, and of Chinese soldiers and civilians were published in books and papers and sold as postcards. Such images presented their subjects as objects of information and, often, as evidence of the contrast between a "civilized" Japan and the "backward" peoples it ruled or fought. People in these photographs rarely had control over how they were posed, captioned, or sold.

Historians therefore insist that a photograph is not an objective record simply because a camera made it. Several operations intervene between a scene and the printed image. The photographer chooses what to photograph, from where, and when; the subjects may pose, or be posed; the exposure and processing choices affect what is visible. After the image is made, it can be cropped, retouched, captioned, and combined with text, and the caption can change its meaning entirely. A picture of soldiers at rest, captioned as a victory, becomes a different thing from the same picture captioned as a march. And the circulation of the image, in a newspaper, an album, or a postcard, determines its audience and its effect. Treating photographs as constructed evidence does not mean treating them as lies; it means asking what questions a particular photograph can answer and what it cannot.

Division III — Theater, Performance, and the Transformation of the Stage · Section 65.11

Kabuki in the Meiji Era: Reform, Modern Theaters, and Famous Actors

Kabuki, the dominant commercial theater of Tokugawa cities (Chapter 46), survived the Restoration but had to adapt to a new political and cultural order. Its position was awkward. It had been a plebeian entertainment, associated with the pleasure quarters and regarded by Confucian officials as morally dubious, and in a government that wished to show Japan as civilized to Western observers, it was an easy target of criticism for its violence, sexuality, and what reformers called unrealistic or crude plots. Yet it commanded a loyal audience and employed hundreds of people. The question of what kabuki would become in a modern state occupied actors, playwrights, managers, and officials for decades.

Reform came in several forms. Kawatake Mokuami, the leading playwright of the late Tokugawa and early Meiji eras, wrote plays that reflected the new society, including zangiri-mono ("cropped-hair plays") that portrayed the Meiji world of Western clothes and new technologies. The leading actor Ichikawa Danjūrō IX pursued what became known as katsureki-geki ("living history plays"), which aimed at historical accuracy in costume and dialogue and rejected what he considered the extravagances of older plays. Onoe Kikugorō V cultivated a more realistic and subtle style in contemporary and domestic plays, and Ichikawa Sadanji I contributed his own range; together with Danjūrō and Kikugorō, he is remembered as one of the three great actors of the era known by the shorthand "Dan-Kiku-Sa." A Theatrical Reform Society (Engeki Kairyōkai), established in 1886 by officials and literati, including the scholar-official Suematsu Kenchō, sought to raise the status of the stage by promoting reform of plays and theaters; its goals included building modern theaters and encouraging respectable audiences, including women.

Efforts to make kabuki respectable culminated in a notable event in April 1887, when the emperor attended a kabuki performance at the residence of the foreign minister Inoue Kaoru, with Danjūrō IX, Kikugorō V, and Sadanji I performing. This tenran kabuki ("kabuki viewed by the emperor") conferred prestige, and the actors invoked it for years. The architecture changed too. The Shintomi-za, opened in Tokyo in 1878 with gas lighting and a Western-influenced façade, was an attempt to create a modern theater. The Kabuki-za, which opened in 1889 under the sponsorship of the journalist Fukuchi Gen'ichirō and others, was designed to look like a Western building outside while holding a traditional kabuki stage inside. The Imperial Theatre (Teikoku Gekijō), opened in 1911 in Tokyo with the support of leading financiers, offered Western-style seating and a Western-style building, a sign of how the commercial and respectable elite wished to see the theater. Seating itself changed, as the floor-box (masu) arrangements in older theaters gave way, in newer ones, to chairs.

Even so, kabuki remained rooted in older conventions. The roles of women continued to be played by onnagata, male actors who specialized in female roles, and the hereditary family system continued to govern who could perform which roles. Legal restrictions on women performing on stage were relaxed in the Meiji period, but professional kabuki remained a male theater. By the early twentieth century, a few large companies and the Shōchiku business, founded by the brothers Ōtani Takejirō and Shirai Matsujirō in Kyoto in 1902 and expanding into Tokyo and ultimately taking over the Kabuki-za, were consolidating the commercial management of theaters. Kabuki's survival thus depended on a combination of reform, prestige, and business consolidation.

Section 65.12

Shinpa: Political Theater, Kawakami Otojirō, and Contemporary Drama

A different kind of theater grew out of political activism. In the late 1880s, as the Freedom and People's Rights Movement faced repression, activists known as sōshi began to stage plays, called sōshi shibai ("activist theater"), that dramatized political ideas and current events. They were often amateur and unsophisticated, but they reached audiences who might not read political newspapers. Sudō Sadanori and Kawakami Otojirō were among the best-known early practitioners. Kawakami, born in Hakata in 1864, had been a political activist and a popular street performer of satirical songs, and he built a theatrical career on contemporary subject matter, topical references, and a lively, accessible manner.

After performances in Sakai in February 1891, Kawakami's troupe appeared at the Nakamura-za in Tokyo in June 1891 and won a substantial audience, dramatizing recent events, including the 1882 attack on the political leader Itagaki Taisuke. The company then found a larger audience through the First Sino-Japanese War, staging plays that dramatized the conflict in vivid, topical productions. Kawakami's war plays drew large crowds and showed that contemporary events could sustain a commercial theater. Contemporary plays of this kind came to be called shinpa ("new school"), in contrast to kyūha ("old school"), the label applied to kabuki. As shinpa moved away from its political origins, it turned increasingly to melodrama, adapting popular novels such as Ozaki Kōyō's Konjiki Yasha, Kikuchi Yūhō's Ono ga Tsumi, and Tokutomi Roka's Hototogisu for the stage. Shinpa actors, including Ii Yōhō, Kitamura Rokurō, and Kawai Takeo, built reputations playing in these emotionally intense dramas, and shinpa became a staple of commercial theaters, particularly in Tokyo and Osaka, throughout the Meiji and Taishō periods.

Shinpa used contemporary dress and settings, spoken dialogue closer to everyday speech, and plots from newspaper serials and current events. It also depended on the onnagata in its early phases, though actresses gradually appeared. Its relation to the press was close: serials in newspapers provided stories that audiences already knew, and theatrical publicity appeared in the same papers. It also had a relationship with early film. Shinpa-style melodrama and its performers supplied much of the content of early Japanese films, as Section 65.22 shows.

Historical Vocabulary
Shinpa, Shingeki, and Onnagata

Shinpa ("new school") designates the popular contemporary theater that grew from sōshi shibai and became melodrama drawn from newspapers, novels, and current events. Shingeki ("new drama") designates a separate movement that aimed to build a modern, literary, realist theater based on translated and original plays, often influenced by European drama. Onnagata are male actors who play women's roles, a practice rooted in kabuki, where women were barred from the stage in the Tokugawa period.

These terms are often confused. Shinpa and shingeki are not interchangeable: shinpa was commercially oriented and melodramatic, shingeki was reformist and literary, and each defined itself partly against the other and against kabuki. Neither "replaced" kabuki, which continued to draw audiences throughout the period. The English phrase "new theater" can obscure these differences.

Section 65.13

Kawakami Sadayakko, Women Performers, and International Theater

Kawakami Sadayakko (born Koyama Sada in Tokyo in 1871) was a geisha who married Kawakami Otojirō and became the first woman of the company to perform leading roles, and one of the earliest Japanese actresses to achieve international fame. Her career illustrates the changing opportunities and the continuing limits facing women performers. In the 1890s, when the Kawakami troupe was performing in Japan, women's roles were still mostly played by men. Sadayakko's appearances on stage, initially in a few roles and increasingly in leading parts, were thus novel, and her visibility made her a public figure.

In 1899 the company traveled to the United States, where it performed in San Francisco and other cities, and in 1900 to Europe, where it appeared in London and at the Paris Exposition. Their repertoire presented Japanese stories, combat scenes, and dance, and it was adapted for foreign audiences with a view to what they would find appealing. Sadayakko was an especially celebrated performer, and European artists and critics wrote about her. Subsequent claims about her influence on named European artists and composers have been repeated widely and sometimes embellished; historians treat the broad point, that she was a sensation in Paris and London, as well documented, while treating specific claims of influence with caution. The company's tours were not a straightforward showing of "authentic" Japanese theater. They were commercial productions designed for the market, mixing elements of kabuki, shinpa, and invented novelties, and they confirmed, as photography had, that foreign audiences were interested in a certain image of Japan.

Sadayakko later continued to perform and, in 1908, established a school for training actresses, the Imperial Actress Training School (Teikoku Joyū Yōseijo), which helped open the stage to women as a profession. Her husband died in 1911, and she retired from the stage in 1917. The wider context was one in which the question of women on stage was contested: moralists criticized actresses as improper, performers faced stigma and economic dependence on patrons, and the legal and social status of "actress" (joyū) as a profession was uncertain. Matsui Sumako, who rose to fame in the realist theater in 1911 and later in a celebrated commercial production, is another example of a woman who became a public figure at considerable personal cost. Women performers thus occupied a paradoxical position: new media made them famous, and fame gave some of them unusual independence, but the social and economic constraints on them remained severe.

Section 65.14

Shingeki, Tsubouchi Shōyō, and New Experiments in Realist Theater

Alongside the commercial reform of kabuki and the popular melodrama of shinpa, a third movement sought to create a modern theater based on literary drama. Its leading intellectual figure was Tsubouchi Shōyō (1859–1935), a novelist, critic, and professor at Waseda University, who translated Shakespeare, wrote essays on theater reform, and argued that Japan needed a drama that combined the dignity of literature with the power of performance. In 1906 Shōyō and others, including the critic Shimamura Hōgetsu, founded the Literary Arts Society (Bungei Kyōkai), which promoted drama through lectures, training, and productions. Its repertoire included translations of Shakespeare and of Henrik Ibsen. In 1911, with the Imperial Theatre as venue, the society staged Ibsen's A Doll's House with Matsui Sumako in the role of Nora, a production that attracted wide attention and sparked public discussion of the "new woman" and the position of women in marriage.

A second experiment came from a different quarter. In 1909 the young director Osanai Kaoru (1881–1928) and the kabuki actor Ichikawa Sadanji II (1880–1940) founded the Free Theater (Jiyū Gekijō), modeled on the independent theaters of Europe, such as André Antoine's Théâtre-Libre. It staged its first production, Ibsen's John Gabriel Borkman, in November 1909. It was small, intellectually ambitious, and financially precarious, and it ran only for some years, but it introduced directing as a discipline, emphasized ensemble acting and naturalistic staging, and trained a generation of theater people. Together the two movements are often called early shingeki, and the term came to designate the modern, Western-influenced theater that developed more fully in the 1920s.

The relationship among kabuki, shinpa, and shingeki must be described carefully. They competed for audiences and prestige, and they borrowed from one another. Kabuki actors joined shingeki experiments, as Sadanji II did; shinpa adapted both novels and, at times, European plays; shingeki borrowed from the professional skills of stage practitioners trained in older traditions. Their audiences overlapped but were not identical: shingeki's audience was small, urban, and educated; shinpa's was broader and included many women; kabuki's retained its traditional and increasingly prestige-oriented following. All three continued in the twentieth century. A narrative in which "modern theater" simply replaced "traditional theater" misses this coexistence.

  • Entirely new genres appeared: a political and then melodramatic theater (shinpa) and a literary, translation-based theater (shingeki)

Who experienced change most directly? Actresses and young directors faced the most dramatic changes in opportunity; kabuki's hereditary actors experienced the pressure to adapt while maintaining continuity. Who experienced greater continuity? Many regional and working-class audiences continued to attend older entertainments with relatively little change. Why the pattern matters: It shows that cultural modernization in Meiji Japan produced layering and coexistence rather than simple replacement.

Section 65.15

Professional Performers, Theater Audiences, and Commercial Entertainment

The business of theater changed along with its aesthetics. Theater owners and managers (zamoto and, later, corporate companies) arranged leases, hired actors, paid for sets, and sold tickets, and they competed for the best performers. Because a star actor could fill a house, actors' contracts, their positions in the hierarchy of their families, and their relations with managers shaped what was staged. The consolidation of theater ownership under companies such as Shōchiku turned a patchwork of independently managed houses into networks that could organize tours, share actors, and standardize seating and pricing. By the 1910s, the Tokyo and Osaka theatrical worlds were increasingly corporate.

Audience conventions were varied and social. Older theaters organized seats by status and price, with box seats (sajiki) and the floor boxes (masu) at the more expensive end and gallery seating at the other, and the buying of tickets often passed through "teahouses" attached to theaters, which arranged seating, meals, and refreshments for patrons. Theatergoing could take an entire day. Audiences responded vocally, called out actors' stage names at key moments, and sometimes included people who attended mainly to see particular performers. In the newer theaters, the arrangement of seating and the expectation of silence were more like those of Western theaters, a change that some patrons welcomed and others resisted.

Theaters were part of larger entertainment districts. Asakusa in Tokyo, whose Rokku zone was developed in the 1880s following the redevelopment of Asakusa Park, housed theaters, storytelling halls, exhibitions, and eventually cinemas; in Osaka, Dōtonbori and the amusement quarter of Sennichimae had a similar function. In 1890 the twelve-story Ryōunkaku tower, the "Cloud-Surpassing Pavilion," opened in Asakusa, and in 1912 the Shinsekai amusement district with its tower opened in Osaka. Crowds, food stalls, advertising, and the presence of commerce in sexual entertainment gave these districts a distinctive character, and they became the laboratories in which new media forms, including cinema, would be tested before audiences. Touring companies carried repertoire to provincial theaters, though provincial audiences saw a smaller range of productions and often later than city audiences. Actors' publicity relied on posters, newspaper notices, postcards, and photographs, anticipating the star system of cinema.

Before Cinema: Utsushi-e, Magic Lanterns, and Visual Entertainment

When motion pictures arrived in Japan in the mid-1890s, they entered a country with a vivid tradition of projected and performed images. Utsushi-e ("projected pictures") were shows in which painted glass slides were projected by a magic lantern, an apparatus that had come to Japan in the eighteenth century through Dutch learning and become a popular entertainment. Performers manipulated slides, sometimes moving them or using multiple lanterns to create the impression of motion, and told stories as the images appeared, often in the genres of ghost tales and moral stories. This was not a silent medium: projection and narration were joined, and the performer's voice gave the images their meaning. The National Film Archive of Japan documents utsushi-e and notes the links between this tradition and later live narration of films.

Other forms of visual spectacle also prepared audiences. Misemono ("shows") of the Tokugawa and Meiji eras included peep shows, panoramas, shadow plays, and displays of curiosities at temple precincts and entertainment quarters. In the Meiji period, lantern slides were used in education and propaganda: slides of Sino-Japanese War scenes, for example, were exhibited with lectures. Western-style panoramas, circus acts, and later electric illuminations added to the repertoire of visual entertainment in places like Asakusa. These forms shared with film the idea that images could be exhibited to a paying audience as a spectacle.

Three features of this earlier culture shaped the reception of cinema. First, viewers expected performance to accompany image, and narration was part of the experience. Second, entertainment was commercial and located in particular districts, so the new medium would naturally be sited there. Third, audiences were accustomed to thinking of images as representations of stories already known to them, from kabuki, storytelling, and woodblock prints, and early film often built on this familiarity. These continuities help to explain why film exhibition in Japan developed so quickly and in the particular forms it did.

Section 65.17

The Introduction of Motion Pictures, 1896–1897

Motion-picture technology reached Japan within months of its commercial launch in Europe and the United States, through the import networks that already supplied new machines and goods to the treaty ports. Several devices appeared in close succession, and sources differ in details of dates and venues, so students should treat the sequence as established in outline rather than in every particular. Thomas Edison's Kinetoscope, a peep-show device in which one viewer at a time looked into a cabinet, was publicly exhibited at the Shinkō Club in Kobe from 25 November 1896. The Lumière Cinématographe, which projected moving images onto a screen for an audience, was publicly demonstrated at the Nanchi Enbujō in Osaka on 15 February 1897; other projection systems and exhibitors followed in additional cities. These included the Cinématographe developed by the Lumière brothers, brought to Japan by the Kyoto textile businessman Inabata Katsutarō, who had been a schoolmate of Auguste Lumière in Lyon and who arranged for the apparatus and a Lumière cameraman, Constant Girel, to come to Japan, and Edison's Vitascope, which was also imported. Inabata's role illustrates the way commercial and personal networks tied Japanese businessmen to the technological centers of Europe.

These first screenings were exhibitions of novelty. Programs consisted of very short films, lasting typically less than a minute, showing street scenes, trains, workers leaving factories, dancers, and other everyday subjects, usually from foreign producers. Audiences reportedly found the experience astonishing, but the evidence for particular reactions comes largely from newspaper reports and later reminiscences and should not be treated as a transparent record of what everyone felt. Exhibitors were often entrepreneurs and showmen, and the machines were expensive and delicate, requiring skilled operators. Early shows were held in rented halls, theaters, and temporary venues, and exhibitors traveled between cities with their equipment. Because the programs consisted of short pieces without sustained narrative, a lecturer or setsumeisha (explainer) would introduce the films, a practice that grew out of lantern shows and storytelling.

Japanese audiences also began to see films of Japan. The Lumière company's cameramen filmed in Japan in 1897–98 and produced views of daily life and dance that were then circulated abroad, which raises the question of the Japanese view as an exotic subject once more. At the same time, Japanese exhibitors and photographers began to acquire cameras and to film for themselves.

Section 65.18

The First Japanese Films: Actualities, Trick Films, and Momijigari

Japanese film production began almost immediately, but identifying "the first Japanese film" is not straightforward, and responsible accounts avoid doing so. Several different milestones can be distinguished: the first film shot in Japan (by a foreign cameraman, in 1897), the first films made by Japanese filmmakers, the earliest surviving Japanese-made film, and the first film with a plot. Each yields a different answer, and the survival of early films is partial, so what is called the earliest film in the archives may not be the earliest ever made.

Shibata Tsunekichi (1850–1929), a photographer employed by the Tokyo firm Konishi Honten, was among the earliest Japanese people to make films. In 1897–98 he filmed street scenes and actualities in Tokyo, and in 1898 he made short trick films, including Bake-jizō ("The Ghost Jizō") and Shinin no Sosei ("Resuscitation of a Corpse"), which used simple camera tricks such as stop-motion substitution to produce apparitions. The ghost and transformation tricks resembled those of kabuki and of contemporary European trick films, and they show how early filmmakers exploited the new medium's capacity to produce effects impossible on stage. In November 1899 Shibata filmed Momijigari ("Maple Viewing"), a short record of a kabuki dance-drama performed by Ichikawa Danjūrō IX and Onoe Kikugorō V, two of the most famous actors of the age. The film is usually described as the oldest surviving Japanese film of significant length and, in the form in which it survives, it is a fragment; Japanese heritage authorities have given it formal protection as a cultural property.

What Momijigari shows is as important as its date. It filmed a canonical kabuki play, a dance-drama in which a warrior encounters a woman who is revealed to be a demon, with leading actors, from a fixed camera position, in a single continuous shot. The film was not a "movie" in the later narrative sense but a recording of a stage performance, and this reflects the relationship of early cinema to the theater: cinema borrowed the prestige and the repertoire of kabuki, and filmmakers saw their work as capturing the art of famous performers. Historians also note that preserving the actors' art may have been part of the purpose. The picture quality and the surviving state of the film mean that scholars have to be cautious about conclusions regarding performance style, since a short, fixed-camera film of a long dance cannot show everything.

Section 65.19

Benshi: Narrators, Interpretation, and the Spoken Art of Silent Cinema

The benshi (also called katsudō benshi, "moving-picture explainers") are the most distinctive feature of Japanese early film exhibition. A benshi was a performer who stood or sat beside the screen and spoke during the film: explaining, narrating, giving voices to characters, commenting, and entertaining. The popular image of the benshi as a figure reading intertitles is inadequate. Early films from abroad had few intertitles and were short, and most Japanese audiences did not understand their foreign settings and conventions, so the benshi's job was in part to translate and interpret: to tell the audience where the story was set, who the characters were, and what was happening, often adding explanatory commentary and cultural bridges. Later, as films grew longer and more complex, the benshi developed a more elaborate art of vocal characterization, emotional delivery, and dramatic pacing.

The roots of the benshi lay in earlier narrative performance. Utsushi-e narrators, storytelling in the rakugo, kōdan, and rōkyoku traditions, the gidayū narration of bunraku, and the speakers at street entertainments all supplied models of how to bring a visual or staged story to life with the voice. The earliest explainers at film showings drew on these traditions and often had backgrounds in them. Somei Saburō, documented at the Asakusa Denkikan from 1906, became an influential early Tokyo benshi; his subsequent work helped establish a more controlled explanatory style. As film audiences grew, leading benshi acquired reputations and could attract audiences on their own. By the 1910s benshi sometimes divided roles among several speakers, one for each major character, in a practice called kakeai, which resembled stage dialogue.

Historians caution against projecting the benshi of the late 1920s, with their celebrity, their elaborate rhetoric, their unions, and their large followings, back onto the 1890s and early 1900s. In the earliest period the explainer was more like a lecturer, accompanying a program of short films that needed introduction. The benshi's art developed as film length and narrative complexity increased, and by the 1910s had become a recognized specialization. It then continued to evolve, and became a point of debate in the 1920s about whether the benshi enhanced or obstructed the medium, a controversy outside this chapter. What the early history shows is that Japanese cinema was a live performance art from its beginning, and that the benshi was a creative interpreter whose work shaped the meaning of the film for each audience.

Historical Vocabulary
Benshi (Katsudō Benshi)

The benshi was a live narrator of motion pictures. The title "katsudō benshi" combines katsudō ("moving") and benshi ("orator, speaker"). Benshi explained, interpreted, voiced, and commented on films during exhibition, and their performance was integral to the experience.

The English term "silent film" is misleading for Japan. Early screenings were accompanied by speech, music, and sometimes sound effects, and the benshi's performance could vary from showing to showing and from theater to theater. The same film could therefore differ in meaning depending on who narrated it. Because the live performance left few direct records, historians reconstruct it from reviews, memoirs, posters, scripts that survive, and the accounts of audiences.

Section 65.20

The Movie Screening as a Live Event: Music, Benshi, and Audiences

A film screening in the 1900s and early 1910s was a composite event rather than the showing of a single film. A program might include several short films of different kinds, such as foreign comedies, actualities, scenic views, trick films, and Japanese-made performance films, sometimes interspersed with live acts. The film was projected by a machine operator who hand-cranked the projector or, later, used a motorized one, and who had to guard against the risk of fire from the highly flammable film stock. The benshi sat or stood near the screen, often at a small stand with a light, and spoke through the performance. Musicians, whose ensembles might use traditional Japanese instruments, Western instruments, or a mixture, provided accompaniment that matched the mood of the scene, and the music could draw on the repertoire of kabuki and of Western popular tunes. In some programs, called rensageki ("chain drama"), live stage scenes alternated with filmed scenes within a single story, and these hybrid productions flourished in the years after the mid-1900s.

The audience was part of the performance. People came to see the novelty of moving images, to hear a favorite benshi, to be in the company of others, and, in cinema districts, as a regular form of leisure. Audiences were mixed in age, gender, and class, but the cheaper seats attracted working-class and youthful viewers, and the sheer variety of the audience meant that theaters addressed different groups with different strategies. Police officers attended performances to monitor content and public order, and theaters, being dark, crowded, and flammable, were also subject to regulation about safety. Because seating, ticket structures, and conventions varied by theater and city, generalizations about "the" movie audience must be tentative.

Everyday Life and Environment

Attending an Early Japanese Motion-Picture Exhibition

Reconstructing an evening at an early film show requires combining evidence from posters, newspaper advertisements, memoirs, and surviving programs. A typical visit to an Asakusa cinema in the 1900s would have begun in the bustle of the entertainment district, with its signboards, vendors, and the barkers who announced the program. Inside, the audience entered a darkened hall, where seating could be on benches or on the floor, depending on the venue. The program typically consisted of several short films, each introduced and narrated by a benshi whose voice rose and fell with the action, supplemented by live music. Between films, the lights might come up, patrons might buy snacks, and the hall might be noisy with talk.

The details varied, and the evidence is uneven. We know much more about city audiences than rural ones, and we know more about the performances that were remembered than the routine ones. What the reconstruction makes clear is that going to the cinema was a social, aural, and communal experience, not a solitary act of watching, and that it was a part of the larger entertainment economy of the Japanese city, placed among theaters, storytelling halls, and food stalls, rather than something separate from it.

Section 65.21

From Traveling Exhibitions to Permanent Cinemas and Film Production

In the first years, film was exhibited by itinerant showmen who traveled with their equipment, rented theaters and halls, and moved on. This was a precarious business: the supply of new films was limited, rental costs were high, and each show required the arrival of the equipment and its operator. The key transformation was the shift to permanent cinemas, which gave exhibitors a stable audience and a fixed location. In 1903 the Denkikan opened in Asakusa's Rokku district and is generally described as Japan's first permanent cinema, a venue dedicated to film rather than a theater that occasionally showed films. Its success encouraged others, and by the end of the decade Asakusa had a concentration of cinemas, and similar specialized theaters existed in Osaka, Kyoto's Shinkyōgoku, and other cities.

Permanent cinemas created demand for a regular supply of films. Importers brought in foreign films, notably from the French company Pathé, whose films were widely distributed in Japan, and from other European and, increasingly, American producers, and Japanese firms such as Yokota Shōkai, Yoshizawa Shōten, and Fukuhōdō emerged as importers, exhibitors, and, increasingly, producers. The foreign supply was cheap and varied, but domestic films filled a particular demand for stories and performers audiences knew, and they offered exhibitors a product they could control. In 1908 Yoshizawa Shōten, led by Kawaura Ken'ichi, built a daylight glass-stage studio on Gyōnin-zaka in Meguro, Tokyo, widely documented as Japan's first purpose-built film studio; in the same year Yokota Shōkai began producing films in Kyoto. Thus the same period saw the growth of an infrastructure of exhibition, distribution, and production.

Cause and Effect

Technology, Audiences, and the Emergence of a Film Industry

Long-term causes

A mature commercial entertainment economy, a literate and urbanizing population, a tradition of narrated visual spectacle (utsushi-e, storytelling), and trade networks that brought foreign machinery quickly to Japan.

Short-term causes

The availability of foreign apparatus after 1896; the profitability of exhibitions; the war with Russia, which increased demand for topical films; and the shortage of imported films that matched Japanese tastes.

Event or transformation

The shift from traveling exhibitions to permanent cinemas (1903 onward), then to domestic production studios (1908 onward), and finally to consolidation into a large integrated company, Nikkatsu (1912).

Immediate effects

Regular programs, specialized cinema districts, a market for Japanese-made films, and the first recognized film performers and directors, together with new concerns about regulation and public morals.

Long-term consequences

A film industry organized around studios, distribution chains, and stars, which would expand in the 1920s into a central institution of urban mass culture (Chapter 70).

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the transformation. Technology made film possible, but exhibition practice, audience tastes, existing performance traditions, business strategies, and regulation determined what kind of industry resulted.
Section 65.22

The Early Japanese Film Industry: Makino Shōzō, Nikkatsu, and Screen Performance

The early film industry was shaped by the stage. Many of the first Japanese films were recordings or adaptations of kabuki scenes and of shinpa melodramas, and many performers, directors, and writers came from theater. Two traditions emerged, associated with two cities. In Kyoto, which had a long theatrical history and large stages and storage for sets, studios specialized in kyūgeki ("old drama"), films set in the premodern past featuring sword fighting and historical heroes, drawing on kabuki and the traditions of storytelling and popular fiction. In Tokyo, studios concentrated more on contemporary dramas based on shinpa, often with male actors in women's roles, called onnagata, since actresses had not yet become common in film.

Makino Shōzō (1878–1929), a theater manager in Kyoto, became the central figure of the Kyoto tradition. In 1908 he made Honnōji Gassen ("The Battle of Honnō-ji") for the Yokota company, and he went on to produce hundreds of short films. He worked with the stage actor Onoe Matsunosuke (1875–1926), who had performed in small theatrical companies and who became the first major star of Japanese cinema. From 1909 onward Matsunosuke starred in a long series of period films, and his stylized, athletic performances, drawing on kabuki-derived stage fighting and gesture, along with his wide-eyed expressiveness, earned him the nickname "Medama no Matchan" ("Matchan of the Eyes"). Historians describe him as an early screen personality and an important figure in the development of screen performance, but caution that the fully developed star system, with its elaborate publicity, was a later development. In his day, Matsunosuke's fame rested on the popularity of his films with urban and working-class audiences, including many children, and on the studios' use of his name in advertising.

In 1912 four companies, Yokota Shōkai, Yoshizawa Shōten, Fukuhōdō, and M. Pathé Shōkai, the Japanese company led by Umeya Shōkichi that had distributed films acquired from France's Pathé company, merged to form Nippon Katsudō Shashin Kabushiki Kaisha, known as Nikkatsu. It brought together production, distribution, and exhibition under a single corporate structure and soon established a Tokyo studio at Mukōjima (1913) alongside its Kyoto studio, and its Tokyo and Kyoto branches maintained the contemporary-drama and period-drama division. A competitor, Tenkatsu, was founded in 1914. By the mid-1910s, then, Japan had an organized film industry with studios, stars, genres, and a distribution system. The shift to a more continuous, narrative-driven cinema, the entry of actresses as screen performers, and the movement to reform film language belong to the next decade and to Chapter 70.

Division V — Magazines, Illustration, Popular Entertainment, and Commercial Culture · Section 65.23

Commercial Magazines, Serialized Fiction, and the Expansion of Print Entertainment

Magazines developed alongside newspapers, and they served readerships newspapers could not. The earliest, such as the Meiroku Zasshi (1874–75), published by the Meirokusha intellectual society, were vehicles for enlightenment thought and had small audiences. Later magazines were commercial products. Tokutomi Sohō's Kokumin no Tomo (1887–98), published by the Min'yūsha, combined essays on politics and society with literature, and Jogaku Zasshi (1885) addressed educated women and reformers. The Tokyo publisher Hakubunkan, founded in 1887 by Ōhashi Sahei, became the leading magazine house and launched Taiyō in 1895, a large general-interest monthly that combined articles, fiction, photographs, and illustrations and aimed to appeal to a national readership. The National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics and other scholarly bodies have assembled searchable corpora of magazines such as Meiroku Zasshi, Kokumin no Tomo, and Taiyō, which are useful for studying the language and contents of the reading market.

Fiction was central to the market. Literary magazines published the work of leading writers, and newspapers serialized novels, as discussed in Section 65.4. Ozaki Kōyō's Konjiki Yasha, serialized in the Yomiuri from 1897, Kikuchi Yūhō's Ono ga Tsumi, and Tokutomi Roka's Hototogisu became widely read, were adapted for the stage and later for film, and show how a story could move between media. The practice of serialization, in which a story appeared in installments, taught readers to return and gave publishers a means of building loyalty. Writers' fame grew with their publication, and Higuchi Ichiyō, who wrote in the 1890s and died young, became known through magazines. The expansion of print entertainment also fed the rental-library and cheap-book trades, and publishers increasingly experimented with illustration, photographs, and design.

The relationship among newspapers, magazines, and books was one of competition and cross-promotion. Newspapers advertised books and magazines; magazines excerpted and reviewed books; novels moved from serials to books; and plays and later films drew on all three. The result was a commercial culture in which a successful story could be exploited in multiple formats, a pattern that would become more systematic in the following decades.

Section 65.24

Cartoons, Political Satire, Illustrated Humor, and Early Modern Manga

Japan had a long tradition of humorous and satirical images, from medieval picture scrolls to the comic sketches of Tokugawa artists. Katsushika Hokusai's Hokusai Manga (early nineteenth century) used the word manga for sketchbooks of varied drawings, and this is the older meaning of the word, which should not be confused with the modern comic. In the 1860s the English artist Charles Wirgman, who lived in Yokohama, published The Japan Punch, a satirical magazine modeled on London's Punch, and a later foreign resident, the French artist Georges Bigot, produced satirical drawings in Japan in the 1880s. These models, and the example of European satirical journals, inspired Japanese satirical magazines. Marumaru Chinbun, which began in 1877, combined caricature with sharp commentary on politics, and its editors were prosecuted under the press laws of the era.

The key figure in the creation of a modern cartoon tradition was Kitazawa Rakuten (1876–1955), who worked for the newspaper Jiji Shinpō. In 1902 he began a humorous illustrated supplement, Jiji Manga, which popularized the use of "manga" for cartoon art, and in 1905 he founded Tokyo Puck, a color satirical magazine modeled on the American magazine Puck, which commented on politics, social customs, and the issues of the day. Rakuten's cartoons used the visual vocabulary of Western political cartoons, including caricature, allegorical figures, and lampoons of officials, and he developed original series of humorous strips. His work also reflected the attitudes of his time, including patriotic and imperial perspectives and stereotyped depictions of foreigners and colonial peoples, which students should read critically. Other cartoonists, among them Okamoto Ippei, who joined the Asahi in 1912, helped make cartooning a respected newspaper art.

Historians caution against equating these Meiji cartoons directly with later manga. The mass-market children's and youth manga of the mid-twentieth century grew from different institutions and, in many respects, different forms: the storytelling comic strip and then the long serialized comic book. Meiji cartoons were mostly single-panel or short-strip satire in newspapers and magazines, intended for adults. They are nonetheless part of the genealogy, because they established illustrated humor as a commercial genre, created a profession of cartoonists, and developed a vocabulary of visual shorthand. The study of cartoons as sources also shows how newspapers could criticize indirectly: an image could carry political commentary that a written sentence might not.

Section 65.25

Children's, Youth, and Women's Magazines: Segmented Reading Publics

As schooling expanded (Chapter 64), publishers discovered that students, children, and women formed valuable and distinct markets. Hakubunkan launched Shōnen Sekai ("Boys' World") in 1895, and under the editor Iwaya Sazanami it offered fiction, science, and articles for boys, and later published Shōjo Sekai ("Girls' World") in 1906. The publishing house Jitsugyō no Nihonsha issued Shōjo no Tomo ("Girls' Friend") in 1908, and the firm that would become Kōdansha, established as the Dai Nihon Yūbenkai in 1909, launched Shōnen Kurabu in 1914. Women's magazines, including Fujin Sekai (1906) and, in 1916, Fujin Kōron, addressed women as readers, offering advice on household management, fashion, literature, and, increasingly, social issues.

These magazines created segmented reading publics, each defined by age, gender, and education. They typically combined fiction, practical articles, readers' columns, and illustrations, and they cultivated a sense of community through reader participation. Readers' contributions, which appeared in specialized columns, show that these publications offered young readers a voice and a network, though under editorial supervision. The magazines also reflected and transmitted dominant ideas about gender: boys' magazines emphasized adventure, patriotism, and self-improvement, while girls' and women's magazines emphasized domestic virtue, though many also explored the tension between these expectations and young women's aspirations. Girls' magazines, in particular, began to develop a distinct sentimental literary culture and visual style.

The social basis of this publishing was the expansion of education and urban middle-class households with disposable income. Magazine readers were disproportionately urban and relatively well-off, since the price of a monthly was not negligible for a poor family, and readership extended downward through lending, sharing, and used purchases. The history of these magazines connects to the history of women's education in Chapter 64 and to the consumer culture of Chapter 70, but their commercial origins in the 1890s and 1900s belong here.

Section 65.26

Popular Storytelling, Variety Entertainment, and Recorded Sound

Spoken-word entertainment remained a major commercial form. In entertainment halls called yose, audiences listened to rakugo (comic storytelling), kōdan (historical storytelling in a dramatic, lecturing style), rōkyoku or naniwabushi (sung narrative with shamisen accompaniment), and various musical and novelty acts. These halls were numerous in cities and offered an inexpensive evening's entertainment. The relationship with print was close: from the 1880s, stenographers recorded performances, and publishers issued them as books. The rakugo performer San'yūtei Enchō's stories, printed in stenographic form, reached readers who never attended the hall, and historians of literature note their influence on the movement toward written prose that resembled the spoken language. In the 1900s and 1910s, rōkyoku performers like Tōchūken Kumoemon became enormously popular; his 1907 Tokyo Hongō-za engagement and recordings issued from 1912 illustrate the interplay of publicity, live performance, and the emerging sound market. Meanwhile kōdan was mass-produced in cheap books and in magazines such as Kōdan Kurabu, which was launched in 1910.

Women performers formed another popular genre. In the 1890s a fashion for female gidayū chanters, who performed the narrative of the puppet theater, attracted devoted young male fans, and their enthusiasm prompted moralists' criticism. Popular songs also spread. Political activists' "speech songs" of the 1880s, which used popular tunes to carry political messages, evolved into commercial songs; and the song "Kachūsha no Uta" ("Katyusha's Song"), associated with a 1914 stage production of Tolstoy's Resurrection in which Matsui Sumako played the lead, was among the first major hit songs sold in recorded form, though precise sales claims should be treated with caution.

The phonograph introduced a new relation between performance and audience. Edison's phonograph was demonstrated in Japan in the late 1870s, and from the early 1900s foreign companies recorded Japanese performers, with disc recording giving way over time to a domestic industry. The Nipponophone company, established in 1910 under the American businessman Frederick W. Horn, began producing records in Japan, and it recorded storytellers, musicians, and singers. A recording was fundamentally different from a live show: it was reproducible, portable, and independent of the performer's presence, and it separated the performer's voice from the performer's body. Early records were expensive and phonograph owners were relatively few, so the effect of recorded sound was limited compared to that of print or film before the 1920s; the major expansion lies in the period examined in Chapter 70.

Section 65.27

Advertising, Posters, Publicity, and the Commercialization of Attention

All of these industries needed to attract attention, and they developed a shared set of techniques. Newspapers carried display advertising for books, medicines, cosmetics, and theatrical performances; theaters and film exhibitors posted illustrated placards and hired street parades of musicians and costumed advertisers (chindon'ya) to announce programs; and manufacturers began to use brand names, trademarks, and slogans. Patent medicines were among the heaviest advertisers, and the Jintan breath freshener, introduced in 1905 with a distinctive trademark and an extensive advertising campaign, is an often-cited example of branded consumer advertising. Advertising agencies, such as the company that became Dentsū, emerged to broker space in newspapers.

Posters and printed ephemera combined the traditions of woodblock design with new lithographic printing. Poster artists worked for theaters, publishers, and manufacturers, and in the 1910s the retailer Mitsukoshi commissioned artists to produce posters that drew on the image of the fashionable woman, a topic that belongs more fully to Chapter 70. The picture postcard, made available for private publishing in 1900, was both advertisement and collectible, and postcards of actors, geisha, famous places, and military events circulated in large numbers. Publicity events, including exhibitions, parades, and sponsored performances, also drew audiences.

This commercial culture shaped the content of the media. A newspaper that depended on advertisers had reason to cultivate readers with purchasing power; a theater that depended on box-office had reason to publicize its stars; a magazine that depended on circulation had reason to promote its serials. The "commercialization of attention," as some historians call it, was not a distortion of an otherwise pure cultural sphere but a constitutive feature of the new media. Its effects were varied: it financed independent publishing and widened choice, and it also rewarded sensationalism and the standardization of tastes.

Division VI — Audiences, Celebrity, Imperial Representation, and Media Control · Section 65.28

The Making of Modern Celebrities: Actors, Performers, and Film Stars

Fame was not new. Edo-period kabuki actors such as the Ichikawa Danjūrō line were celebrated, and woodblock prints of actors circulated widely. What changed was the means by which fame was produced and the scale on which it could circulate. A modern celebrity became known to people who had never seen him or her perform, through newspapers that reported their doings, magazines that printed their portraits, photographs and postcards sold in shops, advertisements that used their image, and, increasingly, films in which they appeared. A person could now be recognized as a public figure by a national audience, and the image could circulate independently of the person.

The performers who achieved this kind of fame in the late Meiji and early Taishō periods included kabuki actors such as Danjūrō IX and Kikugorō V, shinpa actors such as Ii Yōhō, and women such as Kawakami Sadayakko and Matsui Sumako. In cinema, Onoe Matsunosuke is the most significant early example. His success depended on mass appeal rather than on the cachet of a prestigious theater, and his image was used on posters and in publicity. Historians generally treat him as an important precursor of the star system rather than as an exemplar of its mature form: the full apparatus of fan magazines, systematic star publicity, and the cultivation of an offscreen persona for film actors came in the 1920s, and the chapter's account should not attribute that apparatus to the 1910s.

The mechanisms of celebrity also created new vulnerabilities. A performer's image could be sold without his or her control; a scandal could be reported to a national audience; and performers, especially women, faced intense scrutiny. Matsui Sumako's career is an illustration: she became famous in 1911, left the Literary Arts Society after a scandal involving her relationship with Shimamura Hōgetsu, and then built a career with a new company in a series of productions that drew large audiences. Her fame and its costs show that celebrity was an exhilarating and risky condition in the new media.

Section 65.29

Publicity, Performance Reputation, and the New Culture of Recognition

Celebrity was a collaborative product. Theatrical managers hired publicists, placed notices in papers, and arranged for critics to attend; newspapers ran serialized accounts of stage careers and scandals; critics shaped reputations; and fans, though still less organized than in later decades, formed appreciation groups, collected photographs, wrote letters, and followed performers from theater to theater. The relationships among these participants were complicated by commercial interest. A critic who was friendly with a manager might praise a production; a newspaper that carried a theater's advertising might avoid criticism; a performer who cultivated the press might receive favorable coverage.

Historians distinguish between the public persona of a celebrity and the actual person. The image presented in newspapers and photographs was constructed, selective, and responsive to what audiences wanted, and it could diverge sharply from the performer's private life. For performers who had been born into hereditary kabuki families, public identity was bound up with lineage and role names; for newer performers, it had to be built from scratch and could be fragile. The relative scarcity of direct records of performers' own views, apart from some memoirs and interviews that were themselves part of the publicity, makes it difficult to recover the experience of celebrity from the inside. The historian can therefore speak with more confidence about the public construction of fame than about its private reality.

The culture of recognition also extended beyond entertainers. Politicians, military heroes such as the admirals and generals of the Russo-Japanese War, athletes, scholars, and even criminals appeared in newspapers and on postcards and were recognized by a public that had never met them. Photographs and portraits of the emperor, discussed in Section 65.8, had a special status and circulated under official supervision rather than through the open market. The emergence of recognition as a social phenomenon thus reached across entertainment, politics, and the military, and the techniques of publicity learned in one sphere were applied in others.

Section 65.30

Who Consumed Mass Culture? Gender, Class, Age, Region, and Price

The word "mass" implies broad access, but access to the new media was far from uniform. Several factors determined who could read, watch, and listen: the ability to read, which grew rapidly but varied by age, gender, and region; the price of a newspaper subscription, a magazine, a theater ticket, or a film admission; the availability of leisure time; and geographic access to cities and to the railways and post offices that carried printed matter. Urban, literate, and relatively well-off men had the widest access. Women's access increased, especially for young women in educated households, but was constrained by domestic expectations and by moral objections to women attending theaters or cinemas unaccompanied. Children read magazines and attended cinemas, often in groups, and were a significant audience for both. Working people could afford cheaper seats and cheap papers, and cinemas, with relatively inexpensive admission, were more accessible than many other entertainments, though still a significant expense for poor households.

Regional variation was large. The big cities of Tokyo and Osaka, together with Kyoto and Yokohama, concentrated newspaper offices, theaters, and cinemas. Provincial cities had local papers and theaters, but access to the latest films and performances was delayed, and rural communities received newspapers by mail, often a day or more late, and relied on traveling shows, festivals, and periodic visits to town for live entertainment. Villagers shared newspapers, and reading in groups, in village reading rooms or at local meeting places, extended the reach of a single copy. The peripheries of the empire, from Hokkaido and Okinawa to Taiwan and Korea, had their own, often different, patterns, including newspapers published by settlers and by officials and, in some cases, publications by local elites.

Section 65.31

Empire, Foreign Images, Nationalism, and the Representation of Others

The media of Meiji Japan did not simply report on the empire; they helped to define how it was imagined. Newspapers, illustrated magazines, postcards, plays, and films presented the Chinese, Russians, Koreans, Taiwanese, Ainu, Okinawans, and other peoples to a domestic audience, usually from the perspective of Japanese editors and producers. During the war with Qing China, prints and press accounts depicted Chinese soldiers and leaders in ways that mocked and demeaned them, and a language of civilization versus backwardness structured the coverage. In the Russo-Japanese War, images of Russians were more varied, and portraits of heroic Japanese and respectful treatment of Russian prisoners were publicized for foreign as well as domestic audiences. The same media also carried stereotypes of Koreans as weak or dependent in the period of growing Japanese control, and of Taiwan's indigenous peoples as primitive.

Exhibitions provided another venue. At the Fifth National Industrial Exposition in Osaka in 1903, a private entertainment area known as the "Human Pavilion" (Jinruikan) displayed members of several groups, including Ainu, Ryukyuans, and Indigenous Taiwanese, in costumes and settings that presented them as ethnological specimens or exotic attractions. The organizers had also proposed showing Koreans and Chinese participants, but diplomatic and community protests prompted their withdrawal from the public display. The episode provoked protest. Okinawan newspapers and community figures objected to the inclusion of Okinawan women alongside peoples they regarded as inferior, and the objections themselves reveal the hierarchies of the empire: Okinawans, who had been absorbed into the Japanese state, resisted being classed with peoples regarded as "uncivilized," and this reaction also reflected a fear for their standing as Japanese subjects. Korean and Chinese observers also protested proposed displays of their compatriots. The episode illustrates both the commercial use of colonial and minority peoples as spectacle and the capacity of those so displayed to object, though they had little control over the exhibition.

The Ainu were repeatedly photographed, written about, and exhibited, in domestic and international expositions and in the pages of illustrated magazines. They were often presented as a vanishing people, in the language of a "dying race" that obscured the effects of colonization, land loss, and assimilation policy, a subject developed in the chapters on Hokkaido colonization and modern Ainu activism. Ainu communities, for their part, responded in varied ways, some adopting Japanese education and some resisting. Okinawans, Koreans, and Taiwanese also developed their own press and cultural organizations, which gave alternative perspectives, though under colonial or prefectural regulation. The persistent imbalance was that Japanese-owned media had the capital, technology, and access to produce widely distributed representations, while the people represented rarely controlled how they appeared.

Historical Debate

Did Early Mass Culture Expand Public Expression or Reinforce Commercial and Imperial Power?

The question: Historians of mass culture in Meiji and early Taishō Japan disagree about the overall effect of the new media. Did newspapers, theaters, cinema, and magazines widen the range of voices and experiences available to the public, or did they primarily amplify commercial interests and the imperial and nationalist outlook of those who owned and controlled them?

Interpretation A — Expansion of public expression: Some scholars emphasize the growth of diverse publics: new magazines for women and youth, the experiments of shingeki, satirical journals, the ability of ordinary people to read about and discuss national affairs, and the opportunities for performers and writers. They argue that mass media made possible new forms of participation and critical debate.
Interpretation B — Reinforcement of commercial and imperial power: Others emphasize that the media were owned by commercial and sometimes official interests, that they presented a hierarchical view of Asia and of minorities, that state censorship and police supervision constrained dissent, and that spectacle and sensationalism often drew audiences into patriotic enthusiasm rather than critical reflection.

Evidence: The arguments depend on analysis of the content of papers and magazines, the representations of colonized peoples, the practices of censorship, the composition of audiences, and the testimony of contemporaries. The two interpretations are not exclusive; many historians argue that the same media did both, in different measures for different groups.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the relationship between mass culture and democracy and between mass culture and imperial nationalism, and it frames the interpretation of the 1920s and the later period of wartime mobilization.

Section 65.32

Censorship, Moral Regulation, Commercial Power, and the Limits of Public Expression

The state regulated media through multiple, overlapping mechanisms. Newspapers were subject to press ordinances and, from 1909, the Newspaper Law, as Section 65.5 explained, and books and other publications were governed by the Publication Law of 1893 and related regulations, which required registration and authorized officials to ban works deemed harmful to public order or morals. The Peace Police Law of 1900 restricted political assembly and association. The Home Ministry and the police were the principal enforcers, and their authority extended beyond formal law to informal guidance: editors and publishers often anticipated what officials would disapprove of and avoided it.

Theater and entertainment were regulated primarily at the level of police authority. Local police regulations governed licensing of theaters, safety, and the content of performances, and police officers attended performances to look for disorder or offenses to public morals, and could stop a performance. Political plays were especially likely to be banned, and the shinpa movement, which began as political theater, learned to avoid direct political content. Moral regulation touched on sexuality, the portrayal of crime, and the conduct of performers and audiences, and objections to the presence of actresses and young audiences in theaters reflected broader concern about public morality. The regulation of motion pictures developed in the years after 1900 through local police measures that addressed safety, because film stock was flammable, and content; more systematic national and metropolitan film regulation appeared in the late 1910s, a topic for later chapters. The same pattern recurs in the treatment of political crises: after the High Treason Incident of 1910–11, when the state prosecuted anarchists and socialists for an alleged plot against the emperor, the authorities tightened control of the socialist press and of public discussion of radical ideas.

Commercial power also limited expression. Owners and managers could shape what was printed or staged through their business interests, advertisers could influence content, and theaters and exhibition companies could refuse to carry works that were unprofitable or risky. Self-censorship, driven by fear of prosecution and loss of business, was widespread. At the same time, regulation was not uniform. Local practice varied, enforcement depended on the attitude of officials, and audiences and performers found ways to evade or negotiate rules. The picture is not simply one of an all-powerful state silencing a passive public, nor of a free commercial culture; it is one of continuous negotiation among officials, owners, performers, and audiences within a legal framework that gave the state wide discretion.

Division VII — Mass Culture and Historical Synthesis · Section 65.33

The Emergence of a Commercial Public Sphere in Modern Japan

Taken together, the developments traced in this chapter amounted to the emergence of what historians sometimes call a commercial public sphere: a space of shared information, opinion, and entertainment organized largely through businesses rather than through the court, the domain, or the temple. Newspapers carried news and argument; photographs and postcards supplied images; theaters and cinemas offered performance; magazines divided the reading public into segments; advertisers and publicists connected them all. These institutions were interconnected. A story could begin in a newspaper serial, move to the stage as shinpa melodrama, then to film, then to a phonograph record; a performer could be photographed, advertised, and filmed; and a single event, such as a war, could be represented in all of these forms at once.

This public sphere contributed to a wider public conversation, but it remained unequal and contested. It enlarged the number of people who could follow national events and form opinions, offered new occupations to writers, actors, photographers, and artists, and gave some women and young people a public presence. It also reproduced hierarchies of class, gender, region, and empire; it was shaped by the profit motive and by state regulation; and it often presented the nation and its colonial subjects in ways that served the interests of the powerful. The commercial public sphere should therefore be understood neither as an unqualified advance for democracy nor as merely an instrument of manipulation, but as a new institutional environment in which both possibilities existed.

Section 65.34

Continuity and Transformation: The Foundations of Taishō Mass Society

By the mid-1910s a great deal had changed. Japan had daily newspapers with large circulations and national reach; a photographic industry serving both domestic and foreign markets; theaters that included Western-style buildings and a spectrum of genres from kabuki to shingeki; permanent cinemas, a production industry organized in studios and corporations, and a performance tradition, the benshi, that had no precise counterpart elsewhere; magazines for different ages and genders; cartoons, recordings, and advertising; and a vocabulary of fame. Information, images, and performers circulated commercially to audiences that were increasingly large and varied.

Much had also continued. Kabuki's families and conventions persisted; storytelling halls remained crowded; the woodblock-print workshops, though in decline, adapted to new markets; the entertainment districts of Asakusa and Dōtonbori followed patterns set in the Tokugawa period; and the audience's expectation that performance would involve a live human voice shaped even the new medium of film. The new institutions drew on the old: the newspaper's lineage included the kawaraban seller's cry, the cinema's the lantern showman's narration, and the celebrity's the actor-print of the Edo period.

The chapter should not, however, be read as showing that the mature mass society of the 1920s already existed. Radio broadcasting began only in 1925, sound film arrived later, department-store culture and the modern girl belong to the following decade, and the fully developed film-star system, fan magazines, and systematic regulation of film were still ahead. What had been established was the infrastructure and the habits: commercial publishing, advertising, studio production, urban entertainment districts, and a public accustomed to consuming news and images daily. These formed the foundations on which the civil society of Taishō (Chapter 67) and the urban consumer culture of the 1920s (Chapter 70) would build, and the tensions already visible, between expression and control, access and exclusion, and domestic audiences and imperial subjects, would continue.

Section 65.35

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the formation of Japan's commercial media from the 1870s to the mid-1910s. Before proceeding, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of the following foundational points. The modern newspaper was a new kind of institution that grew from earlier print traditions and depended on movable type, postal and rail networks, advertising, and a literate readership; it divided into political and popular forms that gradually converged and was subject to increasingly elaborate state regulation. Photography became a Japanese business from the early 1860s, and its products, from portraits to souvenir albums to news photographs, were constructed, not neutral, records. Theater diversified into reformed kabuki, political and melodramatic shinpa, and literary shingeki, which coexisted and competed. Motion pictures arrived in 1896–97, were quickly adapted to Japanese performance traditions, were accompanied by live benshi narration, and developed through permanent cinemas, studios, and the corporate consolidation of Nikkatsu in 1912. Magazines, cartoons, recordings, and advertising segmented and commercialized the audience, and the new media created modern forms of celebrity. All of these developments were unequal in access, shaped by empire in their representations of others, and constrained by censorship and commercial pressure.

Students should also be able to explain why it is misleading to speak of a "first" newspaper, photographer, film, or benshi without specifying what kind of first is meant, why early cinema in Japan was never silent, why shinpa and shingeki are not the same, and why a photograph is never simply a transparent window onto the past. These distinctions are the core of the historical thinking this chapter asks of its readers, and they will be useful whenever students encounter claims about "firsts" and "origins" in the study of any period.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the emergence of a commercial media culture in Meiji and early Taishō Japan, from the 1870s through the mid-1910s. Its central argument is that newspapers, photographs, illustrated publications, theaters, and motion pictures transformed how people received information, imagined the nation, experienced entertainment, and recognized public personalities, and that this transformation was neither a simple importation of Western technology nor a straightforward continuation of earlier practice. Japanese publishers, photographers, performers, exhibitors, and audiences adapted new technologies to existing traditions while creating institutions that had no exact precedent.

The modern newspaper emerged from the Tokugawa culture of printed news, but it was a different kind of enterprise. The Yokohama Mainichi Shinbun, first issued in January 1871, was the first Japanese-language daily, and in the following decade Tokyo and Osaka acquired political papers and popular papers that competed for readers and advertisers. Movable type, rotary presses, the postal system, the railways, and the telegraph supplied the technical basis, and rising literacy supplied the readership. Advertising and street sales turned the newspaper into a profit-seeking business, and serialized fiction made it a patron of literature. Governments responded with ordinances in 1875 and later, and with the 1909 Newspaper Law, that gave officials wide power to suspend papers and punish editors. Historians therefore describe the press as both a space for criticism and an object of state management.

Photography became a Japanese trade in the early 1860s with the studios of Ueno Hikoma and Shimooka Renjō, working in a field also shaped by foreign photographers such as Felice Beato. Portrait photography changed how people recorded their identities, and the state used it to create images of the emperor that were distributed to schools and offices. The commercial studios of Yokohama produced hand-colored albums for foreign buyers that presented staged and selective images of Japan. In the 1890s and 1900s photographs entered newspapers, magazines, postcards, and colonial and anthropological documentation. The photograph, though persuasive, was a constructed object, shaped by the photographer, the sitter, the publisher, and the market, and the people photographed as "types" rarely had control over their representation.

The theater diversified. Kabuki sought respectability through reform, imperial patronage, and new buildings such as the Shintomi-za and the Kabuki-za, while retaining its hereditary families and its onnagata tradition. Shinpa grew out of political activist theater in the late 1880s and 1890s, led by figures such as Kawakami Otojirō, and became a staple of commercial melodrama drawn from newspapers and novels. Kawakami Sadayakko achieved international fame and helped open the stage to women. Shingeki, represented by Tsubouchi Shōyō's Literary Arts Society of 1906 and the Free Theater of 1909, sought a literary and realist modern drama. These forms coexisted, competed, and borrowed from one another, and no neat sequence of replacement describes them.

Motion pictures arrived in 1896–97 and were immediately absorbed into the existing performance culture. Early film exhibitions were live events in which a lecturer or benshi interpreted and performed the film, and music and sometimes live scenes accompanied the images. The first domestic filmmaking, including Shibata Tsunekichi's trick films and his 1899 recording of Momijigari with Danjūrō IX and Kikugorō V, drew on kabuki and on actualities. The opening of the Denkikan in 1903 marked the first permanent cinema, the Meguro studio of 1908 the first purpose-built studio, and the 1912 merger that created Nikkatsu the consolidation of an industry. Makino Shōzō and Onoe Matsunosuke helped define period-drama cinema in Kyoto, while Tokyo studios developed contemporary melodrama. Careful historians distinguish these milestones rather than crediting a single "first."

Magazines, cartoons, recordings, and advertising expanded and segmented the commercial public. Taiyō and other general-interest magazines addressed a national readership; Shōnen Sekai, Shōjo Sekai, and women's magazines addressed younger and female readers; Kitazawa Rakuten's Tokyo Puck and earlier satirical journals established a commercial tradition of cartooning; storytelling halls, stenographic publishing, and the early phonograph industry brought spoken and sung performance into new forms. Advertising agencies, posters, trademarks, and picture postcards created a culture of commercial publicity. These media together produced new forms of celebrity: performers whose images circulated independently of their presence, and whose fame was built through newspapers, photographs, postcards, and film.

Access to these media was unequal. Urban, literate, and relatively affluent men had the greatest access, while women, rural communities, and poor households had less, though cheap papers, shared reading, and inexpensive cinema admissions extended the reach of mass culture. Regional differences were considerable, and entertainment concentrated in Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, and Yokohama. At the same time, the people who appeared in media as subjects, including Ainu, Okinawans, Koreans, Taiwanese, and Chinese, had little control over their representation, as the 1903 Osaka "Human Pavilion" and the visual rhetoric of wartime prints and postcards illustrate, though they sometimes protested and developed their own publications.

The state regulated these media by press ordinances, publication laws, police supervision of theaters and exhibitions, and, after 1900, restrictions on assembly. Enforcement varied, and commercial interests, self-censorship, and negotiation among officials, owners, performers, and audiences shaped practice. The wars with China and Russia were both stimuli to commercial media and occasions of official control, and the media helped create the patriotic enthusiasm and the disappointments that marked the period.

By the mid-1910s Japan had the institutions of a commercial public sphere: newspapers with national reach, a photographic industry, a spectrum of theaters, permanent cinemas and studios, segmented magazines, and a culture of celebrity. Much of this rested on continuities with Tokugawa practice, and the fully developed mass society of the 1920s, with radio, sound film, department stores, and the mature star system, still lay ahead. The foundations examined in this chapter nonetheless shaped what followed: they made possible the public debates of Taishō democracy (Chapter 67) and the urban consumer culture of the modern city (Chapter 70), and they established patterns of commercial communication, state regulation, and unequal representation that would continue to structure Japanese public life.

Key Terms

Kawaraban
Irregular single-sheet woodblock news broadsides sold in Tokugawa cities; an important precedent for, but not identical to, the modern daily newspaper.
Nishiki-e shinbun
Early Meiji illustrated news prints made by ukiyo-e artists, combining the visual conventions of woodblock printing with news reporting.
Ōshinbun
"Large papers": political newspapers aimed at educated readers, written in formal prose and focused on politics and public affairs.
Koshinbun
"Small papers": popular newspapers in colloquial style, with local news, crime, entertainment, and serialized fiction for broader readerships.
Print capitalism
The profit-driven commercial production and distribution of printed matter for a market, including newspapers, magazines, and books.
Furigana
Small phonetic characters printed beside kanji to indicate their reading, which made newspapers accessible to readers with limited training in Chinese characters.
Newspaper Ordinance (1875)
A Meiji regulation, issued with a Libel Law, that restricted criticism of officials and gave authorities power to suspend or ban papers.
Newspaper Law (1909)
A law governing the registration and conduct of newspapers that retained wide powers of suppression for the Home Minister.
Albumen print
The dominant nineteenth-century photographic print, made from a glass negative on paper coated with egg white; the basis of Yokohama souvenir photographs.
Wet collodion process
A photographic process using a glass negative coated and developed while wet; it made reproducible prints possible and underlay early commercial studios.
Yokohama shashin
Hand-colored albumen photographs made in Yokohama for foreign buyers and domestic customers, depicting staged scenes and views of Japan.
Goshin'ei
Official photographic portraits of the emperor and empress distributed to schools and offices, an example of photography used for imperial representation.
Kabuki
The commercial theater of the Tokugawa cities, performed by male actors, which adapted in the Meiji era through reform, patronage, and new theaters.
Onnagata
Male actors specializing in female roles in kabuki and in early shinpa and film.
Sōshi shibai
Activist theater of the late 1880s that dramatized political ideas and events, a precursor of shinpa.
Shinpa
"New school" theater: popular contemporary drama and melodrama that developed from sōshi shibai and drew on newspapers and novels.
Shingeki
"New drama": a movement to create a modern, literary, realist theater based on translation and original plays, represented by the Literary Arts Society and the Free Theater.
Utsushi-e
Magic-lantern projections of painted slides accompanied by narration, a popular visual entertainment that preceded cinema.
Kinetoscope
Edison's peep-show motion-picture viewer, first shown in Japan in 1896.
Cinématographe
The Lumière brothers' combined camera and projector, introduced to Japan in 1897.
Benshi
Live narrators of motion pictures who explained, voiced, and performed films, central to Japanese film exhibition.
Rensageki
"Chain drama": a hybrid form alternating live stage scenes with filmed scenes within one story.
Kyūgeki
"Old drama": period films, often sword-fighting dramas, developed in Kyoto and associated with Makino Shōzō and Onoe Matsunosuke.
Nikkatsu
The company formed in 1912 by the merger of four film firms, the first large integrated Japanese film business.
Yose
Urban entertainment halls where rakugo, kōdan, rōkyoku, and other acts were performed.
Rakugo, kōdan, rōkyoku
Comic storytelling, historical lecture-style storytelling, and sung narrative with shamisen, respectively: spoken-word genres that thrived commercially and were published in stenographic form.
Picture postcard
Illustrated postcard, authorized for private production in 1900, which became a mass collectible and advertising medium, notably in the Russo-Japanese War.
Jinruikan
The "Human Pavilion" at the 1903 Osaka exposition, which displayed peoples including Ainu, Ryukyuans, Taiwanese indigenous people, and Koreans as ethnological exhibits.
Celebrity
Fame produced and circulated through reproducible media, so that a person is recognized by audiences who have never seen them in person.
Commercial public sphere
A space of public information, opinion, and entertainment organized through businesses, and unequal in access and influence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a society reorganized the way it knew itself. The newspaper, the studio portrait, the theater poster, and the film program were not merely new things to read and see; they were new ways of being a member of a public, of recognizing people one had never met, and of sharing, at the same moment, in events one had not witnessed. The institutions that produced these experiences were businesses, and their commercial logic, as much as any political program, shaped what the public knew and imagined.

The chapter also shows the importance of adaptation. The newspaper drew on the news sheet, the cinema on the lantern show and the kabuki stage, and the benshi on the storyteller. Technologies that arrived from abroad were rapidly made into Japanese institutions, in forms that differed from their counterparts elsewhere. This pattern, selective borrowing and creative adaptation rather than imitation, connects to the broader argument of this book about the Meiji transformation.

At the same time, the chapter reveals persistent tensions that carried forward. The commercial public sphere extended access and created opportunities, but it was bounded by class, gender, region, and literacy, regulated by an authority that claimed wide discretion, and structured by an imperial vision in which Ainu, Okinawan, Korean, Taiwanese, and Chinese people were frequently represented rather than heard. These tensions would intensify in the democratic movements of Taishō, the consumer society of the 1920s, and the wartime mobilization of the 1930s, when the same media would be turned to purposes their early publishers could scarcely have foreseen.

Students who understand the media of this period as constructed, commercial, adaptive, unequal, and contested are well prepared for the following chapters, in which Japan's participation in the First World War, the Rice Riots, and the flourishing of Taishō civil society and urban culture will show what an established mass-media environment made possible.
