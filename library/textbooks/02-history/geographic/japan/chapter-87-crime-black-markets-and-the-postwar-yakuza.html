Crime, Black Markets, and the Postwar Yakuza

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Who ruled the streets of Japan after defeat: the state, market organizers, or men able to make others obey? The answer varied by place, livelihood and political authority.

On August 5, 1946, Japanese police entered the outdoor market near Shibuya Station in Tokyo. The photographs that survive show officers forming a cordon, examining stalls and escorting a woman trader. The archival caption records approximately 280 armed officers and the seizure of goods that could not legally be sold through uncontrolled channels. For the police, the operation demonstrated a claim to public order. For a trader whose livelihood depended on street commerce, confiscation could mean the loss of food or working capital. Neither the photograph nor its caption tells us whether the person detained belonged to a gang. That distinction is the starting point for this chapter.

The postwar yami-ichi had grown because ordinary systems of production and distribution could not supply daily necessities reliably. Chapter 85 described that survival economy. But a street market required places to sell, goods to circulate, agreements over space, and some means of resolving quarrels. Who supplied those rules when the state was damaged, rationing was unreliable, and property rights were contested? Some sellers formed associations. Existing tekiya networks organized stalls; bakuto and other groups adapted to commercial opportunity. In some places an intermediary provided genuine services. In others influence was secured by demands, intimidation, and violence. The difference between a marketplace and a protection racket is not always visible in a photograph.

It would be misleading to suggest that Japanese cities were suddenly devoid of government in 1945. Police departments continued, town offices functioned unevenly, and the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers supervised a Japanese administration that retained extensive capacities. Yet jurisdiction was complicated by American military personnel, military goods, and directives issued during a rapid program of occupation reform. A street vendor might confront Japanese officers one day and American military police the next. Commercial leaders could negotiate with officials or exploit uncertainty about who had the right to authorize selling on a station approach or a bomb-cleared lot.

Japan’s imperial collapse further complicated these relationships. Koreans, Taiwanese and Chinese residents lived and worked in a country that had recently ruled much of the region through unequal legal institutions. Their political identities and rights changed unevenly after surrender. Commercial disputes involving them could become arguments about nationality, discrimination, policing, and foreign diplomacy. The deadly Shibuya confrontation of July 19, 1946 was not the same event as the August police raid. Adam Cathcart’s documentary study reports six Taiwanese residents and one Japanese policeman killed in the July incident, while exploring the wider consequences for Sino-Japanese and American occupation relations.

This chapter also examines the influence of older gambling and vending organizations, the recruitment of some demobilized soldiers, criminal violence against merchants, the political uses of private muscle, and the 1947 Police Law that became effective in March 1948. It asks why certain organizations expanded while many informal sellers never entered criminal groups. The famous later yakuza syndicates were not already fully formed nationwide in August 1945. Their postwar transformation occurred through local competition, economic change, patronage and successive state responses.

The principal evidence includes archival photographs and films, municipal histories, Japanese economic and police legislation, occupation records, period journalism, and modern scholarship. These sources have different strengths. A filmed raid records an action, not the motives of everyone shown. A police allegation is not necessarily a conviction. A later gang memoir may memorialize loyalty while concealing extraction. By keeping these distinctions in view, the chapter explains how emergency commerce and organized coercion intersected without confusing them or treating national and ethnic identities as evidence of crime.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish survival-driven informal trading from organized criminal coercion and private market administration.
  2. Explain how defeat, demobilization, repatriation, inflation and urban destruction changed commercial opportunities.
  3. Define bakuto, tekiya, oyabun–kobun, yakuza and later bōryokudan usage without reproducing origin myths.
  4. Analyze how stall allocation, security, brokerage and recurring fees could create authority or extortion.
  5. Explain the overlapping responsibilities of Japanese police, occupation command and U.S. military police.
  6. Compare the geography of market power in Tokyo, Yokohama, Osaka, Kobe and other localities.
  7. Use an archival film and photographs critically without inferring guilt or gang membership from appearance.
  8. Explain the postimperial status of Koreans, Taiwanese and Chinese residents without collective criminal attribution.
  9. Reconstruct the July 19, 1946 Shibuya confrontation separately from the August 5 raid.
  10. Assess the relation between organized violence, employment, business patrons and political activity.
  11. Explain the December 1947 Police Law and its March 1948 implementation.
  12. Identify the limits of statistics, later memoirs and popular portrayals in researching the emerging postwar yakuza.

Historical Context

Japanese commerce had been regulated tightly during the war. Price controls, rationing and compulsory production requirements affected both legal and illicit exchange; unauthorized selling therefore preceded August 1945. As shortages intensified, households traded outside official channels while government authorities attempted to preserve the supply system. The collapse of the empire compounded those difficulties by interrupting trade and returning soldiers and civilians to an archipelago whose cities had been heavily bombed. Earlier chapters traced those military and social disruptions; here they matter because they made profitable locations, control of transport, and knowledge of supply routes unusually valuable.

Before 1945, gambling circles, itinerant vending organizations and groups of political violence specialists had already formed relationships with merchants, employers and officials. Those backgrounds help explain why some people possessed organizational resources when emergency markets appeared. But the 1945–1952 period introduced new conditions: the arrival of Allied forces, the growth of station-front stalls, changed relations among former colonial subjects and Japanese institutions, and occupation pressure to dismantle hierarchical associations. The historical vocabulary itself changed; later police categories cannot simply be carried backward into the first days of surrender.

Japan’s postwar state remained active. Under SCAP supervision Japanese officials enforced rationing laws and investigated ordinary criminal cases; American military police handled matters involving military discipline and many transactions connected to servicemen. The occupations of cities such as Yokohama and Kobe differed from the experience of inland markets, and the legal status of Korean, Taiwanese and Chinese residents created additional uncertainty. The Japanese Constitution, effective May 3, 1947, and the new Police Law, promulgated December 17 that year and operative from March 7, 1948, reshaped the formal organization of public authority without instantly resolving the everyday problem of enforcement.

The chapter’s central method is comparative. It reads photographs and footage alongside contemporary laws, organizational histories and academic research, and it gives special attention to the gaps among legal rules, official action and lived experience. The 1946 Shibuya records illustrate why particular dates matter. A photograph of a later raid does not document a prior confrontation; a claim about a named market leader does not automatically identify every seller as his follower. This discipline preserves the history of ordinary traders, minorities and victims of coercion alongside the institutional development of organized crime.

Defeat, Demobilization, and the Crisis of Public Order

Japan's surrender did not dissolve the state, but it unsettled the relations among government, private commerce, and ordinary life. Officials who had organized production and rationing during the war still sat in their offices, even as factories stopped operating, transport connections failed, and millions of people looked for food and work. Burned neighborhoods left gaps in the urban landscape. Returning soldiers and civilians from overseas entered communities already crowded with evacuees, demobilized workers, and families searching for missing relatives. The end of wartime surveillance did not mean that every form of regulation vanished; rather, rules persisted unevenly, and their practical enforcement depended on local officials, police resources, military occupation priorities, and the willingness of people to comply.

The phrase "breakdown of public order" therefore needs examination rather than repetition. A police report might count an unauthorized sale as a violation even when the seller was exchanging a small quantity of rice or clothing to sustain a household. It might describe a crowd as disorderly without identifying the negotiations that had already occurred between merchants and neighborhood representatives. Conversely, calling a market a place of survival could obscure an extortionist who demanded payments under threat of violence. Social disorder, administrative incapacity, survival commerce, and organized criminal action were different developments, though all could occur on the same street. Historians must ask what a particular offense involved, who reported it, who exercised authority, and which people were protected or exposed.

The demobilization of the imperial armed forces mattered for reasons extending beyond the number of men returning. Military service had interrupted civilian employment; people brought home habits of hierarchy, organizational experience, sometimes access to weapons, and varied memories of coercion and obedience. Some returned to families and farms. Others found work in ports, transport, street vending, or construction. Some were recruited into organized violence. The existence of the last group does not justify treating veterans as a criminal population. Individual career records, contemporary membership surveys, and institutional histories are needed before making claims about the relationship between military background and gang membership. The broad fact of demobilization provides context, not an explanation of every later criminal act.

Markets sprang up where buildings had been cleared before bombing or destroyed by air raids. Station approaches gathered commuters, displaced people, returning travelers, soldiers, and goods moving through illicit or semi-legal channels. Everyday commodities acquired high value under rationing and inflation. Transporters, stall holders, property claimants, brokers, and people able to intimidate rivals all sought a share in this economy. Commercial relationships nevertheless required an element of regularity: sellers needed customers to return, buyers needed to believe they could receive goods, and vendors needed a place to display wares tomorrow. A person able to settle quarrels or reserve spaces could become influential without holding formal public office. The same ability could turn into domination if enforced by force.

The occupation introduced another layer of unequal authority. The Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers directed the Japanese government but did not replace every Japanese police officer or municipal administrator. American military police handled offenses involving occupation personnel and could intervene in transactions where U.S. goods or soldiers were involved. Japanese police enforced domestic economic rules and criminal laws under occupation supervision. These jurisdictions overlapped in streets where Japanese buyers negotiated with foreign servicemen, or where goods diverted from military supply passed into local stalls. Confusion about who could arrest whom sometimes encouraged opportunism, but it also forced cooperation among authorities who had different priorities.

Residents experienced this system unevenly. A merchant with a dependable supplier, a landlord with recognized title, and a family with ration entitlements could seek official remedies, although those remedies were unreliable. A homeless teenager or newly arrived repatriate might rely on personal connections or a market employer. A Korean or Taiwanese resident faced the additional instability of postimperial legal status and possible discriminatory treatment. Women traders could encounter police enforcement and market harassment in ways not captured by the biographies of male gang leaders. Public order meant different things to these people: the prevention of violence, access to stalls, an end to arbitrary confiscation, or simply a reliable way to obtain food.

Chapter 85 explained the necessity of informal exchange. Chapter 87 examines a narrower question: how economic disruption made room for private authorities who claimed the right to control commercial space and sometimes used criminal violence to maintain it. That inquiry begins before 1945, because postwar bosses did not invent every organization they used. It also rejects the retrospective notion that defeat produced a fully formed modern yakuza system overnight. The underworld transformed through local struggles, state decisions, older commercial customs, and later consolidation. The point of departure is an unsettled urban environment, not a vacuum in which no rules existed.

Bakuto, Tekiya, and the Longer History of Organized Violence

The origins of Japan's organized-crime networks cannot be reduced to a single lineage. Two categories recur in historical accounts: bakuto, associated with gambling, and tekiya, associated with itinerant peddling, festival stalls, and arrangements governing street commerce. Neither label described a uniform nationwide syndicate. Gambling operations varied by region, venue, patrons, and their relations with police or local elites. Peddling organizations likewise combined commercial services, mutual obligations, rights over locations, and sometimes coercive practices. Their histories illuminate the available organizational forms in 1945, but do not establish a simple, unbroken genealogy from Tokugawa gamblers or vendors to every postwar gang.

The distinction between activities is essential. Gambling could be illegal and organized without involving the control of a street market. A peddler could rely on an association to secure a stall and resolve a quarrel without participating in an extortion racket. Conversely, a person styled as an organizer might use violence to exclude independent sellers. These categories were not mutually exclusive in personal careers, and their meanings shifted over time. A group that began with itinerant vending might expand into finance, transportation, or armed protection as opportunities changed. Historians use the terms to reconstruct relations of work and power, not to assign a moral identity automatically to everyone covered by the label.

Boss–follower relationships were often described through oyabun and kobun, words evoking parent and child. Such ties might provide introductions, commercial guidance, housing, assistance during illness, or help in conflicts. They could also create obligations difficult to refuse and make punishment or expulsion expensive for a subordinate. A family-like vocabulary did not erase material inequality. Leaders might retain control over earnings, grant access to preferred trading sites, demand unquestioning service, and call on followers to intimidate competitors. The apparent intimacy of the relationship could help establish trust where legal contracts or courts were unavailable, while also disguising the asymmetry of authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Bakuto, Tekiya, Yakuza, Bōryokudan

Bakuto designates gamblers or gambling-associated groups; tekiya denotes itinerant peddlers and street-vending organizers whose activities ranged from legitimate commerce to coercion. Yakuza has changing historical meanings and is not a precise identifier for every wartime or postwar market association. Bōryokudan is a later police and statutory label for violent organized groups; using it without qualification in 1945 is anachronistic.

These terms name overlapping but distinct activities, social identities, and administrative categories. Students must date their usage and distinguish a person’s occupational background from verified criminal conduct.

Modern political history further complicates the picture. Eiko Maruko Siniawer's research demonstrates how violence specialists moved within relationships involving political activists, election organizers, businessmen, and state agencies before the Second World War. The overlap was not universal. Some organizations possessed strong political patrons; others remained largely commercial or local. To say that political violence and gangster networks intersected is not to suggest that every party, policeman, or vendor belonged to a criminal conspiracy. Specific evidence is needed to identify who hired whom, for what purpose, and under which circumstances.

The imagery of the "honorable gangster" belongs to a separate evidentiary category. Later memoirs, films, promotional histories, and ceremonies sometimes present yakuza figures as heirs to samurai courage, defenders of ordinary merchants, or guardians of an ancient code. Such narratives may reveal how organizations sought legitimacy in the twentieth century, but they should not be accepted as descriptions of their origins. Tattoos, ritualized drinking, or elaborate oaths are poor substitutes for dated records of transactions, membership, violence, police encounters, and the distribution of proceeds. A useful history asks when a custom was documented and how it functioned, rather than repeating a story because the story is familiar.

The term yakuza itself has a history of changing usage. It came to be applied broadly to gamblers, racketeers, and organized-crime participants, but different contemporary observers employed it in different ways. The modern administrative label bōryokudan, often translated as violent group, belongs principally to later police classification and legislation. Applying that later category without qualification to a 1945 street association distorts the period's language. Likewise, neither police descriptions of "gangs" nor proprietors' claims to respectable merchant status should be treated as definitive without examining conduct and organization. A group could call itself a traders' association and still engage in intimidation; a police accusation of affiliation required corroboration.

Prewar structures mattered because they supplied personnel, habits, and resources at a moment when public authority was insecure. Vendors knew trading routes, established locations, festival schedules, and the etiquette of negotiating spaces. Gamblers could possess contacts, credit relationships, and experience organizing activities beyond formal regulation. Those resources became adaptable to bomb-cleared station precincts and emergency markets. Yet the postwar setting changed the demands placed on them: extraordinary consumer shortages, large numbers of displaced men, military material entering civilian circulation, and occupation policies aimed at dismantling what reformers saw as feudal hierarchies. Continuity of organizational technique thus coexisted with profound change in economic opportunity and political context.

Section 87.3 · Division 1 — Defeat and Inherited Networks

Displaced Men, Former Soldiers, Repatriates, and New Recruits

The social recruitment of postwar market organizations is often summarized in a phrase about "discharged soldiers becoming gangsters." The phrase captures a real movement of some veterans into street commerce and organized groups, but it is too broad to stand as an explanation. Millions of former servicemen returned to civilian society, and the overwhelming majority did not enter criminal organizations. The meaningful question is which men, in which cities, entered which forms of work, through what contacts, and under what pressures. The loss of military employment, disrupted industry, and the scarcity of regular wages made street selling attractive to many people whose only available assets were physical labor, an item to trade, or a personal connection.

The occupation-era Tokyo evidence gives an unusually useful point of reference. Hatsuda Kōsei cites a July 1946 survey of 994 members of the Tokyo Stalls and Commerce Association identifying 275 war veterans. This is a measure of veterans within a specified association at a particular time, not a national estimate of yakuza membership. The figure nonetheless shows that former military service could be important in the personnel of organized vending. It also reminds the reader that a stallholders' association and a crime syndicate were not synonymous. Establishing whether a particular veteran used force, merely held a stall, or served as a labor organizer requires more detailed records than a membership count supplies.

Some men entered the market economy through ordinary commercial pathways: transporting goods, guarding overnight stores, repairing structures, or borrowing money to buy stock. Others used experience in command structures and personal loyalty to assemble groups able to claim territory. Wartime black-market experience also mattered. Illegal trading had not begun after surrender; shortage and rationing had already encouraged unauthorized commerce during the war. A trader familiar with concealed supply routes might adapt quickly to the postwar economy without acquiring a distinctly criminal organization. Still another participant might obtain access to military surplus or goods diverted from depots and use that advantage to establish dominance over neighboring sellers.

Personal accounts of individual recruitment can be vivid but require careful testing. Autobiographies often compress a complicated life into a turning point: a veteran comes home, finds society disordered, and joins a boss who offers a place to belong. Such stories can be true in particular cases, yet they also fit retrospective narratives of masculine loyalty. Administrative membership lists, contemporary interviews, property records, and police files can reveal less dramatic factors such as a relative already working at a stall, access to inexpensive goods, or the need to support children. The historian should not replace the words of participants with a purely structural explanation, but neither should the structure be allowed to disappear behind heroic self-description.

Repatriates from Japan's former overseas empire entered the same precarious labor markets. Returnees from Korea, Taiwan, China, Manchuria, and other regions experienced loss of property and changed legal status, but their histories were not interchangeable. Japanese repatriates could be unfamiliar with the locality to which they returned. Former colonial subjects remaining in Japan faced uncertainty over citizenship and political recognition. Neither condition inherently predisposed anyone to crime. Market entry could offer a livelihood because capital costs were comparatively low and existing institutional barriers prevented access to regular employment. Group associations sometimes offered support and negotiation with local officials; their presence should not be taken as evidence of collective criminality.

Women and younger people complicate the standard story of recruitment into the underworld. Men occupied most positions in the recognized histories of bosses, but women worked as vendors, cash handlers, household suppliers, and managers of family shops. Girls and boys also participated in small exchanges, errands, and salvage, though their circumstances require evidence beyond gang records. A market protected or controlled by a male organization rested on labor performed by people who did not appear in its membership lists. Studying these workers changes the question from the rise of famous leaders to the social relations that sustained their authority and made markets profitable.

Recruitment during the occupation was therefore not a single pipeline from defeat to organized crime. It was a series of intersecting decisions and opportunities within a disrupted labor market. Former soldiers could contribute organizational capacity; experienced vendors provided commercial knowledge; city residents and repatriates supplied labor; local patrons sometimes offered access to land or police contacts. The criminal dimension emerged where organizations secured advantage through threats, violence, illegal gambling, extortion, or corrupt dealing. Understanding that threshold allows historians to analyze a group's conduct instead of defining its members by military service, poverty, ethnicity, or the place in which they worked.

From Informal Commerce to Control of Territory

A crowded street market required organization even when many of its transactions violated rationing or price laws. Sellers needed an address where customers could find them; carts needed room to pass; merchandise had to be protected from rain and theft; and disputes over stalls needed resolution. In some settings vendors reached agreements among themselves. In others an existing traders' association or tekiya leader allocated spaces, issued tickets, organized cleaning, or negotiated with property owners and police. Such activities explain why an informal leader might gain influence without employing violence at every turn. The difficulty is determining when the services represented collective self-government and when a powerful intermediary compelled sellers to accept terms they could not negotiate.

Physical space was valuable because the wartime city had been rebuilt neither quickly nor evenly. A stall beside a station entrance might attract customers throughout the day; a stall on a side street might sell little. Control of entry could determine whether a household earned enough to eat. When a leader charged a fee for a desirable space, that payment could have several interpretations: rent authorized by a landowner, an association due covering shared expenses, a payment for protection, or extortion. Identifying the difference requires asking who owned the land, who set the fee, whether the payment was recorded, whether sellers could refuse, and what happened when they did. The same outward exchange of cash might conceal different relations of power.

Protection is especially ambiguous. Vendors faced theft, fraudulent goods, street fighting, police seizure, and rivalry over customers. A group that could deter theft or settle quarrels might offer a service. But if that group also threatened unprotected vendors, created disorder, or excluded independent competitors, the demand for protection was partly the product of its own coercion. This circular relationship is central to the economics of racketeering. A payment that initially seemed a practical response to insecurity could become a recurring tribute. Power over multiple stalls also allowed organizers to influence prices and the movement of goods without formally owning every business.

Cause and Effect

Why private market authority expanded

Long-term conditions

Wartime rationing; older street-trading arrangements; gambling networks; prewar systems of private political violence.

Short-term shocks

Destroyed retail space; food shortages; demobilization; unsettled land rights; weak or inconsistent enforcement.

Mechanisms

Stall allocation, storage, private dispute resolution, patronage, exclusion, recurring payments, threats.

Uneven consequences

Reliable selling locations for some traders; dependence, inflated prices, intimidation, and displacement for others.

Compare the services performed with the means used to compel compliance. A service does not become legitimate merely because it fills a public gap.

Local government sometimes tolerated or even collaborated with market leadership. Authorities lacked enough personnel to administer every bomb-cleared lot, feared the consequences of displacing impoverished traders, and wanted recognizable intermediaries through whom instructions could pass. Hatsuda's urban history documents both government tolerance and conflicts over market regulation. Such collaboration did not automatically certify the leader's honesty; it could make private power more durable by conferring apparent legitimacy. A market boss able to tell vendors that he spoke for municipal officials possessed leverage even if no formal legal authority had been delegated. Conversely, officials could use organizers to enforce relocation or payment decisions while avoiding direct responsibility for their consequences.

Commercial control extended beyond the stall itself. Storage areas, loading points, access to railway freight, connections to wholesalers, and movement of controlled goods could be as important as the right to stand on a particular corner. A group positioned between producer and consumer might demand a cut at several stages. Small merchants then faced increasing costs, while consumers paid higher prices. These mechanisms mattered even when a market displayed an energetic scene of buying and selling. The visible liveliness of the street did not reveal how profits were distributed or whether traders were free to change suppliers or leave the association.

Because the informal economy served urgent needs, its regulation presented a moral and political dilemma. A police officer enforcing food controls could deprive a family of the goods it intended to sell for its next meal. An organizer defending stalls against indiscriminate seizure might be celebrated by traders. Yet the same organizer could use the language of protecting the poor to justify payments enforced by threats. Historians should avoid both a blanket condemnation of illegal exchange and a nostalgic identification of the market boss with social welfare. Each interpretation risks ignoring people whose choices were restricted by poverty, police power, or criminal intimidation.

The transition from commerce to territorial authority was thus not an inevitable sequence but an unstable possibility. Some markets developed regular shops and negotiated property agreements; others endured violent rivalry or repeated raids. Territory was not merely a colored area on a gangster map: it consisted of specific stalls, routes, storage spaces, permissions, and relationships. Control changed as police policy, reconstruction, and consumer supply changed. To understand criminal power in occupied Japan, it is necessary to see the street as both a workplace and a contested jurisdiction, with multiple institutions claiming the right to decide what could be sold and by whom.

Who Could Enforce the Rules? Japanese Police, Occupation Troops, and Military Police

Enforcement in occupied Japan operated through several partially overlapping systems. Japanese police remained responsible for domestic criminal investigation, economic controls, traffic, and local order, subject to occupation supervision. U.S. military police enforced military discipline and intervened in dealings involving servicemen, weapons, military supplies, and prohibited exchanges. The occupation's senior command could direct the Japanese government to change regulations or conduct operations, while municipal officers worked with limited manpower and imperfect information. A black-market trader could therefore face scrutiny from different authorities depending on what was being sold, who supplied it, and whether military personnel were involved.

The distinction mattered because Allied personnel were not ordinarily answerable to Japanese criminal courts during the occupation. A Japanese civilian accused of purchasing unauthorized military goods could be handed to Japanese police while a U.S. serviceman faced separate military investigation. Different forums meant different protections, procedures, and outcomes. Although joint operations occurred, cooperation in the street did not imply equal jurisdiction. Japanese officers could be made to appear subordinate in an encounter filmed or supervised by occupation personnel. Those power relations affected what evidence was preserved and how an event was publicly described.

A particularly revealing visual record is the film held by Shōwakan and sourced to the U.S. National Archives, identified as NARA 342-USAF-11067. Its catalogue dates the Kyoto black-market footage to May 28, 1946. The silent film shows interactions between U.S. military police, Japanese traders, Japanese police, and American servicemen. The catalogue describes searches, the confiscation of American cigarettes and other items, Japanese civilians being transferred to Japanese officers, and a serviceman taken in a military vehicle. The film makes the different authorities visible in a way that a generalized account of "police crackdowns" does not.

That conflict would become more explicit in the territorial politics of Tokyo and the violent encounters examined below.

The Geography of Market Power: Tokyo, Yokohama, Osaka, and Kobe

Geography shaped the possibility of commercial control. Postwar markets did not appear on an empty map; they formed around railway stations, established entertainment districts, freight corridors, temple precincts, docks, and sites made available by wartime destruction. A busy terminal collected commuters and goods moving between city and countryside. A port provided access to shipping and military supply. A bomb-cleared lot could be occupied quickly, but its legal owner might later demand restitution or rent. These different landscapes offered distinct opportunities for stall owners, brokers, police, and organized groups. Treating the whole country as a single "black market" erases how local geography influenced authority.

Tokyo illustrates the variety. Shinjuku and Shibuya drew crowds through their stations and surrounding commercial approaches. Shinbashi connected railway traffic and office areas; Ueno and Asakusa possessed older histories of entertainment and selling. Markets could occupy alleys, cleared squares, shrine approaches, and sites where buildings had burned. A leader's claim to "control" a market often meant power over a particular intersection, a collection of stalls, or a daily arrangement for cleaning and access rather than ownership of an entire ward. Even within one district, associations could compete or coexist. Hatsuda's architectural research stresses continuity with prewar retail spaces and the significance of postwar reconstruction, an approach that checks the tendency to imagine market geography solely through gang territories.

The Shinbashi district offers an unusually well-documented transition. Photographs preserved in Japanese and American archives show traders and crowds in February 1946; later records describe closure and relocation. The precise meaning of closure, however, depends on what institution was ordered to stop operating and whether vendors subsequently sold goods nearby. An official announcement could end a market organization without removing every informal transaction. The contrast between a bustling photograph and a dated enforcement record warns against converting either moment into a permanent picture of the locality. Urban space changed as railway property, municipal plans, policing, and private merchants' ambitions intersected.

The spatial lesson is that markets were both emergency institutions and foundations for later commercial districts. Structures once described as temporary sometimes became more permanent shops or indoor markets; other stalls were removed to clear roads or implement reconstruction plans. Their changing status altered the value of local leadership. A person skilled at organizing movable stalls might lose influence when regular leases and building codes returned; another who obtained land or business contacts could preserve it. Understanding organized crime in the postwar city therefore requires looking at architecture, transport, and the regulation of space as closely as at police files and famous criminal names.

Tekiya Bosses, Stall Allocation, and the Commercial Rules of the Street

An informal market that opened each morning and operated until night could not function through spontaneous exchange alone. Someone usually determined where vendors stood, how carts entered, whether passageways remained open, and how arguments over access were settled. At some markets these duties belonged to a negotiated association. At others a tekiya boss claimed the power to assign stalls, collect money, and settle disputes. Such leadership built on precedents in itinerant commerce and festival selling, where recurring visits and relationships with local communities had long required coordination. The postwar emergency enlarged the stakes by bringing many more sellers into spaces whose ownership and permitted uses were uncertain.

Stall allocation was not simply a matter of placing tables. The difference between a spot beside a station gate and one at the edge of the market could determine sales. A boss could reward followers with better positions, reserve spaces for relatives, or require payments from newcomers. In some cases the fees paid for shared expenses, maintenance, sanitation, lights, or contact with authorities. In other cases they enriched a leader or his associates. The evidence that matters includes fee records, permitted access, complaints, and the consequences of nonpayment. Without such evidence, the existence of a boss and a collection system does not establish either benevolent organization or criminal extortion.

These organizations also arranged relations with outside institutions. A local police station might prefer to deal with one recognized representative rather than hundreds of individual vendors. A landlord might require a guarantor for temporary use of cleared land. A municipal department might request that sellers move from a road or remove dangerous structures. A market leader who could speak for the group occupied a valuable brokerage position. That ability could protect vendors by making collective negotiation possible. It could also allow the leader to promise compliance while concealing unequal treatment among members, or to enforce decisions more harshly than officials had authorized.

Historical Vocabulary
Protection, brokerage, extortion and territorial control

Brokerage connects buyers, sellers, or officials and can be lawful or unlawful. Protection may describe genuinely negotiated security, but a demand backed by threats is extortion. Territorial control denotes practical influence over places, access, and rules, not necessarily legal ownership.

Ask who set a fee, whether it paid for a real service, whether refusal was possible, and which institution could enforce a complaint.

The occupation authorities regarded some boss–follower relationships as survivals of a hierarchical social system they sought to reform. Hatsuda's analysis of SCAP records notes files devoted to the "oyabun system" and the scrutiny of tekiya leaders. This viewpoint belonged to a larger project of democratization, but it should not be taken as proof that every market association was politically reactionary or that hierarchy explained all its activities. Administrators could perceive a family-like arrangement as unacceptable while merchants valued its practical services. The same structure could contain reciprocal assistance and genuine domination. Historical judgment must distinguish official ideological categories from the documented operation of particular groups.

Violence was one way to sustain control, but not the only one. Access to reliable supplies, knowledge of vendors' debts, social reputation, and influence with local officials could make obedience advantageous even without an explicit threat. Such power could become coercive when sellers lacked realistic alternatives. A newcomer refused an association card might find there was no other place to sell nearby. A vendor who challenged a fee might lose a coveted location or face harassment. The possibility of punishment changed bargaining conditions even when most days passed without fighting. This helps explain how market governance could persist through routine relationships rather than constant open confrontation.

Postwar records also show the difficulty of defining legitimate merchants. A person selling goods in violation of a price ceiling might still pay rent to a property owner and wages to an assistant. A shop with a municipal permit could be supplied through an illicit wholesaler. A technically unlicensed stall might become the seed of a later regular shop. The border between legal and illegal commerce moved as rationing, licensing, and reconstruction policies changed. Such movement gave intermediaries further leverage: they might offer help navigating official permissions, but could also exploit uncertainty by claiming that payment would prevent an inspection or seizure.

The most productive historical interpretation treats the tekiya boss as a possible form of private governance whose conduct must be established in each locality. A boss who negotiated for traders, organized sanitation, or resolved conflicts exercised authority; one who extorted payments, ordered assaults, or intimidated witnesses exercised criminal coercion. These descriptions can apply to the same individual at different moments. The economic capacity to organize a street market made power possible, while the mechanisms used to acquire and maintain that power determine whether the history becomes one of cooperation, domination, or both.

Gambling, Loans, Brokerage, and Diversifying Rackets

Market control was only one source of income for organizations active in postwar urban life. Gambling remained important to some bakuto groups, though the forms of gambling and the locations in which it occurred differed. Unauthorized games offered organizers a return through a share of wagers or charges for entry and protection. Informal credit could make it possible for vendors to buy supplies before they had sales revenue. Transport services, brokerage, entertainment businesses, and resale channels likewise generated income. The fact that these activities sometimes appeared together in one group does not mean that every vendor's association also operated gambling rooms or controlled loans. Their combinations varied with local opportunities and available personnel.

Gambling profits depended on continuing participation and on keeping operations open despite police scrutiny. The organizer needed premises, equipment, trusted assistants, means of settling disputes, and arrangements for collecting unpaid debts. Authority could arise from a capacity to enforce obligations without resort to courts, because the gambling activity itself might be prohibited. This dependence on private enforcement created scope for violent collection, but evidence of a gambling venue alone is not evidence of assault. Historians must distinguish the structure of a transaction from the methods used when it went wrong. Police records may document one side of such disputes; participants' testimony and local reporting can add or complicate the picture.

Informal loans were particularly consequential during inflation. A vendor who could not obtain bank credit might borrow from a supplier, a relative, an association, or a lender working outside formal rules. The sum advanced could support a household or allow purchase of stock; the terms could also trap a borrower in recurring obligations. The relationship became coercive when a lender demanded repayment through violence, seizure, forced labor, or exclusion from the market. Yet not all personal lending was usury, and a high interest rate in a volatile economy must be understood in context rather than treated as sufficient proof of an organized racket. The relevant evidence includes repayment terms, actual collections, complaints, and the distribution of risk.

Brokerage offered a different advantage. People with contacts at railway depots, ports, former military warehouses, or established wholesale houses could locate goods unavailable through official distribution. A broker might arrange storage or transportation and earn a fee for bringing together buyers and sellers. Some merchandise was lawfully obtained, while other goods had been stolen, diverted from rationed supplies, or removed from former military inventories. The origin of a commodity mattered for legal classification and for understanding who profited. Historical accounts should not infer theft simply from the presence of American cigarettes or military-looking equipment in a market; the specific chain of possession needs to be demonstrated.

Protection services also had varied meanings. A merchant might pay someone to guard inventory overnight and regard the payment as wages for work. Another might be compelled to pay a group that threatened the shop if protection was refused. The distinction turns on choice, threats, and alternatives. A criminal group could blur it deliberately by presenting itself as indispensable to safety in a district whose insecurity it helped create. This is why an analysis of organized crime must account for local authority as well as prohibited activities. The question is not merely which products were illegal, but how certain actors acquired the power to impose terms on others.

These ventures changed as reconstruction progressed. When regulated wholesale distribution began functioning more reliably, one avenue of illicit arbitrage could narrow. When temporary stalls became permanent shops, ownership and lease arrangements might reduce the scope for market bosses, though some organizers adapted by entering regular businesses or obtaining influence through contractors. Crackdowns against one activity could redirect capital and personnel toward another. Such shifts cannot be dated uniformly across Japan, and evidence for later drug enterprises or nationwide syndicate consolidation should not be projected backward into the first months after defeat. The occupation years were a period of experimentation and adjustment, not the completed architecture of the later underworld.

The larger analytical point is that crime and commerce were connected through specific mechanisms rather than through a generalized atmosphere of corruption. Gambling supplied proceeds, credit created dependency, brokerage controlled information and goods movement, and protection arrangements shaped access to security. Any of these activities might be lawful, unlawful, voluntary, or coercive depending on circumstances. Reconstructing the conditions allows a historian to explain why an organization became influential without presenting illegality as an ethnic identity, a personal essence, or an inevitable response to poverty.

A Police Raid as Local History: Traders, Arrests, and Competing Claims

On August 5, 1946, police conducted a documented operation against the outdoor market at Shibuya in Tokyo. Photographs preserved by the Shōwakan archive, derived from U.S. National Archives records, depict officers surrounding the selling area, examining merchandise, and escorting a woman trader. The catalogue describes approximately 280 armed police officers and specifies items including rice, potatoes, and cloth that were prohibited from free sale. These details make the incident concrete: police were enforcing controls on particular goods in a crowded marketplace, rather than undertaking an abstract campaign against a generalized underworld. The photographs show particular actions at a particular moment; they cannot alone establish the market's full economy.

The date is crucial. This August police operation followed the distinct violent Shibuya confrontation of July 19, examined in Section 87.11. The two events involved the same broader district and occurred within weeks of each other, but they were not the same incident. A careless narrative could combine them into a single story of gang warfare or repression. The archival captions for August 5 focus on a market raid and prohibited merchandise. They do not by themselves identify every trader's affiliations, nor do they allow conclusions about responsibility for violence in July. Keeping those chronologies separate is a basic test of source discipline.

Photography seems to supply direct proof because it records bodies, clothing, goods, and police equipment. Yet it preserves only what a photographer selected and what an archive later catalogued. One frame may show a police cordon but not the negotiations preceding it. Another may show a detained trader but not the grounds for detention or any later judgment. The captions may have been written by the original photographic unit or subsequently summarized and translated, adding layers of interpretation. An officer lifting a sack cannot tell us whether the owner was a desperate seller, a professional dealer, or an intermediary operating on behalf of a coercive group. Identifying these distinctions requires administrative files, press reports, court records, and testimony.

Korean, Taiwanese, and Chinese Residents in the Postimperial City

The end of the Japanese empire transformed legal identities and everyday relationships in cities where people from Korea, Taiwan, and China had lived, worked, studied, or been brought under unequal wartime conditions. Some had migrated voluntarily in search of employment; others had experienced coercive mobilization or restricted choices. Surrender did not immediately settle the status of these populations. Japan had lost its colonies, but administrative categories, documents, residency, access to work, and relations with the occupation authorities changed at different speeds. A Korean resident who wished to return home faced different decisions from someone who intended to remain. Taiwanese residents and people associated with the Republic of China could invoke relationships with the victorious Allied powers, yet their practical standing in local disputes was contested. These complexities belong at the center of the market story.

The label "third-country national," used in some occupation-era settings, could obscure these differences. It sometimes suggested that formerly colonized residents were neither ordinary Japanese subjects nor occupying Allied soldiers, but it was not a neutral description of their full legal rights. SCAP's treatment of former colonial subjects could vary with security, repatriation, nationality, and diplomatic concerns. Some residents found a degree of autonomy unavailable under imperial rule, while others encountered continued prejudice, uncertain police protection, or restrictions in commercial activity. A textbook should explain the distinction among nationality, political status, social identity, and actual treatment, rather than place all minorities into a single administrative or racial category.

Black markets became important settings for this transition because they lowered certain barriers to entry. An individual with modest stock and contacts might establish a stall more easily than a regular shop, whose opening required capital, premises, and approvals. Former colonial residents sometimes developed associations to defend commercial interests, seek recognition, or negotiate with police. Such organizations could be viewed suspiciously by Japanese market leaders who claimed authority over spaces occupied by people unwilling to accept their terms. The resulting disputes were about money and territory, but also about the collapse of an imperial hierarchy. Men formerly associated with privileged Japanese institutions confronted individuals who no longer accepted the same subordinate position.

Historians cannot resolve these conflicts merely by choosing the label most familiar today. They should identify which institution applied a category, on what date, and in relation to which decision. That attention also prevents an important distortion: the postwar activities of residents from former colonies were not detached from decades of labor recruitment, colonial law, migration, and the wartime economy. The confrontation in Tokyo was part of the afterlife of an empire whose social geography extended beyond its vanished borders.

The Shibuya Incident of July 19, 1946

On July 19, 1946, the Shibuya district became the site of a violent confrontation involving Taiwanese residents, Japanese police, and actors associated with contested commercial authority. Its significance extends beyond the immediate street fighting. The incident occurred after Japan's imperial rule over Taiwan had ended and as occupation authorities worked to establish a new political order. People who had been subjects of a Japanese empire now occupied changing positions in a city governed under Allied supervision, while Japanese police continued to exercise powers familiar from the imperial period. Adam Cathcart's study places the confrontation within the wider history of empire's dissolution and emphasizes how its repercussions traveled through Japanese, Chinese, Taiwanese, and American political channels.

The circumstances need careful reconstruction because accounts disagree about responsibility, motivation, and the sequence leading to gunfire. Conflict over market space and police treatment had been escalating. Participants, observers, and authorities described these disputes through different political vocabularies: public order, protection of traders, resistance to discrimination, criminal violence, or national insult. Retrospective narratives sometimes simplify the affair into a fight between two criminal gangs. That formulation conceals the role of police, the presence of former colonial subjects with contested status, and the diplomatic consequences of how their actions were labeled. A serious chapter should identify the actors from dated records rather than assigning every person the same affiliation.

On the day of the confrontation, a crowd and vehicles associated with Taiwanese participants entered the area amid rising tension, and armed violence occurred in interactions with Japanese police. Adam Cathcart’s published reconstruction reports six Taiwanese residents and one Japanese police officer killed, with additional injuries; the figures are attributed to that scholarly account rather than offered as an independently collated final tally. Other summaries and source traditions have varied, and the numbers of those killed, wounded, arrested, and tried must not be conflated. The secure historical point is that lethal force transformed a contested market dispute into an international political issue. What each participant intended, whether force was justified, and which account of the opening exchange is most reliable remain questions for source criticism.

The police presence made the incident different from an ordinary rivalry between sellers. Japanese officers asserted public-order authority, yet the former colonial status of some participants created a special problem for the occupation. How should civilians from Taiwan be treated after sovereignty over the island had changed? Could Japanese authorities exercise the same powers they had held before surrender? Which governmental or diplomatic representatives could raise objections? None of these questions had easy or universally accepted answers. The conflict thus revealed the limits of separating local criminal investigation from the history of empire, diplomacy, and occupation policy.

Reporting also mattered. Japanese newspapers, police statements, overseas Chinese communications, and subsequent scholarly accounts did not merely describe an already settled event; they selected actors and causes, emphasized or omitted violence, and framed claims of victimhood and authority. The category of "riot" could suggest collective disorder. A narrative of "police defense" could privilege the official account of who initiated force. A narrative of "colonial resistance" could risk obscuring commercial rivalries or harm to bystanders. Historians should compare these frames and identify the documents supporting specific assertions. Cathcart's transnational approach is valuable precisely because the affair cannot be understood through a single national archive.

The aftermath involved investigation, contentious legal proceedings, and diplomatic representations, though a full judicial reconstruction would require close use of case files. For the purposes of this chapter, the most important consequence is that violence over urban commerce exposed unresolved questions about political belonging and police jurisdiction. Merchant leaders, residents, police, and occupation authorities were drawn into arguments whose meaning exceeded the ownership of a stall. The incident challenged any simple distinction between "Japanese order" and "foreign disorder," not least because the power relations of colonial rule remained embedded in the background of the confrontation.

July 19 must not be conflated with the photographed August 5 Shibuya market raid. The latter documents a later police operation directed at prohibited goods; the former concerns a violent confrontation with postimperial and diplomatic dimensions. Their proximity in time makes comparison useful, but each must be grounded in its own record. Read together, the cases show that the struggle over street commerce occurred on several levels: routine regulation of goods, disputes over private market power, policing and the use of force, and competing claims to rights after empire. Those layers are more historically important than a sensational retelling of the number of combatants.

The July confrontation is sometimes embedded in later narratives of market bosses who allegedly defended Japanese commercial space. Such retrospective accounts must be compared with the order of events recorded at the time and with testimony from Taiwanese participants. Even a factual statement that a named leader was present would not establish that the leader commanded every participant or determined the police response. Similarly, describing the incident as an outcome of tension following repatriation does not explain who brought weapons, how warnings were delivered, or why lethal force followed. A well-supported narrative distinguishes background causes from immediate triggers and subsequent interpretations. This discipline is especially necessary where national humiliation, community solidarity, and police reputation gave different actors reasons to emphasize selected aspects of the episode.

Beyond Shibuya: Regional Conflicts and Different Local Outcomes

Shibuya became particularly visible because it occurred in the occupied capital and attracted international attention. It was not, however, the only setting in which former colonial subjects, local organizers, police, and market traders disputed authority. Research on urban markets also points to conflicts associated with Hamamatsu in April 1948 and Tsubetsu in September 1948. These cases suggest that the tensions did not disappear once the immediate crisis of 1946 had passed. They also warn against turning a famous Tokyo confrontation into a universal model for all regional experiences. Different towns possessed different commercial patterns, ethnic populations, police resources, and histories of migration.

The Hamamatsu and Tsubetsu cases should be introduced with precisely the level of certainty the sources permit. Hatsuda cites them as examples of conflict involving market leadership and non-Japanese traders, but a brief reference in a secondary study does not establish every participant's motive, casualty figure, or judicial outcome. A complete local history would examine contemporary Japanese reporting, police records, and community sources. Until that work is done, the textbook can explain why these places matter and what comparative questions they raise without inventing dramatic details. The temptation to fill archival gaps with a familiar story of ethnic gangs fighting must be resisted.

Regional variation begins with the available commercial space. In one city, markets may have formed in railway approaches and bomb-cleared central districts; in another, a shrine or rural transport junction may have supplied the principal selling area. The relation between market bosses and municipal offices could range from negotiated toleration to confrontation. A locality with close ties among established retailers, wholesalers, and police might channel disputes through familiar intermediaries. A place transformed by rapid repatriation and unsettled property claims could generate sharper competition. None of these circumstances alone predetermines violence, but they change how disputes arise and how authorities respond.

Negotiation deserves equal attention. The May 1946 Tokyo contacts between market bosses and Chinese and Korean organizations indicate that people recognized shared commercial interests even amid suspicion. They could agree on trading practices, attempt to limit prohibited sales, or seek mechanisms for settling disagreements. The surviving report cannot establish whether such understandings were honored, and it may represent the perspective of intermediaries rather than ordinary members. Nonetheless, acknowledging negotiation prevents a misleading account in which racialized conflict appears inevitable. Commercial cooperation was possible because sellers and buyers depended on continuing access to markets, and violence threatened the incomes of participants on every side.

Police intervention could likewise produce different results. Officers who treated a confrontation as a public-order problem might attempt separation, arrests, or negotiation. Where authorities relied on a powerful market leader as intermediary, they risked reinforcing the leader's territorial claim. If the police regarded a community organization through inherited colonial prejudices, enforcement could deepen distrust. Yet there were also officials and associations that sought workable solutions. A history attentive to decision-making will identify differences among police units and local governments rather than describing the entire institution as uniformly complicit or uniformly impartial.

The regional perspective changes how historians assess organized crime's transformation. A gang's authority might strengthen through a victory in a local dispute, but it could also weaken when police restricted street stalls or merchants negotiated independent legal premises. In some towns the most durable organizations may have been commercial associations not primarily known for violence. Elsewhere criminal groups exploited a shortage of security. Studying only spectacular incidents therefore exaggerates the frequency of armed confrontations and conceals the routine practices that allowed markets to operate. The historian needs both records of disorder and quieter evidence of leases, permits, association meetings, and changing retail construction.

The comparison also brings the history of empire into local Japan. Former colonial subjects did not cease to be historical actors when imperial institutions collapsed; they reorganized networks and pursued livelihoods within changing legal conditions. Japanese returnees, local workers, and merchants had their own varied interests. The resulting city was not simply divided into two opposing populations. Rather, multiple identities and organizations overlapped, and a conflict became ethnicized through political language as well as actual inequalities. The lesson from looking beyond Shibuya is that organized coercion emerged from particular configurations of place, power, and memory—not from an inherent disposition of any community toward violence.

Comparison can also be structured around the language of administrative success. A police department might report restoring order after dispersing traders, while a merchants' association could describe the same operation as the destruction of a functioning local economy. A neighborhood record might show that a new market opened elsewhere almost immediately. Each perspective captures a different outcome. In studying Hamamatsu or Tsubetsu, students should first establish a short reliable sequence of events, then map the participants and institutions, and only afterward ask how much of the conflict was territorial, political, economic, or ethnicized. The method matters more than forcing every case into a predefined pattern derived from Shibuya.

Section 87.13 · Division 3 — Conflict, Imperial Legacies and Politics

Victims, Coercion, and the Social Costs of Criminal Governance

The language of "providing order" can make organized coercion sound administrative and benign. To evaluate a market organization, historians must examine who bore its costs. A leader who supplied guards and settled disputes might seem useful to participating merchants, while a small trader excluded from a stall or threatened for refusing payment experienced the same authority differently. Violence could make an organization appear stable because those who feared punishment complied publicly. Absence of visible resistance is therefore not evidence of consent. The most revealing records often concern complaints, damaged property, unpaid obligations, displacement, or efforts to find police protection.

Extortion in a commercial district could take different forms. A trader might be ordered to pay a recurring fee unrelated to any genuine service, to purchase stock from a designated supplier, or to surrender a profitable location to an associate of a boss. Threats could target merchandise, family members, or future access to work. The documentary difficulty is that formal complaints were risky: a person dependent on the district might fear retaliation and might also fear admitting violation of rationing rules. Police records consequently reflect not simply how often coercion occurred, but how safe it was to report and which complaints officials chose to investigate. A low number of reported cases cannot be taken as proof of security.

Women and young traders faced additional vulnerabilities that gang-centered histories rarely record. They could be financially responsible for dependents, lacked access to powerful acquaintances, or faced harassment while carrying goods or working at stalls. Surviving photographs show women among those subject to enforcement, but an image of detention does not disclose their experience before or after the event. Oral accounts and social-history studies can add evidence, provided that retrospective memories are dated and their limitations acknowledged. The question is not whether women belonged to the recognized hierarchy of a gang, but how that hierarchy shaped access to livelihoods and personal safety for people outside it.

Consumers were also affected. When intermediaries monopolized limited goods or demanded payments at multiple points, prices could rise beyond what poorer households could afford. Yet some market organizers helped maintain supplies where official channels failed. These possibilities must be held together rather than resolved through moral labels. An intermediary could improve the regularity of delivery while extracting excessive profits. A broker might provide real information and also use threats to prevent competitors from entering. For those purchasing food, the practical issue was whether the market offered affordable goods and whether they could reach it without intimidation or police interference.

The relationship between coercion and corruption deserves particular attention. A criminal leader who obtained unofficial assurances from an official could convince traders that resistance was futile. A policeman who ignored complaints might do so because he was bribed, overstretched, concerned about public hunger, or uncertain about occupation policy. Without records, motives cannot be assumed. Reconstructing a chain of personal contacts, payments, orders, and enforcement choices is more useful than invoking "corruption" as a universal explanation. The risk of such dealings was that the state might appear to recognize a private coercive authority, making it harder for vulnerable traders to distinguish legal obligations from demands made under threat.

Violence itself had indirect consequences. Public fighting disrupted commerce, brought police raids, and could encourage residents to avoid a district. People who had not participated in the quarrel lost income or experienced injury. Conflict involving former colonial subjects could intensify discriminatory narratives that harmed residents without any connection to the events. A sensational newspaper story might depict whole communities as dangerous, making it harder for ordinary members to seek redress. The social costs therefore extended well beyond gang members and immediate targets to commercial life, policing legitimacy, and the fragile reintegration of people displaced by the war.

Evaluating claims that gangs "governed" the street requires a standard centered on the governed rather than the leaders. Did merchants have meaningful alternatives? Could they dispute fees, leave an organization, or report crimes safely? Were rules applied predictably and equally? Did the arrangement reduce violence or create fear? Such questions reveal that public order is not simply the absence of fighting; it includes rights, accountability, and access to livelihood. The history of the postwar underworld matters because it shows how private institutions could fill real gaps in regulation while reproducing inequalities and coercion that an officially democratizing state claimed to oppose.

The language of victimhood must also be handled carefully when participants in an illegal market sought protection. A vendor's violation of economic regulations did not deprive that person of the right to be free from assault, threats, or discriminatory policing. Conversely, a merchant's poverty did not excuse violence against a rival. Separating these judgments is fundamental to a rights-based history of public order. It directs attention toward the actual harm caused and the remedies available, not toward the social respectability of the complainant. This approach helps explain why an officially democratic occupation could produce experiences of injustice: newly proclaimed liberties depended on institutions capable of protecting people who possessed little property, influence, or recognized political standing.

Section 87.14 · Division 3 — Conflict, Imperial Legacies and Politics

Named Organizations and Leaders: Transformation without Mythology

It is tempting to narrate the postwar underworld through a small set of famous leaders, but retrospective fame distorts the scale of their authority in 1945. The Yamaguchi-gumi, established before the war and associated with the Kobe area, was one organization among many operating in a changing waterfront and urban economy. Its later prominence should not be projected backward as if it already possessed nationwide reach at the moment of surrender. Contemporary accounts of other local organizations show that control remained contested and often confined to particular districts or activities. A textbook should explain what a group actually did at a specified date, rather than describe its future accomplishments in the past tense.

Taoka Kazuo became central to the Yamaguchi-gumi's postwar history, and biographical treatments often depict his personal capacity for toughness, organization, and expansion. These claims need evaluation through independent evidence rather than acceptance of celebratory group narratives. The relevant historical questions concern connections to labor and transport, strategies for incorporating rivals, the use of violence, and relationships with legitimate businesses and officials. A personal story can illustrate a wider transformation if its chronology and institutional context are secured. Otherwise it becomes a substitute for explanation, encouraging readers to see a vast change in Japan's underworld as the achievement of one charismatic individual.

Kobe's port setting mattered because cargo movement, warehouse labor, shipping companies, and transport services created opportunities for groups able to supply workers or enforce contracts. The dividing line between a labor broker and a coercive organization might be difficult to see from an outside report. A contractor could organize men for a difficult task and receive legitimate compensation. A group that threatened independent workers, coerced hiring, or attacked competitors crossed into a different form of authority. Postwar shortages and the rebuilding of commercial infrastructure made such distinctions especially important. Connections between informal violence and legitimate employment should be investigated case by case rather than used to portray an entire occupation or industry as criminal.

Different cities produced distinct organizational pathways. Tokyo's postwar market bosses frequently built authority around stalls, property arrangements, and negotiations with officials. In the Kansai region, combinations of waterfront labor, gambling, and protection had different weights. Some organizations retained older bakuto or tekiya identities; others emerged as more loosely arranged groups of men known for fighting or intimidation. Terms for such groups were used inconsistently by police and journalists. The emergence of a recognizable modern yakuza identity therefore involved later classification and consolidation as much as immediate 1945 experience. Organizational histories must keep their own dates visible.

Quantitative claims are especially vulnerable to anachronism. Membership totals from the 1960s, counts of affiliated groups compiled decades later, and modern police designations cannot demonstrate the scale of organized crime in 1946. Nor does a photograph of tattooed men or a newspaper's label of "yakuza" establish a leader's control of an entire market. Better evidence includes dated association registers, named criminal cases, records of employment contracts, property transactions, and reliable reports of rivalries. Scholars such as Peter Hill have used the economics of protection and the relationship between gangs and the state to clarify what distinguishes organized crime from ordinary unlawful exchange.

The role of hierarchy must also be examined critically. Organizations might publicize father–son bonds or traditions of mutual help, but followers could face obligations enforced by threats, debt, or the absence of alternative work. Conversely, internal organization was not always as uniform as later depictions suggest. Alliances could change, branches might act independently, and individuals could shift between street vending, gambling, regular employment, and coercive activity. A name on a group history is not sufficient proof of continuous membership or identical conduct over time. The historian must reconstruct practices rather than assume that every group conformed to a single national model.

The significant postwar transformation was the increased capacity of some violent intermediaries to move across sectors: from informal markets to transport, construction, gambling, protection, and politically connected activities. This expansion developed unevenly and was shaped by police policy and economic reconstruction. A leader's later influence helps explain why researchers have studied him, but it does not erase the agency of merchants, workers, officials, or victims whose actions and constraints made his influence possible. Chapter 87 should therefore present named groups as cases within a wider institutional history, not as protagonists in a legendary account of an underworld empire.

A regional organizational case should be narrated through three time scales. The first is the prewar history of association and economic activity. The second is the immediate post-surrender period, when local actors responded to shortages, population movement, and weak regulation. The third is the later expansion visible in accounts of the 1950s and 1960s. Conflating them produces a false impression of continuous size and purpose. It also makes it difficult to explain change, because an outcome is presented as an original intention. Keeping these time scales separate reveals what postwar leaders could actually foresee and how competitors, employees, authorities, and residents shaped results that later organizational histories attributed chiefly to their founders.

Section 87.15 · Division 3 — Conflict, Imperial Legacies and Politics

Crime, Political Brokers, Anti-Communism, and Violence Specialists

Organized violence in postwar Japan intersected with politics partly because both depended on control of people and public space. A leader able to mobilize a group of men could provide security at an event, intimidate protesters, assist employers during labor disputes, or pressure a political rival. The exchange might be justified publicly as protecting order and privately as a paid service. The historian's task is to distinguish allegations, documented contracts or payments, police investigation, and later memories. The existence of an overlap between underworld groups and political actors does not demonstrate that all conservative, nationalist, or anti-communist organizations employed criminal violence.

Eiko Maruko Siniawer's history of violence specialists shows that these relationships had deep roots in modern Japanese political life. The occupation did not invent the practice of political actors seeking private muscle; it transformed the circumstances in which such labor could be supplied. Postwar elections, social movements, labor unions, and disputes over land or property all generated contention. In an environment of uneven policing and economic scarcity, someone who could deploy followers quickly might be useful to employers, politicians, or local patrons. The same capacity threatened civil liberties when it was used to silence speech, obstruct assembly, or intimidate workers exercising recently recognized rights.

The beginnings of the Cold War reshaped official priorities. Occupation authorities initially emphasized dismantling militarism, revising institutions, and supporting democratic reform; subsequent fears about communism and labor militancy altered the political environment. Some scholarship examines contacts between occupation officials and organized-crime intermediaries in that context, including assistance in confronting left-wing mobilization. Such relationships are serious claims requiring named documents, dates, and actors. They must not be generalized into a claim that SCAP itself operated through a single nationwide criminal network. The occupation was a complex bureaucracy containing competing agencies and policies, and local contacts could differ sharply from formal directives.

Labor disputes exposed especially sharp contradictions. An organization that supplied men to confront strikers might portray itself as defending work or property. From the perspective of workers facing violence, the same intervention could be a form of political suppression. Assessing a particular case requires evidence about who initiated confrontation, what employment relationship existed, and how police responded. Business relationships supplied additional incentives. A firm needing labor quickly, an owner hoping to regain a disputed property, or a market organizer seeking official protection might find private intermediaries attractive. Where payment was made for legitimate services, a record may resemble an ordinary contract. Where threats secured compliance or diverted earnings, the same institutional connection became a channel for criminal activity. The difficulty of separating legal and illicit transactions is precisely why organized crime cannot be understood only through the biographies of gang leaders. It operates within networks of demand, protection, and selective enforcement, some of which connect to otherwise conventional institutions.

The historical debate over informal governance versus coercive extraction runs through these cases. One interpretation emphasizes that weak public institutions left gaps in security and dispute resolution which private groups could fill. Another points out that groups with a capacity for violence could profit by manufacturing insecurity and then selling protection. Both identify possible mechanisms, and both require local evidence. A merchant's testimony that a boss kept order may be sincere; a different merchant's account of forced fees may also be true. The historian must ask whose interests were served, who could leave without penalty, and whether violence was directed primarily at real external threats or at maintaining monopoly power.

Political connections influenced the emerging underworld but did not determine its entire trajectory. Market regulation, police reform, regional commercial opportunity, and individual strategies all mattered. Some private groups adapted as wartime controls loosened; others lost influence through crackdowns or redevelopment. Political patrons could confer protection, yet changing governments and policies could also expose intermediaries to risk. By refusing a simple conspiracy narrative or a romantic tale of neighborhood guardians, the chapter can show how violent authority was reproduced through specific exchanges with employers, officials, and political organizations during a period of contested democratization.

Evidence of political connections should be graded by what it can demonstrate. A photograph of a politician with a locally notorious figure proves a meeting or shared event, not necessarily the commissioning of intimidation. A newspaper accusation may document public controversy rather than a settled fact. An employment record or payment voucher establishes a transaction but still requires interpretation of the work performed. A court finding may be stronger evidence of specific conduct while remaining limited to the charges adjudicated. Historians should resist turning a chain of association into a chain of proven wrongdoing. This caution does not shield powerful figures from scrutiny; it makes documented collusion more significant by separating evidence from insinuation.

Section 87.16 · Division 4 — Police Reform and Transformation

The Police Law of 1947 and the Reorganization of Public Security

Policing was itself being reconstructed during the occupation. The prewar Home Ministry had exercised extensive authority over public order, surveillance, and social regulation. Its association with imperial administration made police reform part of the broader democratization agenda described in Chapter 84. The Police Law, Law No. 196, was promulgated on December 17, 1947, and took effect on March 7, 1948. That distinction between enactment and implementation matters, because a reader cannot assume the new system was operating everywhere in December. The law's text declared responsibilities for protecting life and property, apprehending suspects, and maintaining public safety, while expressly warning against interference with constitutional rights.

The reform sought to reduce centralized control through public safety commissions and a division of responsibility between municipal police and a National Rural Police structure. Under the framework, qualifying cities, towns, and villages organized their own police, while areas outside their jurisdiction were served by the national rural system. The public safety commission model was designed to provide civilian oversight and protect the police from the unchecked political power associated with the old system. This was an important constitutional change, but the existence of commissions did not ensure immediate accountability in every district. Recruiting personnel, financing departments, organizing communication, and adapting officers' habits all required time.

Local autonomy could also complicate investigations that crossed jurisdictional boundaries. A seller might transport goods through several towns, a market organization might have contacts in different wards, and a criminal group might exploit differences in enforcement. Officials needed channels for identification, information sharing, and cooperation across forces. The 1947 law acknowledged these problems in provisions concerning criminal statistics, communication, and authority beyond jurisdiction. Yet local police budgets and capacity varied, and the wider security environment remained under occupation supervision. The reform therefore redistributed responsibility while leaving practical questions about coordination unresolved.

Decentralization could make officials more responsive to neighborhood grievances, but it could also increase the influence of local patrons over small police departments. These outcomes were possibilities rather than automatic consequences; a reliable explanation requires evidence of particular decisions, budgets, personnel, and complaint procedures.

The official institutional histories also identify shortcomings, including a multiplicity of local forces that complicated coordination. These later assessments are useful but should be read alongside contemporary legislation and debates. An official history may emphasize the need for subsequent reform, while a civil liberties advocate might stress the value of decentralization. Organized groups responded to this changing environment in different ways. A gang able to operate across municipal boundaries might seek protection from patrons in several jurisdictions. A tekiya association could negotiate stall permissions with local officials who had newly defined powers. A small trader might welcome a police force more accountable to residents, or might fear that familiar market bosses retained informal influence. Where courts and officers actually pursued violence, private coercive authority could be constrained. Where enforcement targeted minor economic violations but neglected intimidation, the underlying hierarchy might persist. The direction of change depended on choices made during implementation, not merely on legal language.

The 1954 Police Law created a later nationwide system headed by the National Police Agency, and its date should be treated only as a forward reference here. It would be anachronistic to describe the occupation-era police as though the 1954 organization were already in place. The 1947–1948 transition is the relevant institutional setting for Chapter 87. It illustrates the larger contradiction of postwar democratization: Japan was extending constitutional safeguards and local accountability while confronting markets and criminal organizations whose power depended partly on the weakness, selectivity, or fragmentation of public enforcement.

Crackdowns, Licensing, Urban Redevelopment, and the Limits of Measurement

The state's campaign against black markets changed over time. In the immediate months after surrender, local authorities often tolerated commerce because rationing and established distribution could not meet demand. By 1946 police operations intensified, and officials sought to transform street markets into more regular commercial facilities. The Ōhara Institute's historical chronology records a July 28, 1946 Home Ministry instruction directing closure of black markets and their conversion to regulated markets, with action beginning in August. This policy was not uniformly implemented in every locality. Enforcement differed according to local political priorities, economic conditions, access to alternative retail space, and the capacity of officers to supervise sprawling markets.

Several types of official action need to be separated. A raid might confiscate controlled goods, arrest suspects, or disperse sellers. Licensing could legalize some stalls while excluding others. Redevelopment might require structures to be removed from a road or private lot even when their merchandise was lawful. A new shop building could provide a stable location but also impose rents unaffordable to small traders. These processes overlapped, but their purposes and effects were not identical. A market described as "closed" in an administrative report might continue to exist in a different building or under a different legal arrangement. Conversely, the survival of some stalls did not mean the former organization retained its leadership or income.

The occupation's attitude toward organized vendor leadership also changed. Hatsuda's research identifies a period of more systematic post-August 1946 directives and official concern about tekiya boss–follower relationships, followed by notable pressure against bosses in 1947. Occupation reformers associated such hierarchies with political and social practices they wished to dismantle. Yet attacks on the leadership structure did not resolve the need for market administration. A market could continue only if questions of access, sanitation, rent, and supply were addressed. When authorities failed to provide alternative arrangements, informal governance could reappear under another name.

Urban reconstruction was a powerful but sometimes understated force. Groups could seek income in gambling, financing, labor control, or protection services, while some members left for ordinary work. It should not be summarized as a smooth passage from lawlessness to modern legality, because access to legitimate markets and public protection remained uneven.

Each source measures something different. Together they can show how public institutions reclaimed certain spaces while private coercive arrangements changed their form. Section 87.18 · Division 4 — Police Reform and Transformation

Newspapers, Police Labels, Film, and the Making of the Postwar Yakuza Image

The postwar underworld exists in historical records partly through the categories assigned by observers. Police reports may identify criminal suspects, journalism may describe "gang wars," and later films may present a recognizable yakuza figure whose clothing and conduct seem timeless. These representations are valuable evidence of public attitudes, but they are not interchangeable with evidence of what happened. A police label might be produced to justify an investigation; a newspaper headline might exaggerate drama; and a film could organize events around the moral transformation of a heroic outsider. The historian must ask who produced each representation, when, for what audience, and with what institutional interests.

Occupation censorship added another complication. Reporting on Allied military activities could be restricted, and the wider environment of information control affected what newspapers printed about public disorder. Japanese authorities and occupation officials sometimes wished to present enforcement as successful, especially when photographs or films documented cooperation. Such materials may foreground the arrest or search while omitting hunger, failed ration distribution, or the subsequent disposition of cases. They may also portray a crowded market as a threat to public order even when the most common activities were ordinary purchase and sale. Visual evidence is indispensable but cannot replace records of policy and social experience.

The July 1946 Shibuya confrontation shows the stakes of classification. A narrative centered on "foreign rioters" might erase former colonial subjects' grievances and legal uncertainties. A narrative of heroic resistance might minimize injuries or competing accounts of who used force. A gangster-history retelling might recast the affair as a battle for territory while obscuring diplomatic responses. Historians need a vocabulary precise enough to distinguish a criminal offense, a political demonstration, a community organization, and an urban commercial dispute. Ethnic identity, police suspicion, or appearance in a crowd is not proof of organizational membership or guilt.

Later gangster cinema and memoir literature contributed to a different kind of historical memory. Films popularized images of honor, loyalty, sacrifice, and conflict between older and newer values. Those themes are important to twentieth-century Japanese culture, but they may tell us more about the concerns of filmmakers and audiences than about the operation of a specific 1946 market. The familiar claim that a gang boss defended poor traders against a corrupt state may contain an element of remembered experience while excluding people who paid coerced fees. Historical writing should read such depictions alongside reports of extortion and violence instead of choosing one dramatic version as the truth.

Contemporary academic scholarship supplies alternative approaches. Urban historians examine building arrangements, market leases, municipal policy, and the persistence of streets and shops. Social historians study vendors, consumers, displaced people, and former colonial subjects. Historians of organized crime analyze protection, hierarchy, violence, and political patronage. These perspectives can correct each other's blind spots. A city map alone may miss intimidation; a police arrest file may miss ordinary commerce; a memoir may emphasize personal loyalty while concealing financial arrangements. Strong interpretation comes from their comparison, not from reliance on a single genre of source.

Terminology also needs dating. Words such as yakuza, bakuto, tekiya, and gurentai were used in shifting ways, and later classifications such as bōryokudan should not be projected backward as though they were precisely defined in 1945. The media's tendency to combine all varieties of disorder into a single underworld category may simplify the story at the cost of historical precision. The distinction is especially consequential when reporting involves Koreans, Taiwanese, or Chinese residents: a group mobilized around community rights should not be described as a gangster syndicate without evidence of organized coercive activity.

The production of an underworld image is itself a historical development. Private organizations could exploit reputations for toughness, journalists could amplify them, police could seek resources by emphasizing threats, and later cinema could transform them into cultural symbols. Public perception could influence enforcement and even how groups chose to present themselves. Chapter 88 will examine the broader cultural world of the occupation; here the point is narrower. Representations of crime matter because they affected who was feared, who was protected, and how the history of postwar authority came to be remembered, often at the expense of less dramatic but more typical lives.

Section 87.19 · Division 4 — Police Reform and Transformation

From Occupation Markets to the Emerging 1950s Underworld

By the early 1950s, the conditions that had produced the first emergency street markets were changing. Food supplies and ordinary commercial distribution gradually improved, urban reconstruction created new buildings and transport arrangements, and police institutions had undergone major reform. The end of the occupation in April 1952 altered sovereignty without eliminating U.S. military bases or every legal and economic legacy of the preceding years. Some market associations survived in transformed premises, while others lost spaces as authorities cleared streets or resumed regular planning. The familiar image of an endless black market therefore misrepresents the transition: specific markets rose, adapted, relocated, or disappeared at different times.

The value of controlling commercial space changed with these developments. During the most acute shortages, a station-front stall could generate income through access to scarce goods. When ordinary shops reopened, the right to occupy a roadside table became less exclusive, although profitable locations still mattered. Leaders who had depended on informal control of stalls faced choices: negotiate legal leases, move into other trades, retain influence through local political contacts, or use coercion to defend declining privileges. Some individuals left organized groups. Others used their organizational experience and capital to pursue more durable enterprises. No single response characterizes the whole underworld.

The Kansai waterfront illustrates one path of adaptation. Organizations associated with transport and labor could find new opportunities as shipping and manufacturing recovered. In Kobe the Yamaguchi-gumi gradually expanded its influence; its later nationwide prominence should be understood as an outcome of consolidation after the immediate occupation emergency, not as a fact already accomplished in 1945. Competition, alliances, economic investments, and changes in policing mattered alongside the biographies of leaders. Local protection and labor arrangements could evolve into more complex organizations, but the timing differed from the history of temporary street markets in Tokyo.

Political change also mattered. Cold War priorities and conflicts over unions and the role of the state altered demand for private muscle. The economic policies commonly associated with the Reverse Course influenced the availability of work, the regulation of commerce, and the opportunities for groups linked to employers or political patrons. Those broader reforms belong principally to Chapter 89; Chapter 87 needs only to explain how they changed the environment in which criminal intermediaries operated. Likewise, the Korean War's influence on production and port activity, treated in Chapter 90, provides context for the expansion of some businesses and labor networks without proving that all such growth benefited criminal groups.

Police reform did not proceed in a straight line from wartime centralization to permanent municipal autonomy. The 1947 law established the early occupation system that took effect in 1948; the subsequent 1954 law created a new national police framework. The latter falls beyond the principal chronology but helps students see why 1948 arrangements should not be mistaken for the permanent modern system. Crime groups adapted to changing jurisdictions and resources, just as police authorities revised their methods of coordination. The relationship between organized violence and the state was dynamic, with periods of cooperation, confrontation, selective enforcement, and reorganization.

The social consequences of the transition were uneven. Some former street vendors entered recognized commercial districts and could rely more heavily on leases, predictable suppliers, and regular customers. Other people lost informal livelihoods when clearances were enforced. Former colonial subjects continued to confront complex questions of nationality and residency; their rights and economic options were not resolved merely by the disappearance of a market stall. Victims of extortion might gain more effective police protection, but organizational violence could continue in different industries. Reconstruction changed the settings of vulnerability as much as it reduced some forms of scarcity and disorder.

The chapter's central transformation, then, is not that the black markets automatically "became the yakuza." It is that existing and newly formed intermediaries found opportunities in the collapse and reconstruction of public order, and that a portion of them developed more durable systems of coercive commercial and political authority. Many black-market traders never joined these groups, and many criminal organizations drew income from activities beyond street vending. Recognizing this separation allows the history to move toward the 1950s without confusing survival, commerce, violence, and later criminal branding. The occupation years supplied important conditions for change, but their outcome was contested and far from inevitable.

Section 87.20 · Division 5 — Synthesis and Review

Who Ruled the Streets? Informal Authority, Coercion, and the Remaking of the Postwar Underworld

The history of crime and informal markets in occupied Japan begins with defeat, but it cannot be explained by defeat alone. War destruction removed buildings, production and distribution broke down, soldiers and civilians returned from overseas, and millions of households sought ways to earn and purchase necessities. The emergency commerce that arose in these conditions provided a livelihood to people who had little access to regular employment. It was therefore neither a single criminal enterprise nor an expression of pure freedom from regulation. Markets had rules, space was valuable, and people competed for authority over sellers and customers. Their existence revealed the limits of public institutions while creating opportunities for private organization.

The legacies of bakuto and tekiya mattered because networks of gamblers and vendors already possessed practices for organizing labor, settling disputes, assigning commercial space, and enforcing obligations. Their adaptation to bomb-cleared districts was significant but uneven. Some leaders performed useful functions for sellers and negotiated with officials; some used violence or fear to extract payments and exclude competitors; some did both. The critical historical distinction lies not in the title a leader claimed but in the relationship between service, consent, accountability, and coercion. A payment that reflected mutually agreed expenses differed from one demanded under threat, even when both appeared in a market register as a fee.

Japanese policing and occupation authority were equally central. The May 1946 Kyoto film shows overlapping American and Japanese enforcement, while August 1946 Shibuya photographs document a large raid directed at controlled merchandise. These sources reveal state action without proving the guilt or organizational affiliations of every trader. The July 19 Shibuya confrontation, a distinct incident, exposed another problem: the dissolution of the Japanese empire had unsettled the legal position and political claims of Korean and Taiwanese residents. Violence over urban commerce could become a dispute about nationality, rights, policing, and international authority. The fact that community organizations sometimes negotiated with market bosses also cautions against presenting ethnic conflict as unavoidable.

During the occupation, police reform promised democratic accountability and decentralization. The Police Law of December 1947, implemented in March 1948, reshaped the institutions responsible for public security; it did not instantly eliminate extortion or guarantee equal protection. Enforcement against price violations, raids on market stalls, and later urban redevelopment changed who could sell and where. These measures could weaken certain private powers and also displace vulnerable vendors. As ordinary distribution recovered, particular sources of illicit profit declined, but organizations adapted through gambling, labor brokerage, protection, and other sectors. Reconstruction transformed rather than simply extinguished opportunities for coercive control.

The historical record itself requires discrimination. Contemporary police labels, photographs, commercial records, newspapers, later memoirs, and gangster films answer different questions. A photograph may identify a raid and its location without explaining a person's motive. A statute reveals institutional purpose without demonstrating effective implementation. A gang memoir may record meaningful experience while embellishing honor and continuity. A social history of markets may demonstrate livelihoods and urban reconstruction while saying too little about intimidation. A balanced account tests these sources against one another and declines to invent certainty about disputed casualties, memberships, or territorial boundaries.

When public institutions could not guarantee order, who ruled the streets? The answer changed by locality and activity. Municipal officials, Japanese police, occupation commanders, property owners, vendors' organizations, community associations, and violent intermediaries all asserted claims. The legitimacy of those claims depended on more than the ability to compel obedience. Merchants needed access to work without arbitrary tribute; residents needed protection from violence; former colonial subjects needed fair treatment; and people accused of economic offenses deserved procedures distinguishing suspicion from guilt. Those are the standards by which historians can assess private governance and state enforcement alike. The emergence of the postwar yakuza was a contested institutional transformation inside this larger struggle over commerce, rights, and authority, not the inevitable successor to every black-market stall.

Chapter Summary

After surrender in August 1945, Japan’s cities faced damaged buildings, broken supply systems, unemployment, repatriation and uncertain property rights. Black markets emerged in these conditions as important places for obtaining goods and earning income. Their survival function did not erase the fact that some transactions violated economic controls; nor did it make all traders members of criminal organizations. The history of informal commerce must therefore be separated from the history of organized coercion, even when both occurred in one district.

The longer history of bakuto and tekiya helps explain the presence of experienced organizers, lenders and private enforcers in the postwar city. Those networks were neither uniform nationwide organizations nor direct continuations of a timeless samurai code. Boss–follower relationships could furnish assistance and predictable rules while also exposing subordinates to dependence and punishment. Later yakuza mythology should be tested against dated records of employment, trade, financial obligation and violence.

Demobilized soldiers and repatriates entered markets alongside civilians who had lost factories, homes or suppliers. A July 1946 association survey reported by Hatsuda identified 275 veterans among 994 Tokyo stall association members. That statistic says something about the composition of a particular commercial organization, not the number of criminals in Japan. Social background, gender, ethnicity and poverty cannot substitute for evidence of individual conduct.

Market leaders acquired influence through control of valuable locations, stall allocation, storage, transport and relationships with officials. Fees could pay for genuine services, but they could also become forced payments. The key distinction concerns the voluntariness of arrangements, the existence of threats and whether vendors could refuse or seek redress. Private organization may have compensated for gaps in public administration while making vulnerable merchants more dependent.

Japanese and American policing intersected in market districts. A May 1946 Kyoto film records U.S. military police investigating transactions and Japanese officers receiving civilians. The film establishes a visible enforcement arrangement but does not prove the guilt or organizational affiliations of those shown. Similar limitations apply to the August 5 Shibuya market-raid photographs, whose archive captions report about 280 officers and the seizure of controlled goods.

The July 19 Shibuya confrontation must remain distinct from the August police operation. Its participants and political meanings were shaped by the collapse of empire, Japanese policing and contested claims to the status of Koreans, Taiwanese and Chinese residents. Cathcart’s reconstruction reports six Taiwanese and one Japanese policeman killed. The event had effects beyond a local commercial quarrel, drawing occupation authorities and Chinese representatives into a dispute about rights, authority and representation.

Regional cases show that Tokyo did not supply a complete national pattern. Other cities combined different station, port, temple and market settings with different policing and migration histories. Negotiations among associations sometimes occurred where later narratives emphasize only violent confrontation. Evaluating organized crime requires examining the actual practice of coercion and the harm experienced by sellers, consumers, workers and families rather than relying on reputations of famous bosses.

The 1947 Police Law, implemented on March 7, 1948, sought more accountable and decentralized policing. Enforcement against unauthorized trade was accompanied by licensing, relocation and urban redevelopment. These policies changed commercial opportunity and could both reduce some forms of private control and deprive poorer traders of stalls. Photographs, legal texts, police figures and later testimony measure different things, so none should be mistaken for a complete record of criminality.

By the early 1950s the sources of illicit profit and the structures of retail were changing. Some gangs and organizers adapted through gambling, transport, labor brokerage or political connections; many informal traders returned to ordinary commerce. The later power of major yakuza groups emerged through uneven consolidation, not automatic succession from the black markets of 1945. Chapter 87 thus shows the postwar underworld developing at the intersection of economic necessity, contested urban authority, colonial legacies and the unfinished reconstruction of democratic public institutions.

Chapter Reflection

The struggle over Japan’s postwar markets reveals that public order was not simply restored from above. Residents, traders, police, occupiers and organizers negotiated its meaning on streets whose commercial value had changed through war. In the emergency economy, private arrangements could help people survive, but the power to impose those arrangements created opportunities for exploitation. The difference between security and extortion often turned on whether ordinary traders possessed genuine alternatives and could seek protection against threats.

This history also uncovers the persistence of empire after imperial institutions collapsed. The July 1946 Shibuya conflict cannot be understood fully as a story of local crime because it involved former colonial subjects and disputes about legal and political status. Contemporary reporting and later retellings could turn particular conflicts into stereotypes about whole communities. Distinguishing individuals, organizations and identities is therefore part of the historian’s ethical and analytical obligation.

Police reform illustrates another difficulty of reconstruction. A law could establish democratic accountability and promise respect for civil liberties while an officer in a street market still faced shortages, rival claims, political pressure and uncertain institutional arrangements. The effectiveness of reform had to be measured through its operation and consequences, not merely its language. Later changes in the economy and in state institutions redirected criminal opportunity without guaranteeing the disappearance of organized coercion.

The most durable lesson is methodological. Photographs, official files, police labels, legal texts, memoirs and films each preserve a fragment of the past and express a perspective. Studied together, they replace the myth of an inevitable gangster takeover with a more complex history of work, coercion, institutional change and political belonging. Chapter 88 turns to occupation culture; Chapter 87 has shown why images of the underworld must always be separated from the evidence for how it exercised power.
