Part II — Yamato and the Formation of Ancient Japan

Prince Shōtoku, Diplomacy, and Early Statecraft

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 607, a Yamato envoy named Ono no Imoko crossed the sea toward the court of the Sui emperor carrying a letter whose opening words would be remembered, debated, and reinterpreted for well over a thousand years: a message from the "son of heaven in the land where the sun rises" to the "son of heaven in the land where the sun sets." Whatever the letter's authors intended by that language — and later chapters of this textbook will show that intention is precisely what remains contested — the mission it accompanied belonged to a court that was, in these same years, also carving twelve new cap ranks for its officials, circulating a set of moral and political injunctions in seventeen articles, and patronizing Buddhist temples and teachers on a new scale. None of these initiatives, taken alone, amounted to a modern state. Taken together, they mark a court reaching for new tools of order.

This chapter examines the Yamato court during the reign of Queen Suiko, roughly from 592 to 628, and asks a single governing question: how did this court use rank, moral-political principle, Buddhist patronage, and continental diplomacy to experiment with more ordered rulership, before the fuller institutions of the mid-seventh-century imperial state existed? Chapter 11 left the Yamato elite in the aftermath of a violent succession and religious crisis: Mononobe no Moriya defeated and killed in battle, the collapse of Mononobe dominance at the central court, the Soga lineage newly ascendant, and a queen, Suiko, placed on the throne within a political order still built from cooperating and competing aristocratic houses. This chapter picks up that story and follows it forward through roughly three and a half decades of court experimentation.

Readers meeting this period for the first time typically encounter it through a single name: Prince Shōtoku, celebrated in later tradition as the visionary author of Japan's first constitution, patron of Buddhism, and far-seeing statesman who single-handedly set the country on the road to a modern, centralized government. This chapter treats that reputation with considerable care. The historical prince behind the legend — most reliably referred to by his given name, Umayado — was a real and significant figure in the Suiko court. But the fully formed "Prince Shōtoku" of later devotion, biography, and national memory is, in substantial part, a construction of the eighth century and after, built up by court chroniclers, Buddhist institutions, and later political interests that had their own reasons for magnifying his role. Separating the historical prince from the remembered saint is one of this chapter's central tasks, and it is also one of this textbook's clearest lessons in historical method: even famous, seemingly well-documented figures can turn out to be composite images, assembled over time from fragments of genuine history and considerable later elaboration.

This chapter also insists on a second, related correction. Suiko herself is too often reduced to a placeholder — a queen whose reign is remembered chiefly as the backdrop for a male regent's supposed genius. Soga no Umako, the head of the dominant Soga lineage and Suiko's own maternal uncle, is likewise sometimes reduced to a scheming power broker operating behind a compliant throne. Neither picture withstands scrutiny. The evidence instead suggests a court in which authority was shared, negotiated, and exercised jointly among a reigning queen, an immensely powerful lineage head, and a prince whose position combined royal descent, personal reputation, and Buddhist patronage. Understanding how these three figures, and the broader court around them, worked together — and where their interests diverged — is essential to understanding what "statecraft" meant at Yamato in this period.

The initiatives examined here — the Twelve Cap Ranks of 603, the Seventeen-Article text of 604, expanding Buddhist temple-building and patronage, and the resumption of direct diplomatic missions to the Sui court beginning around 600 and continuing through 607 and after — did not create anything resembling the bureaucratic, taxation-based imperial state that would take shape only decades later, in the aftermath of the Taika reforms examined in Chapter 13. Hereditary lineage status, the uji-kabane system inherited from earlier generations, and locally rooted elite power remained the basic structure of Yamato politics throughout Suiko's reign. What changed was something more subtle but still historically significant: the vocabulary, symbols, and diplomatic posture through which the court began to imagine centralized, ordered, and morally legitimated rule — imagining it, in some respects, well before the institutions existed to make it a governing reality.

This chapter's evidence comes overwhelmingly from sources compiled long after the events they describe. The Nihon Shoki, completed in 720, and the Kojiki, completed in 712, are the primary narrative sources for Suiko's reign, but both were written roughly a century after Umayado's death by court historians serving the interests of the eighth-century imperial state — a state whose rulers had strong reasons to present their own institutions as the natural culmination of earlier reforms, and strong reasons to celebrate a heroic royal ancestor whose reputation could support Buddhist institutions and imperial prestige alike. Buddhist temple records, inscriptions, and later hagiographic biographies of Shōtoku add further layers of testimony, each shaped by its own devotional and institutional purposes. Chinese sources, particularly the dynastic history of the Sui, offer an independent — though also selective and self-interested — outside perspective on the diplomatic missions. Reading all of this evidence responsibly requires exactly the kind of source criticism this textbook has practiced since its treatment of Yamatai in Chapter 6: asking who wrote a given account, when, for what audience, and with what stake in the outcome.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to describe the Suiko court's political experiments accurately and specifically — without either dismissing them as insignificant or inflating them into something they were not. This distinction prepares directly for Chapter 13, which examines the Taika reforms, the Jinshin War, and the law-code state that these earlier experiments helped make thinkable, but did not themselves create.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political configuration of the Suiko court after 592, including the roles of Suiko, Soga no Umako, and Prince Umayado.
  2. Distinguish the historical Prince Umayado from the later remembered figure of "Shōtoku Taishi," and explain how and why the later image developed.
  3. Explain the purpose and limits of the Twelve Cap Ranks of 603 without describing them as a modern meritocratic civil service.
  4. Analyze the Seventeen-Article text of 604 as a statement of political and moral principle rather than a constitution in the modern institutional sense.
  5. Trace the growth of Buddhist patronage at the Suiko court and connect it to claims of political and moral authority.
  6. Explain why renewed contact with Sui China mattered strategically, culturally, and diplomatically for the Yamato court.
  7. Evaluate the evidence for the diplomatic missions of 600 and 607, including the famous "sun rises / sun sets" letter, with appropriate source criticism.
  8. Distinguish ceremonial, ideological, and diplomatic forms of centralization from administrative and territorial centralization.
  9. Assess the achievements and limits of Suiko-era statecraft as precedents for, rather than examples of, the Taika-era state.
  10. Identify the major primary sources for this period and explain the interpretive challenges each presents.
  11. Avoid anachronistic descriptions of Suiko-era Japan as a bureaucratic or fully centralized state.
  12. Connect this chapter's court experiments to the institutional transformations examined in Chapter 13.

Historical Context

Chapter 11 traced the conflict between the Soga and Mononobe lineages over Buddhism's place at the Yamato court, a conflict that culminated in 587 with the military defeat and death of Mononobe no Moriya, the collapse of Mononobe dominance at the central court, and the consolidation of Soga dominance under Soga no Umako. That same chapter examined the violent and contested succession that followed, in which a series of short-lived rulers gave way, in 592, to the enthronement of Suiko, a royal woman closely connected to the Soga lineage through her mother, as ruler of Yamato. Chapter 11 was careful to reject two oversimplified readings of this outcome: the idea that Umako now simply ruled through a puppet queen, and the idea that Suiko's kingship was merely ceremonial while real power lay entirely elsewhere. This chapter continues from that more balanced starting point.

It is essential to remember, as Chapters 8 and 9 established, that Yamato in this period was not a centralized territorial state with uniform administration extending outward from a fixed capital. It was instead a shifting alliance of powerful lineages, or uji, each controlling its own dependent populations, land, and ritual prerogatives, bound together by a royal lineage that claimed preeminent status and ritual authority but that governed as much through negotiation, marriage alliance, and religious legitimacy as through direct command. Continental contact — with Baekje, Silla, Goguryeo, and successive Chinese states, examined in Chapter 9 — had already brought writing, craft technology, and administrative ideas into this world well before Suiko's reign, and Buddhism, introduced formally in the mid-sixth century and examined in Chapter 10, had already become entangled in elite politics by the time of the Soga–Mononobe conflict.

The broader East Asian setting also shifted dramatically in these same decades. After a long era of recurrent political fragmentation following the fall of the Han, interrupted by the brief Western Jin reunification around 280 and its subsequent collapse, the Sui dynasty reunified China in 589, reestablishing a single powerful imperial court at the center of the East Asian diplomatic world. This reunification changed the calculations of every neighboring polity, including the states of the Korean peninsula and Yamato itself. A reunified, assertive continental empire made direct diplomatic engagement newly urgent, newly risky, and newly prestigious — a dynamic this chapter explores in detail in its later sections.

Against this background, the Suiko court's initiatives of the early seventh century should be understood neither as an isolated flowering of exceptional genius nor as empty gestures without consequence. They were the responses of a lineage-based aristocratic court, newly reconstituted after violent conflict, to a set of genuine pressures: the need to order relations among elite houses more predictably, the desire to draw on Buddhist and continental models of legitimate rulership, and the strategic and cultural imperative to engage directly with a newly reunified Chinese empire. The chapters that follow — Chapter 13 on the Taika reforms and the emergence of a genuinely centralized imperial state — will show how these early seventh-century experiments were built upon, transformed, and in important respects superseded by later, more consequential institutional change.

Major People, Institutions, and Features

Reference — People, Institutions & Concepts

Suiko
Ruler of Yamato from 592 to 628, traditionally regarded as the first securely attested reigning queen of the Yamato royal lineage; a daughter of an earlier ruler and closely connected to the Soga lineage through her mother, she presided over the court initiatives examined in this chapter and should be understood as an active participant in, not merely a ceremonial figurehead for, the period's statecraft.
Soga no Umako
Head of the Soga lineage and Suiko's maternal uncle, the dominant aristocratic figure at court following the collapse of Mononobe dominance in 587, examined in Chapter 11; continued as the single most powerful lineage leader throughout most of Suiko's reign until his death in 626.
Prince Umayado (later remembered as Shōtoku Taishi)
A royal prince closely associated with Suiko and Umako, traditionally credited with central authorship of the Twelve Cap Ranks, the Seventeen-Article text, and much of the court's Buddhist patronage and continental diplomacy; the historical figure and the later idealized "Shōtoku Taishi" of Buddhist and national tradition must be carefully distinguished, as Section 12.2 explains in detail.
Kan'i Jūnikai (Twelve Cap Ranks)
A system of court rank established in 603, distinguishing twelve grades of official status through cap colors associated with Confucian-derived virtue terms, operating alongside, rather than replacing, hereditary lineage (uji-kabane) status.
The Seventeen-Article text (traditionally called the "Seventeen-Article Constitution")
A set of moral and political principles promulgated in 604, addressing harmony, proper official conduct, hierarchy, and the ruler's authority, drawing on Confucian, Buddhist, and continental Legalist political ideas; not a constitution establishing governing institutions in the modern sense.
Ono no Imoko
The Yamato envoy who led the major diplomatic mission to the Sui court in 607, carrying the letter invoking the "land where the sun rises" and the "land where the sun sets," discussed in Sections 12.7 and 12.8.
Sui dynasty
The Chinese imperial dynasty that reunified China in 589 after centuries of division, ruling until 618; its reassertion of centralized continental power reshaped the diplomatic environment in which Yamato operated during Suiko's reign.
Sui shu (Book of Sui)
The official Chinese dynastic history of the Sui period, compiled under the succeeding Tang dynasty; an important independent source for the Yamato missions of 600 and 607, offering a Chinese court perspective that both complements and complicates the Japanese chronicle account.
Nihon Shoki
The court chronicle completed in 720, the principal Japanese narrative source for Suiko's reign; compiled roughly a century after the events it describes, under the direction of the eighth-century imperial court, whose interests shaped its presentation of earlier rulers and reformers.
Kojiki
An earlier chronicle, completed in 712, covering genealogy and royal succession in a somewhat different register from the Nihon Shoki; a secondary but still valuable source for this period.
Hōryūji
A major Buddhist temple complex associated with Prince Umayado, traditionally dated to 607, containing some of the world's oldest surviving wooden structures; its early building history and precise relationship to the historical prince remain subjects of ongoing architectural and archaeological research.
Uji-kabane system
The inherited system of lineage-based elite status, introduced in earlier chapters, that continued to structure Yamato aristocratic society throughout the Suiko period even as the cap-rank system was introduced alongside it.

Section 12.1

The Suiko Court After 592

Chapter 11 closed with the collapse of Mononobe dominance at the central court in 587 and a subsequent, contested succession that saw several rulers occupy the Yamato throne in rapid and sometimes violent succession before Suiko's enthronement in 592. This was not a peaceful, orderly transfer of power in any modern sense. It was the outcome of a lineage-based political struggle in which the Soga house, under Umako's leadership, had emerged as the dominant force at court, but dominance did not mean uncontested control. Other lineages retained land, dependents, ritual prerogatives, and, in some cases, armed followers of their own. Suiko's enthronement represented a resolution of the immediate succession crisis, not the disappearance of the underlying competitive structure of Yamato aristocratic politics.

Suiko's own position combined several sources of legitimacy. She was a daughter of an earlier ruler, giving her direct royal descent; she was connected to the Soga lineage through her mother, giving her a strong relationship with the court's most powerful house; and she had, by the time of her enthronement, considerable personal experience of court life and ritual, having served earlier as a royal consort. These combined qualifications made her a plausible compromise choice for a court seeking stability after violent conflict — not, as some older popular accounts imply, merely a convenient figurehead selected because a woman's rule was assumed to be nominal. The evidence instead suggests that Suiko actively presided over court audiences, religious observances, and diplomatic exchanges throughout her reign, and that decisions of state were understood to require her participation and, ultimately, her authority as sovereign.

The central political problem facing this reconstituted court was not merely who would rule, but how a court built from competing aristocratic lineages could govern more predictably and coherently going forward. The crisis of 587–592 had demonstrated, in the starkest possible terms, how quickly religious controversy and succession disputes could escalate into open warfare among elite houses. A court that wished to avoid repeating that experience had good reason to experiment with new tools: clearer markers of status and service that did not depend entirely on lineage alone, statements of shared political and moral principle that could bind officials of different houses to common standards of conduct, and closer engagement with continental models of ordered, prestigious rulership. The initiatives examined in the remainder of this chapter — cap ranks, the Seventeen-Article text, expanded Buddhist patronage, and renewed diplomacy with Sui China — should be understood as responses to precisely this problem, developed jointly by Suiko, Umako, and Umayado rather than imposed by any single actor upon a passive court.

Section 12.2

Prince Umayado and the Problem of "Prince Shōtoku"

Few figures in early Japanese history present historians with a sharper contrast between documented fact and accumulated legend than the prince conventionally known today as Shōtoku Taishi — "Crown Prince of Sacred Virtue." This section addresses that contrast directly, because understanding it correctly shapes how every subsequent section of this chapter should be read.

The historical figure is most cautiously referred to by his personal name, Umayado, a royal prince, son of an earlier Yamato ruler and thus a member of the royal lineage, who was closely associated with both Suiko and Umako from early in Suiko's reign. The Nihon Shoki credits him with a position of central importance in court affairs beginning around 593, associates him with the promulgation of the cap-rank system and the Seventeen-Article text, and describes him as a patron of Buddhist temple-building, most famously Hōryūji. He died in 622, six years before Suiko herself.

These claims are plausible in general outline: a royal prince closely tied to the dominant Soga lineage, playing an important role in court deliberations and Buddhist patronage during this period, is consistent with everything else historians know about how Yamato court politics operated. But plausibility in general outline is not the same as secure documentation of specific acts, words, or motivations, and this is precisely where the later legend has done the most damage to careful historical understanding. Over the century between Umayado's death in 622 and the completion of the Nihon Shoki in 720, and continuing for centuries afterward through Buddhist hagiography, temple foundation legends, and later imperial ideology, the prince's reputation grew dramatically. Later tradition credited him with author­ship of Buddhist commentaries, with miraculous abilities including simultaneously hearing the petitions of ten or more people, with prophetic foresight, and with a comprehensive vision of centralized, virtuous government that anticipated reforms actually carried out by later generations. By the medieval period, Shōtoku Taishi had become an object of religious devotion in his own right, venerated in some Buddhist traditions as a bodhisattva or as a founding patriarch of Japanese Buddhism, his image reproduced in temple statuary and popular religious art across the country.

This accumulated tradition did not develop randomly. Buddhist institutions had strong reasons to magnify the reputation of a royal patron who could lend prestige and legitimacy to their own foundation stories. The eighth-century imperial court that commissioned the Nihon Shoki had reasons of its own to present a heroic royal ancestor as the intellectual forerunner of the centralized institutions that court had itself, by 720, largely completed — a narrative that made the eighth-century state appear as the natural fulfillment of a wise prince's early seventh-century vision, rather than the product of later, often violent, political struggle examined in Chapter 13. Later political and religious movements, including nationalist historiography in the modern period, found further uses for an idealized founding statesman whose image could be adapted to a wide range of later purposes.

Responsible historical practice does not require discarding the tradition entirely, nor does it require treating every element of the later Shōtoku legend as equally suspect. It requires, instead, distinguishing carefully among different kinds of claims: claims recorded relatively close to the events themselves and consistent with independent evidence, such as Chinese diplomatic records, deserve more confidence than claims that appear only in much later hagiography; claims about specific political acts, such as association with the 603 and 604 initiatives, are more securely grounded than claims about miraculous personal qualities; and claims that serve an obvious later institutional or religious interest deserve particular scrutiny. Throughout the remainder of this chapter, the name "Umayado" is used when referring to the historical prince and the documented political role attributed to him in relatively early sources, while "Shōtoku Taishi" or "the Shōtoku legend" is used when referring specifically to the later, more elaborated cultural and religious figure. Students should treat this distinction as a recurring historical-method lesson, not a pedantic naming convention.

Section 12.3

Suiko, Umako, and Shared Court Government

Popular retellings of this period frequently ask, implicitly or explicitly, "who really ruled Yamato during Suiko's reign?" — as though authority must have rested securely with a single individual, whether Suiko, Umako, or Umayado, with the other two serving merely as supporting figures. This framing, this textbook argues, asks the wrong question. The more historically useful question is how authority was distributed and exercised among these three figures and the wider court around them, and what that distribution reveals about the nature of Yamato government in this period.

Suiko held the position that mattered most for legitimacy within Japanese court tradition: she was the reigning sovereign, the figure through whom royal ritual authority, court audiences, and formal diplomatic communications with foreign courts were, according to that tradition, channeled. Here the evidence requires particular caution, since it does not point in a single, tidy direction. The Sui shu's account of the 600 mission, discussed further in Section 12.8, does not describe a reigning queen at all: it describes a Wa ruler referred to as Tarishihiko, said to have a wife and a crown prince — a picture that does not map neatly onto the Japanese chronicle tradition placing Suiko on the throne at exactly this time. This discrepancy does not disprove Suiko's reign, which is independently and consistently attested in Japanese sources, but it does mean that the Chinese account cannot be used to corroborate her sovereignty directly, and it is a reminder that the two textual traditions describing this period do not always align. What can be said with more confidence is that, within the Japanese chronicle record, Suiko is consistently presented as presiding over court audiences, religious observances, and major decisions throughout her reign, and there is no securely documented case in which major court decisions were carried out in open defiance of her wishes.

Umako, for his part, controlled resources that no amount of ritual prestige alone could match: the landholdings, dependent populations, and armed followers of the Soga lineage, together with extensive personal wealth built up over decades at the center of Yamato politics. His position after 587 was, in practical terms, unrivaled among the court's aristocratic houses, and his continued influence throughout Suiko's reign — evidenced by his prominent role in court affairs recorded in the Nihon Shoki right up to his death in 626 — meant that no significant court initiative was likely to proceed without at least his acquiescence, and probably his active support.

Umayado occupied a distinctive third position: a member of the royal lineage himself, with a claim to eventual succession that was never in fact realized, closely allied with both Suiko and the Soga house through kinship and shared political interest, and increasingly associated — whether primarily through his own initiative or through collaborative court deliberation that later tradition simplified into individual authorship — with the specific policy initiatives of 603 and 604 and with expanding Buddhist patronage. His position combined royal prestige, personal reputation for learning and piety, and close alliance with Soga power, without the independent material base that made Umako's position so formidable or the sovereign ritual authority that Suiko alone possessed.

Rather than asking which of the three "really" held power, this textbook asks students to understand Suiko-era government as a plausible, if informal, coalition: a sovereign queen providing legitimacy and formal authority, a dominant lineage head providing material power and political muscle, and a royal prince providing intellectual and religious initiative closely tied to both. The specific initiatives examined in the sections that follow likely emerged from some process of deliberation among court figures, but the surviving sources — compiled roughly a century later by chroniclers working within their own narrative and institutional interests — do not allow historians to reconstruct that process, or the precise individual contributions of Suiko, Umako, Umayado, and other unnamed officials, with real confidence. Later tradition's tendency to credit Umayado alone with visionary authorship of the period's reforms should be understood as a simplification — one with real historiographical consequences, since it obscures both Suiko's active sovereignty and Umako's continuing centrality to how anything actually got done at the Yamato court, even though the precise mechanics of decision-making at this court cannot be securely recovered.

Section 12.4

The Twelve Cap Ranks, 603

In 603, the Suiko court established a new system of personal court rank known as the Kan'i Jūnikai, or Twelve Cap Ranks. The system organized officials into six grades, each named for a Confucian-derived virtue term — commonly rendered in English as Virtue, Humaneness, Propriety, Sincerity, Justice, and Knowledge — and associated with a corresponding cap color; each of the six color-and-virtue grades was then further divided into a "greater" and "lesser" rank, distinguished by shade, producing twelve ranks in total rather than twelve entirely distinct colors. An official's cap rank was, at least in principle, displayed visibly at court, marking status in a way that could be recognized immediately in court ritual and audience.

Understanding what this system did and did not accomplish requires care. It is tempting, especially for students encountering the system for the first time, to describe it as an early meritocratic civil service — a mechanism through which talented individuals, regardless of birth, could rise through demonstrated ability and service to the state. This description significantly overstates what the evidence supports and should be avoided. The cap-rank system operated alongside, not in place of, the older uji-kabane system of hereditary lineage status that had structured Yamato aristocratic society for generations. Access to cap rank in practice remained heavily concentrated among members of established elite lineages; there is no securely documented case from this period of an individual of genuinely low or non-elite birth rising to prominent cap rank purely through demonstrated merit, and the structural conditions of Yamato society — in which land, dependents, and political influence remained concentrated in hereditary lineage hands — make such social mobility inherently unlikely regardless of the formal rules governing rank.

What the system more plausibly accomplished was something narrower but still significant: it gave the court a new tool for distinguishing individual standing and service within the existing aristocratic order, a tool that operated at the level of the person rather than automatically following lineage membership alone. A cap rank could, at least in principle, be granted, withheld, or elevated by the ruler in ways that lineage status itself could not easily be altered, giving Suiko's court a limited but real mechanism for recognizing individual service, rewarding loyalty, and structuring court hierarchy and precedence more explicitly than lineage status alone allowed. It also drew visibly on continental — ultimately Chinese and, through Chinese influence, Korean peninsular — models of court rank and official hierarchy, situating Yamato court practice within a broader East Asian vocabulary of ordered rulership, a theme this chapter returns to in Section 12.9.

Students should therefore understand the Twelve Cap Ranks as an early court-ordering experiment operating within, and dependent upon, the continuing dominance of hereditary lineage politics — not as evidence that Yamato had begun dismantling that dominance. The genuinely far-reaching reorganization of elite status, land tenure, and administrative service belonged to the Taika-era reforms examined in Chapter 13, which built upon, but went considerably beyond, what the 603 system had attempted.

Section 12.5

The Seventeen-Article Text, 604

The following year, 604, the court promulgated a set of seventeen articles addressing the conduct expected of officials and, more broadly, the moral and political principles that should govern the court. This document has long been known in English by the misleading title "the Seventeen-Article Constitution," a translation this textbook deliberately avoids using without qualification, because the word "constitution" carries modern institutional connotations — a foundational legal document establishing governmental structures, defining offices, allocating powers, and constraining rulers — that do not accurately describe what this text actually is or does.

Read on its own terms, the Seventeen-Article text is better understood as a statement of political and moral exhortation: a set of principles, addressed primarily to officials, emphasizing harmony and the avoidance of factional conflict; respect for hierarchy and proper deference toward superiors; diligence, fairness, and freedom from bribery in the conduct of official duties; and reverence for the ruler's authority, described in the text using language that draws on both Confucian political philosophy — with its emphasis on hierarchical social harmony and the moral cultivation of officials — and Buddhist principles, reflecting the same court context of intensifying Buddhist patronage examined in Section 12.6. Some articles also draw on ideas associated with earlier Chinese Legalist statecraft, particularly in their concern for consistent, impartially applied standards of official conduct. The text's famous first article, urging that "harmony is to be valued" and that officials should avoid factional discord, speaks directly and pointedly to the very problem this chapter has identified as central to the post-587 Yamato court: the danger that competition among powerful lineages could again escalate into open conflict.

What the text does not do is establish governing institutions, define specific offices and their powers, allocate territorial administration, or create any of the machinery of taxation, provincial government, or codified law that would later characterize the Taika and ritsuryō state examined in Chapter 13. It is prescriptive and hortatory rather than institutional and structural. Students should therefore ask, as this section's framing question suggests, not "did the Seventeen-Article text create a new government?" — it did not — but rather "what kind of political and moral order does this document imagine, and how does that vision relate to the political realities of the Suiko court?" The text imagines a court unified by shared moral standards and deference to a single ruling authority, standing in implicit contrast to the fractious, lineage-driven conflict that had so recently brought Yamato to civil war. Whether court officials in practice lived up to these standards, or whether the text functioned mainly as an aspirational statement with limited enforceable effect, is a question the surviving evidence cannot definitively answer — a point examined further in the Historical Debate feature later in this chapter.

Traditional attribution credits Umayado with sole authorship of the Seventeen-Article text, an attribution that later hagiography treated as further evidence of his singular wisdom. Given this chapter's broader caution about the later Shōtoku legend, students should treat this attribution as plausible but not certain in its details: the text may reflect a broader process of court deliberation involving Suiko, Umako, and other officials, but the surviving sources do not allow that process, or Umayado's precise contribution to it, to be reconstructed securely.

Section 12.6

Buddhism and the Political Language of the Court

Chapters 10 and 11 traced Buddhism's difficult introduction into Yamato and the way religious controversy became entangled with the succession and lineage conflicts of the 580s. By Suiko's reign, the central question was no longer whether Buddhism could survive at court — Moriya's defeat had removed the strongest organized court-level opponent of expanded Buddhist patronage and created far more favorable conditions for its growth — but rather how that patronage would be woven into the court's evolving language of legitimate, ordered rulership.

This period saw a marked expansion in elite Buddhist patronage: temple construction proceeded on a larger scale than before, court-sponsored Buddhist ritual became a more regular feature of political life, and Buddhist learning — including doctrine, ritual practice, and associated continental knowledge such as calendrical calculation, medicine, and craft techniques imported alongside Buddhist teachers and texts — became an increasingly valued form of elite cultural capital. Hōryūji, traditionally dated to 607 and closely associated with Umayado, exemplifies this expanding patronage. Students should distinguish that traditional 607 foundation date from the history of the surviving physical complex: the original temple buildings were destroyed by fire in 670, and the major structures standing at the site today — among the oldest surviving wooden buildings in the world — were rebuilt in the decades that followed, dating from the late seventh and early eighth centuries rather than directly from Umayado's own lifetime. The site nevertheless later became closely bound up with the Shōtoku legend discussed in Section 12.2, and its early foundation and rebuilding history remain active subjects of architectural and archaeological research.

The connection between Buddhist patronage and political authority in this period should be understood carefully. Buddhist teaching offered the Yamato court access to a sophisticated continental vocabulary of moral and cosmological order — ideas about compassionate, virtuous rulership, about the ruler's responsibility for the spiritual as well as material welfare of subjects, and about the prestige that flowed from association with a religious tradition already deeply established in Baekje, Silla, and China. Patronizing Buddhist institutions on a visible scale allowed the court, and particularly figures like Umako and Umayado, to claim moral and cultural authority that reinforced, without replacing, the older forms of authority rooted in lineage status and ritual connection to kami worship. Buddhist and kami-centered ritual practices coexisted at the Suiko court rather than one displacing the other, a pattern of accommodation, examined more fully in Chapter 47's later treatment of religion under different political conditions, that would remain characteristic of Japanese religious life for centuries.

It is important, in discussing this expanding patronage, not to let Umayado's later reputation as Japan's foundational Buddhist statesman crowd out the roles of Suiko and Umako. The Nihon Shoki records Suiko herself sponsoring Buddhist ritual and temple-building in her own right, and the Soga lineage's association with Buddhist patronage, established already in the events examined in Chapter 10, continued and intensified under Umako's leadership throughout this period. Umayado's Buddhist reputation, however genuine its early seventh-century foundations, was built upon a base of court-wide patronage in which he was an important, but not the sole, participant — a pattern this chapter has already identified as characteristic of Suiko-era statecraft more broadly.

Section 12.7

Looking Outward: Why Yamato Needed Continental Diplomacy

Chapter 9 established that Yamato's relationships with Baekje, Silla, Goguryeo, and successive Chinese states had long shaped the archipelago's access to writing, craft technology, administrative ideas, and Buddhist teaching. By the early seventh century, the strategic and cultural stakes of continental engagement had risen sharply, for reasons rooted directly in developments on the Asian mainland.

The Sui dynasty's reunification of China in 589 ended nearly four centuries of political division dating back to the fall of the Han dynasty. A reunified Chinese empire, commanding vastly greater resources, military capacity, and administrative sophistication than any of the divided regional states that had preceded it, immediately became the dominant force in East Asian diplomacy, and its relationships with neighboring polities — including Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla on the Korean peninsula, as well as more distant states such as Yamato — took on new urgency. For the peninsular kingdoms, Sui power represented both an opportunity for alliance against regional rivals and a genuine military threat, one that would culminate in a series of costly Sui campaigns against Goguryeo in the early seventh century. For Yamato, more distant from direct Sui military pressure but deeply enmeshed in peninsular diplomatic and military affairs through its long-standing relationship with Baekje, examined in Chapter 9, the reunification of China raised urgent questions about how best to engage with, learn from, and position itself relative to this newly dominant continental power.

Direct diplomatic engagement with China offered the Yamato court several distinct kinds of value, beyond the immediate strategic calculations surrounding the Korean peninsula. It offered access to continental administrative, legal, and calendrical knowledge that Yamato's own institutions, still built largely around lineage-based governance, notably lacked. It offered access to Buddhist teaching and texts at their most prestigious continental source, reinforcing the court's expanding Buddhist patronage examined in Section 12.6. And it offered a form of prestige in its own right: participation in the East Asian diplomatic order centered on the Chinese court had long been understood, across the region, as a marker of a polity's standing and legitimacy — a dynamic already visible in the third-century Wei dynastic history's account of Himiko's diplomatic missions, examined in Chapter 6, and now renewed under very different political circumstances nearly four centuries later.

It is against this background that the Yamato court's resumption of direct missions to the Chinese imperial court, examined in the next section, should be understood — not as an isolated diplomatic curiosity, but as a calculated response to a transformed continental environment, pursued by a court that had, through the initiatives examined in Sections 12.4 through 12.6, already begun experimenting with new tools of order and legitimacy at home.

Section 12.8

Missions to Sui China

The Chinese dynastic history of the Sui period, the Sui shu, records a mission from the ruler of Wa arriving at the Sui court around 600, describing the Yamato ruler — named in the account as Tarishihiko, and said to have a wife and a crown prince — and certain features of court governance and custom that broadly, though not perfectly, align with what other evidence suggests about Yamato society in this period. This mission is not mentioned in the Nihon Shoki, a discrepancy that has attracted considerable scholarly discussion: possible explanations include deliberate omission by the eighth-century chroniclers, perhaps because the mission's outcome, its description of a male ruler where Japanese tradition places Suiko on the throne, or the Sui shu's description of Yamato customs was considered unflattering or inconsistent with the image the Nihon Shoki's compilers wished to present, or simple loss of record over the intervening century. Whatever the explanation, this discrepancy is itself an important lesson in source criticism: a significant historical event, independently documented by one credible source, can be entirely absent from another source compiled for different purposes and audiences, and historians must weigh such gaps carefully rather than assuming that either source's silence or testimony is automatically decisive.

The 600 account presents a second, more technical puzzle directly relevant to Section 12.4. The Sui shu's description of Yamato court practice at this early date already includes a system of twelve court ranks organized around the same six paired virtue terms later associated with the Kan'i Jūnikai of 603. This creates a genuine chronological problem that historians have not resolved with certainty: either the Chinese account reflects information gathered somewhat later than the mission's nominal 600 date and folded back into the record of that mission, or the rank system described to Sui informants had already existed in some earlier or provisional form before its formal promulgation in the Japanese chronicle's 603 entry, or the relationship between the two textual traditions' chronologies is more complicated than either "created in 600" or "created in 603" can capture on its own. Students should treat the 603 date, like most specific dates in this chapter, as the Japanese chronicle tradition's own account of when the system was formally established, rather than as a fact independently and precisely confirmed by the Chinese source.

The mission of 607, led by the envoy Ono no Imoko, is considerably better documented and has become, deservedly, the most famous diplomatic episode of Suiko's reign. Both the Nihon Shoki and the Sui shu record this mission, though with differing emphases and, on some points, differing details, allowing historians an unusually direct opportunity to compare a Yamato source against an independent continental account of the same event. The letter Imoko is said to have carried, addressed from the ruler of Wa to the Sui emperor, is most famously remembered for its opening invocation of the "son of heaven in the land where the sun rises" addressing the "son of heaven in the land where the sun sets" — a formulation preserved in the Sui shu rather than the Nihon Shoki, which gives a different account of the letter's content.

Historians have long debated what this striking language actually claimed and how the Sui court received it. The Sui shu itself reports that the Sui emperor found the letter's phrasing displeasing, instructing his officials that a document framed in such terms, addressed by a peripheral ruler to the Sui emperor, should not be brought to his attention again in that form — testimony that, if accurately reported, suggests Sui officials read the letter's language as an inappropriate claim to a status uncomfortably close to equality with the Chinese emperor, rather than as the conventionally deferential language expected from a tributary ruler. This reaction, whether or not fully or precisely recorded, cautions strongly against reading the letter as straightforward proof that Yamato had achieved, or even fully intended to assert, anything resembling modern sovereign equality with China. The political and cosmological vocabulary of "son of heaven," deeply rooted in Chinese imperial ideology, may have carried different connotations, or been deployed with different specific intentions, at the Yamato court than at the Sui court receiving it — a genuine interpretive gap that the surviving evidence does not allow historians to close with confidence. What the episode does demonstrate clearly is that the Yamato court, whatever its precise intentions, was willing to frame its communications with China in unusually assertive terms for a relatively distant and, from a Sui perspective, minor polity — a striking indication of the court's own developing sense of its ruler's dignity and standing, even if that self-conception exceeded what Sui China was prepared to grant it.

Despite this friction, the diplomatic relationship continued. A Sui mission arrived at Yamato the following year, 608, and a further Yamato mission returned to China accompanied not only by diplomatic envoys but by Japanese students and Buddhist monks sent specifically to study continental learning — an important and often underemphasized dimension of these exchanges, since it shows the relationship functioning as a channel for the transmission of knowledge and technique, not merely as a sequence of formal diplomatic gestures. Some of these students would spend many years in China, returning with administrative, legal, and Buddhist learning that later became available to Yamato court figures. Two of the students sent abroad in 608, Takamuko no Kuromaro and the monk Min, exemplify this connection concretely: Min returned in 632 and Takamuko in 640, and both were subsequently appointed to advisory positions (kunihakase) in the reform government established after 645, examined in Chapter 13. Their careers show a documented, if limited, channel between the diplomatic openings of this chapter and the institutional reforms that followed, even though the experience of most other students and monks who traveled abroad in this period cannot be traced with comparable precision.

Section 12.9

What Did "Centralization" Mean in the Early Seventh Century?

Having examined rank, moral-political text, Buddhist patronage, and diplomacy individually, this section asks students to consider them together and to answer directly a question this chapter has raised repeatedly: in what sense, if any, did the Suiko court move toward "centralized" government?

The word "centralization" can describe several quite different historical processes, and careful historians distinguish among them rather than treating the term as a single, uniform measure of political development. A stronger and more explicit court hierarchy — the kind the Twelve Cap Ranks attempted to establish — is not the same thing as a functioning bureaucracy staffed by officials appointed and removable according to consistent administrative rules; Suiko-era Yamato achieved something closer to the former than the latter, since cap rank operated as an overlay upon, rather than a replacement for, hereditary lineage power. Enhanced royal prestige and ceremonial authority — visible in Suiko's active role in court ritual, in the moral language of the Seventeen-Article text exalting the ruler's position, and in the assertive tone of the 607 diplomatic letter — is not the same thing as direct territorial administration reaching into every province and district; Yamato in this period still governed most of the archipelago indirectly, through the cooperation and self-interest of local elite lineages, rather than through appointed provincial officials answerable to the center in the manner Chapter 13 will describe for the post-Taika state. Imported political models, drawn selectively from Confucian, Buddhist, and Legalist continental sources, are not the same thing as the complete implementation of those models in actual governing practice; the Seventeen-Article text's vision of harmonious, hierarchically ordered court conduct was an aspiration articulated in continental philosophical language, not a description of Yamato political reality as it actually functioned, which continued to be shaped by lineage rivalry, negotiated authority, and locally rooted power that the text itself, in its opening plea for harmony, implicitly acknowledges as a persistent problem.

Understood this way, the Suiko court's achievement was real but specific: it developed a considerably richer vocabulary, set of symbols, and set of diplomatic postures through which centralized, ordered, and morally legitimated rulership could be imagined and articulated, well before the administrative and territorial machinery necessary to make that vision a governing reality had been built. Cap ranks gave the court a new language of individual status; the Seventeen-Article text gave it a new language of official virtue and hierarchical obedience; Buddhist patronage gave it a new language of moral and cosmological legitimacy; and the Sui missions gave it a new, internationally recognized diplomatic voice, however imperfectly that voice was received abroad. None of these initiatives redistributed land, established formal taxation, appointed provincial governors answerable to the center, or codified law in the manner of the reforms Chapter 13 will examine. What they did was prepare the ideological and symbolic ground on which those later, considerably more consequential institutional reforms could be built — and could be understood, by the court that carried them out, as a natural extension of principles the Suiko court had already begun to articulate.

Section 12.10

The Limits and Legacy of Suiko-Era Statecraft

The years following Umayado's death in 622 and Umako's death in 626 reveal how much the underlying structure of lineage-based Yamato politics had, in fact, persisted beneath the court's new rank system, moral-political rhetoric, and diplomatic engagement. Succession politics within the Soga lineage itself, and between the Soga house and other aristocratic and royal claimants, grew increasingly tense in Suiko's final years and erupted into open conflict not long after her death in 628 — conflict that Chapter 13 will show escalating dramatically in the following decades, culminating in the violent overthrow of Soga dominance in 645 and the beginning of the Taika reforms. This trajectory demonstrates clearly that the initiatives examined in this chapter, whatever their symbolic and institutional significance, had not resolved the fundamental competitive dynamics of Yamato aristocratic politics; they had, at most, provided a temporary framework of shared court language and ritual practice within which that competition continued.

At the same time, it would be equally mistaken to dismiss Suiko-era statecraft as inconsequential simply because it did not yet produce a centralized administrative state. The Twelve Cap Ranks established a precedent for personal, ruler-granted status that the Taika-era reformers would draw upon and considerably expand. The Seventeen-Article text's language of hierarchy, harmony, and official virtue anticipated themes that would recur explicitly in the Taika-era edicts and, later, in the ritsuryō law codes examined in Chapter 13. The generation of students and monks sent to study in Sui and, subsequently, Tang China returned with administrative and legal knowledge that later reformers had available to them, and later tradition credits at least some participants in the Taika-era reforms with continental study connected to missions of this kind, though the precise chain of personal influence between any specific returning student and any specific later reform cannot always be traced with confidence from the surviving sources. And the expanded Buddhist patronage of Suiko's reign established institutions, architectural traditions, and a pattern of court-sponsored Buddhism that would continue to shape Japanese political and religious life for centuries, examined further in Chapters 14 and 15.

Students should therefore close this chapter holding two conclusions together rather than choosing between them. The Suiko court did not create a centralized imperial state; hereditary lineage power, negotiated authority among competing houses, and limited direct administrative reach remained the basic realities of Yamato government throughout this period, and the succession violence that followed Suiko's death demonstrates how little the underlying competitive structure had actually changed. At the same time, the court's experiments in rank, moral-political principle, Buddhist patronage, and continental diplomacy were genuine and historically significant precedents — a body of new political vocabulary, symbol, and international engagement that provided intellectual resources and, in some cases, continentally trained personnel later available to the reformers examined in Chapter 13, whose own institutional transformation would prove far more consequential.

Historical Vocabulary
Kan'i Jūnikai

The Kan'i Jūnikai, or "Twelve Level Cap and Rank System," established in 603, assigned court officials one of twelve personal ranks, organized into six virtue-linked color groups — each corresponding to one of six Confucian-derived virtue terms — with a "greater" and "lesser" grade distinguished by shade within each color group, producing twelve ranks in total. Unlike hereditary lineage status, cap rank was, in principle, granted by the ruler to an individual rather than inherited automatically through birth into a particular uji.

Students should resist describing this system as a meritocratic civil service. In practice, access to rank remained concentrated among members of established elite lineages, and the system operated alongside, not instead of, the older uji-kabane structure of hereditary status. Its significance lies in providing the court with a new, individually focused tool for recognizing service and structuring precedence — a modest but real innovation within a still overwhelmingly lineage-based political order.

Historical Vocabulary
"Wa" and "Sui"

Chinese sources of this period, including the Sui shu, continued to refer to Yamato and its rulers using the older name Wa, the same term Chapter 6 introduced in connection with third-century Chinese accounts of Himiko. Students should recognize that "Wa" in these sources refers to the Yamato polity as perceived and labeled from a Chinese diplomatic perspective, not to a self-applied "Japanese" national identity; the archipelago's rulers had not yet adopted the term "Nihon," which would come into more regular use only in the following century, examined in Chapter 13.

The Sui dynasty (581–618) reunified China in 589 after a long era of recurrent post-Han political fragmentation, briefly interrupted by the Western Jin reunification around 280, and reestablished a powerful, centralized continental empire at the heart of the East Asian diplomatic order. Its rise reshaped the strategic calculations of every neighboring polity, including Yamato, and its relatively short duration — it was overthrown and replaced by the Tang dynasty in 618 — meant that the diplomatic opening examined in this chapter would, within Suiko's own lifetime, need to be renewed with an entirely new Chinese imperial house.

Paraphrase of the Sui shu's report of the letter's opening and the emperor's reaction (not a verbatim translation of the quoted wording): The letter is said to have identified its sender as the sovereign of the land where the sun rises, addressing the Sui emperor as sovereign of the land where the sun sets, and the Sui emperor is reported to have found this framing displeasing, instructing his officials not to bring him further correspondence phrased in this manner.

Selected Articles of the Seventeen-Article Text

Creator: Traditionally attributed to Prince Umayado; preserved in the Nihon Shoki · Date: promulgated 604; recorded in a chronicle completed in 720 · Type: Court moral-political text

Context: The Seventeen-Article text survives only as transmitted within the Nihon Shoki, compiled roughly 116 years after its traditional date of promulgation. Paraphrase of themes from selected articles (not verbatim quotation): The opening article urges that harmony be valued above all and that factional discord among officials be avoided; other articles call for officials to act without envy, to avoid bribery and personal profit at the expense of duty, to defer properly to superiors, to judge disputes fairly, and to recognize the ruler's authority as the source of proper order at court.

Analysis: Read together, these themes describe an aspirational vision of a unified, hierarchically ordered court, free of the factional violence that had so recently convulsed Yamato politics. The text's repeated emphasis on avoiding factionalism and bribery suggests it was responding to real, perceived problems in court conduct, rather than describing an already-achieved harmony. Students should note that the text prescribes behavior; it does not establish the institutional machinery — courts, offices, enforcement mechanisms — that could reliably compel that behavior.

Historical Debate

How Historical Is the Prince Shōtoku of Later Tradition?

The question: How much of the later Shōtoku Taishi tradition reflects the genuine political role of the historical Prince Umayado, and how much reflects later Buddhist, court, and national elaboration constructed after his death?

Interpretation A — A substantial historical role, later embellished: Some historians argue that Umayado genuinely held a central and distinctive position in Suiko-era statecraft, closely associated with the cap-rank system, the Seventeen-Article text, and expanding Buddhist patronage, and that later tradition, while adding miraculous and hagiographic elements, did not invent his political importance from nothing — it exaggerated and idealized a role that was already substantial.
Interpretation B — A largely constructed composite figure: Other historians emphasize how heavily the Nihon Shoki's account of Umayado was shaped by eighth-century imperial and Buddhist institutional interests, and how much of the specific detail attributed to him — sole authorship of major texts, precise policy initiatives, miraculous qualities — cannot be independently verified against sources contemporary with his lifetime, suggesting that "Shōtoku Taishi" as commonly remembered is, in large part, a later composite figure assembled to serve later purposes.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same core sources — the Nihon Shoki, the Kojiki, later Buddhist hagiography, and the more limited independent Chinese record — but differ in how much interpretive weight they place on the gap between Umayado's death in 622 and the Nihon Shoki's completion in 720, and on how skeptically they read claims that align conveniently with later Buddhist or imperial interests.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how confidently later chapters, including Chapter 13's account of the Taika reforms, can describe intellectual continuity between Suiko-era statecraft and later institutional change, and shapes how cautiously students should treat popular accounts crediting a single visionary prince with foundational achievements.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports a substantial historical role for Umayado, and one that supports skepticism toward the fuller Shōtoku legend, and explain which standard of evidence you find more persuasive for evaluating a figure with this particular history of later religious and political elaboration.

Historical Debate

Did the Reforms of 603–604 Meaningfully Centralize Yamato Government?

The question: Should the Twelve Cap Ranks and the Seventeen-Article text be understood as substantial steps toward centralized government, as limited gestures within an overwhelmingly lineage-based order, or as something more specifically ideological and ceremonial that prepared the ground for later administrative centralization?

Interpretation A — Substantial political innovation: Some historians emphasize that the cap-rank system and the Seventeen-Article text represented genuinely new tools of court order — individually granted status and an explicit statement of hierarchical, harmony-based political principle — that had no close precedent in earlier Yamato governance, and that these tools meaningfully strengthened the ruler's position relative to the aristocratic lineages.
Interpretation B — Limited reform within a lineage-based order: Other historians stress that both initiatives operated entirely within, and dependent upon, the continuing dominance of the uji-kabane system, that no redistribution of land or administrative authority accompanied them, and that the succession violence following Suiko's death demonstrates how little the underlying structure of aristocratic competition had actually changed.
Interpretation C — Ideological and ceremonial centralization preceding administrative centralization: A third position, and the one this chapter favors, holds that the 603–604 reforms are best understood as developing a new vocabulary and symbolic apparatus for centralized, legitimate rulership — without yet building the administrative and territorial machinery to realize it — and that this ideological groundwork mattered precisely because it provided vocabulary, precedent, and, in some documented cases, continentally trained personnel later available to the Taika-era reformers of Chapter 13.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same body of evidence — the cap-rank and Seventeen-Article texts themselves, the continuing prominence of lineage politics throughout Suiko's reign, and the succession conflict following her death — but differ in how much weight they assign to symbolic and ideological change as opposed to concrete institutional change.

Why it matters: This question directly shapes how students should understand the relationship between this chapter and Chapter 13: whether the Taika reforms represent a sharp break from Suiko-era statecraft, a direct continuation of it, or — as this textbook argues — a much more consequential institutional realization of ideas the Suiko court had already begun to articulate.

Student task: Using specific evidence from this chapter, argue for one of the three interpretations above, or propose your own synthesis, and explain what kind of additional evidence would most help historians resolve the question with greater confidence.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Inside the Suiko Court's Experiments

Note: because the surviving sources for this period were compiled generations after the events they describe, the perspectives below describe categories of experience reasonably inferred from the court's documented initiatives, not verified first-person testimony from named individuals other than those the chronicles directly record.

Suiko as reigning sovereign: Presiding over court ritual, diplomatic audiences, and the promulgation of new court initiatives, Suiko's position depended on holding together the legitimacy of royal descent, a working alliance with the dominant Soga lineage, and the practical cooperation of a court still organized around competing aristocratic houses.
A lesser official newly granted cap rank: For an official outside the uppermost tier of long-established lineages, a granted cap rank may have offered a rare, individually recognized form of status and court precedence — though one that could not, on its own, provide the land, dependents, or political weight that only hereditary lineage membership still reliably conferred.
A member of a rival aristocratic lineage: For a lineage leader outside the Soga circle, the Seventeen-Article text's call for harmony and deference to the ruler's authority may have read simultaneously as a genuine appeal to shared court order and as a document whose emphasis on obedience served, conveniently, the interests of the dominant Soga-aligned court around Suiko, Umako, and Umayado.
Ono no Imoko, carrying the 607 letter to the Sui court: As the envoy responsible for physically delivering a document whose language the Sui emperor found displeasing, Imoko occupied a genuinely precarious diplomatic position, dependent on Sui court protocol and goodwill for his own safety and for the success of the broader mission, regardless of how the letter's wording had been decided at Yamato.
A young student or monk sent to study in Sui China: Leaving Yamato for an extended, uncertain period of study on the continent, such a student gained direct access to administrative, legal, and Buddhist learning unavailable at home — knowledge that, for some of these students, would shape the institutional reforms of the following generation examined in Chapter 13.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Lineage Politics and the New Court Language

What Changed

  • The court gained a new system of individually granted, ruler-conferred status through the Twelve Cap Ranks
  • Court officials were, for the first time, addressed by an explicit written statement of shared moral and political principle in the Seventeen-Article text
  • Buddhist patronage expanded in scale and became more closely woven into claims of political and moral legitimacy
  • Yamato resumed direct, high-stakes diplomatic engagement with a newly reunified Chinese empire after a long interval

What Stayed the Same

  • The uji-kabane system of hereditary lineage status remained the basic structure of elite Yamato society
  • Political authority continued to depend on negotiation and alliance among powerful aristocratic houses rather than direct administrative command
  • The Soga lineage, under Umako's leadership, remained the single most powerful political force at court throughout Suiko's reign
  • Succession disputes and factional competition among elite lineages, unresolved by the new court initiatives, resumed openly after Suiko's death

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing that new court language and symbolism developed alongside, rather than replacing, older lineage-based political structures prevents students from mistaking the Suiko court's ideological innovations for the administrative revolution that would only follow in Chapter 13.

Cause and Effect

From Post-587 Reconstruction to a Stronger Idea of Sovereignty

Long-term causes

Decades of accumulating continental contact, Buddhist introduction, and administrative influence from Baekje, Silla, and successive Chinese states had already given the Yamato elite exposure to more centralized models of rulership before Suiko's reign began.

Short-term causes

The violent succession and religious crisis of 587–592, examined in Chapter 11, created an urgent need to order relations among competing lineages more predictably, while the Sui reunification of China in 589 transformed the surrounding diplomatic environment.

Effect

The Suiko court introduced the Twelve Cap Ranks, promulgated the Seventeen-Article text, expanded Buddhist patronage, and resumed direct diplomatic missions to the Sui court, developing a richer vocabulary and symbolism of ordered, legitimate rulership.

Long-term consequence

These initiatives did not themselves create a centralized state, but they supplied precedents, some continentally trained personnel, and ideological vocabulary that later became available to the Taika-era reformers of Chapter 13 as they built a genuinely more centralized imperial order.

Historical complexity: None of these initiatives resolved the underlying competitive structure of Yamato lineage politics, as the succession conflict following Suiko's death makes clear; historians should therefore treat this period's developments as important preparation for later centralization rather than as centralization already achieved.

Everyday Life and Environment

Rank, Ritual, and Service at Court

For officials serving at the Suiko court, daily life increasingly involved new visible markers of status: a colored cap denoting one of twelve ranks, worn at court audiences and ceremonial occasions, alongside the older, less visible markers of lineage standing that continued to determine access to land, dependents, and political influence. Court ritual itself grew more elaborate during this period, incorporating both continuing kami-centered observance and expanding Buddhist ceremony, requiring officials to move between religious registers that coexisted rather than competed in daily court practice.

For the students and monks who traveled to Sui China beginning in 608, daily life took a very different shape: extended residence in a foreign court environment, immersion in continental language, administrative practice, and Buddhist scholarship, and — for those who returned years later — the challenge of translating unfamiliar continental knowledge into a form usable within Yamato's still lineage-based political world. For officials and commoners alike involved in the material work of expanding Buddhist patronage — quarrying stone, felling and transporting timber, and the specialized craft labor of temple construction at sites such as Hōryūji — this period's statecraft was experienced not primarily as abstract political philosophy but as concrete, often demanding, physical labor undertaken at the court's direction.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Yamato court during the reign of Suiko, from 592 to 628, as a reconstituted aristocratic government experimenting with new tools of order in the aftermath of the violent succession and religious crisis examined in Chapter 11. Rather than treating this period as the story of a single visionary prince, the chapter has insisted on understanding it as the product of shared, negotiated authority among Suiko as reigning sovereign, Soga no Umako as the dominant lineage head, and Prince Umayado as a royal figure whose documented political role must be carefully distinguished from the much more elaborate later legend of Shōtoku Taishi.

The Twelve Cap Ranks of 603 introduced a new system of individually granted court status, operating alongside, not in place of, the older hereditary uji-kabane structure of Yamato aristocratic society. The Seventeen-Article text of 604 articulated a set of moral and political principles emphasizing harmony, hierarchy, and proper official conduct, drawing on Confucian, Buddhist, and Legalist continental ideas — a statement of aspiration and exhortation rather than a constitution establishing governing institutions. Buddhist patronage expanded significantly during this period, becoming more closely entwined with claims of political and moral legitimacy while continuing to coexist with kami-centered ritual practice.

Renewed continental diplomacy formed an equally important dimension of Suiko-era statecraft. The Sui dynasty's reunification of China in 589 transformed the surrounding diplomatic environment, and Yamato responded with missions traditionally dated to around 600 and, more famously and better documented, in 607, when the envoy Ono no Imoko carried a letter invoking the "land where the sun rises" and the "land where the sun sets" — language that, according to the Chinese record, the Sui emperor received with displeasure, cautioning historians against reading the episode as proof of an already fully realized claim to sovereign equality. Continued exchange after 607, including the dispatch of Japanese students and monks to study in China, established a channel of continental learning that later became available to the reform generation of the following decades; the careers of Takamuko no Kuromaro and the monk Min, both sent abroad in 608 and both later appointed to advisory positions in the post-645 reform government, offer a documented, if limited, example of this connection.

The chapter has argued throughout that these initiatives are best understood as developing a new vocabulary, symbolism, and diplomatic posture for centralized, legitimate rulership, well before the administrative and territorial machinery necessary to realize that vision existed. Cap rank, moral-political text, Buddhist patronage, and diplomatic assertion together constituted a form of ideological and ceremonial centralization that should be carefully distinguished from the bureaucratic, territorial centralization that would follow only decades later.

The succession conflict that erupted following Suiko's death in 628 — and the further, more violent political upheaval that would engulf the Soga lineage itself in 645, examined in Chapter 13 — demonstrates clearly that the underlying competitive structure of Yamato aristocratic politics had not been resolved by the Suiko court's initiatives. Hereditary lineage power, negotiated authority, and locally rooted elite influence remained the basic realities of Yamato government throughout this period.

At the same time, the chapter has been careful not to dismiss Suiko-era statecraft as inconsequential. The precedents established in cap rank, moral-political principle, Buddhist patronage, and continental engagement supplied both ideological vocabulary and, through returning students and monks, some of the personnel later available to the Taika-era reformers in building the considerably more consequential centralized state examined in Chapter 13 — though, as Chapter 13 will show, that later reform process was itself gradual, contested, and substantially reshaped by retrospective chronicle-writing.

The chapter has also placed sustained emphasis on historical method, particularly on distinguishing the historical Prince Umayado from the later, more elaborate figure of Shōtoku Taishi constructed by eighth-century chroniclers, Buddhist institutions, and subsequent generations of devotional and national memory. This distinction serves as a broader lesson in evaluating sources compiled long after the events they describe, especially where those sources served the institutional interests of the compilers.

Finally, the chapter has situated Yamato's early seventh-century statecraft within its wider East Asian context, showing how the Sui reunification of China reshaped the strategic and cultural environment in which Yamato's rulers operated, and how continental models of rank, moral philosophy, and Buddhist rulership were selectively adapted, rather than passively imitated, at the Suiko court.

Students who understand the Suiko court's achievements and limits accurately — neither a fully centralized state nor an inconsequential interlude, but a genuine and historically significant set of precedents — are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 13, where the institutional machinery only imagined in this chapter's political and moral vocabulary finally begins to be built.

The 604 statement of moral and political principle addressing court harmony, hierarchy, and official conduct; not a constitution establishing governing institutions.

Political philosophy emphasizing hierarchical social harmony, moral cultivation of officials, and proper relationships of duty and deference; one of the continental traditions drawn upon in the Seventeen-Article text.

An earlier Chinese political tradition emphasizing consistent, impartially applied standards of official conduct; a secondary influence visible in some articles of the Seventeen-Article text.
Sui dynasty
The Chinese imperial dynasty (581–618) that reunified China in 589, reshaping the East Asian diplomatic environment in which Yamato operated.

The official Chinese dynastic history of the Sui period, an important independent source for the Yamato missions of 600 and 607.

The term used for the Yamato diplomatic missions sent to the Sui court in this period.

The court chronicle completed in 720, the principal Japanese narrative source for Suiko's reign, compiled roughly a century after the events it describes.

An earlier chronicle, completed in 712, offering a secondary but valuable source for this period's genealogy and succession.

A title rooted in Chinese imperial cosmology, invoked in the disputed 607 diplomatic letter in ways that raised questions of relative status between the Yamato and Sui courts.
Hagiography
Idealized, often devotionally motivated biographical writing; a key category for understanding the later elaboration of the Shōtoku legend.

The historical method of evaluating who created a source, when, for what audience, and with what interests, essential to this chapter's treatment of the Suiko-era evidence.

A term this chapter divides into ceremonial/ideological centralization, achieved in part during Suiko's reign, and administrative/territorial centralization, achieved only later under the Taika-era reforms examined in Chapter 13.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the story of Yamato court politics, introduced in Chapters 8 and 9 and complicated by the religious and succession crisis of Chapter 11, into a period of genuine, if limited, political experimentation. It has shown that a court reconstituted after violent conflict turned to new tools — individually granted rank, moral-political principle, expanded Buddhist patronage, and renewed continental diplomacy — not because these tools amounted to a modern state, but because they addressed real and pressing problems of order, legitimacy, and international standing.

The chapter's careful treatment of Prince Umayado and the later Shōtoku legend matters beyond its immediate subject. It demonstrates that even a figure central to a nation's later self-understanding can turn out to be, in significant part, a construction assembled generations after his death by institutions with their own interests in his memory. Students should carry this lesson of source criticism into every subsequent chapter of this textbook, particularly Chapter 13's account of the Taika reforms, which draws on similarly retrospective chronicle sources compiled by a state eager to present its own institutions as the natural fulfillment of earlier wisdom.

The chapter has also planted an important seed for the rest of the book: the recognition that ideological and ceremonial change can precede, and help make possible, institutional and administrative change, without the two being the same thing. This distinction anticipates the textbook's continuing attention — developed further in Chapter 13 and beyond — to how imported political vocabulary, once adapted and localized, eventually reshapes governing practice in ways its earliest adopters could not fully have anticipated.

Students who understand Suiko-era statecraft as a genuine field of political experimentation — shaped by shared authority, continental engagement, and a still-unresolved struggle among aristocratic lineages — are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 13, where the Taika reforms, the Jinshin War, and the ritsuryō law codes transform these early seventh-century precedents into a considerably more consequential centralized imperial state.
