Part III — The Han Empire
16

Han Expansion, Frontiers, and the Silk Roads

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

On a watchtower in the desert of the far northwest, a soldier in the first century BCE records what he sees. A signal fire was raised at dusk; a horseman passed at dawn; the grain ration for the tower was counted; a letter to a family far to the east was written on a slip of wood and left for a courier. The soldier is a small part of a very large enterprise. Behind him lies a chain of towers stretching hundreds of kilometers along the edge of a corridor of oases. Before him lie deserts, mountains, and the lands of peoples whom his government regards as rivals, partners, or subjects, depending on the year. He knows nothing of the phrase “Silk Road,” which would be coined nearly two thousand years later. What he does know is that his life depends on grain that must be carried for months, and on a state that wants the corridor held.

This chapter examines the moment when the Han state, whose internal workings Chapter 15 described, met other states, peoples, environments, and networks beyond its agrarian heartland. Its central problem is how conflict, diplomacy, migration, frontier administration, and long-distance exchange transformed both the Han Empire and the wider Inner Asian world. The result was neither simple conquest nor simple trade, but a frontier system built from warfare, diplomacy, settlement, exchange, and mutual adaptation.

The chapter's argument can be stated briefly. Han expansion was not a simple march westward, and the Silk Roads were not created by a single expedition. The Han frontier emerged through prolonged interaction with powerful Inner Asian states and communities. Warfare, diplomacy, marriage alliances, gift exchange, military colonies, migration, and commercial networks gradually tied the Han Empire more closely to Central Asia. These connections expanded imperial power but also imposed enormous fiscal, military, and human costs, while the routes later called the Silk Roads remained decentralized networks shaped by many peoples rather than a single Han-controlled road. A signature idea follows: frontiers were zones of interaction, not fixed lines on a map.

The chapter follows a sequence from geography to systems. It begins with the environments and peoples of the frontier (16.1), then examines the Xiongnu confederation on its own terms (16.2), the alternating diplomacy and warfare of the early Han (16.3), the shift under Emperor Wu toward aggressive expansion (16.4), the missions of Zhang Qian and Han knowledge of Central Asia (16.5), the exchange networks conventionally called the Silk Roads (16.6), the goods and ideas that moved along them (16.7), and the social world of garrisons and colonies (16.8). It closes by placing the Han in Eurasian history (16.9) and by asking students to trace routes on a map (16.10).

What changed? A dynasty that had initially accepted an unequal, negotiated peace with the Xiongnu turned, under a fiscally stronger state, to prolonged war, then built a chain of garrisons, colonies, and diplomatic relationships reaching into the Tarim Basin. Han goods, especially silk, entered wider exchange networks, and Han officials gained knowledge of distant societies. What continued? Diplomacy never disappeared: marriage alliances, gifts, border markets, and negotiated relationships persisted alongside war, and the fundamental logistical problem of supplying distant frontiers remained. Who was affected? Soldiers and their families, conscripted laborers, settlers, farmers whose taxes paid for campaigns, Xiongnu pastoralists and their subordinate groups, oasis rulers and townspeople, merchants and intermediaries across Central Asia, and the horses and livestock whose loss shaped every campaign.

The evidence is unusual in variety. The great court histories, Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian and Ban Gu's Book of Han, narrate policy, campaigns, and foreign peoples from the perspective of Han court writers. Excavated administrative documents from watchtowers and postal stations at sites such as Juyan and Dunhuang show the daily life of frontier garrisons. Archaeology of Xiongnu and Central Asian burials, oasis settlements, and trade goods provides evidence that is independent of Chinese writing. Together these sources allow historians to see the frontier from several sides, though the Xiongnu themselves left no surviving histories of their own, and their story must be reconstructed largely from outside accounts and archaeology.

This chapter connects directly to its neighbors. Chapter 15 supplied the fiscal, administrative, and household systems that made frontier campaigns possible; Chapter 16 shows what those systems were asked to bear. Chapter 17 will explore the intellectual and cultural life of Han China, and Chapter 18 will examine how stresses within the system contributed to the empire's crisis. Beyond the Han, the frontier world described here connects to the later Silk Roads of the Tang (Chapter 27), to the Inner Asian states that later ruled parts of northern China (Chapters 20 and 22, 35 and 36, and 38 and 39), and to the Qing incorporation of Xinjiang and Mongolia (Chapter 48), reminding students that the meaning of these borderlands changed repeatedly across Chinese history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the environments and peoples of the Han frontier, and explain why the frontier was a shifting zone of interaction rather than a fixed border.
  2. Explain the political organization and strategic logic of the Xiongnu confederation, avoiding the characterization of the Xiongnu as merely raiders.
  3. Analyze heqin diplomacy and early Han relations with the Xiongnu, and explain why war and diplomacy were alternating tools rather than opposites.
  4. Evaluate the causes, methods, costs, and limits of Emperor Wu's expansion, connecting it to the fiscal system studied in Chapter 15.
  5. Assess the historical significance of Zhang Qian's missions, distinguishing information gathering from the creation of exchange networks.
  6. Explain why historians speak of Silk Roads in the plural and describe how goods and people moved through networks of intermediaries.
  7. Interpret evidence for trade and cultural exchange, distinguishing the movement of objects from the movement of people and from direct political contact.
  8. Describe the organization and social life of Han garrisons and colonies, using excavated frontier documents as evidence.
  9. Distinguish conquest from sustained frontier control, and explain what “colonization” meant in the Han context.
  10. Compare transmitted court narratives, excavated documents, and archaeological evidence, and evaluate their strengths and limits.
  11. Connect Han frontier history to wider Eurasian history and to the internal history of the Han state.

Historical Context

Long before the Han, the peoples of the steppe, the oases, and the northern and western edges of the agrarian plains interacted with the states of the Central Plains. Chapter 10 noted that Warring States kingdoms such as Qin, Zhao, and Yan built defensive walls and adopted mounted warfare in response to northern neighbors, and Chapter 13 described Qin's campaigns in the Ordos region and the construction of frontier defenses. The Han inherited these frontier problems. It also inherited an administrative form, the commandery, that could be extended to new territories, and a fiscal system based on registered households, examined in Chapter 15.

By the time Han was founded in 202 BCE, a new power had risen on the steppe. Under a ruler named Modu, the Xiongnu united many pastoral groups into a confederation that dominated the eastern and central steppe and pressed on the northern border of the Han. Chapter 14 introduced the crisis around Pingcheng in about 200 BCE, when the Han founder was surrounded by Xiongnu forces and forced to negotiate. That episode shaped early Han policy: for several decades, court leaders preferred accommodation through marriage alliance, gifts, and regulated exchange, because the Han state was recovering from civil war and could not easily sustain a large campaign.

The geography of the region structured every relationship. The steppe belt across northern Asia favored mounted herding societies whose mobility allowed rapid movement over long distances. South of the steppe lay the Ordos loop of the Yellow River and the northern edge of the agricultural plains. West of the agricultural heartland, the Hexi Corridor, a chain of oases between the Qilian Mountains and the deserts to the north, formed a natural passage to the Tarim Basin, a great desert basin ringed by mountains and dotted with oasis city-states. Beyond the Tarim lay Central Asian regions such as Ferghana, Sogdiana, and Bactria, with their own long traditions of urban and commercial life. To the northeast, the Han faced peoples in Manchuria and the Korean peninsula; to the south and southwest, the Han met communities in Lingnan, Yunnan, and the highlands whose relationships with the state were distinct from those of the northern frontier and are noted in the Regional and Frontier Context section.

Students should not imagine that the frontier was empty land waiting for Han settlers. It was inhabited by pastoral confederations, oasis communities, farmers, traders, and local rulers, each with its own interests. Nor should they imagine that the empire's frontier had the same meaning as a modern border. Han authority varied greatly: some regions were incorporated as commanderies with registered households, some were garrisoned zones, some were client states whose rulers were granted titles and gifts, and some remained outside effective Han influence. What is called the Han frontier is therefore best understood as a shifting political zone.

The stakes of this history extend beyond the Han. The relations between agrarian and pastoral societies, the incorporation of borderlands, the movement of goods across Eurasia, and the debates about the costs and benefits of expansion recur throughout Chinese history. But students should resist reading later imperial experiences back into the Han: the frontier policies, military organization, and diplomatic practices of the Han had their own logic, and this chapter seeks to explain them in their own context.

Section 16.1

The Han Frontier World

To understand the Han frontier, begin with geography rather than war. Three kinds of environment met along the northern and western edges of the empire. Behind the frontier lay the agrarian imperial core, the Yellow River plains, the Wei valley, and the other farming regions described in Chapter 15, which produced the grain, cloth, and taxes that supported the state. Beyond that core lay frontier commanderies and garrisons, thinly settled zones where the state maintained watchtowers, granaries, and small farming colonies. Beyond these lay the mobile pastoral and oasis worlds, the steppe grasslands of the north and the desert-ringed oases of the west, where societies lived by herding, small-scale irrigation agriculture, and trade.

The northern steppe was a vast belt of grassland stretching across Inner Asia. Its societies depended on horses, sheep, goats, cattle, and, in some regions, camels, moving seasonally between pastures. Mobility was both a way of life and a military advantage: pastoral groups could concentrate quickly, strike, and withdraw, and their horses gave them a speed that agrarian infantry armies struggled to match. South of the steppe, the Ordos region, the area enclosed by the great northern bend of the Yellow River, lay between pastoral and agricultural zones and was contested between them. Farther west, the Hexi Corridor ran between the Qilian Mountains to the south and deserts to the north, a series of oases that formed a natural route between the Han heartland and the Tarim Basin. The Tarim Basin itself was ringed by mountains and dominated by the Taklamakan Desert, with oasis towns along its northern and southern rims that were supplied by mountain streams. These oases were connected by routes that skirted the desert and led toward Ferghana, Sogdiana, Bactria, and beyond.

Several geographic facts shaped what states could do. Steppe mobility favored mounted forces and made pastoral groups hard to catch. The oases enabled travel across otherwise impassable deserts, so control of an oasis meant control of water, supplies, and passage. Supply distance limited Han military power: a large army operating hundreds of kilometers from the agricultural core required grain, fodder, animals, and transport, and much of what it carried was consumed on the way. Rivers, grasslands, passes, and mountain ranges structured movement, and seasonal patterns of grass and water determined when campaigns and migrations were feasible. Chapter 15 pays off here: frontier campaigning depended on taxes, grain, transport, horses, labor, officials, and record-keeping drawn from the internal system that students have just studied.

The frontier was not empty. The steppe was home to the Xiongnu and to other groups, some subordinate to them and some independent. The Hexi Corridor, before Han expansion, had been home to groups such as the Yuezhi and others whose relations with the Xiongnu shifted. The Tarim oases were small kingdoms, each with its own ruler, its own irrigation works, its own languages and traditions, and its own alliances. In the northeast, the Han met the peoples of Manchuria and the northern Korean peninsula; in the south and southwest, the Han encountered polities in Lingnan and in the highlands of Yunnan and Guizhou, whose relationships with the empire are discussed in the Regional and Frontier Context section. Each of these societies had interests of its own and acted on them.

For this reason, the phrase “Han frontier” should be treated with care. It did not designate a continuous line, and it was not a modern national border. Han authority took several forms: direct administration through commanderies with registered households; garrison zones supported by military colonies; client relationships in which local rulers held Han titles and received gifts while retaining their own governments; and regions of influence in which the Han presence was episodic. The Han frontier was a shifting political zone, and its shape changed with campaigns, alliances, and the fortunes of neighboring powers.

Section 16.2

The Xiongnu Confederation

The Xiongnu are known primarily through Chinese sources, especially the Records of the Grand Historian and the Book of Han, and increasingly through archaeology. Chinese writers often described them as barbarians, and modern readers sometimes inherit that framing by picturing the Xiongnu simply as nomadic raiders. That picture is inadequate. The Xiongnu were a large, sophisticated Inner Asian political confederation, capable of diplomacy, negotiation over exchange, coalition building, military mobilization, and control over important routes and populations. To understand why they mattered, students must try to see them from their own geopolitical logic, not solely as a problem for Han rulers.

Traditional accounts credit Modu, who became chanyu (supreme ruler) in the later third century BCE, with consolidating the confederation. According to Sima Qian, Modu defeated the Donghu to the east, pressed the Yuezhi to the west, and brought many groups under Xiongnu authority. The confederation was organized in a way that combined a central ruler with regional leaders. The eastern and western wings were led by high-ranking nobles, and the steppe was divided into sections under subordinate rulers, with military organization tied to this structure. The details come from Chinese accounts and must be read critically, but archaeology, including elite burials in Mongolia and southern Siberia with imported goods and rich furnishings, confirms that the Xiongnu world included wealthy elites and complex social hierarchies.

Several principles help explain Xiongnu power. First, pastoralism did not mean political primitiveness: mobile herding societies were capable of sophisticated organization, and the management of animals, pastures, and seasonal movement required coordination. Second, mobility was a strategic advantage, since a mounted force could choose when and where to fight and could retreat beyond the reach of an agrarian army. Third, Xiongnu power rested on alliances and subordinate groups as well as on military force; the confederation was a network of relationships in which leaders distributed rewards and received loyalty, and its cohesion depended on the chanyu's ability to provide for followers. Fourth, relations with agrarian states involved exchange as much as warfare. Xiongnu political economy incorporated exchange with agrarian societies: grain, textiles, metal goods, and luxury goods from agrarian societies were valuable, and the chanyu could distribute them to sustain his authority. Border markets, gifts, and negotiated arrangements were therefore central to Xiongnu political economy, not incidental to it.

This last point is essential for understanding the pattern of raid, negotiation, and renewed raid. From the Xiongnu perspective, pressure on the border could be a means of obtaining better terms, and negotiated peace with regular gifts was a way of securing resources without the risks of war. From the Han perspective, this looked like extortion. Both perspectives had an internal logic, and interpreting one as simple aggression and the other as simple defense misses the interplay. It also helps explain why a mobile confederation could pose a persistent challenge to a wealthy agrarian empire: the Han had wealth but faced a difficult and expensive task in projecting force across steppe distances, while the Xiongnu could combine coercion and trade to obtain what they wanted at lower cost.

Students should also avoid equating the Xiongnu with the “Huns” who appear in European history centuries later. Some scholars have proposed connections, but the relationship between the Xiongnu and later Hunnic groups is debated, and this chapter does not treat them as identical. Similarly, the Xiongnu were not culturally or ethnically uniform; the confederation included groups of varied origin and language, and the categories used by Chinese sources may not correspond to the self-identifications of the people themselves. The Xiongnu left no surviving histories, and much about their language, beliefs, and internal politics remains uncertain.

Section 16.3

War and Diplomacy on the Steppe

The relationship between the Han and the Xiongnu in the early decades was shaped by the crisis around Pingcheng in about 200 BCE, introduced in Chapter 14. The Han founder, campaigning in the north, was surrounded by Xiongnu forces and escaped after negotiation. The episode showed the limits of Han military capacity at that time, and court advisers concluded that a costly campaign against the steppe was neither wise nor affordable. Out of this situation emerged the arrangement known as heqin.

Historical Vocabulary
Heqin (和親) and chanyu (單于)

Heqin is often translated as “peace through marriage alliance,” but it refers to a broader diplomatic arrangement. In the early Han it combined recognition of the Xiongnu ruler as a counterpart, marriage arrangements involving women of the imperial house or court, regular gifts of silk, grain, and other goods, agreed frontier limits, and regulated markets. It was negotiated, periodically renewed, and periodically broken.

Chanyu is the title, in Chinese transcription, of the Xiongnu supreme ruler. The Han court's records describe relations in terms of kinship, with the chanyu and the emperor as “brothers” in some periods, a form that carried different meanings for each side. Both terms should be understood in context, not as simple equivalents of “tribute” and “king.”

Heqin was adopted around 198 BCE and, with interruptions, shaped relations for several decades. Under it, a woman from the imperial house or court was sent to the chanyu, the Han provided regular gifts, and the two sides agreed to respect each other's territories, with border markets allowing exchange. Some later emperors renewed the arrangement; others faced Xiongnu incursions and negotiated new terms. Correspondence reported in the histories, including letters attributed to the chanyu and the Han court, shows both sides using diplomatic language to assert status, and it shows the Xiongnu leader presenting himself as a ruler of a great confederation who had subdued many peoples.

Students should not reduce heqin to a story of humiliating tribute paid because Han was weak. It combined diplomatic recognition, marriage arrangements, gifts, negotiated peace, frontier markets, and periodic breakdown and renewed violence. It was diplomacy within a contested interstate relationship, and it served Han interests in several ways: it bought time for recovery, reduced the frequency of raids, and allowed the Han to concentrate on internal consolidation, the recovery described in Chapters 14 and 15. Early Han rulers often chose accommodation because sustained steppe warfare was expensive and uncertain. From the Xiongnu side, heqin provided a stable flow of goods that supported the chanyu's authority. Neither side saw it as simply a defeat.

The word “tribute” also needs care. Goods moved in multiple directions and carried different political meanings depending on which court described the exchange. Han sources often present gifts to the Xiongnu as bestowals from a superior and Xiongnu goods as tribute; Xiongnu leaders, in the correspondence preserved, treated exchanges as arrangements among equals or as payments due. The physical exchange might look identical, while its meaning depended on who was telling the story. Later chapters will return to the problem of describing diplomacy in terms of a simplified “tributary system.”

Heqin repeatedly broke down. Xiongnu groups raided the border, Han officials debated whether to fight or negotiate, and the terms shifted. Some Han advisers argued that heqin merely fed the Xiongnu and encouraged further demands; others held that it was a practical necessity. The point is that war and diplomacy were not opposites. They were alternating tools within the same frontier strategy, used by both sides according to circumstances. The long period of accommodation also allowed the Han state to accumulate the resources and institutions, discussed in Chapters 14 and 15, that made a change of policy possible.

Section 16.4

Emperor Wu and Imperial Expansion

In the reign of Emperor Wu (141–87 BCE), Han policy toward the steppe changed. Traditional narratives mark the turning point with the failed ambush at Mayi in 133 BCE, an attempt to lure a Xiongnu force into a trap that fell apart when the Xiongnu discovered the plan. Over the following decades, Han armies campaigned repeatedly against the Xiongnu. It would be misleading to attribute this shift to a single cause or to the emperor's personality alone. Several conditions combined.

First, the Han state had by now recovered from the early upheavals and possessed stronger finances and institutions, as Chapter 14 described. Second, concern about Xiongnu power, and about the Xiongnu's ability to combine pressure on the border with influence in the west, encouraged advisers to seek a more active policy. Third, strategic opportunities appeared: the possibility of allies in the west, the chance to control frontier corridors, and access to horses and Central Asian partners. Fourth, court politics and imperial ambition mattered; the emperor supported officials and commanders willing to undertake campaigns, and some advisers argued forcefully for and against them. The result was a shift from accommodation to a more aggressive policy that combined warfare, colonization, and diplomacy.

Among the commanders most associated with these campaigns were Wei Qing and Huo Qubing, relatives of the imperial family who rose through military service. Rather than cataloguing every campaign, students should focus on those with strategic consequences. In 127 BCE, Han forces moved into the Ordos region, and the government established commanderies there, shifting a contested zone into the Han sphere. In the following years, campaigns led by Huo Qubing and others struck into the Hexi region, and Han authority was extended along the corridor, with the establishment over subsequent decades of commanderies such as Jiuquan, Wuwei, Zhangye, and Dunhuang (the dates vary in the sources). In 119 BCE, a major campaign sent large forces north of the desert into the steppe. These campaigns weakened Xiongnu control over the Hexi corridor and changed the balance of the frontier, though they did not destroy the confederation.

The key connection to Chapter 15 is that expansion required an interventionist fiscal state. The salt and iron monopolies, taxes on commercial wealth, currency reform, transport systems, granaries, horse procurement, conscription, and colonization discussed there should now be seen as resources consumed by expansion. Armies required grain and fodder carried over long distances, transport laborers, remounts, weapons, and clothing, and the government mobilized households through the registration and service systems described in Chapter 15. Because sustained steppe warfare required enormous numbers of cavalry mounts and remounts, including prized Inner and Central Asian stock, the state promoted horse breeding and sought horses from Central Asia. The scale of the effort placed heavy strain on the fiscal and administrative system.

The costs were enormous, and students should not accept the heroic formula that Emperor Wu “opened the West.” Transmitted sources report catastrophic horse losses in the campaigns of 119 BCE, large numbers of soldiers killed or displaced, and heavy burdens on farming households through taxes, conscription, and labor. Frontier settlement required moving large numbers of people. The campaigns were not an uninterrupted series of Han victories: some failed, some ended in disaster, and the gains were uneven and strategically limited. The case of Li Ling, a commander who surrendered to the Xiongnu in 99 BCE, and of the historian Sima Qian, who spoke in his defense and was punished, shows that Han officials disagreed about the war, and that criticism could be costly. Later in the reign, the court, according to the transmitted sources, moved toward retrenchment and a more defensive posture, and the salt and iron debate of 81 BCE, discussed in Chapter 15, included sharp criticism of frontier expenditure.

Han expansion under Emperor Wu also extended in other directions, though these fall outside the chapter's main frame. Han forces moved against the state of Nanyue in the south, against polities in the southwest, and in the northeast against the kingdom in northern Korea, establishing commanderies in each area. These campaigns extended the empire's reach and, like those in the north and west, brought new populations, new administrative burdens, and new relationships. They are noted in the Regional and Frontier Context section.

The Xiongnu were not destroyed. After the Han campaigns, the confederation suffered losses and shifted its center of gravity northward, but it continued to be a major power. In the following decades, internal divisions, succession disputes, and environmental pressures weakened it, and in the mid-first century BCE a civil war opened between rival claimants. Huhanye Chanyu sought Han support and visited the Han court (traditionally in 51 BCE), receiving recognition and gifts, while his rival Zhizhi moved westward before being defeated by a Han force in 36 BCE. Huhanye's alignment with the Han, and the marriage of a Han court woman, Wang Zhaojun, to Huhanye in 33 BCE, show that diplomacy continued alongside the military record. In 48 CE, the confederation formally divided into Northern and Southern Xiongnu, and the Southern Xiongnu settled within Han frontier regions.

Section 16.5

Zhang Qian and Central Asia

Few figures in Chinese history have been more mythologized than Zhang Qian, and this section begins with the corrections. Zhang Qian was not the first person from the Han world to travel west; he was not the discoverer of Central Asia, which was already home to established societies with their own histories; and he was not the founder of the Silk Road, since the networks he encountered existed before him. His importance was political and informational. He brought back reliable, detailed information about societies west of Han territory, and that information changed how the Han court imagined its strategic position.

According to the histories, Zhang Qian, a court official, was sent around 139 or 138 BCE to seek an alliance with the Yuezhi, a people who had been driven westward by the Xiongnu and who, the Han hoped, would be willing to join a campaign against their old enemy. Zhang Qian's party was captured by the Xiongnu and he was held for about a decade, during which, the sources report, he married and had a child. He eventually escaped and continued westward, reaching Dayuan (Ferghana), then Kangju, and the Great Yuezhi north of the Oxus. The Yuezhi had already established dominance over Daxia (Bactria), which lay principally south of the river. The Yuezhi, however, were not interested in renewing war against the Xiongnu, having established themselves in fertile lands far from their old enemy. Zhang Qian returned around 126 BCE, again passing through Xiongnu territory, and reported to the court. A later mission, around 119 BCE, sought relations with the Wusun in the Ili region.

What Zhang Qian brought back was a description of a world beyond the Han frontier: states of settled farmers and city dwellers in Ferghana, Sogdiana, and Bactria; pastoral peoples such as the Wusun; and the network of oasis states of the Tarim Basin. Sima Qian's account preserves Zhang Qian's reports on these lands, including their populations, products, military strength, and relations with the Xiongnu. The report also noted that he had seen goods from Sichuan in Bactria, which had reached the region, he was told, through India, suggesting an exchange route to the southwest that was unknown to the Han court. Historians treat such details with interest and caution: they show that goods were already circulating across long distances through intermediaries, and they show the Han court's growing awareness of those networks.

Zhang Qian's missions therefore illustrate a change that occurs when a state acquires reliable information about distant societies. The Han court could now think about the west in terms of specific polities, resources, and possible partners. It learned of the horses of Ferghana, reputed to be larger and stronger than Han horses. It saw the possibility of building relationships with the Wusun and the Tarim oasis states to isolate the Xiongnu. It acquired a sense of the commercial possibilities in exchange with Central Asia. This knowledge helped redirect policy, and the campaigns and diplomacy that followed, including the war against Dayuan, drew on it. But the information did not create the networks he encountered; it made them visible to the Han court.

The wars against Dayuan illustrate both the ambition and the costs of the new policy. After Dayuan refused to give the Han the horses it sought and killed Han envoys, the emperor sent an army under Li Guangli in 104 BCE. The expedition suffered heavy losses and failed. A second, much larger expedition in 102–101 BCE laid siege to Dayuan's capital and secured a settlement, including the delivery of horses. The Han obtained the famed “heavenly horses,” but at great cost in men, animals, and resources. The campaign also demonstrated to the oasis states of the Tarim Basin that Han armies could reach them, and Han influence in the region increased, though it depended on continuing military and diplomatic effort.

Section 16.6

The Silk Roads

Historical Vocabulary
The “Silk Roads” (a modern term)

The phrase “Silk Road” was popularized in the nineteenth century, when the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen used a term for silk routes in his writings on Central Asia. No ancient traveler used it. Today, historians prefer the plural, Silk Roads, or a phrase such as “the networks later called the Silk Roads,” to indicate that the term refers to many overlapping routes and exchanges, not a single road.

The term is useful shorthand but can mislead. It suggests a single highway, a single commodity, and a continuous journey between two endpoints. The historical reality, as this section explains, was a shifting network of routes, markets, intermediaries, and political arrangements, in which silk was only one of many goods.

The conceptual heart of this chapter is a correction: there was no single Silk Road. Ancient exchange across Eurasia operated through multiple overland routes; chains of oases; intermediary merchants; steppe networks that moved goods and animals; southern routes through the highlands of the southwest and into South and Southeast Asia; and, later, maritime connections that are treated in subsequent chapters. The routes shifted with politics, climate, and commercial opportunity. Some were seasonal, some depended on the goodwill of local rulers, and some were closed by conflict.

Goods rarely traveled from one end of Eurasia to the other with a single merchant. Instead, exchange typically moved in stages: a producer sold to a regional merchant, who sold to an intermediary, who carried goods to an oasis market, where another intermediary bought them and carried them onward, until they reached a distant consumer. At each stage, goods might change hands, be resold at a markup, be taxed, or be used as gifts or payment. Very long-distance goods thus moved through multiple intermediaries, and the merchants who handled them were often from communities of Central Asia, including the oasis states and Bactrian and other Central Asian merchants (the large Sogdian caravan networks belong especially to later centuries), rather than from the empires at the two ends. A single item might pass through dozens of hands over months or years.

Silk was important, but it was not the only commodity, and it did not function only as a commercial good. In the Han world, silk textiles served as luxury goods, diplomatic gifts, payments to soldiers and officials, state allocations, and, in some contexts, a medium of exchange. The Han government gave large quantities of silk to Xiongnu leaders under heqin, to oasis rulers, and to allies, and it paid officials and soldiers in silk and other goods. Silk moving westward was therefore embedded in political relationships as well as commercial ones, and much of it may have reached Central Asia through gift and diplomatic exchange, not only through trade.

Politics shaped the routes. The Han's establishment of garrisons, commanderies, and later the Protector General of the Western Regions in 60 BCE provided protection and supply points along the Hexi Corridor and into the Tarim Basin, and this presence made travel safer and more predictable in some periods. But the Han did not control the whole network. The Xiongnu, the oasis kingdoms, the Yuezhi and their successors, and other powers each controlled segments, taxed traffic, and shaped the flow of goods. When Han power waned, as it did in parts of the first century BCE and again in the Eastern Han, the routes did not disappear; they continued under other arrangements. The emphasis, therefore, should be on network effects, not road imagery: the routes were webs of relationships among many peoples, and no single state owned them.

Southern routes also mattered. Zhang Qian's report of Sichuan goods in Bactria pointed to a route through the southwest and India, and Han officials attempted to open a passage through the highlands, but were frustrated by local rulers and difficult terrain. Maritime connections along the southern coast, which developed more fully in later centuries, are treated in Chapters 27 and 37. It is thus useful to think of the Silk Roads as a system of overland, highland, and, increasingly, maritime routes, in which the Han state was one participant.

Section 16.7

Trade Goods and Cultural Exchange

The goods that moved across the networks were varied. From the Han world went silk textiles, lacquerware, bronze mirrors, iron goods, and other manufactured products. Toward the Han came horses, which the state prized above all for military use; camels and other animals; metals and precious stones such as jade from the Khotan region; glass; and various aromatic and agricultural products. Later Han sources attribute the introduction of crops such as grapes and alfalfa to the contacts following Zhang Qian's missions, though the dating and transmission of particular plants is debated and probably involved multiple channels and periods. Musical instruments, artistic motifs, techniques, and ideas also moved.

Students should be careful about what such evidence proves. A familiar textbook mistake is to assume that a foreign object found in a Chinese site shows a merchant traveled from its place of origin. The principle to teach is that object movement is not the same as person movement, and neither is the same as direct diplomatic contact. When a Roman-style glass vessel or a Central Asian textile turns up in a Han-period tomb, historians ask a series of questions. Did the object move through intermediaries? Was it a diplomatic gift? Was it locally imitated, so that the style traveled rather than the object itself? Was it brought by a merchant, an envoy, a soldier, or a migrant? Was it made locally by a craftsperson familiar with foreign models? Each possibility implies a different kind of connection.

The same principle applies in the other direction. Han silks and lacquerware have been found in the burials of Xiongnu elites in Mongolia and in sites across Central Asia, and Han-style textiles appear far to the west. These finds demonstrate that Han goods moved over long distances, but they do not by themselves show that Han merchants traveled with them; the goods may have come through gifts to Xiongnu leaders, through oasis intermediaries, or through markets along the way. Similarly, the presence of Han officials at frontier garrisons does not show that Han people traveled all the way to distant Central Asian markets. Han goods could travel very far without direct Han political control.

Exchange also included knowledge and cultural forms. Artistic motifs, methods of metalworking, techniques of horsemanship, musical forms, foods, and languages moved through the networks, as did religious ideas. Students should note that the major Chinese development of Buddhism, which came to Han territory along these routes, belongs later in the book: the early transmission of Buddhist ideas is treated in Chapter 17 and its expansion in Chapter 23, and this chapter simply notes that the networks were channels for many kinds of transmission. The process was rarely a one-way diffusion from a source to a recipient; rather, ideas and objects were adapted, combined, and reinterpreted as they moved.

The economic effects of long-distance exchange on ordinary Han people were mixed. Luxury imports were consumed mainly by elites, and the state's interest in horses and in diplomatic gifts placed demands on producers of silk and other goods. Weavers, as Chapter 15 emphasized, produced textiles that could enter gift and payment systems as well as household use. Frontier communities depended on supplies and markets that connected them to the interior and to neighbors beyond the frontier. For oasis populations and Central Asian merchants, the exchange networks offered opportunities to profit as intermediaries, while also exposing them to pressure from competing powers.

Section 16.8

Garrisons, Colonies, and Frontier Society

This section is the social-history center of the chapter, because it shows what a frontier empire actually required. The frontier was inhabited by soldiers, settlers, farming colonists, officials, convicts or relocated populations where documented, merchants, postal workers, local oasis peoples, pastoral communities, and families. The chain of dependencies can be summarized as follows: grain stores, watchtowers, postal stations, military farms, record-keeping, supply convoys, horses, labor, and family settlement. Conquest was not the same as sustained frontier control. A campaign could seize territory quickly; keeping it required people, supplies, bureaucracy, and settlements.

Historical Vocabulary
Military colony (tuntian, 屯田) and “colonization”

A military colony was a settlement in which soldiers or settlers farmed land to supply garrisons, reducing the need to transport grain from the interior. Some were staffed by soldiers who farmed in addition to their duties; others by families relocated by the state. The Han state organized these settlements, supplied them with tools, seed, and, at times, animals, and integrated them into commanderies.

The word colonization is used in this chapter to mean state-directed settlement, military agriculture, migration, and administrative incorporation of newly acquired lands. It should not be equated with nineteenth-century European colonialism, whose economic, legal, and ideological structures differed. Han practice nonetheless involved coercion, displacement, and the imposition of imperial administration on existing communities, and its effects on local peoples should not be minimized.

Han frontier defense relied on lines of walls, watchtowers, and beacon stations that were extended westward from the north through the Hexi Corridor toward Dunhuang and the passes beyond. Garrisons were organized in a hierarchy of units, from headquarters down to individual towers staffed by small detachments. Signal fires and smoke warned of approaching riders; patrols examined the ground for tracks; and couriers carried messages between towers and to higher offices. Postal stations provided relay points, lodging, and supplies for officials and envoys traveling along the routes. The frontier commanderies also included granaries and storehouses, and small farming settlements produced grain for the garrisons.

The transmitted histories report that large numbers of people were relocated to the northwest under Emperor Wu, though the figures are uncertain, and that the government supported settlement by providing land, tools, and supplies. Soldiers themselves farmed in the colonies, and some families accompanied them. Convicts and other dependent populations were sometimes sent to the frontier as labor. Over time, frontier communities included people of varied origins: Han settlers from different regions, local residents incorporated into commanderies, oasis peoples, and people of Xiongnu and other Inner Asian origin who served or settled. Frontier society was not culturally homogeneous. It was a mixed society shaped by contact, cooperation, and tension.

The best evidence for this world comes from excavated administrative documents. Thousands of wooden slips and tablets have been recovered from watchtowers and garrison sites at Juyan, Dunhuang, and elsewhere in the northwest, beginning with early twentieth-century expeditions and continuing with later excavations. They include duty rosters, inventories of weapons and grain, records of signal fires and patrols, ration distributions, personal correspondence, legal proceedings, and lists of soldiers and supplies. They reveal a structured, document-driven system in which even a remote tower's activities were recorded, reported, and audited. They also show individuals: soldiers complaining about shortages or seeking news from home, officials pressing subordinates, and families making arrangements. These sources are the frontier counterpart to the household registers of Chapter 15, and they show the fiscal and administrative system at its outer edge.

Frontier control was costly and fragile. Garrisons had to be supplied, often from distant sources. Soldiers were exposed to hardship, disease, and the risk of attack, and desertion and illness are recorded. Settlers faced harsh environments, and irrigation and farming in arid zones demanded continuous labor. The state's presence depended on continued investment, and when campaigns or budgets shifted, frontier positions could weaken. In the Eastern Han, as Chapter 18 will discuss, changes in fiscal capacity, military organization, and the settlement of non-Han peoples within frontier regions altered the character of frontier defense.

Students should also consider the perspectives of the peoples who lived in the regions the Han incorporated or influenced. Oasis rulers weighed the benefits of Han protection and trade against the costs of tribute, garrisons, and the risk of Xiongnu retaliation, and many shifted allegiance depending on circumstances. Pastoral communities in the Hexi Corridor and the Ordos were displaced or absorbed as the Han moved in. The Han sources largely present these developments from the standpoint of the state, and the experiences of local peoples must be inferred from archaeology and from scattered remarks.

Section 16.9

Han China in Eurasian History

By this point, students should see Han China as one major state within a wider Eurasian system, not as an isolated civilization suddenly connected to “the West.” Several conclusions follow from the chapter. Inner Asian societies shaped Han policy: the Xiongnu confederation determined the terms of early Han diplomacy and the direction of later campaigns. Han military expansion reshaped frontier politics, weakening Xiongnu control in some regions, drawing oasis states into new relationships, and displacing or incorporating local populations. Central Asian states pursued their own interests, and the rulers of Ferghana, the Wusun, and the Tarim oases negotiated with the Han when it suited them and resisted when it did not. Exchange networks predated Han expansion and expanded through many actors. Han goods could travel very far without direct Han political control. And imperial connectivity brought both opportunities and costs.

A strong synthesis is that the Han did not simply extend China into Eurasia; interaction with Eurasia changed what the Han state had to become. It needed more cavalry and horses, frontier expertise, revenue, diplomatic skill, long-distance intelligence, settlements, and administrative flexibility. It created offices such as the Protector General and developed systems of garrisons, colonies, and postal stations. It experimented with fiscal interventions that Chapter 15 described. The relationship between the agrarian core and the frontier was reciprocal: the core supplied resources, while the frontier reshaped the demands placed on the core.

The Eastern Han illustrates the continuing contingency of these relations. After the disruptions at the end of the Western Han and the interregnum, Han influence in the Tarim Basin had to be reestablished. In the later first century CE, the commander and envoy Ban Chao, operating with limited Han forces and relying heavily on local allies and troops from oasis states, restored Han influence across much of the Tarim Basin. In 89–91 CE, Han forces and allied groups, including the Southern Xiongnu, inflicted major defeats on the Northern Xiongnu. Their political position subsequently weakened sharply; some groups may have moved westward while others surrendered, were absorbed into neighboring populations, or remained active farther west and northwest. Their relationship to the later Huns remains debated. Ban Chao's subordinate Gan Ying is reported to have traveled toward the far west in 97 CE, reaching Tiaozhi (Mesene), generally located near the head of the Persian Gulf, and gathering information about the land the Chinese called Daqin, generally identified with the Roman world. Han influence in the west again waned in the second century, in part because of pressures on the frontier and within the empire, and the Southern Xiongnu, settled within Han borderlands, became part of a changing frontier society whose descendants will reappear in Chapter 20.

The wider Eurasian context included the Parthian state to the west, the successors of the Yuezhi in Bactria who formed the Kushan state, and the Roman Empire farther still. Contact between the Han and Rome was almost entirely indirect. The Han sources describe Daqin from second-hand reports, and Roman writers describe the Chinese, whom they called Seres, in similarly indirect terms. A report in the Eastern Han histories mentions an embassy claiming to come from a Roman ruler in 166 CE, arriving by sea, though its nature is debated. Students should not imagine that Han and Roman merchants routinely met face to face; the trade between the two worlds passed through many hands, and intermediaries such as Parthians, Central Asian merchants, and others profited from it.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. The Han frontier was a shifting zone of interaction. Historical Evidence

Historians of the Han frontier work with three broad types of evidence, each with characteristic strengths and limits.

Transmitted court histories. The Records of the Grand Historian and the Book of Han contain chapters on the Xiongnu and on the “Western Regions,” including accounts of Zhang Qian, the war with Dayuan, and the states of Central Asia. They were written by court historians, drawing on official documents, reports of envoys, and other sources, and they reflect Han court perspectives, ideas about foreign peoples, and the purposes of their authors. They contain speeches, letters, and estimates of population and military strength that cannot always be verified. They are indispensable for narrative and policy, but they present the Xiongnu and Central Asian societies largely from outside.

Excavated frontier documents. Wooden and bamboo documents from Juyan, Dunhuang, and other sites reveal daily frontier administration: duty rosters, inventories, letters, and reports. They provide direct evidence of how garrisons operated and what soldiers and officials experienced. But they survive by chance, come from particular sites, and reflect the concerns of administrators, not the perspectives of local civilians or pastoral neighbors.

Archaeology. Excavations of Xiongnu elite tombs, Central Asian and oasis sites, garrison ruins, and trade goods provide evidence independent of Chinese writing. Burial goods show wealth, trade contacts, and cultural practice; settlement remains show irrigation and urban life. Interpretation requires caution: an object's origin does not by itself indicate how it traveled, and burials reflect funerary custom as well as daily life.

Using these sources together helps correct the biases of each. Students should ask, for every source: who made it, from what position, for what purpose, and whose perspective is absent? The Xiongnu, the oasis rulers, and the ordinary frontier residents left few or no accounts of their own, and much of their history must be reconstructed indirectly.

Primary Source Window: Sima Qian and Ban Gu on Zhang Qian's Mission

Creators: Sima Qian (Records of the Grand Historian, c. 100 BCE) and Ban Gu and collaborators (Book of Han, 1st century CE) · Date represented: c. 139–126 BCE · Type: Later historical narrative, not Zhang Qian's own surviving diary

Sima Qian, a court historian who wrote within a generation of the events, included Zhang Qian's information in his chapter on Dayuan, and the Book of Han gave him a fuller biography.

Paraphrase of the accounts (not a translated quotation):

The narratives describe an envoy sent to seek the Yuezhi as allies against the Xiongnu, his capture and long detention, his escape and journey to Ferghana and beyond, and his return with descriptions of the lands he visited: their cities, farmers, horses, and rulers. They report that he saw goods from the Han southwest in Bactria and learned that they had come by another route, and they present his information as valuable for Han strategy toward the western states.

Analysis: These accounts are our principal evidence for Zhang Qian's missions and for early Han knowledge of Central Asia. They show how the court understood the west and why it valued information about horses, states, and possible allies. But they are court narratives, shaped by later concerns, including the emperor's policies and the historian's aims; they may compress or dramatize events, and their descriptions of foreign societies rely on Zhang Qian's reports and on the assumptions of Han writers. They do not give the perspectives of the Yuezhi, the Xiongnu, or the Central Asian rulers and merchants Zhang Qian met, and they say little about the networks he encountered beyond what mattered to the Han court.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Han Expansion Create the Silk Roads or Enter and Reshape Older Networks?

The question: Popular accounts often credit Han expansion, especially Zhang Qian's missions, with “opening” the Silk Road. Historians ask how far Han actions created new exchange networks and how far they entered and altered existing ones.

Interpretation A — Han expansion as a major catalyst: Some historians emphasize that Han garrisons, the Protector General, diplomatic missions, and the demand for horses and the supply of silk stimulated exchange, provided protection and infrastructure along the Hexi Corridor and the Tarim, and integrated the eastern end of Eurasia more closely into long-distance networks. On this view, Han policy substantially expanded the volume and regularity of exchange.
Interpretation B — Han expansion as entry into and reshaping of older networks: Others emphasize that steppe, oasis, and Central Asian exchange existed long before the Han, that many actors, including the Xiongnu, Yuezhi, Bactrian and other Central Asian merchants, and Tarim oasis states, drove it, and that the Han entered a preexisting system. On this view, Han policy changed the balance of power and demand within the networks but did not create them.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely one of scale and emphasis, and it involves both interpretation and evidence: how to weigh the stimulus of Han state activity against the antiquity and autonomy of the networks, and how far the surviving evidence, weighted toward Han writing and Han-area archaeology, biases the picture.

Evidence: Support for A draws on Han administrative records, the establishment of garrisons and offices, and the spread of Han goods. Support for B draws on evidence of earlier exchange in the steppe and Central Asia, reports attributed to Zhang Qian, as preserved by Sima Qian and Ban Gu, of goods already circulating, and archaeology of non-Han sites. Many historians treat the two as complementary: the Han neither created the networks nor was irrelevant to them.

Why it matters: The question shapes how we understand agency in Eurasian history, whether change is credited to a single empire or to many actors, and how we read the surviving sources.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, evaluate which interpretation is better supported, or explain what additional evidence, such as more excavations at oasis and steppe sites, would help resolve the question.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Frontier

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources, not quotations from named individuals.

A Han frontier soldier: Posted to a remote watchtower, he depends on supplies from far away, stands watch, farms in season, and records signals. His hardships are recorded in duty rosters and letters, and his service is compelled by the registration and service system of Chapter 15.
A relocated settler household: Moved to the northwest under state direction, the family receives land and tools but faces a harsh environment, isolation from kin, and obligations to the state. For them, colonization means displacement and hard labor as well as a chance to hold land.
A Xiongnu pastoralist or leader: Part of a mobile confederation, he seeks grazing, goods, and security. Han campaigns and the loss of key corridors threaten herds and pastures; negotiated exchange offers resources; and the chanyu's authority depends on distributing them.
An oasis ruler: He governs a small kingdom whose survival depends on water, trade, and balancing stronger powers. He may accept Han titles and gifts, host garrisons, and pay for protection, while keeping options open with the Xiongnu and other neighbors.
A merchant or intermediary: He buys and sells across stages of a network, profiting from differences in prices and taxes and dependent on safe passage. He may be from an oasis or Central Asian community, and he experiences the states around him as sources of both opportunity and risk.

These perspectives should not be treated as equally powerful. The soldier and the settler were compelled by state authority, the pastoralist faced military pressure, and the ruler and merchant had more room to maneuver than those whose labor sustained the frontier.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Heqin Diplomacy to Military Expansion and Frontier Administration

What Changed

  • Han policy shifted from accommodation to sustained warfare, then to a mixed system of garrisons, colonies, offices, and diplomacy.
  • The Han acquired detailed knowledge of Central Asia and established formal administrative presence in the Hexi Corridor and the Tarim Basin.
  • The fiscal and administrative burden of the frontier increased substantially.
  • Frontier populations became more mixed through settlement, relocation, and incorporation.

What Stayed the Same

  • Diplomacy, marriage alliances, gifts, and border exchange persisted alongside war.
  • The logistical problem of supplying distant frontiers remained.
  • The Xiongnu and Inner Asian societies remained major actors with their own interests.
  • Exchange networks continued to operate through many intermediaries.

When Did Change Occur, and What Was Its Pace?

The shift began in the 130s BCE and accelerated through the campaigns of the 120s and 110s BCE, with institutional consolidation continuing into the first century BCE. It was abrupt in the decision to go to war and in the campaigns themselves, gradual in the building of garrisons, colonies, and administrative offices, uneven across regions (the Ordos and Hexi were transformed more than distant Central Asia), and contested at the Han court, where officials disagreed about the war and its costs. Eastern Han developments show that the changes were reversible.

Who experienced change most directly? Soldiers, relocated settlers, and pastoral and oasis communities in the Ordos, Hexi, and Tarim regions experienced the most direct change, as did farming households whose taxes and labor supported campaigns. Who experienced greater continuity? Farming households in the interior continued their basic pattern of household labor, though under heavier demands; merchants and intermediaries in the networks continued established practices; and pastoral communities farther from the fighting continued their way of life. Why it matters: The pattern shows that frontier policy was not a fixed inheritance but a set of choices shaped by capacity, opportunity, and resistance, and that change at the frontier affected the interior.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Did Emperor Wu Abandon Reliance on Accommodation?

Long-term causes

The persistent Xiongnu challenge along the northern frontier; a fiscal and administrative system, inherited from Qin, that could be expanded; decades of recovery under early Han rulers; and a strategic geography in which control of the Ordos and Hexi mattered.

Short-term causes

Stronger finances and stores accumulated in the early Han; growing confidence in Han military organization; court advocates of a more active policy; and interest in horses and western allies.

Immediate trigger and context

The Mayi ambush of 133 BCE, following debates at court about renewing heqin, and the availability of commanders such as Wei Qing and Huo Qubing.

Event or transformation

A shift to sustained campaigns, the establishment of commanderies in the Ordos and Hexi, the colonization of the northwest, diplomacy with Wusun and Tarim states, and the war against Dayuan.

Immediate effects

Reduced Xiongnu control over key corridors; heavy casualties and horse losses; heavy burdens on households through taxes, labor, and conscription; new offices and settlements.

Long-term consequences

A durable Han presence in the northwest and, intermittently, the Tarim Basin; greater integration into Eurasian networks; continuing fiscal strain and debate (including the 81 BCE discussions); and a model of frontier administration later dynasties would adapt.

Historical complexity

The shift cannot be reduced to the emperor's ambition. It required fiscal capacity, institutional development, strategic opportunity, court politics, and the actions of the Xiongnu and Central Asian states, and its results were mixed and contested.
Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: Life at a Northwestern Han Garrison

The following reconstruction draws on the kinds of documents recovered at Juyan and Dunhuang, and does not describe a specific individual. A small detachment lives in a watchtower of packed earth and brick, with a signal platform above and living quarters below. Soldiers take turns on watch, check the ground outside for tracks, maintain signal fires, and record each day's events. Rations of grain and other supplies are distributed according to lists; shortages are noted and reported. Some soldiers farm plots or repair walls; others escort couriers or guard granaries.

Documents preserve the concerns of ordinary people: requests for clothing and supplies, complaints about delays in pay, letters to relatives, notes about illness. Officials audit stores, punish neglect, and reward good service. Families sometimes live nearby, and some soldiers are accompanied by household members. Traders and travelers pass through, and the station provides food and lodging for authorized travelers. Contact with local peoples occurs through trade, labor, and diplomacy.

This life was very different from that of interior farmers described in Chapter 15, yet it was tied to them: the grain, cloth, and tools came from household production and taxation. Students should avoid imagining the frontier as a uniform military camp; it was a working society of farmers, soldiers, officials, and traders, with women, children, and local residents, in an environment of scarcity and risk.

Regional and Frontier Context

The northwest and north were not the only frontiers of the Han. In the south, Han forces under Emperor Wu moved against Nanyue, a state centered on the Guangzhou region in Lingnan whose ruling elite combined Han and local elements, and established commanderies there around 111 BCE. In the southwest, the Han pursued contact with polities in what is now Yunnan and Guizhou, establishing commanderies and interacting with local rulers whose relationship to the state varied. In the northeast, Han forces defeated the northern Korean kingdom of Gojoseon around 108 BCE and established commanderies, including Lelang, that remained sites of Han presence and cultural interaction for centuries. Each of these regions had its own peoples, languages, and political traditions, and the Han presence took different forms, from direct administration to distant recognition. As in the northwest, the experiences of local populations are poorly documented in Han sources.

Regional variation also shaped the northwest itself. The Ordos and Hexi were incorporated more thoroughly than the Tarim Basin, where the Han presence relied on offices, alliances, and small garrisons. Oasis states such as Loulan and Kucha maintained their own governments and often shifted alignment. Students should avoid equating territorial claims with effective control: Han influence varied greatly across frontier regions and over time.

China and the Wider World

The Han frontier connected China to a vast Eurasian world. The Xiongnu confederation was one of several steppe powers, and its history is part of Inner Asian history as well as Chinese history. Central Asian states, the Kushan successors of the Yuezhi, and the Parthians were also great regional powers, and Han diplomacy operated within a landscape of many states. Korean polities and the peoples of Lingnan and the southwest interacted with the Han in ways that later shaped the histories of Korea, Vietnam, and the southwestern highlands. Han goods reached Central Asia and beyond through intermediaries, and knowledge of the Han world spread accordingly.

Students should treat these interactions as active historical processes. Chinese diplomacy, migration, trade, and military activity shaped the frontier, but so did the decisions of the Xiongnu, oasis rulers, and Central Asian merchants. Later chapters will revisit Inner Asian and northern conquest states in the Sixteen Kingdoms and Northern Wei (Chapters 20 and 22), the Liao, Western Xia, and Jin worlds (Chapters 35 and 36), and the Mongol and Yuan periods (Chapters 38 and 39); Chapter 27 will trace the Tang-era Silk Roads, and Chapter 48 will examine the Qing incorporation of Inner Asia, each within its own historical context.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Han state, whose internal workings Chapter 15 described, met other states, peoples, environments, and networks beyond its agrarian heartland. Its central argument is that Han expansion was not a simple march westward and that the Silk Roads were not created by a single expedition. The Han frontier emerged through prolonged interaction with powerful Inner Asian states and communities, and warfare, diplomacy, marriage alliances, gift exchange, military colonies, migration, and commercial networks gradually tied the Han Empire more closely to Central Asia.

The chapter began with geography. Frontier environments, from the steppe and the Ordos to the Hexi Corridor and the Tarim Basin, structured movement, supply, and power. The frontier was inhabited by pastoral confederations, oasis communities, farmers, traders, and local rulers, and it was a shifting political zone, not a modern national border.

The Xiongnu confederation was a sophisticated Inner Asian political power. Its leaders combined military force, alliances, and control over exchange, and its relations with the Han combined war and diplomacy. Understanding the Xiongnu on their own terms explains why a mobile confederation could challenge a wealthy agrarian empire.

Early Han rulers, after the crisis at Pingcheng, adopted heqin, a diplomatic arrangement combining marriage alliance, gifts, and regulated exchange. It was not merely humiliating tribute; it served Han interests by buying time and reducing conflict, and it served Xiongnu interests by providing resources. War and diplomacy were alternating tools within the same strategy.

Under Emperor Wu, the Han shifted to sustained warfare, made possible by the recovery and fiscal expansion described in Chapters 14 and 15. Campaigns brought control of the Ordos and Hexi, but at heavy human, animal, and fiscal cost, and they were not an uninterrupted series of victories. The Xiongnu were weakened but not destroyed.

Zhang Qian's missions provided reliable information about Central Asia. His importance was political and informational; he did not discover Central Asia or create the routes he encountered. The information helped redirect Han policy, as in the costly war against Dayuan, and made networks visible to the Han court.

The Silk Roads were a decentralized network of routes and intermediaries, and silk was one good among many, serving as a gift, payment, and commodity. Object movement, person movement, and direct diplomatic contact must be distinguished, and cultural exchange included knowledge, motifs, techniques, and ideas moving in many directions.

Garrisons, colonies, and frontier society revealed what a frontier empire required: grain, watchtowers, postal stations, military farms, record-keeping, supply convoys, horses, labor, and settlement. Conquest was not the same as sustained control. Excavated documents from Juyan and Dunhuang give a rare view of daily frontier life, though they come largely from the administrative side.

Han China was one major state in a wider Eurasian system. Inner Asian societies shaped Han policy, Central Asian states pursued their own interests, and the Han state itself was changed by its frontier experience, needing more cavalry, revenue, diplomacy, intelligence, and administrative flexibility. Eastern Han developments show that Han influence in the west was contingent and reversible.

The chapter's larger significance is that it shows Han power as neither simple conquest nor simple trade, but a frontier system of warfare, diplomacy, settlement, exchange, and mutual adaptation. It prepares students for Chapter 17's cultural and intellectual history and Chapter 18's account of the stresses that eventually undermined the empire.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 16 matters because it shows that the Han Empire was not a self-contained world. Its power was tested, shaped, and extended through interaction with the Xiongnu, the oasis states, Central Asian societies, and the peoples of its other frontiers. Understanding those interactions helps students see Han history as part of a wider Eurasian story.

The frontier patterns of this chapter shaped later Chinese history. Later dynasties confronted steppe powers, administered borderlands, negotiated with neighboring states, and participated in long-distance exchange, sometimes adapting Han practices and sometimes departing from them. But students should avoid projecting a single frontier policy across two millennia: the meaning of the Hexi Corridor, the Tarim Basin, and the steppe changed with each era.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of this textbook: the interdependence of state and society, the agency of many peoples, the costs of expansion, and the need to read sources critically. Chinese civilization appears here as connected, adaptive, and contested, one participant in a Eurasian world. The Han frontier was neither simple conquest nor simple trade, but a system built from warfare, diplomacy, settlement, exchange, and mutual adaptation.

As students turn to Chapter 17, they should carry forward an understanding of the material and political systems that shaped Han life, and remember that the ideas, arts, and beliefs examined there were also shaped by contact with the wider world. Chapter 18 will then show how the pressures within this system contributed to the empire's crisis.
