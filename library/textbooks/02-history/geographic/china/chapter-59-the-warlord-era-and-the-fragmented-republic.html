Part X — Revolution, Republic, and War

The Warlord Era and the Fragmented Republic

China: A History of a Civilization · Approximately 1916–1928

Chapter Opening

After 1916 the Republic of China did not disappear. It had a capital, a flag, ministries, a parliament that met on and off, diplomats accredited abroad, and a seat among the victorious powers of 1918. What it lacked was an agreed answer to the question of who held the right to command the guns.

In the early morning of 1 July 1917, residents of Beijing woke to find that the Republic had been declared over. Dragon flags reappeared over some shops and houses, and a decree announced that the boy Puyi, who had abdicated in 1912, was again emperor of the Great Qing. Twelve days later the restoration was gone, defeated by republican troops who had marched on the capital and shelled the quarters of the general who had engineered it. Few episodes capture the character of the period more sharply. The restoration was absurd in its aims and brief in its life, yet it was carried out by a serving general with several thousand loyal soldiers, and it was ended not by a vote or a court but by another general's army. Republican institutions survived the episode, and so did the armed men who had shown that they could override them.

This chapter studies the years from the death of Yuan Shikai in June 1916 to roughly 1928, a period conventionally called the warlord era (junfa shidai). It asks why the Republic fragmented into competing military and regional governments, how those governments actually exercised authority, and why political fragmentation coexisted with commercial growth, institutional experimentation, and a flowering of intellectual life. The chapter's defining argument is that the era was both the breakdown of national political authority and the emergence of competing regional systems of military, fiscal, and administrative power. Neither half of that statement is adequate without the other.

The people affected were nearly everyone in China, but unequally. Peasants in the corridors between Beijing, Wuhan, and Mukden saw armies requisition grain, animals, and labor, and in some provinces paid land taxes years in advance. Merchants in Shanghai and Tianjin built mills, banks, and department stores under a patchwork of Chinese and foreign jurisdictions. County magistrates were appointed by one regime and pressed by another. Soldiers, drawn largely from the poor, endured hunger, irregular pay, and violence, and inflicted it on others. Students, teachers, and journalists argued about what a republic ought to be. Women in new factories, schools, and offices were beginning to occupy social space that had been narrower a generation earlier.

Much changed. The authority of the central government weakened to the point that provincial and regional commanders controlled most revenue and troops. The formal source of legitimacy shifted from the Yuan-era presidency to contested claims about constitutions, parliaments, and "legal continuity." Much also continued. The ministries, the diplomatic corps, the customs and salt administrations, the railways, the banks, the chambers of commerce, the lineage and village institutions, and the cultural traditions of the Chinese-speaking world all carried on. Even the generals themselves mostly claimed to serve the Republic, and most of the largest northern commanders fought to control the national government rather than to leave it.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Historians draw on government gazettes, military telegrams, provincial gazetteers, tax regulations, newspapers published in treaty ports, diplomatic archives, the records of the Chinese Maritime Customs, surveys by Chinese and foreign social scientists, memoirs, missionary and consular reports, and photographs. Almost every one of these sources was produced by a person with a stake in how the period was described. Reformers, Nationalists, Communists, and foreign observers all had reasons to emphasize chaos, and the name "warlord" itself was popularized as a term of condemnation. Part of the historian's task is to look behind the label.

The chapter also connects China to wider histories. China declared war on Germany in August 1917, while Britain and France, beginning in 1916, recruited roughly 140,000 Chinese laborers to work behind the Allied lines; Japanese loans and Soviet advisers, British naval power and American diplomacy, and the rules of the treaty-port system all shaped Chinese politics. Fragmentation in China was a regional problem and a global one, and Chinese factions often negotiated with foreign governments on terms of their own choosing.

Two boundaries should be kept in mind. This chapter explains the military and political geography of China in the mid-1920s and why many Chinese came to see that geography as intolerable. The May Fourth movement and the wider intellectual transformation are treated in Chapter 60, and the reorganized Nationalist and Communist movements and the Northern Expedition are treated in Chapter 61. Here the Northern Expedition appears only as an outcome, and students should resist reading the history of 1917, 1920, or 1924 as if its success were already known.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Republic's institutions survived Yuan Shikai's death while the authority to command armies fragmented.
  2. Distinguish constitutional legitimacy from effective territorial control, using the Beijing governments and the Guangzhou governments as cases.
  3. Trace the principal political and military turning points from 1916 to 1926, including the 1917 crisis, the 1920 Zhili–Anhui War, the two Zhili–Fengtian wars, and the 1924 Beijing coup.
  4. Define junfa, dujun, and clique, and explain why the Anhui, Zhili, and Fengtian alignments should not be treated as permanent political parties.
  5. Analyze how taxation, borrowing, railways, salt and customs revenue, and opium-related income shaped the strength of regional regimes.
  6. Compare the experiences of rural and urban communities, and of regions exposed to war with regions under stable regional administration.
  7. Evaluate regional state-building in Shanxi and elsewhere, distinguishing administrative capacity from benevolence or democracy.
  8. Assess the commercial, industrial, and financial development of cities such as Shanghai, Tianjin, and Wuhan during political fragmentation.
  9. Analyze how Japan, Britain, the United States, and the Soviet Union dealt with Chinese factions without reducing those factions to foreign proxies.
  10. Distinguish the Republic's territorial claims in Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet from actual administrative control.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of warlordism, and explain what evidence supports each.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Yuan Shikai's death leave a republic with working institutions but no agreed holder of military authority?
  2. What does it mean for a government to be legitimate without being able to control the armies, revenue, or provinces it claims to govern?
  3. Why did the major northern commanders mostly fight over control of Beijing instead of seceding from the Republic?
  4. How were regional armies recruited, paid, and supplied, and what did this mean for the people living near them?
  5. Why did some regional regimes build schools, police forces, and administrative systems while others devoted nearly all revenue to war?
  6. Why did commerce and industry grow in some cities while rail lines, villages, and provinces were repeatedly fought over?
  7. How did foreign loans, treaty rights, and diplomatic recognition both limit and expand the choices open to Chinese political actors?
  8. Is "age of chaos" an adequate description of the period, and what would a more precise description look like?

Historical Context

The Qing dynasty ended in February 1912 with the abdication of the child emperor Puyi, negotiated by Yuan Shikai, the commander of the modernized Beiyang (Northern) Army, and the revolutionaries who had proclaimed the Republic at Nanjing. Chapter 57 traced that transition and Chapter 58 followed its sequel: the presidency of Yuan Shikai, the election and suppression of the Nationalist Party, the dissolution of parliament in 1914, Yuan's failed attempt to establish a monarchy in 1915–1916, and his death on 6 June 1916. Students should keep one point from Chapter 58 in mind. Yuan had governed through a personal network of military officers, not through a stable party system or an institutionalized civil bureaucracy. When the center of that network disappeared, there was no replacement for it.

The late Qing had already transformed the relationship between the central government and the provinces. After the mid-nineteenth-century rebellions (Chapter 53), regional armies and provincial financial networks had grown in importance, and the New Policies of the 1900s (Chapter 56) had created new provincial assemblies, new modern-style armies, and new fiscal arrangements. When the dynasty fell, provinces declared independence from the Qing one by one in 1911 (Chapter 57). The Republic that emerged thus inherited a state in which provincial authorities already controlled much of the revenue and in which armies were tied to commanders by personal loyalty as much as to the nation by law.

The geographic setting matters. China's most densely populated regions, the North China Plain and the Yangtze valley, were linked by a small number of railways, the most important of which ran north to south between Beijing and Hankou and between Tianjin and Pukou (opposite Nanjing), and northeast between Beijing and Mukden (Shenyang). Armies moved along these lines and fought for them. Beyond them, the interior depended on rivers, roads, and mule tracks. Sichuan, ringed by mountains and reachable from the east by the Yangtze gorges, could be isolated from the politics of the north for long periods. The southern coast, especially Guangdong, had long maritime connections with Hong Kong and Southeast Asia. Manchuria, a region of fertile plains with a growing migrant population, was crossed by railways controlled partly by Japan and partly by Russia (later the Soviet Union). The geographic meaning of "China" in this period also remained contested: the Republic claimed the territory of the Qing empire, including Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet, but did not administer all of it (Section 59.20).

Economically, most Chinese were farmers. They paid taxes in silver or copper or, increasingly, in paper currency; they marketed grain, cotton, and other products through local markets; and they relied on lineage, village, and market-town institutions for credit, dispute resolution, and security. The treaty-port world of the coast and the Yangtze, shaped by the treaty system described in Chapter 52, lay beside this agrarian economy rather than replacing it. Foreign powers held extraterritorial privileges, administered concessions, and, through the Chinese Maritime Customs Service, supervised the collection of tariff revenue that served as security for foreign loans and indemnities. This arrangement gave the Beijing government some stable income at the price of foreign supervision.

Finally, the international moment mattered. Europe's great powers were absorbed by the First World War from 1914 until 1918, which reduced European competition in Chinese markets and left Japan, with the Twenty-One Demands of 1915 and its seizure of German holdings in Shandong, the most assertive foreign power in the region. After the war, the Washington Conference (1921–1922) redrew the diplomatic order of the Pacific, and the Soviet Union emerged as a new player. Chinese politicians and generals operated inside this world and sometimes used it skillfully.

Historical Evidence

The sources for the warlord era are abundant, and most of them are slanted in predictable ways. Government records and gazettes from the Beijing ministries preserve decrees, appointments, budgets, and laws, but they describe what the capital claimed to govern, not necessarily what it controlled. Military telegrams were issued openly as circular telegrams (tongdian) addressed to the nation and the press, so they are propaganda as much as communication, designed to portray a commander as the defender of peace, the constitution, or the people. Local gazetteers (difangzhi) compiled at the county and provincial level, including those compiled in the 1910s and 1920s, preserve information about taxation, schools, population, and local events, though compilers were usually members of the local elite.

Tax records, budgets, and provincial financial regulations reveal how regional regimes raised money, although many extra levies were improvised and left few records. Newspapers, especially Shenbao in Shanghai and the English-language press of the treaty ports, reported military developments and public opinion, often under the constraints of foreign jurisdiction or local censorship. Diplomatic archives, from British, American, Japanese, and Soviet sources, are rich in information about leaders and negotiations, but they reflect foreign priorities and prejudices. The records of the Chinese Maritime Customs Service and the Sino-Foreign Salt Inspectorate provide unusually systematic statistics.

Social surveys carried out by Chinese and foreign researchers in the 1920s and early 1930s document rural land tenure, household budgets, and village economies, though they were often conducted after the period under study. Memoirs by participants, including former officers, officials, and missionaries, add vivid detail but were frequently written years later and for self-justification. Photographs, maps, and film record cities, armies, and rural life, but they too were taken by particular observers for particular audiences. Students should keep one rule in mind: the label "warlord" was usually applied by opponents, and a source that uses it carries a judgment.

Section 59.1

China After Yuan Shikai: The Crisis of Republican Authority

Yuan Shikai died of uremia on 6 June 1916, three months after abandoning the monarchy he had proclaimed in December 1915 (Chapter 58). The attempt to become emperor had alienated many of his own officers as well as the provinces. Yunnan had rebelled in December 1915, other southwestern provinces had followed, and several of his senior commanders had refused to support him. In the final months of his life he governed as a president whose subordinates were openly hedging.

Succession was determined by the constitutional framework. Li Yuanhong, the vice president, became president, and Duan Qirui, Yuan's most influential general and a former war minister, was premier. Li had a modest reputation as a moderate and was widely seen as a figure around whom different groups could agree. He had little independent military power, however, and Duan commanded the support of much of the Beiyang officer corps. From the start, therefore, the national government divided authority between a president with legitimacy and a premier with followers.

A second structural problem was military decentralization. In July 1916 the government replaced the Yuan-era titles of provincial military governor (jiangjun) and civil governor (xunanshi) with new ones: the dujun (military governor) and the shengzhang (civil governor). The change was meant to restore the distinction between civil and military authority, but in practice a military governor usually held real power in his province, controlled its troops, and influenced appointments and taxation. The central government confirmed appointments that it could not easily make or remove.

A third problem was money. The central government's revenue depended heavily on the customs and salt administrations, whose proceeds were largely committed to repaying foreign loans and indemnities, and on loans negotiated with foreign banks. Land taxes, the largest source of revenue in most provinces, were retained by provinces or by local military commanders. In May 1916, even before Yuan's death, the Bank of China and the Bank of Communications had been allowed to suspend the conversion of their banknotes into silver, a measure that shook public confidence in paper currency in the capital and the northern provinces. A government that could not pay its soldiers or officials on time found it difficult to command either.

Despite these weaknesses, the Republic had resources of legitimacy. The 1912 Provisional Constitution and the 1913 parliament commanded respect among many politicians, journalists, and members of the commercial elite, who had experienced Yuan's authoritarianism and wanted a return to constitutional government. Students should not treat the next decade as a simple story of institutional collapse. It is more accurate to say that institutions continued to function in a political environment where armed commanders could and often did overrule them.

Section 59.2

The Beijing Government and the Struggle to Restore Constitutional Rule

One of Li Yuanhong's first acts as president was to restore the 1912 Provisional Constitution and to recall the parliament elected in 1913, which Yuan had dissolved in January 1914. The parliament reconvened in August 1916. Feng Guozhang, a senior Beiyang commander based at Nanjing, was chosen as vice president in October. Li and Duan thus presided over a government that claimed to be the restored constitutional Republic after the interruption of Yuan's personal rule.

The restoration solved one problem and created others. The parliament contained members of several groupings that had formed after 1913, and its debates about a permanent constitution quickly became entangled in disputes over the powers of the president, the premier, and the provinces. Should the premier, as head of cabinet, be answerable to parliament? Should the president be able to dismiss him? Should the provinces have their own constitutions and substantial autonomy? These were genuine constitutional questions, comparable to those debated in other countries adopting parliamentary systems, but in China they occurred in a setting where the army could change the answer.

Li and Duan disagreed from the start about how the government should operate. Duan interpreted the premier's office as the center of executive authority, which suited his control of the military and his relationships with provincial commanders. Li, supported by many parliamentarians, favored a president with a more active role in appointments. The disagreement was personal, but it also reflected a recurring question in Republican politics: whether legitimate authority derived from the president, from parliament, from the cabinet, or from the military power on which each of them depended.

Beijing's claim to national legitimacy rested on several foundations: the constitutional succession, recognition by foreign governments, control of the ministries, and the international acceptance of its representatives. This is why even militarists who disregarded the government's orders usually wanted to control it. Holding Beijing conferred access to foreign loans, diplomatic recognition, customs and salt revenues, and the symbolic authority of the national state. A commander who simply declared independence from the capital forfeited these advantages.

The limits of constitutional procedure were already apparent. When parliament, president, and premier disagreed, the practical question was usually which of them could count on the loyalty of troops stationed near the capital or of provincial governors who might declare independence. The crisis of 1917 would show how decisively that practical question mattered.

Section 59.3

The Constitutional Crisis of 1917 and Zhang Xun's Qing Restoration

The immediate cause of the 1917 crisis was a foreign-policy dispute. In February 1917, after Germany announced unrestricted submarine warfare, the United States severed relations with Germany and encouraged other neutral countries, including China, to do the same. China broke relations with Germany in March. Duan Qirui and his supporters, aware that entry into the war might bring Allied support, a seat at the postwar settlement, and access to Japanese loans, pressed to declare war. Li Yuanhong, many parliamentarians, and some political figures outside government were doubtful or opposed. Some feared that the war would give Duan an excuse to expand his army, while others suspected that Japan would profit from China's involvement.

The dispute escalated in the spring. In May, after a pro-war demonstration around parliament, several cabinet ministers resigned, and Li dismissed Duan as premier. Duan left for Tianjin, and a number of provincial military governors in the north declared that they were no longer subject to the central government. The sequence demonstrated that the Beijing government could not dismiss a premier supported by the provincial commanders without those commanders breaking with it.

Li then made a fateful decision. He invited Zhang Xun, a general of the old school who had served the Qing, kept his army's queues in defiance of the Republic, and commanded troops at Xuzhou, to mediate between the president and the provincial commanders. Zhang Xun entered Beijing in June with several thousand soldiers. He demanded that Li dissolve parliament, and Li, under pressure, did so on 13 June. On 1 July, Zhang Xun, together with the court scholar Kang Youwei and some other monarchists, declared the restoration of Puyi, who was then eleven years old.

The restoration collapsed quickly. Duan Qirui organized an anti-restoration army at Matang near Tianjin, and republican forces approached the capital. Airplanes dropped a few small bombs on the Forbidden City, a pioneering and ineffective use of military aviation. Zhang Xun's troops were defeated, and he took refuge in the Dutch legation. By 12 July the restoration had ended. Li Yuanhong, whom Zhang Xun had humiliated, resigned, and Feng Guozhang became acting president. Duan Qirui returned as premier, and he was now widely credited as the savior of the Republic.

Students should be careful not to confuse the 1917 episode with Yuan Shikai's earlier monarchy. Yuan's Hongxian empire of 1915–1916 was an attempt by the president of the Republic to convert his office into a hereditary throne, with a new imperial name and ceremonies. Zhang Xun's restoration was an attempt to bring back the Qing, and its supporters were a small group of loyalists, not a mass movement. Its practical significance lay elsewhere. It showed that republican institutions could be suspended by a general with a small army, and it gave Duan the opportunity to claim that he alone had saved the Republic, which he used to justify refusing to reinstate the 1912 constitution and the old parliament. He argued that the Republic had been "re-founded" and that a new provisional parliament should be organized. This decision was bitterly contested and helped produce the division described in the next section.

Section 59.4

The North–South Division and the Constitutional Protection Movement

Duan's refusal to restore the old parliament made many of its members into opponents. Large numbers of them, along with Sun Yat-sen and other supporters of constitutional government, traveled south to Guangzhou in July 1917. In August, they convened an extraordinary session of parliament there, and in September they established a military government that elected Sun Yat-sen as its generalissimo (dayuanshuai). The movement described its purpose as protecting the Provisional Constitution and the legal parliament against Duan's militarist government, and it became known as the Constitutional Protection Movement (hufa yundong).

The Guangzhou government's claims were serious, but its power was modest. Its military strength depended on provincial armies from Guangxi (under Lu Rongting) and Yunnan (under Tang Jiyao), whose commanders had their own regional interests and who valued Sun's constitutional claims chiefly when these served their own positions. The regime controlled little territory beyond parts of Guangdong and Guangxi, and its revenue was limited. In 1918, Sun, frustrated that the southern generals had reorganized the government into a collective directorate and sidelined him, resigned and left Guangzhou. This was a significant lesson: possessing constitutional arguments did not by itself confer authority over armies.

The northern and southern governments each claimed to be the legal Republic. Beijing could assert continuity of administration, recognition by the powers, and control of the ministries. Guangzhou could assert fidelity to the 1912 constitution and the 1913 parliament. In 1918–1919, a peace conference held in Shanghai attempted to reconcile the two sides, but the northern and southern delegations could not agree on the status of parliament or on the apportionment of power, and talks ended in 1919.

The regional politics of the south were complex. Guangdong, the richest province, was controlled at various times by Guangxi troops, by the Guangdong commander Chen Jiongming, and by the movements around Sun Yat-sen. Chen Jiongming drove the Guangxi forces out of Guangzhou in 1920, invited Sun back, and then fell out with him in 1922 over strategy, with Chen preferring to consolidate Guangdong and promote provincial self-government while Sun wanted to use Guangdong as a base for a campaign against the north. The reorganization of Sun's movement after 1922, including Soviet assistance and the creation of the Whampoa Military Academy, is a subject for Chapter 61.

For students, the key point is that the "north–south division" was not a simple conflict between two unified blocs. Northern politics involved competing cliques, and southern politics involved competing provincial commanders, politicians, and revolutionaries. What the two sides shared was a vocabulary of legality and republican legitimacy, along with the practical reality that neither could impose its version on the other.

Section 59.5

Regional Military Governments and the Rise of Warlord Politics

The word warlord in English translates the Chinese junfa (军阀), literally "military clique" or "military faction." It entered widespread use in China in the later 1910s and early 1920s, borrowed from Japanese usage, and was applied to commanders whom critics regarded as using military force to serve personal and factional interests at the nation's expense. Historians continue to use the term, with caution, because it is embedded in the sources and because it points to something real: the dominance of regional and personal military power over the apparatus of the national state.

Historical Vocabulary
Junfa, Dujun, Clique, and Military Governor

Junfa (军阀) was a term of political criticism before it became a historical category. It implied that a commander's authority rested on personal military following, not on legitimate public office. Many people called junfa by their critics regarded themselves as legitimate officials, patriots, or reformers, and most held formal government titles.

Dujun (督军, "military governor") was the official title of the provincial commander from 1916 until the mid-1920s, usually paired with a civil governor, the shengzhang. The title implied subordination to Beijing, but the dujun often chose or approved the civil governor and controlled the province's armed forces and, in many cases, its revenue. Clique (xi, 系) refers to a network of commanders linked by shared military education, regional origin, patronage, marriage, or common interest, such as the Anhui, Zhili, and Fengtian cliques. A clique was not a political party with a platform or membership roll, and its boundaries shifted as alliances and rivalries changed.

Students should treat these terms as tools for describing patterns of power, not as precise categories. Using "warlord" as a simple synonym for "bad general" would remove precisely the variation that the chapter seeks to explain.

The institutional background for warlord politics lay in the late Qing and early Republican armies. Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang had created regional armies in the nineteenth century (Chapter 53), whose officers were loyal to their commanders as much as to the court. The New Army reforms after 1901 created modern-trained units, and Yuan Shikai's Beiyang Army became the most important of them. Its officers were trained in military schools in Tianjin and Baoding, and many of them came from similar regional backgrounds and shared patron–client ties. After Yuan's death, those ties persisted in the absence of a central commander, and they became the basis of regional power.

Why did regional armies become politically independent? Several factors combined. First, the central government could not pay or supply its armies directly, so officers relied on provincial revenue and their own arrangements. Second, soldiers' loyalty ran to their immediate commanders, who recruited, paid, promoted, and protected them. Third, the political uncertainty after 1916 gave commanders reason to hold on to forces as insurance. Fourth, the contest for control of the capital encouraged each commander to enlarge his army to avoid being outmatched. The resulting pattern was an arms race funded by whatever revenue could be extracted.

Commanders also differed in their aims. Some, like Zhang Zuolin in Manchuria or Yan Xishan in Shanxi, built regional bases and sought to defend them, though both also intervened in national politics. Others, like Duan Qirui and later Wu Peifu, pursued control of the national government and the unification of China under their own leadership. Still others, such as the leaders of Sichuan's rival armies, were so preoccupied with local rivalries that they rarely looked beyond their provincial borders. The label "warlord" covers all of these, and a student who treats them identically will misunderstand the period.

Section 59.6

The Beiyang Army and the Formation of Competing Cliques

After 1916 the officer corps of the Beiyang Army fractured into three principal alignments. The Anhui clique took its name from Duan Qirui's native province, though its power rested more on Duan's control of the central government and its military and financial networks than on a regional base. Its prominent figures included Duan, his protégé Xu Shuzheng, and a number of generals and officials who owed their positions to him. The Zhili clique was named for the northern province around Beijing (Zhili, later Hebei). Its early leader was Feng Guozhang, and after his death in 1919 its leadership passed to Cao Kun, based in Baoding and Tianjin, and to his able subordinate Wu Peifu, who built a strong base in central China. The Fengtian clique was led by Zhang Zuolin, who rose to control Fengtian province (now Liaoning) and then the whole of Manchuria, with his capital at Mukden.

These names require care. They were labels applied by journalists, politicians, and later historians to shifting networks, and the membership of each network was fluid. Commanders changed sides, and alliances were formed and abandoned. Duan Qirui and Zhang Zuolin, who fought on opposite sides in 1920, were allies in 1924. Feng Yuxiang, who began his career in the Zhili network, turned against it in 1924. Students should not imagine these cliques as political parties with programs or as stable blocs with permanent boundaries. They were coalitions of personal, regional, and financial relationships.

The Anhui clique's strength came less from a territory than from control of the Beijing government, which gave it access to foreign loans and bureaucratic appointments, and when it lost the capital in 1920 it lost most of its resources.

Section 59.7

Duan Qirui, Anhui Dominance, and Military-Financial Politics

From his return to office in July 1917, Duan Qirui dominated the Beijing government for three years. He refused to restore the old parliament, and in 1917–1918 his government convened a provisional council and then arranged elections for a new parliament. The assembly chosen in 1918 was dominated by members associated with the so-called Anfu Club, a political grouping linked to Duan's supporters, and it elected Xu Shichang, a senior civilian statesman, as president in September 1918. Xu was a respected elder with limited power, so Duan and his associates remained the effective center of government.

Duan's government declared war on Germany on 14 August 1917. China's own participation was largely financial and diplomatic: it seized German assets in China, suspended indemnity payments to Germany and Austria, and gained the status of an Allied belligerent. The much larger human connection with the war had begun earlier and followed a different path. From 1916, British and French authorities, working through their own recruiting agencies and contracts, hired roughly 140,000 Chinese laborers in total (estimates vary, with roughly 100,000 or more under British arrangements and about 40,000 under French ones) to dig trenches, repair roads, load supplies, and clear battlefields. Recruitment therefore preceded China's declaration of war and was not a deployment ordered by the Chinese government, although Beijing permitted it and later cited the workers' service in its diplomacy. Mortality is hard to state precisely: the Commonwealth War Graves Commission records nearly 2,000 deaths in the British Chinese Labour Corps, while totals for the French scheme, and for deaths from disease and other causes outside the commission's records, are uncertain.

The war also gave Duan a justification for expanding his army. With Japanese support, he organized a "War Participation Army" (canzhan jun), which later became the Frontier Defense Army under Xu Shuzheng. The funding came partly from loans negotiated by Nishihara Kamezō, an agent of the Japanese prime minister Terauchi Masatake. These "Nishihara loans" of 1917–1918, totaling roughly 145 million yen, were extended to Chinese government agencies and financed telegraph lines, railways, and military supplies. They gave Japan financial and commercial leverage, and they became notorious when the extent of the arrangements became known. In 1918, China and Japan also signed secret military agreements providing for joint defense against possible enemy action in Siberia and the Russian Far East.

Duan used the loans and the government's authority to build his own coalition. Troops paid by central funds were often loyal to his officers, and positions in the bureaucracy and the parliament were distributed to supporters. The method alienated other Beiyang commanders, who saw that the Anhui clique was using national institutions to strengthen a single network. Zhili generals complained that Duan favored his own followers, southern leaders accused him of subverting the constitution, and students and merchants were angered by his Japanese ties. The popular protests of May 1919, studied in Chapter 60, were directed partly against officials associated with this policy.

Xu Shuzheng's Frontier Defense Army also became an instrument of policy in Mongolia. In 1919, Xu occupied Outer Mongolia and pressured its leaders to abandon the autonomy they had won after 1911, a story told in Section 59.20. The overall pattern was one of military expansion tied to foreign borrowing, with a clique using the state's apparatus to build itself. It was a recipe for opposition, and it eventually produced the coalition that defeated Duan in 1920.

Section 59.8

The Zhili–Anhui War of 1920

By 1920, the coalition against Duan Qirui had grown. Cao Kun and Wu Peifu of the Zhili clique resented Anhui dominance of the government, Zhang Zuolin of Manchuria had his own reasons for opposing Duan's influence, and many politicians and merchants disliked his Japanese connections. Wu Peifu, commanding forces in Hunan during the inconclusive fighting between northern and southern troops, withdrew his army northward in 1920 and issued public telegrams criticizing Duan's policies. The withdrawal was both a military and a political act: it exposed the weakness of Duan's southern campaign and signaled that the Zhili commanders were ready to break with him.

The immediate conflict arose over the status of Xu Shuzheng's Frontier Defense Army and over appointments. Zhili leaders demanded the dismissal of Xu and the disbanding of the army, and Duan resisted. In July 1920, fighting broke out south of Beijing and around Zhuozhou. Duan's forces, reorganized as the "Pacification Army" (Dingguo jun), faced Zhili troops under Wu Peifu and Cao Kun's command, with Zhang Zuolin's Fengtian troops entering the contest on the Zhili side. The fighting lasted only about a week, from 14 to 18 July. Duan's army was defeated, in part because many of its officers were reluctant to fight and because Wu's forces were better coordinated.

The causes of the war ran deeper than the immediate quarrel. Wu Peifu had been commanding Zhili troops in Hunan against southern forces since 1918, a campaign Duan ordered but that left Wu's army to bear the costs while Anhui-aligned units received supplies and appointments. Wu came to see the southern war as a drain that served Duan's interests, and in 1920 he withdrew his forces and issued telegrams urging peace with the south and criticizing the government's policies, appealing to a public weary of the wars and suspicious of Japanese influence. Xu Shuzheng's Mongolian expedition, which ended in embarrassment, and the unpopularity of the Anfu-dominated parliament weakened the Anhui clique's claim to represent national interests, and the protests of 1919 (Chapter 60) had made association with pro-Japanese officials a political liability. Zhang Zuolin, who had his own reasons to prefer a weaker central government and a larger share of influence in Beijing, agreed to join the anti-Anhui coalition, and Cao Kun in Baoding served as its political coordinator. The coalition formed through telegrams, meetings, and secret understandings, which shows how much of the politics of these years took place in negotiation rather than on battlefields.

Duan resigned, and Xu Shuzheng took refuge in the Japanese legation. The victors purged the Anfu politicians from the government and issued orders for the arrest of several of the clique's leaders, a pattern of proscription that later regimes in Beijing would repeat against defeated rivals. For the next two years, Beijing was controlled by a Zhili–Fengtian coalition: Cao Kun's group held central China and the capital region, and Zhang Zuolin's influence reached into Beijing's government while his base remained in Manchuria. Xu Shichang continued as president until 1922.

The significance of the war lay in its demonstration of how national power now worked. The Anhui clique had controlled the government's apparatus, but when its military position weakened, it fell quickly. Control of Beijing depended on a coalition of armies, not on constitutional office, and a coalition could be broken if its members found it advantageous. Also noteworthy was the brevity and limited scale of the fighting: the war was decided in days, with relatively small armies and limited civilian casualties compared with later conflicts, though it caused hardship in the districts it touched. Students should note that these wars were not fought for the Anhui and Zhili "causes" in any ideological sense. The parties were competing for control of institutions, revenue, and prestige, and each used the language of peace, legality, and national interest.

Section 59.9

The Zhili–Fengtian Wars of 1922 and 1924

The Zhili–Fengtian coalition did not last. Wu Peifu, who had become the most prominent military leader in the Zhili group, and Zhang Zuolin disagreed about control of the Beijing government, about the sharing of customs and railway revenues, and about the political direction of the country. Wu also began to promote a program of restoring the 1912 constitution, reconvening the old parliament, and reunifying the country through a civilian-led national conference, a position that won him some public support among constitutionalists and commercial interests. Zhang preferred a government more responsive to his interests, and he was backed by Japanese contacts and Manchuria's economic strength.

In April–May 1922, the First Zhili–Fengtian War was fought in the area south of Beijing and along the railways near Changxindian and Liangxiang. Wu Peifu's forces defeated Zhang Zuolin's armies, and Zhang withdrew into Manchuria, where he declared the autonomy of the Three Eastern Provinces. Wu then pressed for the restoration of Li Yuanhong as president and of the old parliament; Xu Shichang resigned in June, and Li returned. The constitutional arrangement did not last, however. Li, as before, had little independent power, and Cao Kun, who wanted the presidency himself, worked to undermine him. In June 1923 Li was forced out after the capital's police went on strike for lack of pay and after the Zhili group stopped paying government expenses. Li left for Tianjin, taking the presidential seals with him, so that for a time the government lacked the means to certify its own acts.

Wu Peifu's reputation was complicated. He styled himself a defender of constitutional government and national unity, but he also used force against labor. In February 1923, when railway workers on the Beijing–Hankou line attempted to form a union and held a conference at Zhengzhou, Wu's troops violently suppressed the movement. About three dozen workers were killed in what became known as the February Seventh incident. The episode shaped the relationship between organized labor and northern militarists and influenced the strategies of the Communist and Nationalist organizers who had been active among railway workers.

The Second Zhili–Fengtian War began in September 1924. Its immediate trigger was a conflict between Jiangsu and Zhejiang over control of the Shanghai area, the Jiangsu–Zhejiang War (September 1924), in which Zhili-aligned Jiangsu forces fought Zhejiang forces and brought the contest over China's richest commercial city into the Zhili–Fengtian rivalry. Zhang Zuolin then launched an offensive against the Zhili forces at Shanhaiguan, the pass connecting Manchuria with North China. Wu Peifu commanded the Zhili forces there, and Feng Yuxiang, one of his subordinates, was sent to the Rehe front. The war quickly turned when Feng, who had grown dissatisfied with Cao Kun and Wu, withdrew his troops toward Beijing. That decision is the subject of Section 59.10.

Railways governed the logistics of both wars. Armies depended on rail transport to concentrate forces, move artillery, and supply troops, and commanders seized rolling stock and used railway revenues as military income. Shanhaiguan, where the mountains meet the sea, was a chokepoint, and the Beijing–Mukden line carried supplies for Zhang's army. Civilian passenger and freight service was repeatedly interrupted, and the damage to the rail network affected commerce far beyond the battlefield. The consequences of each war were political as much as military: the 1920 war moved Beijing from Anhui to Zhili–Fengtian control, the 1922 war made Zhili dominant, and the 1924 war ended Zhili supremacy, a reminder that no alignment was permanent.

Section 59.10

The Beijing Coup of 1924 and the Reorganization of Northern Politics

Feng Yuxiang was an unusual figure among the northern commanders. Born into a poor family, he rose through the Beiyang ranks, became known for strict discipline in his troops, adopted Christianity (and became known in the foreign press as the "Christian General"), and cultivated an image of austerity and concern for the common soldier. His troops were expected to avoid looting, perform labor, and receive basic education, though like all armies of the period they depended on irregular revenue. In 1924, Feng was serving under Cao Kun and Wu Peifu, and he had grown increasingly resentful of Cao's presidency, which had been secured in 1923 by what many regarded as a corrupt election (Section 59.13).

On 23 October 1924, with the Zhili army fighting Zhang Zuolin's forces at Shanhaiguan, Feng's troops marched back into Beijing, seized the city, and placed Cao Kun under house arrest. Feng posted a proclamation announcing that he would fight no longer and wanted peace negotiated, and his associates soon spoke of a committee government and a conference of leaders including Zhang Zuolin, Sun Yat-sen, and Duan Qirui. Feng allied with Zhang and Duan. The coup stripped Wu Peifu of his base: Wu's forces collapsed, and he retreated south by sea. On 5 November, Feng's government revised the "Articles of Favorable Treatment" that had allowed Puyi to remain in the Forbidden City after 1912, and the former emperor and his household were expelled. Puyi found shelter first with his father and then in the Japanese legation, and in 1925 he moved to the Japanese concession in Tianjin, a move with consequences for Manchuria in the 1930s (Chapter 64).

The new arrangement was a compromise among the victors. In November 1924, Duan Qirui became "provisional chief executive" (linshi zhizheng), a title that avoided the word president and the constitutional framework of 1923. Zhang Zuolin controlled Manchuria and expanded his influence toward Tianjin and eastern China, and Feng reorganized his forces as the Guominjun (National People's Army), whose base was in the northwest, with Soviet advisers and assistance. Feng also invited Sun Yat-sen to Beijing to discuss national unification. Sun, already ill, traveled north and died of cancer in Beijing in March 1925. The conference Duan convened to settle national affairs had little result.

The compromise soon unraveled. In late 1925, a subordinate of Zhang Zuolin, Guo Songling, rebelled against him with Feng's encouragement, and Zhang survived only because Japanese forces in Manchuria discouraged the rebels' advance. Wu Peifu, recovering his position in central China, joined with Zhang in a surprising alliance against Feng's Guominjun, the enemy they now both feared. In March 1926, a demonstration in Beijing protesting foreign pressure on China ended in the shooting of protesters outside Duan's office. About forty-seven people were killed, including students, and the event caused widespread outrage. Feng withdrew from Beijing in April, Zhang Zuolin's troops entered the capital, and Duan Qirui fled. Beijing was subsequently governed by cabinets under the influence of Zhang and Wu until Zhang became, in June 1927, the head of a "military government" claiming national authority.

Section 59.11

Regional Powers Beyond the Beiyang Cliques

A history that followed only the northern cliques would give a false picture, because much of China was governed by commanders whose attention was elsewhere. Yan Xishan in Shanxi was the most durable of them. He emerged as the province's military leader during the 1911 Revolution, had consolidated military and civil authority by 1917, and held effective control of Shanxi, with interruptions, for nearly four decades. Yan kept Shanxi largely out of the northern wars, cultivated a reputation as a pragmatic modernizer, and intervened in national politics only when he judged it safe (Section 59.17).

In the southwest, Sichuan was divided among rival commanders whose armies controlled "defense zones" (fangqu) and competed for revenue and territory. Names such as Liu Xiang, Yang Sen, Deng Xihou, and Liu Wenhui appear in the record, along with older figures. Contemporaries counted hundreds of armed conflicts in the 1910s and 1920s, although the scale of each was generally small. Yunnan was ruled by Tang Jiyao from 1913 until 1927, who commanded an army that intervened in Sichuan, Guizhou, and Guangxi and who supported Sun Yat-sen's government at Guangzhou at times. Guizhou, poorer and more isolated, was governed by shifting commanders and relied heavily on opium revenue.

Guangxi was dominated until 1920 by Lu Rongting, whose "old Guangxi clique" ruled Guangdong for a time, and then by a younger group, the "new Guangxi clique" of Li Zongren, Bai Chongxi, and Huang Shaohong, which unified the province by 1925 and then joined the Nationalists. Guangdong was governed by Chen Jiongming in 1920–1922, who combined military power with an interest in local self-government, and then, after complex struggles, by forces aligned with Sun Yat-sen (Chapter 61). Hunan experimented with a provincial constitution in 1922 under the banner of provincial self-rule, part of the "federalist" movement (lianshengzizhi) that argued that China should be reorganized as a federation of self-governing provinces. Advocates included intellectuals, some provincial elites, and some commanders, and critics, including Sun Yat-sen, saw federalism as a cover for militarist autonomy.

These regional powers matter because they show that the political geography of China was not simply north versus south. The politics of Chengdu, Kunming, Guangzhou, Changsha, and Taiyuan were driven by local rivalries, revenue sources, and security concerns as well as by the struggle for Beijing. A student who looks only at the capital sees only a fraction of the political life of the Republic.

Section 59.12

The Beijing Governments: Presidents, Cabinets, and Political Legitimacy

Between 1916 and 1928, Beijing was home to a succession of presidents, provisional heads of government, and cabinets. Li Yuanhong, Feng Guozhang, Xu Shichang, Li Yuanhong again, Cao Kun, and Duan Qirui each held the highest office at different times, and dozens of cabinets came and went, many of them lasting only weeks or months. This instability is often cited as evidence of failure. It is also true that the ministries of foreign affairs, finance, communications, education, and others continued to operate, that civil servants mostly stayed in post, and that the machinery of government retained a measure of continuity beneath the changing political surface.

The Beijing government's most significant functions were arguably in the field of diplomacy. China participated in the Paris Peace Conference of 1919 and, though its delegation refused to sign the Treaty of Versailles because of the Shandong provisions (Chapter 60), it became a member of the League of Nations through its treaty with Austria and was elected to the League's Council in 1920. At the Washington Conference of 1921–1922, Chinese diplomats secured a treaty confirming China's territorial integrity in principle, an agreement on the return of Shandong to Chinese administration, and the promise of a future conference on tariffs. Diplomats such as Wellington Koo (Gu Weijun) and Alfred Sao-ke Sze (Shi Zhaoji) were skilled negotiators and represented a generation of professional Chinese diplomats who worked for Beijing even as the government itself changed.

Behind the changing heads of state, ministries depended on a corps of civil servants, many trained in the late Qing or abroad, who maintained records, negotiated with foreign missions, ran the post and telegraph systems, and administered what remained of national finance. Their conditions were precarious. The Ministry of Finance relied on domestic bonds sold to Chinese banks, advances from the Bank of China and Bank of Communications, and the surplus from customs and salt after foreign obligations were met, and it often failed to pay salaries on time. In 1921 and again later, professors and staff of national universities in Beijing struck over unpaid wages, and in 1923 the capital's police, whose pay was also in arrears, struck during the pressure on Li Yuanhong. A government unable to pay its own servants depended on the cooperation of whoever controlled the banks and the garrison, which helped make Beijing's politics so responsive to military coalitions.

Recognition was the other side of the same coin. Because foreign governments dealt with the Beijing government as China's government, each change of leadership raised the question of whether the powers would continue to do so. After Feng's coup and Duan's accession as provisional chief executive in 1924, the American, British, and other legations discussed in detail whether to accord de facto recognition to the provisional government, weighing Duan's conservative reputation against worries about the influence of Feng, Sun Yat-sen, and the Soviet Union, as the published U.S. diplomatic correspondence shows. Such deliberations show that foreign recognition was a political resource that shaped, but did not determine, Chinese outcomes.

The difference between nominal national authority and actual control was therefore central. The Beijing government negotiated treaties, appointed ambassadors, and issued laws, and foreign governments recognized it as China's government. It also controlled the customs revenue held in foreign banks after debts were paid, though access to the surplus was a source of constant dispute. But it often could not collect taxes in the provinces, could not dismiss a dujun, and sometimes could not pay its own employees. In the early 1920s, government employees, including teachers at national universities, went unpaid for months, and teachers' strikes and demonstrations took place in the capital.

For students, the practical lesson is that "legitimacy" has several dimensions. The Beijing government had international legitimacy, legal continuity, and bureaucratic competence, but it lacked fiscal and military control. The Guangzhou governments had constitutional and revolutionary legitimacy but limited resources. Individual regional commanders had armies and revenue, but their claims to national authority were rarely accepted. The contest over these different kinds of authority is what gave the period its character.

Section 59.13

Constitutions, Elections, and the Limits of Republican Politics

For most of these years the Republic operated under the disputed 1912 Provisional Constitution, which various groups tried to replace; a formally permanent constitution was promulgated only in October 1923, and its authority was contested from the start. In between, governments relied on a succession of provisional arrangements: the restored 1912 framework from 1916, the provisional council and new parliament created under Duan Qirui in 1917–1918, rival drafts, and the organic laws of the Guangzhou governments. A constitutional drafting committee worked in 1913 and produced a draft, the "Temple of Heaven" draft, which was never promulgated. Further drafts were prepared in 1916–1917 and 1920s, and each became a political battleground. Disputes concerned the balance among president, premier, and parliament, the status of provinces (centralized or federal), and the guarantee of rights. The 1920s federalist movement, with its provincial constitutions in Hunan, Zhejiang, and Guangdong, raised the possibility of a federal China, though in practice provincial constitutions were often adopted by commanders who sought to legitimize local power.

The 1923 election of Cao Kun is the best-known episode. After forcing Li Yuanhong from office in June 1923, Cao's supporters arranged a presidential election by the parliament, which convened in Beijing in October. Contemporary newspapers and later accounts alleged that members were offered payments, commonly reported at several thousand yuan each, to attend and vote for Cao. The election took place on 5 October 1923, and Cao was inaugurated on 10 October, the anniversary of the 1911 Wuchang uprising. The same day, parliament promulgated a constitution, the 1923 constitution, which provided for a parliamentary system and acknowledged provincial self-government in language that appealed to federalists. It was the first formally permanent constitution of the Republic. Its authority was undermined by the circumstances of its passage, and critics dubbed it the "bribery constitution."

The scandal should not obscure the persistence of constitutional politics. Members of parliament, lawyers, journalists, merchants' associations, and educators continued to write, debate, and organize around constitutional themes. The press discussed elections and parliamentary procedure. Chambers of commerce and professional associations petitioned the government. The idea of a republic remained powerful even among those who disliked how the Republic actually worked. At the same time, many Chinese concluded, especially after 1923, that parliamentary government in its existing form had failed, and this conclusion helped the appeal of the more disciplined party organizations that grew in the 1920s (Chapter 61).

Calls for a broader settlement recurred. Wu Peifu's 1922 program of "legal restoration" asked that the 1912 constitution and old parliament be revived, so that the Republic could resume its interrupted legal order; critics noted that it also restored a parliament that Wu's allies could influence. Sun Yat-sen and others called in 1924 for a national convention (guomin huiyi) of representatives of social groups, a different model from parliament. Duan Qirui's government convened a conference on reconstruction (shanhou huiyi) in early 1925, which brought together officials and military figures but produced little. These competing formulas show that the dispute was not only over who ruled but over what kind of body could confer legitimacy: a parliament elected under a limited franchise, a convention of organized groups, or a conference of the powerful.

Political parties existed but were weak. The Nationalist Party (Guomindang) had been banned by Yuan Shikai in 1913, and Sun Yat-sen's reorganized Chinese Revolutionary Party operated in exile and later in Guangzhou. Other groupings in parliament, including the Anfu Club, were loose factions linked to patrons. The Chinese Communist Party, founded in 1921, was a small organization in this period. Elections, where held, were restricted by property and educational qualifications, so that only a small percentage of the adult population voted. Students should understand the Republic's constitutional politics as a real arena of debate and a seriously compromised institution, both at once.

Section 59.14

Warlord Armies: Recruitment, Command, and Military Organization

The armies of the 1920s varied enormously in size, training, equipment, and conduct. Estimates of the total number of men under arms are uncertain, but historians generally agree that the figure grew substantially: from several hundred thousand in the mid-1910s to more than a million by the mid-1920s and perhaps over two million by the late 1920s. Numbers on paper often exceeded real strength, because officers padded rolls to draw pay for nonexistent soldiers.

Most soldiers were recruited from the poor, especially from rural districts affected by famine, flood, or unemployment. Recruitment was typically voluntary, in the sense that men enlisted for pay and food, though the choice was often made under economic pressure. Conscription of the modern kind did not exist; commanders sometimes seized men for labor or service, and defeated armies often absorbed prisoners. A soldier's life could mean regular rations and a livelihood in a disciplined army, or hunger, unpaid wages, and desertion in a poorly financed one. Desertion was common, and so was the practice of whole units changing sides, which helps explain why battles were often decided by defection.

Command relied on personal loyalty. Officers were frequently graduates of military academies at Baoding, Tianjin, or provincial schools, and shared bonds with classmates and teachers. Patronage determined promotion, and commanders cultivated subordinates through gifts, marriage ties, and favors. The loyalty was reciprocal: a commander who failed to pay or protect his men risked losing them. Some commanders, such as Wu Peifu and Feng Yuxiang, invested in training, discipline, and education, and others tolerated plunder as a substitute for pay.

Military modernization also proceeded, unevenly. Commanders purchased rifles, machine guns, and artillery from foreign suppliers, often in violation of the arms embargo that the powers announced in 1919 (and which was widely evaded), and they established arsenals. The Mukden arsenal under Zhang Zuolin was among the largest. A few commanders introduced aircraft and armored trains, and railways, telegraphs, and trucks changed logistics. At the same time, most fighting still relied on infantry carrying rifles, with irregular supply lines and limited medical care. Diana Lary's study of warlord soldiers and other scholarship show that the common soldier's experience was shaped as much by hunger and disease as by combat.

Relations between soldiers and civilians varied with the army and the circumstances. A disciplined force paid its way and restrained its men, and civilians in such regions might experience it as protection against bandits. An unpaid or defeated army was more likely to seize food, animals, carts, and laborers, extort money from merchants, and commit violence. Retreating forces were often the most dangerous. The record contains reports of plunder, of villages abandoned as armies approached, and of orderly garrisons providing security. The truth is that both occurred, and the difference often depended on finance and command.

Section 59.15

Taxation, Public Finance, and the Economics of Military Rule

Armies cost money, and the principal fiscal question of the period was who collected which taxes and for whom. The land tax was the largest revenue source in most provinces. Since the late Qing, it had been collected by county officials and forwarded to provincial treasuries, but now provincial authorities, and often military commanders, kept most of it. Land-tax rates were fixed according to old registers that were outdated and unequal, so actual burdens varied. Commanders added surtaxes (fujiashui), sometimes several times the nominal tax, and in some regions imposed advance collections (yushou), requiring peasants to pay taxes for future years. In parts of Sichuan, where competing armies held "defense zones," land taxes were reportedly collected many years in advance by the 1930s.

Historical Vocabulary
Lijin, Surtax, and Advance Collection

Lijin (厘金) was a transit tax on goods in circulation, created during the mid-nineteenth-century rebellions as a provincial revenue source. Collected at barriers along roads and rivers, it was an important income for provincial authorities and a burden on merchants, who might pay repeatedly as goods crossed jurisdictions. It was not abolished nationally until 1931.

A surtax (fujiashui) was an additional charge attached to a tax, often adopted as a means of raising revenue without changing the official rate. An advance collection (yushou) required payment of a future year's tax, effectively borrowing from taxpayers, and was a sign of fiscal desperation. These terms matter because they show how burdens could rise sharply through administrative improvisation without any change to the formal tax law.

Other revenues included the salt tax, which after the 1913 Reorganization Loan was administered by the Sino-Foreign Salt Inspectorate, with the surplus after loan payments going to the Beijing government. Regional commanders sometimes seized local salt revenues. The customs revenue, administered by the Chinese Maritime Customs Service, was the most reliable income, but after the treaty tariff limited rates to about five percent and foreign loans and indemnities took priority, little remained for the government's discretionary use. Railway revenues were military assets in the north: commanders took control of lines and diverted receipts. Opium-related revenue was important in several regions. Despite the formal prohibition of opium in 1906–1917 and international agreements, commanders in provinces such as Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Shaanxi, and Gansu either taxed opium cultivation and trade or imposed "fines" on farmers who did not grow poppies. This revenue sustained armies while damaging health and agriculture.

Governments also borrowed, from foreign banks, from Chinese banks and merchant groups, and by issuing bonds and paper currency. Domestic loans, sold to banks under pressure, were a major source of funds for Beijing governments, and the banks' willingness to lend shaped the capital's politics. Overissue of paper money was a danger: Zhang Zuolin's Fengtian regime printed large quantities of notes, and their value collapsed in the late 1920s. A commander who issued paper money was effectively taxing everyone who held it.

Cause and Effect

Military Competition and Fiscal Extraction

Causes

Short-term: the contest over Beijing and provincial rivalries encouraged army expansion. Long-term: decentralized late Qing finance, fixed and outdated land registers, the claim of foreign debts on customs and salt, the absence of a reliable national tax system, and personal loyalty in armies.

The transformation

Commanders expanded armies and then raised money from land surtaxes, advance collections, transit taxes, opium revenue, railway receipts, loans, and paper currency. Military spending took up a large share of provincial budgets.

Effects

Immediate: burdens on peasants and merchants, inflation in some regions, unpaid civil servants and teachers. Long-term: erosion of confidence in the Beijing government and in parliamentary politics, strengthening of arguments for a disciplined national party-state, and uneven capacity-building where revenue was used for administration.

Historical complexity

Fiscal extraction was not uniform. Regimes in stable provinces could collect revenue through regular administration, and those in contested provinces extracted more harshly. The same revenue could pay for an arsenal or a school. A single-cause explanation, whether "greed" or "poverty," cannot account for this variation.

Students who encounter an original tax order or military proclamation from this period (for example, in a provincial gazetteer or a reprinted newspaper) should ask who issued it, what authority was claimed, whether the levy was regular or an emergency, and what evidence shows whether it was actually collected. The relationship between a written order and its enforcement is a recurring problem, and it is one reason historians pair official documents with local accounts and social surveys.

Section 59.16

Rural Society, Local Government, and Everyday Insecurity

Most Chinese lived in villages and market towns, and the county (xian) was the lowest level of formal government. The county magistrate, appointed from above, collected taxes, administered justice, and maintained order with a small staff and the cooperation of local elites. In the warlord era magistrates were often appointed by the provincial military governor, sometimes on payment or patronage, and were under pressure to raise revenue. Between the magistrate and the villager stood landlords, lineage heads, merchants, and local notables who managed granaries, schools, temples, irrigation works, and militias. These men could protect villagers from excessive demands or participate in extortion, and their role grew as the state's capacity to provide security declined.

Rural conditions depended on region. In the Yangtze delta, dense commercial agriculture produced silk, cotton, rice, and other crops, and tenancy rates were high; landlords often lived in towns and collected rent. In North China, small owner-cultivators were more common, though many families were close to subsistence, and the risks of drought, flood, and locusts were greater. Two major disasters struck the north in these years: the great North China famine of 1920–1921, in which hundreds of thousands died despite substantial international and Chinese relief, and the Haiyuan earthquake of December 1920 in Gansu, which killed an estimated 200,000 people. Famine relief efforts, including those organized by Chinese and foreign charitable groups, illustrate both the weakness of government capacity and the activity of civil society.

Banditry (tufei) was a pervasive feature of rural life. Bandit groups included desperate peasants, discharged soldiers, and well-organized forces, and the category blurred with that of the soldier, since defeated troops became bandits and bandits were recruited into armies. In May 1923, bandits derailed the express train on the Tianjin–Pukou railway at Lincheng in Shandong, abducted passengers including about two dozen foreigners, and held them in the hills until the government negotiated their release and offered some bandits positions in the army. The incident drew foreign pressure and showed the intersection of banditry, military politics, and international relations.

Villagers responded with self-defense. In North China, groups such as the Red Spears (Hongqiang hui), organized from the late 1910s and especially in the 1920s, used ritual, martial training, and village organization to defend against bandits and soldiers and, in some places, against tax collectors. Their activities show that rural society was not passive. Some Red Spear groups were led by local landowners and others had more popular character, and relations with military authorities varied from cooperation to open conflict. In Guangdong, the organizer Peng Pai began to establish peasant associations in Haifeng in 1922, an early example of rural organizing that would later become significant (Chapter 61).

Everyday Life and Social History

Peasants, Militia Members, Merchants, and Soldiers

Households and work. A farm family in the North China Plain typically grew millet, wheat, sorghum, and cotton on a few acres, with women spinning and weaving, children herding and gleaning, and men handling plowing and marketing. A military requisition of a cart, a mule, or a man could disrupt the planting or harvest and threaten the family's survival. Tax collectors, rent collectors, creditors, and the lineage or village head all had claims on the harvest.

Markets and credit. Market towns held periodic markets, where villagers sold surplus grain, bought salt, cloth, and tools, and borrowed from pawnshops or moneylenders. When armies arrived, markets could close, merchants hide goods, and the supply of coin and notes become uncertain, because different regimes issued money of varying value. Local merchants' associations often negotiated payments to commanders in exchange for protection.

Soldiers. Soldiers' families, if they had them, might depend on remittances that came irregularly or not at all. Many men enlisted to escape hunger; others were seized. A soldier might be disciplined and educated in one army and brutal in another, and often moved from army to army.

Women and children. Women managed households in the absence of men taken by armies, faced greater vulnerability to violence in zones of conflict, and in some regions participated in village self-defense. In the same years, new schools for girls opened in cities, and some women took work in factories, but these changes reached only a small share of women. Evidence for rural women's experiences is thinner than for men's, since most records were produced by male officials and observers.

Health and disease. Famine, floods, and war made households vulnerable to epidemic disease, and opium addiction, widespread in some regions, affected both soldiers and civilians. Missionary hospitals and the new Chinese medical institutions served a small proportion of the population.

Living Beside an Army: A County in a Contested Corridor

A military commander: Responsible for feeding and paying thousands of men with uncertain revenue, a commander might regard requisitions and surtaxes as emergency necessities, and might present them as contributions to a campaign for peace or order. His army's survival depended on revenue, and his officers' loyalty depended on pay.
A county magistrate: Caught between a superior who demanded revenue and a population that could not pay, the magistrate negotiated with local elites, delayed or reduced collections where possible, and sometimes became a conduit for extraction. His own pay might be late, and refusal to cooperate with a commander could cost him his post.
A tenant or small farmer: The surtax and the requisition fell on the household least able to bear them. A farmer might lose a draft animal, be pressed into carrying supplies, or be forced to pay taxes for a year not yet begun, and might join a village defense group or, in desperation, an army or bandit band.
A merchant in the market town: Protection payments, transit taxes, and forced loans reduced profits, while the army's presence also created demand for grain, cloth, and supplies. Some merchants profited from military contracts, and others were ruined by seizures and uncertain currency.
A village teacher: If schools were funded locally, teachers' pay might disappear when taxes went to the army. New-style primary schools, a priority for reformers, depended on local revenue and elite support, and the teacher could be both a transmitter of new ideas and a victim of fiscal crisis.

Note on balance: These perspectives are not equivalent in power. The commander could compel, and the farmer had few means of resistance. Historians distinguish between people who bore costs and people who imposed them, while recognizing that many individuals occupied more than one role.

Section 59.17

Regional Administration, Reform, and State-Building

Recent scholarship has emphasized that some regional regimes did more than fight. Where a commander could secure a stable territory and a dependable revenue base, he sometimes built institutions: schools, police, tax offices, roads, telegraphs, arsenals, banks, and public-health services. Historians describe this as regional or fiscal-military state-building, and they argue that it complicates the picture of the warlord era as pure disintegration. Others stress that these efforts were limited, coercive, and dependent on individual commanders, and that they rarely produced institutions that outlasted their founders.

Shanxi under Yan Xishan is the most studied case. Yan, a graduate of a Japanese military academy and a former Qing officer, took power in Taiyuan in the 1911 revolution and became military and later civil governor. He kept the province out of most national wars and invested in local administration. His program included a system of village government with registered village heads and a hierarchy of subordinate officials, a drive for universal primary education, campaigns against opium, foot-binding, and the queue, tree planting, and the promotion of cotton and other crops, and the development of an arsenal and industrial enterprises at Taiyuan. Yan's slogans stressed discipline, thrift, and "using the people's strength," and his program had a moral and paternalistic tone.

Shanxi's record was mixed. School enrollments and village administration expanded, and the province gained a reputation as orderly. But the system also served Yan's military and fiscal needs: village governments helped collect taxes and recruit men, and the reforms depended on his personal authority. Shanxi's finances were strained by military spending, and the province issued notes that later lost value. Students should note a key distinction. Administrative capacity is not the same as democracy or benevolence. A regime could be efficient and authoritarian, or well-intentioned and ineffective.

Manchuria under Zhang Zuolin provides a second type. Zhang's government benefited from the region's agricultural wealth, the booming soybean export trade through Dalian and other ports, and the labor of migrants from Shandong and Hebei who moved north in the millions in the 1920s, especially at the end of the decade. It maintained a large army and the Mukden arsenal, and also financed railways, mining, and some education. At the same time, the heavy military spending, the issue of inflated paper currency, and Zhang's involvement in wars south of the Great Wall placed strain on the regional economy. Zhang's autonomy was always constrained by Japanese economic and military interests in southern Manchuria (Section 59.19).

The wider lesson is that regional regimes differed in what they could do. Some used revenue to build schools, factories, and roads, others to expand armies, and many did both. Municipal government in Guangzhou, for example, drew up plans for roads and public works in the early 1920s, and in many cities, chambers of commerce, philanthropic societies, and professional groups carried out civic projects with or without official support. The Republic's state-building did not stop; it proceeded in multiple, uneven, and competing forms.

Section 59.18

Cities, Commerce, Industry, and Economic Development

One of the paradoxes of the period is that China's modern industrial and commercial sector grew substantially during it. The First World War reduced European exports to China and raised demand for Chinese goods such as silk, tea, beans, and metals, giving Chinese entrepreneurs room to expand. Scholars such as Marie-Claire Bergère (The Golden Age of the Chinese Bourgeoisie, 1911–1937) have described the years from roughly 1911 to the mid-1920s as a "golden age" of the Chinese bourgeoisie, although the phrase is debated and the prosperity was uneven and ended with postwar competition and new political troubles.

Shanghai was the center. Its International Settlement and French Concession, with separate administrations and legal systems, allowed Chinese and foreign business to operate beyond the reach of the militarists' tax collectors, though not entirely beyond their influence. Shanghai's population, well over a million, included migrants from across the Yangtze delta and further afield. Its industries included cotton spinning and weaving, flour milling, tobacco, matches, printing and publishing (the Commercial Press was the largest publisher in China), and light manufacturing. Chinese-owned cotton mills expanded markedly in these years, and families such as the Rong brothers built flour and textile enterprises. Department stores such as Wing On and Sincere on Nanjing Road sold imported and domestic goods in a new retail setting, and banks, native-place associations, and the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce managed finance and civic life.

Tianjin, the port of North China, was a center for textiles, flour, and trade, as well as a refuge for retired politicians and generals who lived in its foreign concessions. Wuhan, the triple city of Hankou, Hanyang, and Wuchang, was a center of river commerce, ironworks and steel at Hanyang, and a hub of the Beijing–Hankou railway. In the interior, regional capitals such as Chengdu, Changsha, and Jinan had growing commercial sectors, and Guangzhou was a major commercial city tied to Hong Kong. In Nantong, the entrepreneur Zhang Jian developed a regional complex of cotton mills, land reclamation, schools, and other enterprises, a case of local development independent of the central state.

Industrial growth created a new working class, including women and children in textile factories, and new labor conflicts. Strikes, such as the 1922 Hong Kong seamen's strike, the Anyuan coal miners' strike, and the February 1923 railway strike, show that workers organized, sometimes with the help of Communist and Nationalist organizers, and were often met with military repression. Working conditions were harsh, wages low, and hours long. The same economy that created new wealth also created new inequalities and social tensions.

The railways and banks were crucial and vulnerable. Fighting along railways disrupted freight, but commerce adapted, using river steamers, coastal shipping, and alternate routes. Chinese banks, especially a group of private banks in Shanghai and Tianjin, grew in this period, partly by lending to governments, and financial instability was a recurring risk. Political fragmentation did not produce uniform collapse because the economy was not unified; the Yangtze delta, the treaty ports, and Manchuria could prosper while the interior suffered. The economic geography of the 1920s was thus a patchwork, and this unevenness is one of the period's defining features.

Section 59.19

Foreign Powers and Chinese Political Fragmentation

Foreign powers had substantial interests in China and substantial leverage through the treaty system: extraterritorial jurisdiction, concessions and settlements, the customs service, foreign navies on the Yangtze, and loans. But they did not control Chinese politics, and their policies differed and changed. Treating every Chinese faction as the proxy of a foreign state, or treating foreign powers as irrelevant, both misread the period.

Japan was the most active. After the Twenty-One Demands of 1915 and the seizure of Germany's Shandong holdings, Japan cultivated relationships with Chinese leaders, notably Duan Qirui's group in 1917–1918, through the Nishihara loans and military agreements. After the Anhui clique's defeat, Japan shifted to cultivating Zhang Zuolin and maintained its interests in southern Manchuria, where the Kwantung Leased Territory and the South Manchuria Railway were the base of its position. Japanese policy was not monolithic: civilian governments, the army, and the Kwantung Army sometimes disagreed. Britain emphasized trade and the security of the Yangtze region and Hong Kong, and tended to favor stability and the existing treaty arrangements. The United States promoted the Open Door principle and, at the Washington Conference, a framework of multilateral restraint. The Soviet Union, after repudiating tsarist privileges in declarations of 1919 and 1920 (not fully carried through), negotiated an agreement with Beijing in 1924 establishing diplomatic relations, and also offered advice and aid to Sun Yat-sen's movement and to Feng Yuxiang's Guominjun.

Foreign policy was also shaped by coordination and rivalry among the powers themselves. In 1920, a new international banking consortium of American, British, French, and Japanese banks was organized to coordinate lending to China and to discourage competing loans, which Chinese governments often saw as a means of restricting their financial choices; it made few loans in practice. At Washington in 1921–1922, Japanese diplomacy under a more conciliatory civilian leadership accepted the return of Shandong and the Nine-Power Treaty's principles, a shift away from the style of the Nishihara period, though Japanese economic and military interests in Manchuria remained. The United States, after remitting part of its Boxer indemnity share in 1908 for educational use, authorized the remission of the remainder in 1924, a measure that supported Chinese education and shaped American influence. These policies show that the powers pursued different strategies and revised them in response to events in China and to their own domestic politics.

Chinese leaders, for their part, treated foreign support as one resource among many. Duan Qirui accepted Japanese loans to build an army that he hoped would unify the country; Zhang Zuolin took Japanese and Soviet positions into account while preserving his autonomy; Wu Peifu was often described in the foreign press as friendly to Britain and America, but he fought his own campaigns on his own judgment; and Sun Yat-sen, Feng Yuxiang, and others accepted Soviet advice and assistance without becoming agents of Moscow. Interpreting such relationships requires asking what each side wanted and what each could actually deliver. Rumors of foreign backing were also a tool of propaganda, since accusing rivals of serving a foreign power was an effective way to discredit them.

China and the Wider World

Foreign Loans, Japanese Influence, and Diplomatic Recognition

Recognition. The powers recognized the Beijing government as China's government even when it controlled little territory. This gave Beijing control of China's legal diplomatic representation, a share of customs surplus, and the ability to sign treaties, so that capturing the capital remained worth fighting for. The Guangzhou governments received no comparable recognition.

Loans. Foreign loans financed army expansion, railways, and communications. The Nishihara loans are the best-known example, but domestic and foreign banking consortia also lent to Beijing, and the repayment of old debts took priority over many other uses of customs and salt revenue.

Arms. The powers agreed in 1919 to an arms embargo against China, meant to discourage civil war, but it was widely evaded by suppliers, brokers, and commanders. The embargo shows that foreign powers recognized the link between arms and fragmentation, and that their own enforcement was inconsistent.

Chinese agency. Chinese diplomats at Paris and Washington, Chinese commanders negotiating with Japanese and Soviet agents, and Chinese publics boycotting foreign goods all acted as historical agents. Chinese leaders accepted or rejected foreign help according to their calculations, and they could and did take foreign money without surrendering policy. The Washington Conference, for example, was in part a result of Chinese diplomacy and public pressure over Shandong. China's relationship with the wider world was neither simple dependence nor simple independence.

Limits. Treaty privileges persisted, and the Shanghai Mixed Court, foreign gunboats, and the tariff ceiling constrained Chinese sovereignty. The Beijing Special Tariff Conference of 1925–1926, intended to implement the Washington promise of tariff autonomy, collapsed amid political turmoil in the capital. In May 1925, police in Shanghai's International Settlement shot Chinese demonstrators in the May Thirtieth incident, which triggered nationwide protests and strikes (Chapter 61) and made foreign privileges a central target of Chinese nationalism.

The overall effect of foreign involvement was double-edged. Loans and recognition could strengthen particular Chinese leaders, and the treaty system sheltered certain areas from militarist taxation. At the same time, foreign privileges and the intervention of foreign interests angered many Chinese, helping to produce the anti-imperialist nationalism that shaped the politics of the mid-1920s. A military fragmentation that invited foreign interference thus generated a political movement against both.

Section 59.20

Regional and Frontier Context: Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet

The Republic inherited the Qing empire's claims to Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet, but not the Qing's capacity to administer them, and the gap between claim and control widened after 1916. The four regions followed different courses. Manchuria's ruler wished to remain inside the Republic's political system and to shape it, Xinjiang's governor kept a formal allegiance in order to preserve his autonomy, Outer Mongolia's leaders moved toward a separate state, and Tibet's government acted as an independent authority while Beijing and Lhasa interpreted the situation in incompatible ways. In each case, regional decisions mattered as much as the intentions of Beijing or of neighboring powers.

In Manchuria, Zhang Zuolin's authority rested on an army, an arsenal, and a prosperous agricultural export economy, but his room for maneuver was bounded by two foreign railway systems. Japan held the Kwantung Leased Territory and the South Manchuria Railway, and claimed "special interests" in the south; Russia, and after 1917 the Soviet Union, retained a stake in the Chinese Eastern Railway across the north. Zhang's strategy was to bargain with both. He cooperated with Japanese advisers and creditors when it served him and resisted Japanese demands when he could, and in September 1924, a few months after Beijing's own agreement with Moscow, he concluded a separate arrangement with the Soviets over the Chinese Eastern Railway, a sign that he governed foreign relations in his region. Japanese policy was not unified: civilian diplomats generally favored working with Zhang, while elements of the Kwantung Army pursued more aggressive aims. When his subordinate Guo Songling rebelled in late 1925 and advanced toward Mukden, Japanese forces declared a zone around the South Manchuria Railway, which helped Zhang survive and showed how much the balance of power in his own capital depended on Japanese decisions. These tensions led to Zhang's assassination by Kwantung Army officers in 1928, an event that belongs to the outcome of this chapter and the origins of Chapter 64.

Outer Mongolia shows the strongest divergence between claim and outcome. Its aristocratic and monastic leaders had proclaimed autonomy under the Bogd Khan at the end of the Qing, motivated in part by fear of Chinese agricultural settlement and loss of grazing land, and they turned to Russia for support. The 1915 Kyakhta agreement gave Mongolia autonomy under Chinese suzerainty, but Xu Shuzheng's occupation of Urga in 1919 and the cancellation of autonomy in late 1919 and early 1920 were resented by many Mongols and were reversed by events. In 1921, the anti-Bolshevik Baron Ungern-Sternberg seized Urga with a mixed force, and Soviet and Mongolian revolutionary forces then drove him out and established a new government. The Bogd Khan remained a nominal ruler until his death in 1924, after which the Mongolian People's Republic was proclaimed. The Soviet-Chinese agreement of May 1924 acknowledged Chinese sovereignty over Outer Mongolia in principle, yet Soviet support continued for the Mongolian government, so formal recognition and practical politics pointed in different directions. Chinese governments continued to claim the territory, and the status question was not settled until after 1945. Inner Mongolia, by contrast, remained within the Republic's administrative structures, though its princes, Chinese settlers, and northern commanders contested pasture, taxation, and authority.

In Xinjiang, Governor Yang Zengxin ruled from 1912 until his assassination in 1928 by combining a formal acknowledgment of Beijing with minimal practical dependence on it. Beijing's governments sent little money and few troops, and Yang financed the province from local revenue and trade, in particular trade with the Soviet Union, which maintained consuls and agencies in several towns. He kept foreign influence limited, accepted refugees from Russia's civil war while avoiding entanglement in it, preserved the privileges of local princely and religious elites such as the rulers of Hami and Turpan, and used a small, loyal administration, largely of officials from outside the province. For Uyghur and other Muslim communities, the era meant continuity of local institutions under a distant and cautious provincial government, but also taxation, limits on political activity, and limited access to schooling and official posts.

Tibet's government under the Thirteenth Dalai Lama expelled Qing-era Chinese troops in 1912–1913, built a modernized army with British weapons and advice, and extended its authority into the Kham borderlands, where the 1917–1918 fighting with Chinese and Sichuan forces ended in the Rongbatsa truce mediated by the British consul Eric Teichman. Relations with Britain deepened in the early 1920s, including the visit of the British official Charles Bell to Lhasa in 1920–1921, though Britain declined to recognize Tibetan independence formally and continued to describe Chinese suzerainty. Within Tibet, reformers who favored a stronger army and modern institutions met resistance from monastic leaders worried about the cost and the influence of the new army, and the reform program was curtailed in the mid-1920s. The flight of the Ninth Panchen Lama from Tashilhunpo to China in 1923, after disputes with Lhasa over taxation, created a lasting rift. Chinese governments, both in Beijing and later in Nanjing, maintained that Tibet was part of China, while the Tibetan government exercised its own authority; scholars describe this period as one of de facto independence or de facto autonomy, depending on their reading of the legal and political evidence, and the question remains politically contested.

Taken together, the four cases show that the Republic's frontier policy was shaped by the choices of regional rulers and by the aims of Japan, Russia and the Soviet Union, and Britain, whose interests converged on keeping rivals out of key regions while seeking access for themselves. The semi-autonomous Muslim principalities of Hami and Turpan continued under local rulers. Xinjiang's population, predominantly Uyghur and other Turkic-speaking and Muslim communities, with Han, Hui, Kazakh, and other groups, experienced a distinctive provincial administration shaped by distance, trade, and Yang's personal rule.

Tibet. After the fall of the Qing, the Thirteenth Dalai Lama's government expelled Chinese troops from central Tibet and, in 1913, issued a proclamation asserting independence in its own terms. At the Simla Conference of 1913–1914, British, Tibetan, and Chinese representatives negotiated; the Chinese representative initialed but Beijing refused to ratify the resulting convention, and the status of Tibet remained disputed. Tibetan forces fought Chinese and provincial troops in eastern Tibet (Kham) in the late 1910s, and a truce in 1918 fixed an unstable frontier. Lhasa conducted its own administration and foreign contacts, especially with Britain, while the Republic continued to claim Tibet as part of China and to appoint nominal officials. The Republic's claim and actual administrative control were thus very different things.

How to read these cases. The Republic inherited the Qing empire's territorial claims, but not the Qing's military and administrative reach. Frontier peoples, including Mongols, Tibetans, Uyghurs, Manchus, Han migrants, and others, made their own political choices, shaped by local interests and by the interests of neighboring powers. Historians distinguish what governments claimed, what they controlled, what local populations accepted, and what foreign powers recognized, and these were different at different times. Modern governments and communities continue to interpret these years in differing ways, and students should treat claims as claims and evidence as evidence.

Section 59.21

Rethinking the Warlord Era: Disorder, Regional State-Building, and the Limits of Unification

Interpretations of warlordism have changed over time. In the 1920s, Nationalists and Communists condemned warlords as agents of imperialism and feudal reaction, a view that became dominant in China after 1949. Early Western accounts often described the period as one of disintegration and warlord rule. Scholarship from the 1960s and 1970s examined the structure of factional politics and its relation to constitutionalism. Examples include James E. Sheridan's Chinese Warlord: The Career of Feng Yü-hsiang (1966) and China in Disintegration: The Republican Era in Chinese History, 1912–1949 (1975), Hsi-sheng Ch'i's Warlord Politics in China, 1916–1928 (1976), Andrew J. Nathan's Peking Politics, 1918–1923: Factionalism and the Failure of Constitutionalism (1976), and Odoric Y. K. Wou's Militarism in Modern China: The Career of Wu P'ei-fu, 1916–39 (1978). Later work looked at military institutions and social effects, notably Diana Lary's Warlord Soldiers: Chinese Common Soldiers, 1911–1937 (1985) and Edward A. McCord's The Power of the Gun: The Emergence of Modern Chinese Warlordism (1993). Arthur Waldron's From War to Nationalism: China's Turning Point, 1924–1925 (1995) argued that major northern commanders were chiefly concerned with control of the national government. Recent researchers, including those studying regional fiscal-military states, have emphasized the construction of regional institutions. Editorial note: the titles above are given for further reading, and the summaries describe each work's general argument, not specific pages; this chapter's authors have not re-examined each volume, and publication details should be checked against library catalogues.

Historical Debate

Was the Warlord Era Simply an Age of Chaos?

The question: Should the years 1916–1928 be understood mainly as a period of political disintegration, military predation, and national failure, or as a period of competing regional and national projects in which some state-building and economic growth occurred?

Interpretation A — Disintegration and militarization: Historians emphasizing disintegration point to the weakness of the central government, the rise of personal armies, the burden of taxation and requisition, the frequency of wars, the devastation in contested regions, and the collapse of constitutional politics. They argue that the militarization of society inflicted lasting damage and that the failure of parliamentary government discredited liberal alternatives.
Interpretation B — Regional state-building and continuity: Other historians emphasize that regimes such as Shanxi, parts of Guangdong, and Manchuria built administrative, fiscal, and military institutions, that the national ministries and diplomatic corps continued to function, that commercial and industrial development proceeded, that most northern commanders sought to control rather than to destroy the Republic, and that many of the era's institutions and personnel carried into the Nationalist period. Seen this way, warlordism was a stage in the formation of modern Chinese states, not simply an absence of the state.

Evidence: Supporters of A cite military budgets, requisition and tax records, accounts of violence and famine, and the repeated collapse of national institutions. Supporters of B cite provincial education and administrative records, fiscal data from stable regimes, evidence of industrial growth, and the continuing roles of the bureaucracy and professional diplomats. Both sides use the same sources differently and draw on regional variation, which suggests that generalizations about "the" warlord era are risky.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how historians understand the Nationalist Party-state, the origins of later military-political institutions, and the continuities between the Qing, the Republic, and later regimes. It also shapes how contemporary societies tell their own stories about unity and division.

Student task: Using Sections 59.14–59.18, choose one region and assess which interpretation better fits its evidence. What additional evidence (for example, county-level tax records or school enrollment data) would help you decide? Why is a single label difficult to apply to the whole country?

Why did some regimes endure and others collapse? Durability depended on several factors: a secure geographic base (Shanxi, Manchuria, Yunnan), a reliable revenue stream, control of arsenals and railways, a cohesive officer corps, and skillful diplomacy with rivals and foreign powers. Regimes that depended on the capital for resources, like the Anhui clique, were fragile. Regimes without a stable base, like Wu Peifu's after 1924, fell once their armies were broken. By the mid-1920s, however, changes were underway. The wars were becoming larger and more destructive, as railways and modern weapons raised the costs of fighting. The May Thirtieth movement and the rise of anti-imperialist nationalism changed popular attitudes. And a rival model of political organization, the disciplined party-army of the Guangzhou government, was emerging.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Republican Institutions from Yuan Shikai to the Warlord Governments

What Changed

  • Authority over armies shifted from a single presidential network to competing regional and factional commanders
  • Control of revenue moved from the center to provinces and commanders
  • The capital changed hands among coalitions every few years
  • Competing governments claimed to be the legal Republic
  • Commercial and industrial sectors, labor movements, and new political organizations expanded

What Stayed the Same

  • The Republic's name, ministries, diplomatic corps, and international recognition
  • County-level administration and the role of local elites, lineages, and village institutions
  • The claim to inherit the Qing empire's territory
  • The agrarian base of the economy and the tax burden on peasants
  • The ideal of constitutional and national unity, invoked by nearly all factions

Who experienced change most directly? Peasants in contested corridors, merchants subject to shifting taxes, soldiers, urban workers, and officials. Who experienced continuity? Villagers in quieter regions, treaty-port residents, and civil servants in the ministries. Why it matters: The pattern shows that institutions can persist while the distribution of power within them changes, and it helps explain why later governments built on Republican structures.

The Northern Expedition, which began in 1926 and is told in Chapter 61, ended Beijing's governments in 1928, when Nationalist forces reached the capital and Zhang Zuolin, who had retreated toward Manchuria, was killed on 4 June 1928 when Japanese Kwantung Army officers bombed his train near Mukden. His son Zhang Xueliang then led Manchuria, which formally accepted Nationalist authority at the end of 1928. Students should avoid reading the outcome backward. In 1917, 1920, or 1924, observers did not know that a southern army would unify much of the country, and many expected Zhili, Fengtian, or a federal arrangement to prevail. The success of the Nationalists depended on events and choices that were contingent, including Soviet aid, the discipline of the new army, the collapse of rival coalitions, and the mobilization of urban and rural organizations.

Chapter Summary

The death of Yuan Shikai in June 1916 left the Republic of China with a president, a premier, a restored constitution, and a recalled parliament, but without a single commander able to bind the army together. The Beiyang officer corps that Yuan had built divided into networks of personal, regional, and financial relationships, and the central government, short of revenue and dependent on provincial commanders, could not enforce its decisions. The weakness was structural: it grew out of late Qing decentralization, the dependence of armies on local revenue, and the tie between soldiers and their immediate commanders.

The crisis of 1917 exposed the pattern. A dispute between President Li Yuanhong and Premier Duan Qirui over entry into the First World War led to the dismissal of Duan, the rebellion of provincial governors, the entry of Zhang Xun's army into Beijing, and the brief restoration of Puyi. The restoration failed within twelve days, but it demonstrated that civilian institutions could be overridden by generals. Duan's refusal to restore the old parliament prompted a rival government at Guangzhou, where Sun Yat-sen and southern commanders claimed to protect the constitution. The north–south division was real but complex, since each side contained competing groups.

Between 1917 and 1926, control of Beijing passed among shifting coalitions of commanders: the Anhui clique under Duan until 1920, the Zhili clique under Cao Kun and Wu Peifu after the Zhili–Anhui War and the First Zhili–Fengtian War, and then a coalition of Feng Yuxiang, Zhang Zuolin, and Duan after Feng's coup of 1924. These cliques were not parties; they were networks whose membership and alliances changed. The contests turned on railways, finance, and defection as much as on battle, and they were fought for the prizes that the capital offered: recognition, loans, customs and salt revenue, and symbolic authority.

Behind the political contests lay a fiscal system in which commanders financed armies through land surtaxes, advance collections, transit taxes, opium revenue, railway receipts, loans, and paper currency. These burdens fell heavily on peasants and merchants, and in some regions armies requisitioned grain, animals, and labor. Banditry, militia organization, and village self-defense were features of rural life, and natural disasters, including the 1920–1921 North China famine, compounded the hardship. Yet conditions varied: some regions were largely spared, and some armies were disciplined and some were predatory.

Regional regimes differed in what they built. Shanxi under Yan Xishan developed village administration, schools, and industry within an authoritarian framework, Manchuria under Zhang Zuolin invested in infrastructure and arsenals while straining its currency, Sichuan was divided among rival commanders who extracted revenue in advance, and Guangdong was the site of competing municipal, military, and revolutionary projects. The comparison shows that administrative capacity depended on geography, revenue, and leadership, and that capacity was not the same as benevolence or democracy.

The period was also one of economic and urban growth. Shanghai, Tianjin, Wuhan, and other cities saw expanding manufacturing, banking, publishing, and retail, supported by wartime conditions in the world economy, by the protection of treaty-port jurisdictions, and by Chinese entrepreneurship. A new industrial working class organized and was suppressed. Fragmentation did not produce uniform economic collapse, but it left growth uneven and vulnerable.

Foreign powers influenced politics through loans, recognition, treaty privileges, arms supplies, and diplomatic pressure, but they did not simply control Chinese factions. Japan cultivated Duan Qirui and later Zhang Zuolin, Britain and the United States defended treaty interests while seeking stability, and the Soviet Union established relations with Beijing while aiding southern and northwestern movements. Chinese diplomats and politicians negotiated actively. On the frontiers, the Republic claimed the Qing empire's territory but controlled less of it: Manchuria was ruled by Zhang, Outer Mongolia moved toward a separate state, Xinjiang was governed autonomously by Yang Zengxin, and Tibet administered itself.

Historians disagree about how to characterize the era, and the disagreement is instructive. Interpretations emphasizing disintegration and those emphasizing regional state-building each draw on genuine evidence, and the variation across regions makes a single label unsatisfactory. What the period demonstrated to many Chinese was that parliamentary politics without control of armies was fragile, that foreign privileges were a continuing grievance, and that new forms of organization were needed. These conclusions fed the nationalist movements discussed in Chapters 60 and 61.

For the larger history of Chinese civilization, the warlord era is one of several periods, along with the Three Kingdoms and the Five Dynasties, in which political authority divided without ending the cultural, economic, and institutional connections among regions. It was also a formative period for the institutions, personnel, and ideas of the later Republic and of the People's Republic. Understanding it requires attention to both the violence and the construction, to the capital and the provinces, and to the voices of soldiers, farmers, merchants, and teachers as well as commanders.

The network around Duan Qirui, dominant in Beijing from 1917 to 1920.

The network around Zhang Zuolin, based in Manchuria.

The Republic's founding framework, restored in 1916 and a central reference in constitutional disputes.

The brief attempt in July 1917 to restore Puyi as Qing emperor; distinct from Yuan Shikai's 1915–1916 monarchy.
Circular telegram (tongdian)
A public telegram addressed to the press and rivals, used by commanders to justify their actions.
Nishihara loans
Japanese loans of 1917–1918 to Beijing governments associated with Duan Qirui.

The 1920s movement for provincial self-government in a federal China, supported by various intellectuals, elites, and commanders.

An additional charge on an existing tax, widely used to raise military revenue.
Advance collection (yushou)
The collection of taxes for future years, a sign of fiscal desperation.
Lijin (厘金)
A transit tax on goods in circulation, retained by provinces until 1931.

The foreign-administered but Chinese institution that collected tariff revenue and held it as security for debts.

The Sino-foreign administration of the salt tax created after the 1913 Reorganization Loan.

The territory assigned to or seized by a commander in Sichuan and elsewhere, where he collected taxes and maintained troops.

Village self-defense societies in North China using ritual and martial training.

The "National People's Army" created by Feng Yuxiang after 1924, based in the northwest.

The development of administrative, fiscal, and military institutions at the provincial or regional level.
Extraterritoriality
The treaty privilege by which foreigners were tried under their own laws in China.
Washington Conference
The 1921–1922 conference that produced treaties on naval limits and China, including the Nine-Power Treaty and the return of Shandong.

Roughly 140,000 Chinese laborers recruited by Britain and France, beginning in 1916, to work behind Allied lines in the First World War.

The Russian–Chinese–Mongolian agreement recognizing Mongolian autonomy under Chinese suzerainty.
Simla Convention
The 1914 draft agreement negotiated among Britain, Tibet, and China, never ratified by Beijing.

Chapter Reflection

The warlord era matters because it shows what happens when a state's institutions continue while its monopoly over force breaks down. The Republic of China kept its name, ministries, and diplomats, but the power to command armies and collect revenue was spread among many hands. Understanding this condition helps students think about legitimacy, sovereignty, and state capacity as separate things that can come apart.

The period shaped later Chinese history in several ways. It discredited parliamentary government in the eyes of many Chinese, strengthened the appeal of disciplined party organizations, and made the dismantling of foreign privileges and the reunification of the country urgent goals. It also left practical legacies: armies, officers, administrative experiments, financial networks, and industrial enterprises that were taken up by the Nationalist government and, in different ways, by its rivals. The era is a reminder that later regimes inherited and adapted what came before.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of the textbook: the relationship between center and region, the role of frontiers, the importance of fiscal systems, and the interplay of political fragmentation and economic and cultural creativity. Like the Three Kingdoms and the Five Dynasties, the warlord era shows that division did not mean the end of social and cultural connection. And, like those periods, it was interpreted by later governments in ways that served their own purposes.

Finally, the chapter connects China to wider Asian and global history through the First World War, the Washington Conference, Japanese and Soviet policy, and global markets. It invites students to see the Republic as diverse, interconnected, contested, and unfinished: a political system under severe strain, whose people nonetheless built schools, factories, newspapers, and movements, and who debated what kind of China ought to exist. Chapters 60 and 61 turn to the intellectual and political movements that tried to answer that question.
