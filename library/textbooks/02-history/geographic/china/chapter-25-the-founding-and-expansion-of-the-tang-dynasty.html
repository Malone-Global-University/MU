Part V — Sui, Tang, and the Post-Tang Transition

The Founding and Expansion of the Tang Dynasty

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the autumn of 626, only weeks after he had taken the throne, the emperor Li Shimin rode out from Chang'an to the Wei River with a small escort. On the far bank stood the cavalry of Xieli, khagan of the Eastern Turks, whose army had ridden deep into the Guanzhong plain and now sat within sight of the capital. The Tang court's own histories say that the new emperor rebuked the khagan across the water, then agreed to a sworn covenant and a payment of goods, and the Turks withdrew. The scene is worth pausing over. The ruler who is remembered as the model Tang emperor came to power through the killing of two of his brothers, and the first act of his reign was to bargain with a steppe ruler who could, for the moment, threaten his capital.

Chapter 24 traced the reunification of northern and southern China under the Sui and the rapid collapse of that dynasty amid the costs of canal-building, mass mobilization, and disastrous campaigns against Goguryeo. This chapter asks how the Tang emerged from that collapse. It studies a period of roughly nine decades, from the revolt of Li Yuan in 617 to the restoration of the Tang after the reign of Empress Wu in 705. Its central argument is that the early Tang did not replace the Sui with a wholly new political order. The Tang founders came from the same northwestern aristocratic and military world as the Sui rulers, kept much of the Sui administrative, legal, fiscal, and territorial framework, and consolidated it through civil war, institutional adjustment, frontier expansion, and changing claims to imperial legitimacy.

Much changed in these decades. A dynasty proclaimed in 618 in a single city had, by the 620s, defeated or absorbed a dozen rival regimes. By 630 the Tang had broken the Eastern Turkic Khaganate, the strongest power on the northern steppe, and within two decades Tang armies and protectorates reached the oases of the Tarim Basin. Law was codified in a form that would shape legal thought in Korea, Japan, and Vietnam for centuries. And in 690 a woman, Wu Zhao, formally ended the Tang and proclaimed her own Zhou dynasty, an event without precedent in imperial history. Much also continued: the centrality of Guanzhong, the importance of a registered peasantry as the base of taxation and military service, the dependence of emperors on aristocratic and military allies, and the constant, two-sided relationship with the steppe.

The people affected were many and varied. Registered farming households bore the taxes and labor duties on which the state depended. Militia soldiers, defeated rivals and their followers, aristocratic lineages of Guanzhong and the Shandong region, Buddhist and Daoist clergy, Turkic chiefs and oasis kings, and the women of the palace and the imperial family all had their own stakes in the outcome. It is easy to tell this story as the triumph of a few brilliant rulers. This chapter instead treats emperors as actors within networks of kin, officials, generals, and allies, and asks what those networks needed and expected.

Historians face an unusual evidentiary problem here. The most detailed narratives of the founding and of Taizong's reign were compiled under Tang court supervision, and later histories such as the Old Tang History, the New Tang History, and the Comprehensive Mirror rely heavily on them. Those records were shaped by the need to justify how the dynasty won and how Li Shimin took power. Other sources correct or complicate them: legal texts, household registers and documents recovered from Turfan and Dunhuang, tomb inscriptions, the stone reliefs at Taizong's tomb, and, from the steppe, eighth-century Old Turkic inscriptions that present the Tang from the other side of the frontier. This chapter uses these sources to teach students to separate dynastic image from political reality.

The chapter also connects Chinese history to a wider Eurasian setting. The Tang ruling house had Xianbei ancestry through several lines, and it governed through a political language that could be read differently by farmers, aristocrats, Buddhists, Daoists, and Inner Asian peoples. Taizong could be an emperor to his subjects and a khagan to steppe leaders at the same time. The Tang did not simply expand into empty space or absorb passive neighbors. Turks, Tuyuhun, oasis kingdoms, and others made their own choices, and Tang power often rested on alliance and incorporation as much as conquest. Broader questions of trade, Tibet, Korea, Japan, India, and the Islamic world are reserved for Chapter 27; the workings of Tang government and society in daily life belong to Chapter 26.

Students should resist a familiar narrative in which a failed Sui gives way to a glorious Tang "Golden Age." The Sui was a necessary foundation for what followed, and the early Tang was a fragile, contested, and often violent political construction. Whether it would endure was uncertain for decades. By the end of this chapter, students should be able to explain how the Tang won and consolidated power, what it inherited, how it expanded, and how the sources of imperial legitimacy shifted from Gaozu through Taizong to Wu Zetian.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the proclamation of the Tang in 618 began, rather than completed, the process of imperial reunification.
  2. Trace the major stages of the Tang victory over rival regimes between 617 and about 628.
  3. Analyze the political role of Gaozu's reign and why later Taizong-centered histories tend to diminish it.
  4. Evaluate the Xuanwu Gate Incident of 626 and the ways Tang historiography shaped its memory.
  5. Describe the early Tang governing architecture, including the Three Departments, Six Ministries, Censorate, and circuit and prefectural divisions.
  6. Interpret the Tang Code as both a moral and administrative text, distinguishing normative law from judicial practice.
  7. Connect registered households, equal-field allotments, taxation, labor service, and militia duty as one interdependent system.
  8. Assess Tang relations with the Eastern and Western Turks and the oasis states as diplomacy, alliance, incorporation, and warfare rather than simple conquest.
  9. Contextualize Wu Zetian's rise and her Zhou dynasty within existing institutions and political networks.
  10. Compare the sources of imperial legitimacy claimed by Gaozu, Taizong, and Wu Zetian.
  11. Distinguish Tang dynastic propaganda from political reality, using evidence from more than one kind of source.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Tang actually win the empire, and why did some rivals with comparable resources fail?
  2. What did the Tang inherit from the Sui and the Northern Dynasties, and what did it change?
  3. How should historians read court records compiled by the victors of a violent succession?
  4. In what ways was the early Tang state dependent on aristocratic, military, and regional allies rather than on imperial command alone?
  5. Why did Tang law become so influential, and how far did practice match the written code?
  6. What did the Tang relationship with the Turks and the oasis states look like from the standpoint of the peoples of the steppe and the Tarim Basin?
  7. How did Wu Zetian use existing institutions while changing who could claim imperial authority?
  8. What made Tang rule legitimate, and to whom?

Historical Context

The Tang emerged from a world shaped by three centuries of political division and northern state-building. Chapters 20 through 23 showed how, after the collapse of the Western Jin, the north was ruled by a succession of states led by Inner Asian and mixed elites, most importantly the Northern Wei, and how the successor states of the sixth century, Western Wei and Northern Zhou in the northwest, developed a fusion of aristocratic families and military commanders. Chapter 24 showed how one of those families, the Yang, used that base to found the Sui in 581, conquer the south in 589, and rebuild a unified administrative order. The Sui built the capital Daxingcheng, expanded the Grand Canal, standardized institutions, and then exhausted itself in the wars and public works of Emperor Yang's reign. By 616–617 rebellions had spread across Hebei, Henan, the Yangtze region, and the northwest, and the emperor himself was in the south at Jiangdu, isolated from the political center.

Geography shaped who could win. The Guanzhong basin, the plain of the Wei River around Chang'an, was protected by mountains and passes, agriculturally rich, and long associated with the northwestern military elite. Whoever held it had a defensible base and access to the Sui capital and its stored resources. To the northeast lay Taiyuan and the Fen River valley, strategic ground where Li Yuan had his command. The North China Plain, including Luoyang, Henan, and Hebei, was densely populated and productive, but harder to defend and the scene of the heaviest rebel activity. The lower Yangtze, Sichuan, and Lingnan were connected to the north by the Grand Canal and river routes, but they were also distant enough to sustain separate regimes. To the north and west lay the steppe and the Tarim oases, where Turkic khaganates, the Tuyuhun, and small kingdoms had their own political calendars and interests.

The meaning of "China" in these decades needs care. Contemporary sources speak of the great Tang, of the Central Plains, and of the realm within the passes, and usage varied by speaker. Turkic inscriptions later called the Tang and its predecessors "Tabgach," a name derived from the Tuoba ruling house of the Northern Wei, a reminder that steppe peoples had long viewed the northern Chinese elite through the history of Inner Asian rule in China. The population of the Tang state was linguistically and culturally mixed, and the ruling elite included families of Xianbei and other northern descent alongside families that claimed ancient Chinese lineages. The distinction between "Chinese" and "steppe" political traditions was therefore never sharp, and modern national labels do not map neatly onto it.

The economic and demographic setting was also unstable. The Sui had registered almost nine million households in 609, a figure that reflects both its administrative reach and its demands on labor. Warfare, famine, and displacement in the 610s and 620s reduced the population in some regions dramatically, and the Tang inherited a smaller and less securely registered tax base; registered households in the early Zhenguan years numbered only around three million, a figure that reflects both real loss of life and incomplete registration. Rebuilding the registers, feeding the capital, and restoring agricultural production were as important as battlefield victories. Tang leaders drew lessons from Sui overreach, and those lessons shaped policy: restraint in corvée labor, attention to granaries, and a court culture that cultivated the appearance of consultation.

Finally, students should recall that the early Tang inherited a legal, fiscal, and military vocabulary already developed under the Northern Dynasties and Sui: household registration, land allotment under the equal-field system, a tax in grain, cloth, and labor, and the militia units known as fubing. Chapters 22 and 24 warned against imagining any of these as unchanged institutions running seamlessly through time, and that caution applies here. The early Tang used old names for institutions that were repeatedly adjusted in practice.

Section 25.1

The Tang Founding

The year 618 is conventionally the first year of the Tang, but a dynasty proclaimed in one city did not rule an empire. When Li Yuan took the throne in the summer of 618, several substantial regimes controlled Gansu, the Ordos frontier, the Luoyang region, Hebei, the middle and lower Yangtze, and parts of the south. The next decade was a civil war among successor regimes, each claiming or bypassing the legacy of the Sui. Students should therefore distinguish two processes that later histories tend to merge: the founding of a dynasty, which is a political act, and the consolidation of imperial authority, which took years of fighting, negotiation, and institution-building.

Li Yuan (566–635) was no outsider. His family claimed descent from the Western Liang rulers and, more remotely, from the ancient Li lineage of Longxi, though scholars caution that such genealogies were shaped and edited to enhance status. What is better established is his place in the northwestern military elite. His grandfather had been among the leading generals of the Western Wei, his mother came from the Dugu family, whose daughter became the wife of Emperor Wen of Sui, and his own wife came from the Dou family, of Xianbei background. Li Yuan was thus a cousin of Emperor Yang. He held Sui posts under both emperors, and in 617 he was the commander at Taiyuan, a critical garrison guarding the northern approaches against the Turks.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Guanlong Group (關隴集團)

A term popularized by the historian Chen Yinke (1890–1969) for the interlocking aristocratic and military families of Guanzhong and the Longyou region, descended from the ruling circle of the Western Wei and Northern Zhou, from which the Sui and Tang founders came. The concept explains why the early Tang seemed so continuous with the Sui: the same families and networks supplied generals, officials, and marriage partners.

Historians use the term as an analytical shorthand, not as a name that contemporaries used for themselves, and later scholarship has qualified Chen's thesis by stressing the diversity of families within the group and their shifting alliances. It remains useful for asking who benefited from the Tang founding and how Wu Zetian's rise later challenged an established elite.

Sources report that Li Yuan hesitated to rebel. Sui pressure on commanders was intense, the Turks were raiding the north, and rebel bands were operating across Shanxi. His advisers, including the local officials Liu Wenjing and Pei Ji, and his sons, notably Li Jiancheng and Li Shimin, are credited with pushing him toward revolt, though the emphasis on Li Shimin in the surviving accounts reflects later editing. In the summer of 617 Li Yuan declared his rising at Taiyuan, secured the northern frontier by an arrangement with the Eastern Turks, and marched on the capital. Turkic support took the form of horses and a small force, and Tang sources later minimized the dependence, but the arrangement shows that steppe backing was a resource that every serious contender sought. Meanwhile, Li Yuan's daughter, known in later sources as Princess Pingyang, is recorded as raising an army of local people and bandit groups in the Guanzhong region and joining her father's advance; she was buried in 623 with military honors, a distinction her court reportedly debated.

In the eleventh month of 617 Li Yuan's forces took Daxingcheng. Rather than take the throne immediately, he installed a young Sui prince, Yang You, as Emperor Gong, and named the absent Emperor Yang as retired emperor. This maneuver preserved Sui legitimacy while allowing Li Yuan to act as regent with wide authority. In the spring of 618 Emperor Yang was murdered at Jiangdu by officers led by Yuwen Huaji, and in the summer Li Yuan received the abdication of Yang You and proclaimed the Tang. The name came from his hereditary title, Duke of Tang, a title associated with the Taiyuan region. The staged abdication tells us how the founders regarded their claim: the Tang would be a successor to Sui authority, not a repudiation of it. Rival contenders acted similarly, setting up other Sui princes at Luoyang and elsewhere.

The competing regimes deserve to be understood as serious political projects. Xue Ju controlled the northwest around Lanzhou and the Gansu approaches; his son was defeated by Li Shimin in late 618. Liu Wuzhou in the north, backed by Turkic forces, threatened Taiyuan until 620. Li Mi commanded the Wagang rebels and the granaries near Luoyang before being defeated by Wang Shichong and then submitting to the Tang; he later rebelled again and was killed. Wang Shichong, a former Sui general, held Luoyang and the Eastern Capital region and proclaimed his own dynasty in 619. Dou Jiande, a local leader from Hebei, built a durable regime there and was known for relatively orderly administration. In the south Xiao Xian, a descendant of the Liang dynasty's ruling family, ruled from Jiangling, and Du Fuwei controlled parts of the Huai and lower Yangtze region. Each had armies, officials, and regional loyalties, and several sought aid from the Turks.

Why did the Tang prevail? Historians point to several factors. The Guanzhong base was defensible and productive, and the capture of Chang'an gave the Tang access to stored resources and a symbolic center. The Tang policy of accepting surrenders, confirming ranks, and conferring the imperial surname on important defectors converted rivals into stakeholders. The Tang avoided a full-scale war with the Turks in the crucial years by diplomacy and payment. And the Tang's opponents undermined one another: in 621, when Li Shimin besieged Wang Shichong in Luoyang, Dou Jiande marched to relieve him, and Li Shimin split his forces, blocked Dou at Hulao, and captured both rulers. This was the decisive campaign in the north, though not the end of fighting. Resistance in Hebei flared again when Dou Jiande's former commander Liu Heita rebelled in 622–623, a sign that regional resentment toward the new regime was real. Xiao Xian surrendered in 621. Du Fuwei had already submitted to the Tang and entered Gaozu's service, while the later rebellion of his former lieutenant Fu Gongshi in the lower Yangtze region was defeated in 624. Some regional power holders in the far south recognized the Tang in the early 620s, and the Turkic-backed Liang Shidu in the northern Ordos survived until 628.

Students should be cautious with accounts that make Li Shimin the architect of the founding. He was a talented commander, and his victories, especially at Hulao, were crucial. But the earliest accounts of the founding were compiled or revised under his patronage after 626, when it was politically necessary to present his role as central and that of his elder brother, the crown prince, as marginal. Li Jiancheng also commanded armies and campaigned against Liu Heita. The Tang victory was the product of a family enterprise, of Gaozu's political leadership, and of a wide circle of commanders and officials, many of whom had once served other regimes.

Section 25.2

Emperor Gaozu and the New Dynasty

Gaozu reigned for eight years (618–626) and deserves treatment as a ruler in his own right. Later histories, written in a Taizong-centered tradition, sometimes portrayed him as indecisive and dependent on his more dynamic son. The evidence suggests something different: a competent political organizer who rebuilt government in a period of collapse, retained talent from the Sui and rival regimes, and guided the dynasty through its most vulnerable years.

Gaozu's government began as an improvised regime built around Daxingcheng, renamed Chang'an. The early court reused the Sui central offices, and in 618 the Tang reorganized them under the Secretariat, Chancellery, and Department of State Affairs, with the Six Ministries under the last. Names were changed in places, but the architecture was Sui. Selective preservation was practical: personnel who understood the machinery were available, the forms were familiar, and Tang legitimacy rested on being the Sui's heir. Where the Tang disagreed with the Sui was mostly in tone. It denounced Sui extravagance and tried to reduce the appearance of exploitative rule.

Early measures in law and finance set important precedents. In 621 the Tang issued the Kaiyuan tongbao coin, a bronze coin with a standard weight that replaced the debased and multiplying coinages of the late Sui and rival regimes. It would remain the model for Chinese cash coinage for more than a thousand years, and the term "Kaiyuan" later took on a different meaning as a reign name in the eighth century. In 624 the Tang promulgated a new legal code, the Wude code, which followed the structure and much of the substance of the Sui Kaihuang code, together with ordinances for land allotment and taxation. These measures, developed further in 25.5 and 25.6, reveal the character of the early regime: continuity with Sui norms, with adjustments for restoring order.

The regime also needed to settle accounts with regional elites. The Tang typically offered surrendered generals and officials rank and office, allowed local strongmen to remain in place if they submitted, and rewarded loyal supporters with titles and land. Prominent defectors were drawn into the dynastic network in different ways. Du Fuwei and the general Xu Shiji (later called Li Ji) were granted the imperial surname Li, a way to bind them into the ruling house's network, while Li Mi, already surnamed Li, was received with rank and office. Regional administration was arranged through military commands: area commanders (zongguan) in the early years, later restructured, held military and civil authority over groups of prefectures, and branch offices of the central government, such as the Eastern Circuit Secretariat at Luoyang, managed newly conquered regions. Luoyang itself was treated cautiously: after 621 the Tang reportedly demolished Sui palaces there, avoiding the appearance of restoring a rival capital, while retaining the city as an economic and administrative center.

Gaozu's government faced problems that no set of institutions could immediately solve. The Turks remained a threat. Between 619 and 626 the Eastern Turkic khagans Chuluo and then Xieli backed Tang rivals, raided the north, and pressed for tribute. The Tang paid, negotiated, and, at moments, considered dramatic measures: in the mid-620s, Chinese sources report, Gaozu contemplated moving the capital away from the frontier, and was dissuaded by advisers, including Li Shimin. The reports may exaggerate, but they fit a pattern in which the Turkic threat constrained Tang choices.

The most serious problem was succession. Gaozu's eldest son, Li Jiancheng, was the crown prince, with the ceremonial and administrative standing that role implied. Li Shimin, Prince of Qin, was a military hero with his own household staff, scholars gathered at his Institute of Literary Studies, and a following of generals. Li Yuanji, the fourth son, supported Jiancheng. Gaozu's own attempts to keep both sons in balance, and the presence of favored consorts at court who sided with different princes, made the rivalry increasingly dangerous. The Tang founders had come to power in a system where military strength and family loyalty were closely tied, and no clear rule of succession was established. That absence is not peculiar to Gaozu. The Northern Dynasties and the Sui also saw violent successions, and it would characterize the Tang as well for two generations.

Section 25.3

Emperor Taizong and Imperial Consolidation

On the fourth day of the sixth month of Wude 9 (2 July 626) Li Shimin and his supporters ambushed Li Jiancheng and Li Yuanji at the Xuanwu Gate, the northern gate of the palace precinct in Chang'an, and killed both. Gaozu, presented with the outcome, named Shimin heir; within weeks he yielded the throne and became a retired emperor, dying in 635. Li Shimin was crowned in September as Emperor Taizong. The children of the two dead princes were also killed, according to the sources, and the political circle of the former crown prince was broken up.

How students remember the Xuanwu Gate Incident depends heavily on how the sources tell it. The official accounts present Jiancheng and Yuanji as plotting to kill Shimin, so that his action was a defensive strike, and later Tang tradition invoked the precedent of the Duke of Zhou putting down rebellious brothers. Yet the surviving narrative was edited. The record of Gaozu's reign and of the early years was reworked during Taizong's reign, and scholars generally conclude that the accounts were adjusted to reduce the appearance of premeditation and to enhance Shimin's role in the founding. Taizong himself is reported to have pressed officials on how the incident would be written. The safest conclusion is that Li Shimin seized the throne by force, at the cost of two brothers' lives, and that the dynasty's writers spent considerable effort explaining why this was justified.

Once in power, Taizong (r. 626–649) worked to consolidate the regime. His reign name, Zhenguan, denotes an era later regarded as a model of effective government. In the first days he had to confront the Turkic army at the Wei River, as described at the opening of this chapter. Domestically, he retained and rewarded many of Jiancheng's former officials, including Wei Zheng, who had advised the crown prince to eliminate Shimin and who, when confronted, is said to have answered with unusual candor. Taizong's decision to employ him, and other former opponents, was politically shrewd: it neutralized the old crown prince's network and broadcast the message of a magnanimous ruler.

The image of the Zhenguan court is one of open counsel. Ministers such as Wei Zheng, Fang Xuanling, and Du Ruhui, and the great general Li Jing, are portrayed in the record as debating policy with the emperor and correcting him. The most celebrated compilation, the Zhenguan zhengyao (Essentials of Government of the Zhenguan Era), was compiled by the historian Wu Jing in the early eighth century, long after Taizong's death, as a collection of dialogues designed to teach later rulers how to govern. In it, Wei Zheng reminds Taizong that a ruler is like a boat and the people like water, which can carry the boat or capsize it, an image drawn from the classical philosopher Xunzi. Taizong is shown calling Wei Zheng his mirror, and mourning him when he died in 643. Wei Zheng, in the same text, is credited with hundreds of remonstrances.

Historians treat these accounts with care. Advisory institutions did exist: remonstrating officials were part of the Chancellery, chief ministers conferred with the emperor, and drafts of edicts were subject to review. Taizong also reduced some labor demands after the exhaustion of the Sui, ordered cutbacks in palace construction, and set rules requiring repeated review of death sentences, reportedly five memorials in the capital and three in the provinces, after he regretted an execution. Yet the text that most vividly conveys the ideal Taizong is itself a work of political instruction, compiled under later emperors who wanted a model. It tells us what the Tang wished to remember about good rule, not necessarily how often Taizong actually accepted criticism. Taizong could also be angered by remonstrance, and the same sources record his anger. His own writings, including a guide for his heir, present an ideal of self-cultivation and restraint. The image of the "ideal Taizong" is therefore partly the achievement of the political culture that surrounded him and partly a later projection.

Taizong's policies emphasized legitimacy through both military achievement and cultural patronage. He supported the standardization of the Confucian classics, with the scholar Kong Yingda's collective commentary on the Five Classics completed in the 640s, and he expanded state schools at the capital. In 638 he had a register of leading clans compiled, and when the first draft ranked a prestigious Shandong family above the imperial house, Taizong demanded a revision that put the imperial lineage first. The episode is instructive: the ruler of an empire founded by military force still had to negotiate with aristocratic lineages that regarded genealogy as a source of status independent of office, and the northeastern families, in particular, retained social prestige that no emperor could simply abolish.

The later years of the reign brought their own troubles. Empress Zhangsun, remembered as a restraining influence on the emperor, died in 636. A succession crisis in 643 saw the crown prince, Li Chengqian, deposed after a plot, and a contest between his brother Li Tai and Li Zhi, the future Emperor Gaozong, resolved with the influence of Zhangsun Wuji, the empress's brother. The failed campaign against Goguryeo in 645 damaged Taizong's prestige. When Taizong died in 649, he was buried at Zhaoling, a mountain tomb whose stone reliefs of war horses recalled the campaigns of the founding. The dynasty's institutions had become more stable, but the succession problem that began with Gaozu remained.

Section 25.4

Government and Bureaucracy

The governing architecture of the early Tang was built on Sui foundations and refined in the first decades of the dynasty. This section describes how the machinery was constructed. Chapter 26 explains how it operated in everyday life, and it should be read for the fuller history of officials and examinations.

At the center stood the Three Departments (san sheng) and the Six Ministries (liu bu). The Secretariat (Zhongshu sheng) drafted edicts and policy documents. The Chancellery (Menxia sheng) reviewed them and could return drafts for revision, a check on imperial and administrative decisions. The Department of State Affairs (Shangshu sheng) carried out decisions through its Six Ministries: Personnel, Revenue, Rites, War, Justice, and Works. The Sui had already used this arrangement, with the Secretariat under an older name, and the Tang adopted and elaborated it. Taizong, while Prince of Qin, had held the highest position in the Department of State Affairs, and the office was afterward left vacant out of respect, which left the heads of the Secretariat and Chancellery, and other senior officials granted the status of chief ministers, as the real center of policy deliberation. Chief ministers met to discuss and prepare decisions before they were issued. The system encouraged consultation and provided institutional checks, though the emperor could and did override them.

Alongside the three departments stood the Censorate (yushitai), which monitored officials, investigated misconduct, and reported on local conditions, and a set of specialized courts and directorates dealing with rituals, finance, judicial review, palace supplies, and other functions. Remonstrating officials in the Chancellery and elsewhere were expected to criticize policy openly. The Tang thus built oversight into the structure of government, though such institutions worked as far as ministers and emperors allowed.

Local administration retained the Sui pattern of prefectures (zhou) and counties (xian), each headed by an appointed official, with the county as the level where the state met the population. In 627 Taizong divided the empire into ten circuits (dao), geographic units used to organize inspection rather than to create a separate layer of governors, at least at first. These reflect the government's concern with information: central officials needed reports from the localities, and inspectors sent from the capital were a way to obtain them. County magistrates supervised household registration, taxes, corvée, and courts, but relied on local clerks, village heads, and community leaders to carry out their work. The formal structure ended at the county; below it, villages and neighborhoods were organized into small units of registered households, but real order depended on informal authority.

Recruitment drew on several channels. Aristocratic descent and inherited privilege (yin) gave sons of high officials a route to office. Recommendation by senior officials remained important. The examination system, which had precedents under the Sui, produced only a handful of successful candidates in these years, and the early Tang did not depend on it. Its greater growth, and its role in changing the composition of the elite, are matters for Chapter 26, and here it is enough to say that in the early Tang, family and patronage still counted for far more than examination success.

The analytical question here is continuity versus modification. Continuity was deep: the departments, the ministries, the county and prefectural framework, the capital, and many of the personnel came from the Sui. Modification was also real. The Tang emphasized deliberation and the review function of the Chancellery more explicitly, dropped or adjusted particular Sui offices, and, as the Zhenguan period progressed, developed the circuit as an inspection unit that would later become more important. Above all, the Tang used the same administrative tools with a different political style, one that stressed restraint and consultation. The early Tang state also depended on its aristocrats: leading families supplied ministers and generals, and the court balanced the interests of Guanzhong houses, eastern families, and newcomers. Imperial authority was real but not unilateral.

Section 25.5

The Tang Legal Code

The Tang Code is among the most influential legal texts in East Asian history, and understanding it requires attention to its lineage. The Northern Wei, Northern Qi, Northern Zhou, and Sui had each issued codes, with the Sui Kaihuang code of the early 580s the immediate model. The Tang's first code, the Wude code of 624, followed it closely. The Zhenguan code of 637 reduced some penalties and clarified others; the Yonghui code of 651 was revised, and in 653 the code was issued together with an official commentary, prepared by a committee led by Zhangsun Wuji, that explained the articles and posed hypothetical questions and answers about their application. Later editions, especially under Emperor Xuanzong in the 730s, adjusted the text, and modern scholars believe that the version that survives today reflects revisions from the eighth century, so it is safer to describe it as the Tang code as transmitted than as a text fixed in 653.

The code contained roughly five hundred articles in twelve sections, covering general principles, palace security, household and marriage, state property, unauthorized levies, theft, assault, fraud, arrest, and trial procedures. It was a criminal code, and it was accompanied by administrative ordinances (ling), supplementary regulations (ge), and procedural forms (shi) that dealt with government operations, including the land and tax rules discussed in 25.6. Together, statutes, ordinances, regulations, and forms formed a system of law that served both to punish wrongdoing and to organize the state.

The Tang Code specified five categories of punishment: beating with the light stick, beating with the heavy stick, penal servitude, exile, and death, the last by strangulation or decapitation. Within each, the code prescribed grades: ten to fifty blows with the light stick, sixty to one hundred with the heavy stick, one to three years of servitude, exile at distances of two thousand to three thousand li, and execution. Many penalties could be reduced by the payment of copper, and officials and members of privileged categories could receive lighter treatment through a set of "Eight Deliberations," which recognized rank, imperial kinship, and merit. These features remind us that Tang law was explicitly hierarchical.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Ten Abominations (十惡, shi e)

A list of the ten most serious offenses in the Tang Code, treated as violations of the political and moral order, for which the usual mitigations, such as amnesty, did not apply. The list included plotting rebellion, plotting great sedition, plotting to defect to a foreign state, contumacy toward parents and elders, depravity, great irreverence toward the emperor, unfilial conduct, discord within the family, unrighteousness toward superiors and officials, and incest.

The list is a good illustration of how Tang law combined political and family morality. Offenses against the throne and offenses against parents and kin appeared together, reflecting a view that both belonged to a single hierarchical order. The concept had earlier versions in Northern Qi and Sui law and would be carried into later dynasties and into the codes of Korea, Japan, and Vietnam.

Law was both moral and administrative. It reflected a Confucian-influenced view that social relationships were unequal and that punishment should be scaled to status and kinship, so that a son striking a father was punished far more severely than a father striking a son, and a slave who injured a free person more severely than the reverse. The code distinguished free commoners from "base" people, including slaves and bondservants, and drew a line between wives and concubines. It required officials to report crimes, punished them for negligence, and set rules for the conduct of official business. It also weighed intent and circumstance: accidental harm was treated differently from deliberate acts, and different penalties applied to principals and accessories. Its moral vocabulary was integral to its function as an administrative code, since it defined proper conduct within the family, the village, and the government.

Students must distinguish the normative text from judicial practice. The code states what the law required, but the courts often departed from it. Prefectural and county officials had discretion, and their decisions could be influenced by local conditions, the rank of the parties, and the availability of redemption payments. Emperors periodically issued amnesties and modified sentences by edict, and death sentences were subject to review by the throne. Documents recovered from Turfan and Dunhuang show local officials applying legal rules to real disputes, and copies of legal texts found in these places indicate that the code circulated in the provinces, but they cannot give a full picture of practice across the empire. The Tang Code should be treated as evidence of what the state aspired to and how its legal thinkers reasoned, and as a partial guide to how law worked on the ground.

Its long-term influence was considerable. Japan's eighth-century Taihō code drew on it, Korean and Vietnamese states adapted its concepts, and later Chinese dynasties, including the Song, Ming, and Qing, built on its structure, at times retaining much of its language. The Tang Code thus became a common legal vocabulary across East Asia, even where the actual application of law differed widely.

Section 25.6

Land, Taxation, and Military Organization

The early Tang state ran on a chain of linked institutions: registered households → land allotments → taxation → labor and military obligations → state capacity. Each link depended on the others. Without registration, the government could not assign land or assess taxes. Without land and taxes, it could not support officials, soldiers, or the court. Without soldiers and laborers, it could not defend the frontier or maintain roads, canals, and granaries. This section describes the system as the Tang inherited and modified it. Chapter 26 treats agriculture, landholding, and rural society in fuller detail.

Household registration (huji) was the foundation. Households were listed by name, age, members, and property, in registers compiled every few years at the village and county level. Households were also ranked in grades for purposes of taxation and service, and organized into small groups of mutual responsibility. The Tang inherited this practice from the Northern Dynasties and the Sui, which had undertaken major registration drives to identify hidden households. After the upheavals of the 610s and 620s, rebuilding the registers was a core administrative task. The reported household numbers of the early Zhenguan years, around three million, must be read as the outcome of both real depopulation and incomplete registration.

The equal-field system (juntian) was intended to link registration to land. Derived from Northern Wei and Sui practice, the Tang regulations of 624 provided that adult men would receive a nominal allotment of about one hundred mu, part of it returnable to the state at death or old age and part heritable, with smaller provisions for others and larger allotments for officials and privileged groups. The system aimed to give households enough land to pay taxes and to keep land out of the hands of a few large holders. Historians have long debated how far it actually operated. Registers from Turfan and Dunhuang show households with allotments that seldom matched the legal figures, and where land was scarce, in the densely populated eastern plain, the state could not provide the theoretical amounts. The system should be understood as a regulatory framework and an ideal, applied more fully in some regions than others and increasingly eroded in later centuries.

The tax system, known as zu-yong-diao, imposed three obligations on registered adult men: a grain tax (zu), a labor service (yong) that could be commuted through payment in cloth, and a tax in textiles (diao), silk in the silk-producing regions and hemp elsewhere. Alongside these were separate corvée duties for local projects, and various levies. The amounts were modest by comparison with the Sui's exactions, and the tax structure reflected a preference for a stable base of small farmers over heavy, irregular demands. The state also used granaries, including community granaries set up in the years after 628, to store grain against famine. The tax was calculated per adult man rather than by land, which meant that it worked best when registration was accurate and land was distributed fairly evenly, conditions that could not always be met. Ordinary households therefore experienced the state chiefly through the tax collector and the summons to labor service, and the ability to flee, hide, or bribe was a constant limit on what the state could extract.

Military organization was tied to the same base. The Tang relied on militia units known as fubing. Eligible men were selected from registered households, judged by household wealth, physical strength, and number of adult males, and were enrolled in territorial militia regiments. In exchange for service, in rotation at the capital or on the frontier, they received full or partial tax exemptions and normally supplied much of their own equipment. Units were concentrated in Guanzhong and the surrounding region, giving the dynasty a strong core force near its capitals, and they were attached to the Guards of the imperial court. For major campaigns the Tang supplemented them with recruits, tribal cavalry, and allied Turkic forces, and horses were essential: the government established state pasturelands in the northwest, and its stock of horses grew substantially in the following decades, though figures differ. It is important not to imagine the fubing as one unchanged institution running from Western Wei through Tang. Under the Sui, soldiers had been brought more fully into ordinary household registration, and the Tang adjusted the system again, altering the number and distribution of units and the rules of service over time. Chapter 26 discusses its later strain.

The interdependence of these systems explains both the strength and the fragility of the early Tang. When registers were accurate, allotments meaningful, and burdens moderate, the state could raise revenue, field soldiers, and maintain order. When any link weakened, through population growth pressing on land, through hidden households, through rising military demands, the whole chain suffered. Students should keep this model in mind when reading about the later Tang, and in particular the crises of the eighth century treated in Chapter 29.

Section 25.7

Expansion Into Inner Asia

The Tang expansion beyond the agricultural heartland is best understood as a series of interactions with organized political powers, not as the advance of a state into empty space. On the northern steppe stood the Eastern Turkic Khaganate, a confederation of tribes under the Ashina ruling house. To the west lay the Western Turks, who exercised influence over the Central Asian oases. In the Tarim Basin were kingdoms such as Gaochang, Karasahr, Kucha, Khotan, and Kashgar, whose populations spoke a variety of languages and whose rulers had their own diplomatic and commercial interests. Southwest of Gansu was the Tuyuhun kingdom, ruled by a dynasty of Xianbei origin. Each of these had a history of dealings with the Sui and the earlier northern states.

The Eastern Turks were the immediate problem. They had played a role in the civil wars by supporting rivals, and in 626 Xieli's army reached the Wei River. The Tang paid and negotiated, and it prepared to reverse the balance. The opportunity came at the end of the 620s. A harsh winter in 628–629 killed livestock across the steppe. Subject tribes, including the Xueyantuo and the Tiele confederations, rebelled, and discontent grew within Xieli's own court, in part over the influence of Sogdian advisers. Taizong's ministers argued over whether to attack. In the winter of 629–630 the Tang sent armies under Li Jing and others, launched a surprise attack in a snowstorm, and Xieli was defeated and captured. In 630 the Eastern Turkic Khaganate ceased to exist as a united power.

The aftermath displayed the Tang method. Rather than annex the steppe directly, the Tang resettled large numbers of Turks, many in the Ordos region and the frontier prefectures, and brought Turkic chiefs into the Tang system by granting them titles and military rank. Turkic generals served in Tang armies, among them Ashina Sheer, who later led campaigns in the Tarim Basin. Other Turkic groups, including those that did not submit, remained on the steppe under the Xueyantuo, who dominated the region until a Tang-Uyghur alliance overthrew them in 646. This pattern of using one steppe group to check another was typical.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Tian Kehan (天可汗), "Heavenly Khagan"

A title accepted by Taizong in 630 after the defeat of the Eastern Turks, when, according to Tang sources, chiefs of the steppe peoples requested that he take it. From then on, the emperor could be addressed as emperor (huangdi) by his Chinese subjects and as khagan by steppe leaders. Letters to Inner Asian rulers used the title in the following years.

The title matters because it shows that Tang rulership did not operate in only one political language. A khagan was a steppe overlord whose authority rested on military success, tribal loyalties, and the ability to reward followers. By accepting the title, Taizong placed himself within that tradition and gave steppe leaders a recognizable framework for relations with the Tang. Historians debate how far the title signified real overlordship and how far it was a diplomatic courtesy, and the surviving sources come mostly from the Tang court.

Tang expansion into the Tarim Basin followed. After the Western Turkic ruler Tong Yabghu was assassinated in 628, the Western Turks divided into rival factions, weakening their control. The Tang exploited this. In 640 Hou Junji conquered Gaochang (Turfan), whose rulers, the Qu family, had maintained ties to both the Tang and the Western Turks, and the Tang established a prefecture there and created the Anxi Protectorate General (duhufu), a military and administrative command for the western regions. In the 640s campaigns against Karasahr (644) and Kucha (648) extended Tang influence, and the arrangement later called the Four Garrisons of Anxi, centered on Kucha, Khotan, Kashgar, and Karasahr, developed through the campaigns and administrative changes of 648–658, with the Anxi Protectorate firmly based at Kucha by 658. Taizong also defeated the Tuyuhun in 635, though the Tuyuhun remained a contested buffer and later fell to Tibetan pressure. In the reign of Gaozong the Tang defeated the Western Turkic leader Ashina Helu in 657, and the Tang held broad influence, though Tibetan expansion forced a Tang withdrawal in 670, after which the garrisons changed hands repeatedly before Wu Zetian's forces restored them again in 692. Tibet, Korea, and other frontiers are discussed in Chapter 27.

Administration in these regions took several forms. In areas of dense Chinese settlement, the Tang established standard prefectures and counties. In the oases, it combined garrisons and protectorates with local rulers. In steppe and mountain regions, it created "loose-rein" (jimi) prefectures, in which local chiefs were appointed as governors of nominal prefectures, retained hereditary authority and their own followers, and owed the Tang loyalty and sometimes military service rather than registered taxes. By the later seventh century there were hundreds of such units on paper, and their real degree of Tang control varied widely. The system gave Inner Asian leaders titles and legitimacy within the Tang order, and it allowed the Tang to claim an enormous territory without governing it directly.

Turfan documents offer a picture of daily life under Tang rule: registers of households, records of land, and military and postal documents show soldiers, officials, and local residents of varied backgrounds, and they indicate how Tang administrative practice adapted to an oasis society with its own languages and customs. Such evidence complicates the picture in the court histories, which describe the region mainly through military campaigns and diplomatic embassies.

Peoples of the frontier were political actors with their own aims. Turkic chiefs surrendered, allied, or resisted depending on their own calculations. Oasis kings played the Tang, the Western Turks, and later the Tibetans against one another. Merchants, notably Sogdians, shaped commerce and diplomacy and sometimes served in Tang administration. Soldiers stationed in the garrisons married locally, farmed, and settled. To describe this only as the expansion of "China" would flatten it. The Tang was a multiethnic empire, its ruling house itself of mixed northern descent, and its frontier policy relied on incorporation as much as on force. It also had limits: Tang dominance depended on the weakness of rivals, and when the steppe reorganized, the picture changed.

Section 25.8

Empress Wu and Political Authority

Wu Zhao (624 or 625–705; the sources vary) is the only woman in Chinese history to have ruled as emperor in her own name over a unified empire. Her career has often been told as a morality tale, either about a scheming usurper or, in some modern retellings, a woman who overcame prejudice. Neither picture is adequate. The historical question is how Wu used existing institutions, networks, and ideas while changing who could claim imperial authority. Most of the narratives that survive were written by Confucian historians after her fall, many of them hostile, and students should read them accordingly.

Wu's father, Wu Shiyue, had been a supporter of Gaozu who rose to provincial office; her mother came from a family of Sui-period officials. At about fourteen she entered Taizong's palace as a low-ranking concubine. After his death in 649 she was sent, as women of his harem normally were, to a Buddhist convent. Emperor Gaozong, Taizong's son, brought her back to court a few years later, and she rose in rank until in 655 she replaced Empress Wang. The struggle involved significant ministers. Senior officials from the Guanlong establishment, including Zhangsun Wuji and Chu Suiliang, resisted, and Wu's supporters included officials of less established background, among them Xu Jingzong and Li Yifu. Zhangsun Wuji was later forced into exile and suicide in 659. This conflict is often interpreted, following Chen Yinke, as a clash between the old Guanlong aristocracy and a rising group from other regions and social backgrounds, though scholars caution that the alignments were more complex than that.

From about 660 Gaozong suffered from an illness that affected his vision and his ability to work, and Wu increasingly handled memorials and shared decision-making. By the 670s the pair were called the "Two Sages," and she carried the title Heavenly Empress. She sponsored writing projects and used her position to build a circle of scholars and officials. When Gaozong died in 683, his son Zhongzong took the throne, but Wu, as empress dowager, deposed him within weeks and installed another son, Ruizong, while holding real power. Her rule met resistance. In 684 Xu Jingye's rebellion at Yangzhou, accompanied by a famous manifesto denouncing her, failed, and in 688 members of the Tang imperial house rose in revolt and were suppressed, followed by purges of imperial princes and officials.

In 690 Wu took the decisive step. After petitions from officials, monks, and others, Ruizong asked to change his surname to Wu, and she proclaimed the Zhou dynasty, taking the title Sacred Sovereign Emperor and reigning until 705. She made Luoyang the "Divine Capital," where she conducted much of her rule. She adopted new characters, altered ritual forms, and built a ceremonial hall, the Mingtang, in Luoyang in 688 as a place for the enactment of cosmic and imperial rituals, an institution that had classical roots but had rarely been used. The proclamation of the Zhou was not simply a seizure of the throne. It was an act of political construction, drawing on a range of ideas and precedents.

Buddhism played a central role. Monastic propagandists circulated a commentary on the Great Cloud Sutra identifying Wu with Devi Jingguang (Vimalaprabha), a female-bodied bodhisattva prophesied to attain great sovereignty. Wu's later titulature and propaganda also drew on Maitreya imagery. She reversed earlier policies that had ranked Daoism above Buddhism, issued in 691 an order giving Buddhist clergy precedence, patronized temples and monumental sculpture, and is associated with the great Vairocana Buddha at Longmen, dedicated in 675, to which she is said to have contributed. Wu also used omens and prophecies and appealed to classical precedent. Buddhist legitimation gave her a source of authority unavailable to a female ruler in Confucian terms, though she retained Confucian rituals and appointed Confucian scholars, and this combination reveals the plurality of the ideas available to Tang rulers.

She also reshaped the political elite. Wu expanded the examination system, including the addition of a palace examination in 690 and a military examination in 702, and promoted many officials from less entrenched lineages alongside members of established elite families; aristocratic connections nonetheless remained important. Officials such as Di Renjie and Yao Chong served her and later became models of ministerial virtue, showing that a ruler characterized as a usurper in the tradition could also attract respected statesmen. Wu's court, however, was also marked by political terror. She encouraged informing by means of a bronze receptacle into which anonymous denunciations could be placed, and she employed harsh investigators such as Suo Yuanli, Zhou Xing, and Lai Junchen, who staged treason trials against officials and members of the Li family. Later historians dwell on the cruelty, and some of the accounts are likely exaggerated, yet the existence of purges, and their impact on the old aristocracy and the imperial family, is well supported. Wu also promoted her Wu relatives, and in her later years relied on favorites such as the Zhang brothers, whose influence provoked resentment.

Her foreign policy combined continuity with adjustment. In 692 Tang forces recovered the Four Garrisons of the Tarim Basin from the Tibetans, and in 702 she created the Beiting protectorate in the northwest. Her reign also saw pressure from the Khitans in the northeast and a resurgent Turkic state on the steppe, and it did not repeat Taizong's dramatic victories.

In early 705 a coup led by senior ministers, among them Zhang Jianzhi, killed the Zhang brothers and forced the aged empress to yield. Zhongzong was restored, the Tang name returned, and Wu died in December of that year. She was buried beside Gaozong at Qianling, under a great stone stele that, according to tradition, was left uninscribed, a silence that has been read in many ways. Her posthumous title was reduced to empress.

Wu's importance lies less in personality than in what her reign shows about imperial authority. A woman could not have become emperor by ordinary succession, and her reign required new ideas, drawn especially from Buddhism, and new networks of officials. Yet the institutions through which she governed, the departments, the ministries, the examinations, the codes, and the armies, remained. Gender mattered, and later authors used her example to argue against female rule, but her reign is primarily a case study in the political flexibility of the Tang state. The broader social history of women in the Tang, including the legal and family status of women outside the court, belongs to Chapter 26.

Section 25.9

Tang Imperial Power and Legitimacy

This section steps back from narrative to ask what made Tang rule legitimate. Political legitimacy is the shared belief, among the people whose cooperation a ruler needs, that his or her authority is rightful. In the Tang, several sources of legitimacy operated together, and different audiences responded to different ones.

Dynastic inheritance. The Tang founders presented themselves as heirs to the Sui, receiving the abdication of a Sui emperor. This claim was strengthened by their kinship with the Sui ruling house and by their retention of Sui capitals, offices, and legal forms. Yet it was also contradicted by Tang denunciations of Sui misrule, evident in the official history of the Sui compiled under Wei Zheng's direction, which made the last Sui emperor a warning against tyranny. The Tang thus claimed continuity and criticism at once.

Ancestry and genealogy. The Li family claimed descent from the Longxi Li lineage and from Western Liang rulers, and, from the seventh century, from Laozi, whose personal name was Li Er. The Daoist link was made explicit: Gaozu ranked Daoism first among the teachings in 625, Taizong reaffirmed its precedence in 637, and Gaozong in 666 conferred an imperial title on Laozi. Genealogy was a serious matter in an aristocratic society, as Taizong's clan register of 638 showed.

Military victory and effective administration. The Tang legitimated itself by ending civil war, defeating the Turks, and restoring order. Records of Tang victories, such as the reliefs of war horses at Zhaoling, made this visible. Success in war and a functioning tax and legal system were persuasive to a population exhausted by conflict, though claims of effective government were also propaganda.

Confucian political ideals. Rulers were expected to govern with virtue, accept remonstrance, moderate taxation, and perform state rituals. The Zhenguan tradition of the consultative ruler made these ideals concrete. The Mandate of Heaven, discussed in Chapter 8, was available as a vocabulary of justification, but it functioned less as a mechanical formula than as a flexible argument used to explain why one house had replaced another.

Buddhist legitimation. Buddhist ideas of the universal ruler and prophetic texts offered another model, one Wu Zetian exploited most fully. Buddhist monasteries were also landholders, educators, and centers of local community, and state patronage secured their support.

Ritual and law. Imperial ceremonies, the ancestral temple, sacrifices at the altars of heaven and earth, and the enactment of law all displayed the ruler as the center of a cosmic and social order. The Tang Code's treatment of offenses against the throne belongs here.

Inner Asian rulership. The title Tian Kehan and the use of marriage alliances, tribal cavalry, and resettled Turkic elites reflected a tradition of steppe rulership in which the leader's authority depended on personal ties, military success, and generosity. The early Tang elite came from centuries of interaction with the steppe and were fluent in both traditions. Court culture, including music, clothing, and horse riding, reflected this. Students should not imagine "Chinese" and "steppe" political ideas as opposed camps.

What these varied claims show is that Tang emperorship was not culturally singular. The emperor could be a Confucian sage-ruler for scholar-officials, an heir of Laozi for Daoists, a cakravartin-like protector for Buddhists, a khagan for Turkic leaders, and a lawgiver and military victor for the population at large. Different audiences heard different parts of the message, and the ruler adjusted the emphasis as circumstances demanded. Gaozu relied on Sui succession and military victory. Taizong added the image of the virtuous ruler, the Daoist genealogy, and the Inner Asian title. Wu Zetian emphasized Buddhist prophecy, the promotion of talent, and new ritual forms. Each emphasis reflected the political needs of a particular time and the coalition on which the ruler depended. The result was a flexible political language that helped the dynasty survive the challenge of 690–705 and return to the Tang name.

Section 25.10

Chapter Review and Early Tang Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the creation and consolidation of early Tang imperial power between 617 and 705. Students should now be able to answer four questions. How did the Tang actually win the empire? Through a defensible Guanzhong base, control of the Sui capital, a policy of absorbing defectors, diplomacy with the Turks, and the mistakes of rivals, culminating in the victory at Hulao. What did it inherit from the Sui and the Northern Dynasties? Capitals, the central departments and ministries, prefectures and counties, a legal code, household registration, the equal-field and tax system, the militia, and a governing elite. How did early Tang rulers consolidate and expand power? Through institutional refinement, consultation and law, an accommodation with aristocratic families, and the defeat and incorporation of the Eastern Turks and oasis states. How did the sources of legitimacy change? From Sui succession and victory under Gaozu, to the virtuous ruler and Inner Asian khagan under Taizong, to Buddhist prophecy and new ritual under Wu Zetian.

The review should also help students distinguish dynastic propaganda from political reality (the ideal Taizong versus the record of Xuanwu Gate), institutional continuity from innovation (Sui foundations versus Tang adjustments), and military conquest from stable governance (the defeat of the Turks in 630 versus the long problem of holding the frontier).

Historical Evidence — What Sources Tell Us, and What They Do Not

Court-compiled records and later official histories
Veritable Records and court diaries fed into the Old Tang History (compiled 945) and the New Tang History (1060), and the Comprehensive Mirror (1084). They are detailed but shaped by the need to justify the dynasty, edit the record of 626, and, for Wu Zetian, condemn or defend a female ruler. The Sui history compiled under Wei Zheng presents the Sui as a cautionary tale.
The Zhenguan zhengyao
A compilation of dialogues from the early eighth century, valuable for the Tang ideal of government, less reliable as a literal record of conversations.
Law codes
The Tang Code and its commentary show how the state defined order but not how often or how uniformly rules were applied.
Turfan and Dunhuang documents
Household registers, land records, and administrative papers reveal local practice and the gap between statute and application, though they come from oases and cannot represent the whole empire.
Archaeology and art
Tombs and inscriptions of officials, the reliefs at Zhaoling, and the Wordless Stele at Qianling show how the Tang commemorated itself and its rulers.
Old Turkic inscriptions
The Orkhon inscriptions offer a steppe view of Tang relations, written a century later to serve a restored khaganate.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Early Tang Success a Break With the Sui or a Continuation of Sui Institutions?

The question: The Tang built a durable empire within a few decades of the Sui's collapse. Did it succeed by breaking with Sui methods or by inheriting and adapting them?

Interpretation A — Continuation: The Tang founders belonged to the same northwestern elite as the Sui, kept its capitals, central offices, provincial structure, legal framework, land and tax rules, and militia system, and drew on Sui personnel. On this view, associated with Chen Yinke's Guanlong thesis, the Tang was a phase of one Sui-Tang political formation, and the Sui's failure was one of political management, not institutional design.
Interpretation B — Significant modification: The Tang learned from Sui failure, lightened burdens, cultivated consultation, adjusted legal penalties, and built new legitimacy claims. Its historians portrayed the Sui as an example of tyranny, which helped justify Tang rule. On this view, the Tang's political style and policy priorities differed importantly, and the difference helps explain why it lasted longer.

Evidence: Supporters of A point to institutional lineages, the reuse of codes and offices, and overlapping personnel. Supporters of B point to tax and labor policy, the institutional emphasis on review and remonstrance, and the fact that the Tang narrative of Sui failure was written by Tang officials with a stake in the comparison.

Why it matters: The debate concerns how historians weigh institutions against policy and political culture, and how much of a dynasty's success is attributable to its inheritance.

Student task: Choose one institution, such as the legal code or the equal-field system, and evaluate which interpretation it better supports. What additional evidence would you need?

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Tang Founding and Consolidation

Note: these are analytical reconstructions based on patterns in the evidence, not quotations from named individuals.

Gaozu's court: For the founders, the Tang was the legitimate successor to the Sui, restoring order after a catastrophic reign. Victory validated their claim, and generous treatment of defectors was a practical means of securing it.
Defeated regional rivals and their followers: Leaders such as Wang Shichong and Dou Jiande were captured or killed. Their supporters, especially in Hebei, could see the Tang as a conqueror, and the revolt of Liu Heita in 622–623 suggests that resentment at Tang rule and its officials was real.
Aristocratic families: Guanzhong houses benefited from the founding as insiders. Shandong families, with prestigious lineages but less political power, negotiated for status, as the dispute over the 638 clan register shows.
Registered farming and military households: They experienced the dynasty through registration, tax collectors, and labor summonses. Moderate demands and the end of civil war brought relief, but they also bore the cost of campaigns, and their voices survive mostly through official documents.
Turkic elites: Some Turkic leaders accepted Tang rank and service and prospered, while others, later, saw the period as subjugation, as the Bilge inscription suggests. The Tang victory of 630 meant loss of political independence for the Eastern Turks, and it was not a benign unification for them.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Imperial Authority From Gaozu to Wu Zetian

What Changed

  • Legitimacy shifted from Sui succession and victory to include the virtuous, consultative ruler, the Daoist genealogy, the Inner Asian khaganal title, and, under Wu, Buddhist prophecy
  • The reach of the state extended from the Guanzhong and Central Plains core into the Tarim Basin
  • Law was codified with commentary, and the examination system grew in importance
  • For the first time, a woman ruled as emperor in her own dynasty

What Stayed the Same

  • The Sui-derived departments, ministries, prefectures, and counties
  • The dependence of the state on registered households for taxes, labor, and soldiers
  • The role of aristocratic and military families in court and army
  • Recurrent succession violence and dependence on steppe relations

Who experienced what: Officials and aristocrats felt the political shifts most directly. Farmers experienced continuity in registration, taxes, and service. Inner Asian peoples experienced change most sharply. Why it matters: The pattern shows a state that changed its claims more quickly than its administrative machinery.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Defeat of the Eastern Turkic Khaganate, 630

Causes

Long-term: Tang consolidation and stronger armies, access to horses, and memory of the 626 humiliation. Short-term: the severe winter of 628–629, rebellion of subject tribes, divisions within Xieli's court, and Tang diplomacy that lowered Turkic vigilance.

The event

In winter 629–630 Tang armies under Li Jing and others attacked in difficult conditions, defeated Xieli, and captured him. The khaganate's central authority collapsed.

Immediate effects

Resettlement of many Turks in Tang territory, Turkic officers in Tang service, Taizong's acceptance of the title Tian Kehan, and a shift in the steppe balance toward the Xueyantuo.

Long-term effects

Greater Tang freedom to act in the Tarim Basin, but also a long dependence on Turkic soldiers and an eventual Turkic revival in 682, whose leaders remembered the period as subjugation.

Historical complexity: The outcome cannot be attributed to Tang strength alone. Environmental catastrophe, steppe politics, and internal Turkic conflict were essential, and the result was neither permanent conquest nor simple unification.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: Early Tang Households

For most people, the early Tang meant the recovery of a farming household after years of war. Recovering families cultivated grain in the north and rice in the south, spun and wove cloth to meet the textile tax, and sent adult men to labor service. Where the registers and land allotments functioned, households had at least a claim to land; where land was short, families made do with less or fell into debt. Locust plagues and drought in the late 620s brought hardship, and the court's granary policies were a response to the risk of famine. Tang sources tell of the emperor's concern in these years, though such stories are also part of the ruler's image.

Soldiers of the militia lived on farms but carried the burden of rotation to the capital or the frontier, providing their own equipment. Oasis families at Turfan, recorded in registers, combined agriculture with textile work and dealt with officials and merchants of mixed origin. Women appear in law and documents as wives, mothers, and property holders, and in early Tang politics as figures such as Princess Pingyang, Empress Zhangsun, and Wu Zhao, though these elite women were exceptional. Social classification also mattered: the code distinguished free people from bondservants and slaves, whose position was legally inferior. Historians must be careful not to take the concerns of the court for the concerns of the village.

Regional and Frontier Context

Regional and Frontier Context

Guanzhong and Taiyuan formed the Tang core, with the militia concentrated there. Luoyang and Henan were an economic center and the base of Wang Shichong, later treated by Wu as the Divine Capital. Hebei and Shandong had strong local identities and prestigious families, and produced early resistance. The Yangtze and Jiangnan regions supported rival regimes and remained economically important. Sichuan and Lingnan were brought into the Tang order more through negotiation with local leaders. The northeast included the Khitan and Goguryeo, later important. The northwest and the Tarim Basin were the zone of Turks, Tuyuhun, and oasis states. Regions differed in the pace and manner of incorporation, and the capital's experience should not be taken as representative.

China and the Wider World

China and the Wider World

The early Tang was part of a wider Eurasian world. Its relations with the Turks shaped its politics from the start. The Buddhist monk Xuanzang left for India in the late 620s and returned in 645 with texts, a journey that connected Tang China to Indian learning. Tibet under Songtsen Gampo became a major power, and the Tang court arranged a marriage alliance in 641. The Tang cooperated with Silla and fought Goguryeo, and Japan sent embassies and later drew heavily on Tang law. The fall of the Sasanian Empire to Arab armies in the 650s brought Persian exiles to the Tang court. These are treated fully in Chapter 27. The point here is that Tang policy was active: Tang diplomats, generals, merchants, and monks participated in these exchanges as agents, not just recipients.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the early Tang emerged from the political, military, administrative, and social world created during the Northern Dynasties and the Sui, and that its success rested on inheriting and adapting that world. The founding in 618 was a political act, not the completion of unification. Li Yuan, a member of the Guanlong elite and a cousin of the Sui emperor, took Chang'an, installed a Sui prince, and proclaimed the Tang, and he then faced a decade of civil war among successor regimes.

The Tang won through a defensible base, control of the Sui capital, a policy of absorbing defectors, avoidance of full conflict with the Turks, and the weaknesses of rivals, with the campaign at Hulao in 621 the decisive northern victory. Gaozu emerged as a substantive founder who rebuilt government, issued a standard coinage, and promulgated legal and fiscal measures, though later Taizong-centered histories diminished him.

Taizong took power through the Xuanwu Gate Incident of 626, a violent event whose memory was managed by Tang historians. His reign consolidated the regime and established the image of the consultative ruler, largely through sources compiled later, and students must read that image critically. He worked with former opponents, negotiated with aristocratic families, supported the standardization of the classics, and expanded Tang power. His late years were marked by a succession crisis and a failed campaign against Goguryeo.

The governing architecture of the early Tang, with the Three Departments, Six Ministries, Censorate, prefectures, counties, and circuits, retained Sui foundations and modified the political style. The Tang Code, developed through the Wude, Zhenguan, and Yonghui codes and issued with commentary in 653, combined moral and administrative functions and was hierarchical in its treatment of status and kinship. Students must distinguish its normative text from judicial practice, of which documents from Turfan and Dunhuang offer glimpses.

The fiscal and military system linked household registration, equal-field allotments, the zu-diao-yong tax, labor service, and the militia. Its strength depended on accurate registration and moderate burdens, and it was neither uniformly applied nor unchanging.

Expansion into Inner Asia followed the defeat of the Eastern Turks in 630, aided by environmental catastrophe and steppe divisions. The Tang resettled Turks, employed Turkic commanders, used loose-rein prefectures, and established protectorates, culminating in the Four Garrisons of Anxi. Turks, oasis kings, and merchants were political actors, and the Bilge inscription shows how the Turks later remembered the period.

Wu Zetian rose from Taizong's palace to become empress, regent, and, in 690, emperor of the Zhou dynasty. She used Buddhist prophecy, new ritual, examinations, and a network of officials, and she also relied on terror against opponents. She governed through the existing institutions and lost power in 705 to a coup that restored the Tang. Her reign shows both the flexibility of the state and the changing sources of imperial authority.

Legitimacy in the early Tang was multiple: dynastic inheritance, genealogy, Confucian ideals, Daoist ancestry, Buddhist patronage, military victory, ritual, law, and Inner Asian rulership. Different audiences responded to different claims, and Tang emperorship was not culturally singular.

Together these developments explain why the early Tang was both powerful and fragile. Its success depended on alliances among aristocrats, officials, soldiers, regional elites, and Inner Asian peoples, and on institutions that could be strained by population growth, military demand, and succession conflict.

Chapter 26 turns from the construction of Tang power to the way the Tang state and society functioned in practice, including officials, examinations, landholding, commerce, cities, and family life.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a durable empire was built out of the wreckage of another. The Tang did not begin from nothing. It inherited a world of northern aristocrats, registered households, canals and capitals, and steppe relations, and it made that inheritance work through war, negotiation, and adaptation. Understanding this prevents the familiar story of a failed Sui and a glorious Tang, and replaces it with one of continuity and adjustment.

The institutions and ideas examined here shaped later Chinese history and East Asian history. The Tang legal code became a model for legal thought across the region, the administrative departments and ministries structured government for centuries, and the fiscal and military system, with its strengths and strains, set the stage for the transformations of the eighth century discussed in Chapter 29. The experience of Wu Zetian revealed how many sources of authority a ruler could draw on, and how far the meaning of emperorship could be stretched without breaking the state.

The chapter also reveals larger themes: the porousness of the line between "Chinese" and "steppe" political traditions, the role of frontier peoples as political actors, the fragility of official records as evidence, and the importance of aristocratic and local networks in sustaining imperial power. It connects China to Central Asia, the steppe, Buddhism, and the wider Eurasian world, in which Tang emperors, Turkic khagans, oasis kings, monks, and merchants were all participants.

For students of Chinese history, the early Tang illustrates a history that is diverse, interconnected, contested, and unfinished. Its rulers were creative and violent, its institutions adaptive and limited, and its record shaped by the people who wrote it. The next chapters ask how such a state worked in daily life, how it connected to the wider world, what cultural achievements it produced, and how the rebellion of An Lushan would test all of it.
