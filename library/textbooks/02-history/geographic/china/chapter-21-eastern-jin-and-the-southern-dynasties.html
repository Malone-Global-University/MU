Part IV — Division, Migration, and Religious Transformation

Eastern Jin and the Southern Dynasties

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In 317, a Jin prince named Sima Rui established a court at Jiankang, far to the southeast of the old imperial heartland around Luoyang. To contemporaries who still imagined the Yellow River plains as the natural center of imperial politics, his government could easily have appeared temporary: a refugee court waiting for the moment when northern armies would carry it home. It did not go home. Jiankang remained the capital of successive southern regimes for nearly three centuries, and the society that developed around it permanently altered the political and economic geography of China.

Chapter 20 ended with the ruin of the Western Jin. Luoyang fell in 311, Chang'an in 316, and the northern plains passed into the hands of competing states, many of them founded by leaders from Inner Asian communities and served by Chinese-speaking officials and soldiers alike. This chapter follows the other half of that catastrophe. While northern capitals burned, a Jin prince, his advisers, and a stream of northern families moved toward the lower Yangtze, where a court could be reassembled on the foundations of an older regional state, Eastern Wu. The question this chapter asks is deceptively simple: how did a refugee Jin court survive in the lower Yangtze, how did northern migrants and established southern populations create a new political order, and why did the south become increasingly important to the long-term history of China?

The chapter studies the Eastern Jin dynasty (317–420) and the four dynasties that followed it in succession at Jiankang: Liu Song (420–479), Southern Qi (479–502), Liang (502–557), and Chen (557–589). Its central argument is that these were not merely weakened remnants of northern empires. Northern refugee elites, established southern families, military commanders, farmers, merchants, religious communities, and local populations together constructed a durable southern political system. Dynasties changed frequently, and emperors struggled against aristocratic and military power. Yet the Yangtze region became more densely settled, more commercially active, more culturally influential, and more politically central. The chapter therefore distinguishes dynastic instability from regional weakness. Five dynasties fell, but the southern world did not begin from zero each time. Jiankang remained the capital; leading families persisted across regime changes; administrative expertise passed from one court to the next; villages continued to be settled and markets to expand.

What changed during these centuries was considerable. The capital of a Chinese imperial state moved south of the Yangtze and stayed there. Population moved with it, in waves extending into the fifth century, and the registration, taxation, and military organization of the south had to adapt. Jiankang grew into one of the great cities of its age. Rice agriculture, river transport, handicrafts, and coastal commerce expanded. Literary, calligraphic, and Buddhist cultures flourished at courts and in temples, and a distinctive southern elite identity took shape. What continued was equally important: imperial titles, classical education, counties and commanderies, the aspiration to restore a unified realm, the dependence of the state on rural households for grain, cloth, and labor, and the strategic tension between the Yangtze and the northern plains.

The people affected by these developments were far more numerous than the dynastic founders whose names organize the chronology. They included northern aristocrats who arrived with genealogies and libraries but sometimes little land; established Jiangnan families whose ancestors had served Wu; soldiers recruited from migrant populations in the lower Yangtze; peasants who received refugee neighbors, or fled themselves; boatmen, merchants, monks, women who served as regents and household managers, and hill and river communities whom imperial officials described in the sources with terms that reveal more about the officials than about the peoples so labeled. This chapter attempts to keep such groups in view even though the sources overwhelmingly speak with the voices of the court and the literate elite.

The evidence for the period is unevenly distributed and often retrospective. Some documents are nearly contemporary: inscriptions, tomb finds, surviving letters, literary anthologies, and works such as Liu Yiqing's collection of anecdotes about the elite. The great narrative sources, however, were compiled long after the events, mostly under the Tang, and were shaped by the political needs of later dynasties and by a durable rhetoric that portrayed southern courts as decadent. Historians therefore read these texts with attention to who wrote them and why. Archaeology, especially in and around modern Nanjing, adds sculpture, brick tombs, ceramics, and settlement evidence that can qualify the written story.

The chapter connects to larger histories in several ways. Within China, it explains why any later unified empire would need to integrate both a northern world shaped by Inner Asian and Yellow River traditions and a southern world shaped by migration and Yangtze agriculture, a problem taken up in Chapter 24. Within Asia, the southern courts participated in maritime networks reaching Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean and in the transmission of Buddhism. And in the wider comparative history of late antiquity, a period in which many Eurasian societies experienced migration, political fragmentation, and religious transformation, the southern dynasties show that fragmentation could be creative as well as destructive.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the collapse of Western Jin led to the founding of the Eastern Jin at Jiankang in 317–318, and why Eastern Jin was both a continuation of Jin legitimacy and a new regional regime.
  2. Describe Jiankang's geography, its inheritance from Eastern Wu, and its growth as a political, commercial, and cultural center.
  3. Distinguish among elite lineages, ordinary refugees, soldiers, dependents, and established southern residents in the story of northern migration.
  4. Analyze the relationship among the imperial house, great families, and military commanders in Eastern Jin politics.
  5. Trace the rise of Liu Yu and the transfer of power from Jin to Liu Song in 420, and assess why military founders still relied on aristocratic administrative networks.
  6. Evaluate the evidence for agricultural expansion, commercial growth, and demographic change in the south without overstating the extent of the later Tang-Song economic shift.
  7. Compare the political strategies of Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, and Chen and explain the significance of the Hou Jing rebellion.
  8. Interpret a contemporary source, the Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Gathering, and identify what it can and cannot reveal about southern society.
  9. Assess competing explanations for the transformation of the south: northern migration, prior southern development, or interaction between them.
  10. Contextualize relations between southern and northern states as durable political rivalry combined with continuing movement of people, goods, monks, and texts.
  11. Distinguish dynastic instability from regional weakness and explain why the Yangtze region became central to later Chinese history.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why was Jiankang, rather than a newly founded city, the site of the Eastern Jin capital, and what did it inherit from Eastern Wu?
  3. How did northern émigré families, established southern families, and military commanders share, contest, and reshape power?
  4. What does the history of Liu Yu suggest about the relationship between military force and aristocratic administration?
  5. How did migration and earlier southern development each contribute to the growth of the Yangtze region?
  6. Why did dynastic instability not prevent long-term regional development?
  7. How should historians read sources compiled by later dynasties that often criticized southern courts as decadent?
  8. In what ways did north and south remain connected even when they were governed by rival states?
  9. What can elite literary and religious culture tell us about southern society, and what does it leave out?

Historical Context

The story of the southern dynasties begins in the political world of the third century. When the Han Empire fragmented (Chapters 18 and 19), one of the successor states, Wu, was based in the lower Yangtze. Its founders, the Sun family, made Jianye (on the site of modern Nanjing) their capital in 229 and governed a territory that extended, at varying depths of control, across the lower and middle Yangtze and into the southeast and Lingnan. Wu rested on alliances between the Sun rulers and powerful Jiangnan families, on armies that were partly hereditary, and on the productive agricultural lands of the lower Yangtze. When Western Jin conquered Wu in 280, the region did not disappear into a northern empire. Southern families continued to hold local influence, and some, such as the Gu, Lu, and Zhou lineages of the Wu region, later became important in the politics of Eastern Jin. Students should therefore begin by rejecting the notion of an empty or backward south awaiting northern settlement. The Yangtze world of 300 was populated, agriculturally productive, politically experienced, and only partially integrated into the empire ruled from Luoyang.

Geography shaped the possibilities of the region. As Chapter 2 explained, the Yangtze basin is a system of great rivers, lakes, floodplains, and hills. The lower Yangtze and Taihu region offered dense networks of waterways, fertile alluvial land, and access to the sea; the middle Yangtze, centered on Jingzhou and Jiangling, linked the lower river to the Han River valley, to Sichuan upstream, and to the southern lakes and hills. The Huai River region between the Yellow River plains and the Yangtze was a contested corridor, the main zone across which armies, refugees, and diplomats passed. To the south and southeast lay hill country, the coast, and, further away, Lingnan and its port of Guangzhou. Defending the south meant defending river lines, and the Yangtze as a barrier worked in favor of a court that possessed boats, ports, and fortified river towns. Cavalry-based northern armies were formidable on plains but faced difficulty in a landscape of canals, marshes, and rivers.

The political background is the Western Jin collapse detailed in Chapter 20: the War of the Eight Princes, the Yongjia crisis, the capture of Luoyang in 311 and of Chang'an in 316, and the capture and later execution of Emperor Min (Sima Ye) by the Han-Zhao regime. These events destroyed the Jin capitals and much of the northern administrative apparatus, and they set large numbers of people in motion. Some fled south because their own communities had been devastated; others moved with armies or under the leadership of powerful families that organized followers into defensive communities; still others were relocated by force. The south did not receive all refugees. Many northerners remained where they were and adapted to new rulers, while others migrated west, to the northeast, or to the Korean peninsula's borderlands. Yet the southern flow was historically decisive, because it supplied a court with prestigious personnel and, over time, a military and demographic reservoir.

Socially, the Jin world was dominated by prestigious hereditary lineages. The Cao Wei government's system of nine ranks, which used local assessors to evaluate candidates for office, had in practice given great advantage to families that already held office and cultivated networks of marriage, patronage, and learning. Under Jin these tendencies strengthened. Rank in the bureaucracy, family reputation, educational attainment, and social connection became intertwined, and marriage between prestigious lineages became a political resource. The refugee court in the south inherited this social structure and adapted it to an environment where local families also claimed standing. The result was not a fixed nobility on the European model, but a hierarchy of families whose position depended on maintaining office and reputation from one generation to the next.

Culturally, the period was intellectually lively. Scholars debated the nature of the cosmos, human nature, and the Zhuangzi and Laozi texts in the discursive style later called "pure conversation" and associated with the movement known as Xuanxue, or "Dark Learning." Confucian education, especially study of ritual and the classics, remained the basis of elite training. Buddhism, which had entered China centuries earlier, was gaining adherents at courts and in monasteries, a subject Chapter 23 examines systematically. Religious movements such as the Celestial Masters tradition were active in the south as well. Together these currents gave the southern elite a rich but competitive intellectual environment.

Finally, students should keep in mind that "China" was not a fixed territorial or political concept in this period. The Jin court that moved to Jiankang continued to claim the legitimate succession from the unified Jin dynasty and therefore claimed authority over the whole realm, including the lost north. Northern states, in turn, developed their own claims to legitimacy, drawing on Chinese dynastic precedent, Inner Asian traditions of rulership, and Buddhist ideas. In the fifth and sixth centuries, "north" and "south" came to describe durable political systems, and later historians retrospectively named the sequence the Northern and Southern Dynasties. Modern boundaries are unhelpful here: the southern courts governed territory that included parts of several modern provinces, and their effective reach varied considerably from decade to decade.

Section 21.1

The Founding of Eastern Jin

The Eastern Jin did not begin with the fall of Luoyang. Its origins lie in the earlier decision to send Sima Rui, a prince of Langye and a member of the Jin imperial family, to the lower Yangtze. In 307, in the middle of the civil wars that convulsed the Jin court, Sima Rui was posted to Jianye, a city that had been Eastern Wu's capital. He was accompanied by Wang Dao, a young statesman of the Wang lineage of Langye who would become one of the founders of the southern order. At this point no one could know that the posting would be decisive. Sima Rui was one of many princes given regional commands, and his appointment reflected the court's need for military and political presence in the southeast. When Emperor Min ascended the throne in 313, the city's name was changed from Jianye to Jiankang, because the character for "ye" was part of the emperor's personal name and had to be avoided. The renaming is a small example of how imperial ritual and language accompanied even a rapidly failing state.

The first years in the south were precarious. Sima Rui was a northern prince in a region governed by powerful local families whose loyalty could not be presumed. Wang Dao's role was to mediate. According to later sources, he worked to attract the support of prominent southern figures, including members of the Gu and Ji families of the Wu region, and to persuade them that the prince could offer their families a place in a legitimate government. These accounts survive in later histories and anecdotal collections and should be read cautiously, but their broad point is consistent with the outcome: Eastern Jin was built through bargains among the Sima house, northern émigré families, and established southern elites, not by imposing an imported administration on a passive region. The fact that Jiankang was accessible to the southeast's agricultural wealth and to the Yangtze's river transport made it a plausible political base.

News of the northern collapse forced the question of succession. After Chang'an fell in 316 and Emperor Min was taken captive, Sima Rui, who had been acting as regional commander with the support of the émigré and southern elites, took the title King of Jin in 317. In 318, when word arrived that Emperor Min had been executed, he assumed the imperial title and is known to history as Emperor Yuan. Students sometimes find the two dates confusing. They mark two stages of a single process: the assumption of a royal, essentially regency-style title in 317 while the captive emperor was still alive, and the full imperial accession in 318. Both are used in modern scholarship as the beginning of the Eastern Jin, and the discrepancy reflects historical reality rather than a mistake.

The dynasty's name and claims are important. Sima Rui did not describe himself as the founder of a new house. He presented himself as the legitimate heir of the Jin emperors, a continuation rather than a replacement of the dynasty. This claim mattered for two reasons. Domestically, it allowed émigré officials to see themselves as preserving the dynastic order rather than starting a provincial regime. Externally, it committed the court, at least rhetorically, to reconquest of the north. In practice, however, Eastern Jin was a regional regime whose effective control at first covered only part of the Yangtze basin and the southeast. Its territory expanded and contracted over the following century. Modern historians therefore call it "Eastern Jin" to distinguish it from Western Jin (Chapter 20), while noting that contemporaries generally called it simply Jin.

The foundation of the new court depended on a distinctive balance of power. Emperor Yuan needed the support of the Wang family; contemporaries are reported to have said that the Wang and the Sima shared the world between them, a saying often cited to illustrate the unusual influence of the great families. Yet the same sources are equally clear that the emperor sought to build a counterweight to that influence, promoting officials from other families and cultivating military commanders. This attempt provoked the first major challenge, the revolt of Wang Dun in the early 320s (Section 21.4). The early Eastern Jin thus already exhibited what would become the recurring political problem of the southern dynasties: the emperor's dependence on elite families and armies whose interests did not always coincide with his own.

Nor was the new regime simply Luoyang in exile. Court ritual and offices followed Jin precedent, and many ministers were northerners who brought knowledge of protocol and law. But the south required improvisation. The court had few archives, a limited tax base at first, and constant demands for defense. It had to accommodate northern officials who lacked estates, southern families who had estates but limited access to court posts, and soldiers who needed to be fed. Economic and social arrangements, such as tax exemptions for newly arrived households and special administrative units for migrants (Section 21.3), were adjustments to these circumstances. The result was a hybrid: a regime that looked like the Jin of the north while operating in a very different environment.

Historical Evidence
Writing the History of the Southern Courts

Historians of the southern dynasties work with an unusual combination of abundant text and thin documentation. The principal narrative sources are dynastic histories compiled long after the events. The Book of Jin was produced under the Tang in the seventh century, drawing on earlier accounts that are now largely lost. The Book of Song and Book of Southern Qi were compiled in the late fifth and early sixth centuries by scholars who worked under the successor regimes, and the Book of Liang and Book of Chen were completed under the Tang. Every one of these works was written from a particular dynastic vantage point, often to justify a change of regime, and they can be highly selective about which events and persons to emphasize. Later authors also tended to depict southern rulers and aristocrats as decadent, a moral judgment that served the ideological interests of the northern-derived Sui and Tang states that had absorbed the south.

More nearly contemporary evidence exists but is sparse. It includes letters and literary compositions preserved in anthologies, epitaphs and tomb inscriptions, archaeological remains of brick tombs and city sites near Nanjing, ceramics, and collections of anecdotes such as Liu Yiqing's Shishuo xinyu, compiled in the fifth century. The anecdotes are vivid but literary, not neutral records. Census figures preserved in the histories are valuable but reflect what officials managed to register, not the entire population. Students should treat each source as evidence for a particular question and look for ways to combine sources whose biases differ.

Section 21.2

Jiankang and the Southern Political Center

Jiankang was not a city that refugees created from nothing. It had been Jianye, the capital of Eastern Wu, and its location reflected geographic logic that had attracted earlier rulers. The city lay on the south bank of the lower Yangtze, at a point where the river was wide enough to serve as a defensive barrier but where it also connected to a dense network of waterways. Hills to the east and north, including the Zhong Mountain, gave natural fortification, and the Qinhuai River, flowing through the city, linked the capital to the surrounding countryside. To the southeast lay the productive lands of the Taihu basin and the Zhejiang region; upstream the river gave access to Jingzhou and the middle Yangtze. This location made Jiankang a place where grain and other goods could be gathered and where boats could move troops and officials. The Jin court chose it, in effect, because Wu had already shown that it could be a capital.

Under Eastern Jin and its successors the city grew into a complex urban center. It contained a walled palace precinct, government offices, aristocratic residences, markets, temples, and residential districts, and it extended along the Qinhuai. Later tradition associated the elite lineages of Wang and Xie with a residential quarter known as Wuyi Lane. Commercial life was busy: boats brought rice, cloth, and other goods; artisans produced ceramics, metalwork, and textiles; markets served both elite consumers and ordinary households. Buddhist temples multiplied, some founded by emperors and others by aristocratic patrons, and in the sixth century Jiankang was reputed to contain hundreds of them. Sources give population figures that suggest a very large city by the standards of the age, in the range of hundreds of thousands of inhabitants at its height in the early sixth century, but such numbers should be treated with caution because ancient authors were not always careful with census data and because many residents were transient.

The political character of Jiankang was distinctive. As a capital, it concentrated the elite: ministers, generals, imperial kin, scholars, monks, and their households. Political power was exercised through the palace and through the residences of the great families, and in practice much politics took the form of alliances, marriages, and networks that ran through these households. The city was also militarily exposed. It lay only a few days from the Yangtze frontier and depended on troops stationed nearby, especially in Jingkou (modern Zhenjiang) downstream and in the middle Yangtze commands. Commanders at Jingzhou or Jingkou who controlled armies could threaten the capital, as several did. In this sense Jiankang's importance was accompanied by vulnerability, and southern political history is partly the history of how emperors tried to secure the surrounding region.

Archaeology and art history help to make the city visible. Brick tombs in and around Nanjing preserve molded or stamped brick pictures, including a well-known composition of figures identified as the "Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove" together with Rong Qiqi, evidence that elite ideals associated with earlier literary circles were displayed in funerary contexts. Large stone animal sculptures, winged lions and related beasts, stand near the tombs of Southern Dynasties emperors and princes in the Nanjing region, marking a monumental tradition of imperial burial. These remains show the scale of resources concentrated at the capital, although they cannot tell us how ordinary residents lived. Jiankang's literary reputation, meanwhile, rests largely on written sources that were produced by and for its educated elite.

Historical Vocabulary
Six Dynasties (Liuchao, 六朝)

The term "Six Dynasties" has been used in several ways, which is a reminder that historical labels are conventions rather than facts. In one common use, it refers to the six regimes that had their capital at Jianye or Jiankang: Eastern Wu (222/229–280), Eastern Jin, Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, and Chen. In other uses it is applied more broadly to the whole period between the fall of Han and the Sui reunification. Because this chapter concentrates on the last five of the Jiankang-centered regimes, it uses "Southern Dynasties" for Liu Song through Chen and "Eastern Jin and the Southern Dynasties" for the whole sequence covered here.

The term matters because it draws attention to Jiankang as an enduring political and cultural center across several regimes. It invites students to ask what persisted from one dynasty to the next. It also carries risks: it can imply that the south was a single uniform civilization, or that the Wu dynasty and the later dynasties formed a seamless unit. Historians use it as a convenient shorthand while recognizing its limits.

Section 21.3

Northern Migration Into the South

Chapter 20 introduced the mass movements that followed the Western Jin collapse. This section asks what happened after migrants arrived. The first point to make is that migration was not a single event. It occurred in waves, beginning in the years of the Eight Princes' wars and the Yongjia crisis, continuing through the fourth century in response to warfare among northern states, and extending into the early fifth century and beyond. Migrants moved to the lower Yangtze around Jiankang and Jingkou, but also to the middle Yangtze, the Huai and Han valleys, the southeast coast, and farther south. Some traveled in small family groups, some with the followings of prominent lineages, and some as military units. Many died on the road or in the disease-ridden lowlands where they settled. Using a government record from 464 CE, the modern historian Tan Qixiang estimated that successive waves of southward migration since the Yongjia crisis had added roughly one million northerners to the southern population, about one-sixth of the registered Liu Song population. This is a reconstruction from later registration evidence rather than a direct count and should be treated as approximate.

It is essential to distinguish groups within the migrant population. At the top were elite lineages whose members carried genealogies, ritual expertise, and connections to the old capital. Their departure from the north was part of their identity, and they often described themselves as sojourners, awaiting an eventual return. Below them stood a much larger group of ordinary refugees: farmers and artisans who traveled with relatives or neighbors. Soldiers formed another group. Commanders recruited fighters from among the displaced, and some households became military families. Dependents attached to great families also moved with them; the sources refer to clients and bound households whose status ranged from relatively autonomous to strongly subordinated. Finally, there were people who moved involuntarily, such as prisoners and captives taken in wars. Treating all of these as "northern refugees" hides the different experiences and levels of power they possessed.

Equally important, migrants entered an inhabited land. Southern society included established Han-speaking families whose ancestors had lived in the region for generations, farming communities of various backgrounds, communities identified by officials as belonging to other peoples, and river, coastal, and hill populations that officials treated as distinct. In the lower Yangtze, Wu-region families held land and local authority. The northern émigré minority therefore had to find ways of living alongside residents who outnumbered them. Language differences, at least in speech, were noticed; anecdotes in the Shishuo xinyu mention the contrast between the speech of northern elites and Wu dialects, and northern men are depicted as sometimes disdaining southern customs while southern families sometimes resented northern political dominance. Such anecdotes are literary, but they point to real friction.

The state's response was administrative and fiscal. To accommodate migrants, the court created refugee or émigré commanderies and counties, administrative units named after places in the north but located in the south. These units allowed displaced households to be registered under their former place names, to keep a collective identity, and, in the first decades, to enjoy exemptions from taxes and service that reflected their supposedly temporary status. Migrants were often recorded on separate registers, sometimes called white registers, in contrast to the "yellow" registers of long-settled households. The system helped the regime keep track of migrant communities, but it also created inequalities. Migrants with exemptions competed with locals who bore burdens, and powerful families could shelter dependents from the tax collector. As the expectation of returning north faded, the court sought to integrate migrants into ordinary registration. Reforms known as "territorial registration" (tuduan) were carried out in 364 under Huan Wen and again in 413 under Liu Yu, converting refugee households into ordinary local taxpayers and reducing the number of nominal northern places in the south. These reforms met resistance, and the administrative geography of the south remained untidy. Students should therefore avoid picturing a neat bureaucratic system; the units were pragmatic, contested, and often unstable.

Historical Vocabulary
Émigré or Refugee Commanderies (qiao junxian, 侨郡县)

Émigré commanderies and counties were administrative units created in the south for displaced northerners and named after northern places. A refugee commandery might bear the name of a commandery in modern Shandong or Henan while actually being located in the lower Yangtze. The practice symbolized the regime's claim to the north and allowed displaced communities to preserve collective identity, which mattered for elite families with pedigrees to maintain.

The units should not be understood as simple territorial copies of the north. Their boundaries were often vague, they overlapped with older local jurisdictions, and their populations mixed migrants and locals. They were also temporary in intention and long-lived in practice, which gave them a peculiar afterlife: administrative maps of the south contained northern names for generations. Their history shows how the state improvised in order to govern a movement of people that it had not planned.

Migration also shaped the military. The Northern Headquarters army, based around Jingkou, was recruited from northern migrants settled in the lower Yangtze. Organized in the late fourth century under Xie Xuan, it became one of the most effective forces in the south and helped win the victory at Fei River in 383. Because its soldiers were themselves migrants with a stake in defending the Yangtze frontier and, in some accounts, in recovering the north, the army embodied the interplay between refugee status and state power. Its commanders later became political players; Liu Yu's rise to power drew directly on this military base. The same phenomenon appears elsewhere in the south, in Jingzhou and in the Huai frontier, where migrant soldiers formed the backbone of regional armies.

Finally, migration altered the landscape and economy. Migrants cleared land, settled new areas, and increased demand for grain, cloth, and other goods. They brought technical knowledge and, in some cases, crops and methods more familiar in the north, though the dominant agricultural system remained wet-rice cultivation adapted to southern conditions. Migrants also faced disease. Northern texts describe southern lowlands and hill country as unhealthy, associated with vapors and fevers that modern scholars interpret in part as malaria and other endemic diseases to which newcomers lacked immunity. Such perceptions affected settlement patterns and helped fuel a sense of exile among northern elites, expressed in poetry and correspondence. In sum, migration was a demographic, administrative, military, and economic transformation, but one that took place within, and was shaped by, an existing southern society.

Section 21.4

Aristocratic Families and Court Politics

The political heart of the Eastern Jin was the relationship among three centers of power: the imperial household, the great families, and military commanders. Historians have often described this as a system in which prestigious lineages held such influence that emperors were mere figureheads. That description exaggerates. Emperors did have power, and the record shows them repeatedly attempting to strengthen it. But they did operate in a political environment in which no ruler could govern without the cooperation of leading families and the armies that commanders controlled. The most useful way to understand Eastern Jin politics is as a series of shifting balances, not as a constitutional arrangement.

Two lineages dominate the early narrative. The Wang of Langye, represented by Wang Dao, were among the first émigré families to attach themselves to Sima Rui and remained prominent for generations. Wang Dao served several emperors and is remembered as a skilled conciliator who worked to preserve harmony among northerners and southerners. His kinsman Wang Dun followed a different course. As a powerful military commander in the middle Yangtze, Wang Dun resented the court's attempts to limit his authority, and in 322 he marched on Jiankang, seizing the capital. Emperor Yuan died shortly afterward, reportedly in frustration. Wang Dun rebelled again in 324 and died during the campaign, after which the court defeated his forces. The episode shows that great families were not monolithic: Wang Dao stayed at court and helped maintain the dynasty while Wang Dun led an armed challenge. It also shows that the danger to the throne came not only from northern enemies but from commanders within the south.

The Xie of Chenjun rose later. Xie An, a member of the family who initially avoided office and cultivated a reputation for refined leisure, became a dominant figure in the 370s and 380s. He helped block the ambitions of Huan Wen's family, directed court politics, and presided over the defense against Former Qin. His nephew Xie Xuan organized the Northern Headquarters army. The prestige of the Xie is entwined with the victory at Fei River. Other lineages, such as the Yu of Yingchuan and the Huan of Qiao, also held influence at different times, and southern families such as those of the Wu region retained local power and sometimes court positions. Women could also exercise influence. Empress Dowagers, notably Empress Dowager Chu, served as regents for child emperors at several points in the fourth century, and elite women's learning is remembered in sources such as the Shishuo xinyu, including the poet-scholar Xie Daoyun. These accounts are literary and selective, but they indicate that political and cultural authority within elite households was not exclusively male.

Military commanders formed the third pillar. Huan Wen, from the Huan family of Qiao, conquered the state of Cheng-Han in Sichuan in 347, extending Jin power upstream, and led northern expeditions in the 350s and 360s, including a campaign that briefly recovered Luoyang in 356 and a failed attempt in 369. His victories won him enormous prestige, and he used it to dominate the court, even deposing an emperor in 371. But Huan Wen never took the throne. The reasons are debated: elite families such as the Wang and Xie resisted him, his health failed, and the dynasty's legitimacy still carried weight. His career illustrates how military success could translate into political leverage without necessarily leading to dynastic replacement. His son Huan Xuan, however, did seize power in 403–404, proclaiming a short-lived regime, before being overthrown by Liu Yu.

The late fourth century brought additional strains. Court politics were riven by factional conflict, and rulers such as Emperor Xiaowu and his brother the regent Sima Daozi presided over a court in which personal rivalries and reliance on favorites weakened institutions. In 397–398 the court attempted to conscript dependents of great families into the army, a measure that alienated elite households and disrupted local society. This measure contributed to the Sun En rebellion of 399–402, a movement in the southeast associated with Celestial Masters religious networks that attracted many followers and, at its height, threatened Jiankang. Sun En's forces were eventually defeated, in part by commanders from the Northern Headquarters army, including Liu Yu. The rebellion shows that social and religious tensions in the southeast, not only elite rivalry, shaped politics, and that armed defenders of the dynasty could become its future rulers.

Historical Vocabulary
Great Families or Aristocracy (menfa, shizu, 门阀, 士族)

The Chinese terms translated as "great families," "aristocratic families," or "gentry lineages" describe hereditary elite groups whose standing rested on a combination of office-holding, marriage alliances, family pedigree, classical education, landholding, dependents, and political networks. The status of a "great family" was not itself a legally fixed peerage. Jin and Southern Dynasties courts did confer hereditary noble titles and maintained formal systems of titled rank, but the social standing of lineages such as the Wang and Xie rested more broadly on officeholding, pedigree, marriage alliances, education, landholding, dependents, and political networks. A lineage's standing could rise or fall across generations.

The same term is often contrasted with "cold gates" (hanmen), families of lower status who sometimes served as clerks or military officers. This chapter uses "great families" for the elite lineages while avoiding the suggestion of a closed caste. Historians debate how far these families dominated political power and whether their influence declined during the fifth and sixth centuries; the evidence indicates that they remained central to administration and culture even when military men sat on the throne.

Liu Yu's ascent is the culmination of these dynamics. Of relatively modest family background, he rose through the Northern Headquarters army, helped suppress Sun En and later Lu Xun's movement, defeated Huan Xuan in 404 and restored the Jin emperor, and then used his position to dominate the court. He conducted major northern campaigns, conquering the state of Southern Yan in 410 and Later Qin in 416–417, briefly capturing Chang'an, before returning to secure his political position when the recovered territory could not be held. By 420 he had exercised de facto control over the Jiankang regime for roughly sixteen years, built a wide following among officers and officials, and received a series of titles culminating in a claim to the throne. In 420 he compelled the last Jin emperor to abdicate and founded the Liu Song dynasty. The transition is instructive. Liu Yu could not have taken power without military strength, but he governed with the help of aristocratic officials and inherited institutions. The history of Eastern Jin's end is thus not an abstract "collapse of aristocratic rule," but the result of a particular general's long accumulation of power within a system whose structure he largely preserved.

Section 21.5

Agricultural Expansion and Southern Development

Political change in Jiankang was underpinned by slower transformations in the countryside. Between the fourth and sixth centuries, the population of the Yangtze region grew, more land was brought under cultivation, transport and trade networks expanded, and the south's demographic, commercial, and fiscal importance increased significantly. The chapter's argument here must be stated carefully. These developments accelerated a long-term southern development that had been underway under Wu and earlier regimes; they did not complete the far larger economic shift of the Tang and Song periods, in which the south became the dominant economic center of China (Chapters 26, 32, and 37). By 589 the Yangtze region was more important than it had been in 300, but north China's agricultural base, population, and political traditions remained massive.

The agricultural basis was wet-rice cultivation. In the lower Yangtze, farmers worked paddy fields, managed water through embankments, ponds, and channels, and in some districts obtained more than one crop or supplemented rice with other grains, vegetables, fish, and fruit. Migration contributed labor and demand. New settlers cleared land, drained marshes, and extended cultivation into hill margins; landowners and officials organized irrigation and reclamation, and the state sought to tax the additional output. Records from the fifth century describe large private estates in the southeast that enclosed mountains, lakes, and marshland, exploiting fishing, timber, and reclaimed fields, and the court issued regulations limiting the size of such holdings. Enforcement was uneven, since those who held estates were often the officials who were meant to enforce the rules. The regions of Wu, Wuxing, and Guiji in the southeast became known for prosperity, and Jingzhou in the middle Yangtze supplied grain and soldiers.

Other agricultural and craft activities complemented rice. Households raised mulberry trees and silkworms in suitable districts, wove cloth, and paid taxes in textiles as well as grain. Kilns in the southeast, especially in the Zhejiang region, produced stoneware and early celadon-type ceramics that were widely used and traded. Salt production along the coast, iron and copper mining in hill regions, and shipbuilding on rivers and coasts contributed to regional economies. The court also relied on the labor obligations of registered households, from building and repair projects to military service, and on the produce of state-controlled workshops.

Trade grew along waterways. Grain, cloth, salt, ceramics, and other goods moved along the Yangtze and its tributaries and through canals, connecting production zones to Jiankang and to towns. Market towns and river ports appeared where routes met. The southern courts issued coinage, though monetary conditions were unstable. In Liang, for example, the government introduced iron coins in 523, a measure to which private casting and overproduction of iron cash contributed monetary disorder, although the precise effects are difficult to quantify. In some regions grain and cloth continued to serve as media of exchange. Long-distance sea trade also developed, focusing on Guangzhou and its connections to Southeast Asia and beyond. Officials posted to Guangzhou were notorious in the sources for the wealth they could obtain from the foreign trade and local resources, which demonstrates both the opportunities of maritime commerce and the temptations of officeholding. Envoys from Southeast Asian polities and Indian Ocean traders arrived in Chinese courts, bringing aromatics, textiles, and other goods, and Buddhist monks traveled on maritime routes (Section 21.9).

State finance relied on registering households and collecting grain, cloth, and labor. The extra population in the south increased revenue potential, but the fiscal system faced constant leakage. Powerful families sheltered dependents from registration; migrants enjoyed temporary exemptions; and officials sometimes falsified records. The territorial registration reforms of 364 and 413 were attempts to widen the tax base. In the 480s, an effort under Southern Qi to verify household registers provoked a rebellion in the Zhejiang region led by Tang Yuzhi, a reminder that ordinary households sometimes resisted the state's efforts to count them, since registration meant obligations. Fiscal history therefore reveals a continuing struggle among the state, powerful families, and commoners over who bore burdens.

Students should not mistake the growth of Jiankang and the great estates for the typical experience. Well over ninety percent of the population probably lived in villages or small settlements below the level of formal towns. Farming families worked their own plots, rented land, or labored on estates; they paid grain and cloth taxes and served labor duties; they worshiped local gods, attended temple festivals, and depended on kin and neighbors. Regional differences were considerable: a household in the Wu plain enjoyed better access to markets and water than one in the hill country of Jiangxi or the middle Yangtze lakes. The story of southern development is a story of millions of such households as much as of courts and capitals.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household in the Lower Yangtze

The following is a cautious reconstruction based on patterns documented across many sources, not the account of an actual named family. Consider a farming household in the Wu region in the early fifth century. Several generations may share a compound: a senior couple, a married son and daughter-in-law, and grandchildren, with perhaps a landless relative or hired laborer. The household works a few plots of paddy field near a stream, its water controlled through a channel shared with neighbors and maintained by communal labor. In spring the family transplants seedlings; in summer it weeds and manages water; in autumn it harvests, threshes, and stores grain. Women and girls tend mulberry leaves and silkworms, spin and weave, and prepare food. Men and older boys repair embankments, fish, and haul goods on the local waterways. Fish, vegetables, and rice form the diet, with meat on festival days.

The household's burdens come from several sides. It owes grain and cloth to the state and may be called to provide labor for construction or transport, or a male member for military service; each of these obligations depends on registration, which the household may try to manage carefully. It may live near a large estate whose owner controls the marsh, the fishing ponds, and the hills; tenants and dependents on that estate owe part of their produce to the landlord rather than the state, a situation some households find attractive because it can offer protection from official demands, while others resent the loss of independence. A newly arrived migrant household may settle nearby, exempt for a time from taxes and thus a source of envy, but also short of land and vulnerable to fever. Neighbors negotiate over water rights, borrowed tools, marriages, and disputes.

Religious life is local and varied. The household honors its ancestors, makes offerings to river and field spirits, and joins festivals at a nearby temple. Some neighbors follow the Celestial Masters tradition, whose networks were strong in the southeast, while others visit a Buddhist temple or listen to a monk's teaching; the categories overlapped in practice. Markets, held periodically in a nearby town, allow the household to sell surplus rice or cloth and to purchase salt, iron tools, and ceramics. A river boat brings news, goods, and sometimes officials or soldiers. The turmoil of high politics affects this household indirectly: a commander's revolt may lead to requisitions, and a regional rebellion like that of Sun En could draw neighbors into armed movements. Yet most days are shaped by the agricultural calendar and the obligations of kin and community, a reminder that the southern dynasties were built on rural labor.

Regional and Frontier Context

Regions Within the Southern World

The southern courts governed a diverse region, not a single environment. The lower Yangtze and Taihu region, including Jiankang, Wu, Wuxing, and Guiji, was the most densely settled and agriculturally productive zone and the home of many great families. The middle Yangtze, centered on Jingzhou and Jiangling, was a strategic and agricultural area that connected the lower Yangtze to the Han valley, Sichuan, and the southern lakes; commanders there frequently rivaled the capital, and the region was a zone of interaction with communities identified in sources as Man. The Huai and Han frontier, including places such as Xiangyang, Shouyang, and Guangling, was a contested belt where refugees, garrisons, and diplomats crossed between north and south.

Sichuan was held by Eastern Jin after 347 but was politically distinct and at times independent or lost; Liang lost it to Western Wei in 553. Lingnan and Guangzhou connected to Southeast Asian and Indian Ocean trade, and local chieftains and magnates exercised considerable autonomy. In the hill country of the southeast and southwest, communities labeled in official sources with terms such as Shanyue, Man, and Dan lived alongside or beyond the reach of official administration. Their identities were not fixed, and the terms used in Chinese sources were imposed by officials and mix description with prejudice. As the state extended commanderies and counties, these peoples might be registered, taxed, conscripted, displaced, or incorporated through intermarriage and local alliance, while others retained autonomy. Some later southern founders and local magnates, especially in Lingnan and Jiangxi, came from local elites whose families had risen with the expansion of settlement. Students should therefore see the south as a zone of frontier, interaction, and negotiation as well as an established core.

Section 21.6

Liu Song and Southern Qi

The year 420 is more than another dynastic date. Liu Yu's accession as founder of the Liu Song dynasty marked the first replacement of the Sima house in the south, and it revealed how power had actually been distributed. Liu Yu had already exercised de facto control for roughly sixteen years. He had received honors and titles that made him, in effect, the ruler, and he had built a large following of officers and officials. His formal accession in 420, when the last Jin emperor abdicated, was in many respects the ratification of an existing situation, carried out in the ritual forms of abdication that earlier dynasties had used. English-language scholarship usually calls the dynasty Liu Song, after the ruling surname, to distinguish it from the Song dynasty of 960–1279 (Chapter 31).

The Liu Song and the Southern Qi that followed (479–502) can be understood as experiments in strengthening imperial power against entrenched aristocratic families and military commanders. Liu Yu himself, aware of the danger posed by commanders based in the middle Yangtze, reportedly urged that the important region of Jingzhou be governed by members of the imperial house. His successors used princes as regional governors, a strategy that reduced the risk of rebellion by outsiders but created a new one: princes could themselves become rivals. The succession disputes that punctuated Liu Song history, including the murder of Emperor Wen by his own son Liu Shao in 453, demonstrated the fragility of the arrangement. Later emperors struggled to control princes and relatives, and the dynasty's final decades were marked by purges and instability.

Emperor Wen's reign (424–453), known as the Yuanjia era after its reign title, is usually treated as the high point of Liu Song. It is associated with relative political stability, expansion of the fiscal base, and cultural patronage. Sources credit the court with founding schools devoted to Confucian learning, Xuanxue, history, and literature in 438, and the era produced major works of scholarship and literature. It was also a time of northern campaigns. Forces under Emperor Wen's command attempted to recover territory from the Northern Wei in 430 and again in 450, with poor results; the campaign of 450 was followed by a retaliatory Northern Wei advance that reached the north bank of the Yangtze opposite Jiankang in early 451 before withdrawing. The episode showed both the vulnerability of the south to northern cavalry and the strength of the Yangtze as a barrier (Section 21.9). The mixed military record helped feed a political lesson that later southern rulers absorbed: an ambitious northern policy was risky and costly.

Later Liu Song emperors tried other means of reasserting control. Emperor Xiaowu (r. 453–464) reduced the power of princes and relied increasingly on secretariat officials, often men from families of lower status, who could draft edicts and manage state business in the emperor's name. Historians sometimes link this development to the rise of the "cold gate" (hanmen) men who served as clerks and officers. The great families retained their prestige and high civil posts but could be bypassed in the exercise of practical authority. The pattern continued after Xiaowu: rulers used household servants, military officers, and secretaries as instruments of imperial will. This did not eliminate aristocratic influence, since the great families supplied ministers, provided legitimacy, and monopolized the cultural markers of high status, but it altered the balance and made the court more dependent on individual favorites.

A key question is why military founders could seize the throne yet still needed aristocratic administrative networks. The answer lies in the structure of the state. Commanders controlled armies, but ruling required registering households, collecting taxes, drafting edicts, conducting rituals, and staffing offices, tasks that depended on educated men whose training and social connections lay largely within the great families. Families that supplied officials in one dynasty could serve the next with relatively little disruption, and a general who took the throne generally needed their acceptance. The great families, for their part, valued the stability that a functioning dynasty provided and accepted transitions so long as their status was respected. This mutual dependence helps explain how five dynasties could succeed one another in the south without the repeated destruction of social and administrative structures.

Southern Qi arose from the same dynamics. In 479, Xiao Daocheng, a Liu Song general who had risen through military service and court politics, compelled the last Liu Song emperor to abdicate and founded Qi, known in English as Southern Qi. The regime endured only until 502 and was marked by violent succession struggles. The reign of Emperor Wu of Qi (482–493), covering the Yongming era, is remembered as a period of relative stability and cultural activity, notable for literary circles that included the poet Shen Yue and other writers. After his death, palace coups and the misrule of later emperors, including Xiao Baojuan, brought the dynasty to a crisis. Xiao Yan, a commander based at Xiangyang in the middle Yangtze, revolted in 500, marched on Jiankang, and seized power, founding the Liang dynasty in 502. The shift is another demonstration that military commands on the upper river could decide the fate of the capital.

Students should avoid a king-list narrative and instead ask what these regimes reveal about the structure of southern politics. Liu Song and Southern Qi confronted the same problems: how to control princes, how to prevent commanders from becoming rivals, how to reconcile the interests of great families with the needs of the state, and how to raise revenue from a population that was expanding but hard to register. Their responses, including the use of secretariat officials, the appointment of kin as regional governors, and registration reforms, show a state that adapted constantly. Their failures, particularly the vulnerability to palace coups and to frontier commanders, show why dynastic turnover was so frequent. Yet the social and administrative continuity between them, and the steady growth of the region, show that turnover did not mean disintegration.

Section 21.7

Liang and Chen

The Liang dynasty (502–557) deserves more space than its duration alone suggests. Its founder, Xiao Yan, reigned as Emperor Wu of Liang for nearly half a century (502–549), the longest reign of any southern ruler, and presided over a court that was both wealthy and culturally brilliant. Emperor Wu took several measures to strengthen the state and to reshape elite culture. He revived Confucian education, reportedly establishing schools devoted to the Five Classics in 505, reorganized official ranks, and sought to recruit men based on learning as well as birth. His government maintained a long period of relative internal peace. He was also a devoted Buddhist and patron, a subject treated in Section 21.8. Under his rule Jiankang reached the height of its prosperity and prestige, and its literary salons drew talented writers from across the south.

The Liang also faced the structural limits of southern government. The court's finances were strained by extravagant building and by the costs of military operations. In 523 the Liang government began issuing iron cash. Private casting and the rapid proliferation of iron coins contributed to monetary disorder, although the precise fiscal effects are difficult to quantify. Members of the imperial family and favored officials accumulated wealth and privileges, and provincial commands were often held by princes who competed with one another. In the north, the Northern Wei broke apart in the 530s into Eastern and Western successor states (Chapter 22), creating new strategic opportunities and threats. Liang launched military operations, including a 528–529 northern expedition led by Chen Qingzhi that briefly installed a Northern Wei claimant at Luoyang, but such successes could not be sustained. In 547 the Eastern Wei general Hou Jing, facing conflict with his own court, defected and offered his territory to Liang. Emperor Wu accepted, a decision later seen as disastrous.

The Hou Jing rebellion (548–552) was the major political rupture of the southern dynasties. Hou Jing rebelled in 548, crossed the Yangtze with the collusion of a Liang prince, and besieged the palace citadel of Jiankang. The city held out for months but fell in 549, after famine and disease had ravaged its inhabitants. Sources describe catastrophic loss of life, though the figures preserved in later histories should be treated with caution. Emperor Wu died in confinement shortly afterward. Hou Jing installed puppet emperors, later proclaimed his own regime, and ruled a devastated territory until 552, when armies led by Xiao Yi, a Liang prince based at Jiangling, together with commanders such as Wang Sengbian and Chen Baxian, defeated him. Hou Jing was killed while trying to flee.

The rebellion should not be reduced to a story about a single treacherous general. It revealed structural weaknesses: princes whose loyalty to the emperor was conditional, the dependence of the capital on units whose leaders had their own interests, the vulnerability of a rich city with an aging ruler, and the absence of a strong mechanism for succession. It also had demographic and economic consequences. Jiankang was devastated, and much of its aristocratic and literary community was killed, dispersed, or impoverished. Some southern writers and scholars moved to the northern courts, where they influenced cultural life, including the poet and official Yu Xin, who remained in the north after being sent as an envoy. The Liang regime fragmented as princes and commanders fought over the succession. Xiao Yi, who became Emperor Yuan of Liang in 552, chose to rule from Jiangling, a symbol of Jiankang's damaged status. When the Western Wei took Jiangling in 554, Emperor Yuan was killed, and the northern power installed a client ruler, creating a small regime known as Western Liang (555–587) in the middle Yangtze. Liang also lost Sichuan to Western Wei in 553 and much of its territory along the Han and Huai frontiers.

Chen (557–589) rose from the ruins. Chen Baxian was a military commander whose base lay in the far south. He had risen in Guangzhou and Jiangxi, marched north to help defeat Hou Jing, and became the dominant figure among the Liang survivors. After eliminating rivals, including Wang Sengbian, he took the throne in 557 as Emperor Wu of Chen. Historians have observed that the Chen state relied more heavily than its predecessors on local military magnates from the south, including strongmen of Lingnan and Jiangxi, whose rise reflects the growth of settlement and local power in these regions; the old émigré great families, though still culturally prestigious, were less central to military power. The dynasty controlled a reduced territory, mainly the lower and middle Yangtze south of the river and Lingnan, and could not fully restore the wealth of Liang Jiankang. Under Emperor Xuan (r. 569–582), Chen forces under Wu Mingche recovered much of the territory between the Yangtze and Huai in 573. After Northern Zhou destroyed Northern Qi in 577, however, the strategic balance shifted. Wu Mingche's renewed northern offensive was defeated in 578 and he was captured; by 579–580 Chen had lost most of its territory north of the Yangtze.

By the 580s the geopolitical landscape had changed decisively. In the north, the Sui founder Yang Jian (Emperor Wen) took power in 581 and consolidated control (Chapters 22 and 24). Chen, ruled by the last emperor Chen Shubao, whom the sources depict as devoted to pleasure and literary composition, faced a far more powerful neighbor. In 588–589 Sui armies crossed the Yangtze and took Jiankang in early 589, ending the Chen dynasty and the sequence of southern regimes. This chapter treats 589 only as an endpoint. The process by which Sui reunified the realm belongs to Chapter 24. What matters here is the fact that a southern political world had persisted for 272 years after 317, and that the reunification of 589 would be a union of two developed political and social systems, not the recovery of a lost province.

The Liang and Chen story also warns against equating short dynastic duration with continuous chaos. Between the great crises, southern society enjoyed long periods of relative order. The eras of Emperor Wen of Song, Emperor Wu of Qi, and Emperor Wu of Liang were periods of institutional stability, urban growth, and cultural production. The Hou Jing rebellion was a genuine catastrophe, but it was an event, not the whole. The Chen dynasty's ability to govern a smaller but viable territory for more than three decades after that catastrophe shows the resilience of southern institutions and society.

Section 21.8

Buddhism, Literature, and Elite Culture

This section is deliberately selective. Chapter 23 provides the broader synthesis of Buddhism, Daoist institutions, ethnic interaction, family, and cultural transformation across the Age of Division. Here the aim is to show what southern court society looked like through representative examples of its cultural life, and to explain why Jiankang's cultural importance was exceptional in this period.

Calligraphy and literary gatherings provide one window. Wang Xizhi, a member of the Wang family of Langye, served in office in the southeast and is traditionally dated to about 303–361, though some scholars have proposed later dates. He is celebrated as the pivotal figure in the history of Chinese calligraphy, refining running and cursive styles and helping establish forms that blended functional writing with aesthetic refinement. In 353 he took part in a gathering of literati at the Orchid Pavilion near Shanyin, an episode explored in the Primary Source Window in Section 21.10. The event, and the calligraphic tradition associated with it, became foundational to Chinese literary and artistic memory, and it illustrates the cultural aspirations of the southeastern elite: refined sociability, engagement with landscape, and a sense of shared learned identity. The painter Gu Kaizhi, active in the late fourth and early fifth centuries, similarly became a foundational name in painting tradition, though attributions of surviving works to him rely on later copies.

Poetry reflects changing sensibilities. Tao Yuanming, also known as Tao Qian (about 365–427), served briefly as a local official before resigning and living as a farmer-poet; his verses on rural life, wine, and retreat, and his prose piece about a hidden peach-blossom refuge, influenced later generations. His work is often read as expressing a rejection of the political turmoil of the time, though his reputation grew mainly after his death. Xie Lingyun (385–433), from the aristocratic Xie family, wrote descriptions of mountains and waters that are considered founding works of Chinese landscape poetry, drawing on his travels through the southeastern hills; his political career ended in exile and execution, a reminder of the risks of elite life. Later, in the Southern Qi and Liang courts, writers such as Shen Yue, who analyzed the tonal properties of the language, and Xiao Tong, Crown Prince of Liang, who oversaw the compilation of the influential anthology Wen xuan, participated in salons where poetry, criticism, and scholarship flourished. Works such as Liu Xie's Wenxin diaolong and Zhong Rong's Shipin developed literary criticism. The "palace-style" poetry of the sixth century, associated with courtly themes, drew later accusations of frivolity, but modern scholars regard it as an important stage in the development of poetic technique. This body of literature must be handled with awareness that it represents a very narrow, highly educated segment of society.

Buddhism became deeply involved with southern court culture. Buddhist temples in and around Jiankang multiplied, funded by emperors, aristocrats, and other patrons, and monks participated in court debates. The pilgrim-monk Faxian, who traveled to India and returned by sea in 412, worked with the Indian monk Buddhabhadra in translating texts at a Jiankang temple, and later in the sixth century the monk Paramartha arrived by sea at Guangzhou and subsequently traveled in the south, continuing translation work. Debates about the nature of the soul and the relation of Buddhism to Confucian ethics were public issues; an essay by Fan Zhen on the mortality of the spirit, from the Liang era, provoked responses from Buddhist writers and the emperor's circle.

Emperor Wu of Liang embodied court Buddhism. He composed commentaries, presided over lectures and assemblies, promoted vegetarianism, and sponsored temple construction, including the Tongtai Temple in Jiankang. On several occasions, as sources describe, he "renounced" the throne to live as a servant of the monastic community, after which court officials paid large sums to redeem him and return him to power. Historians debate whether these acts expressed sincere devotion, provided ways to obtain resources for monasteries, or served political purposes, and they are best seen as involving all three. The emperor's patronage strengthened Buddhism's institutional presence and placed a distinctive stamp on Liang court culture, though it also drew criticism from those who thought that the monasteries absorbed labor and wealth or that his religious activities distracted from governance. Buddhism was not purely an elite phenomenon: monks and lay associations taught in towns and countryside, and images and temples appeared in local communities, but the sources are heavily weighted toward court and elite patronage.

Southern elite culture also included what scholars have called "pure conversation" (qingtan), the discursive style of debating philosophical questions drawn from the Zhuangzi, the Laozi, and the Book of Changes, and the associated intellectual current of Xuanxue. Anecdotes recorded in the Shishuo xinyu depict figures such as Wang Dao and Xie An in witty exchanges and portray a high value placed on spontaneity, refined taste, and ease. These sources idealize; they should not be taken as a full description of the court, which was also shaped by military threat and factional struggle. Historians emphasize that Confucian ritual and moral education remained the basis of aristocratic training and government. The southern elite was intellectually eclectic, combining Confucian learning, Xuanxue speculation, Buddhist practice, and Daoist religious currents, and this eclecticism, more than any single school, characterized its culture.

Section 21.9

North-South Relations and the Transformation of the South

It is tempting to imagine north and south as two sealed civilizations facing each other across the Huai and Yangtze. The evidence shows something more complex. Political and military rivalry was real and lasting, but people, goods, texts, monks, and technologies continued to cross the line, and the boundary itself moved. This section follows the diplomatic and military relations of the southern courts with the northern states and then considers how those relations shaped the south.

Eastern Jin's early rhetoric committed the court to reconquest. Commanders such as Zu Ti, who crossed the Yangtze in 313 to lead a northern expedition into the Central Plains, and later Yu Liang pursued or proposed recovery of the north, but political constraints and the strength of northern states limited results. The most sustained efforts came from Huan Wen, whose campaign of 356 briefly recovered Luoyang and whose attempt in 369 ended in a costly defeat. These expeditions demonstrate that the Eastern Jin state could project force northward, but that holding conquered territory was another matter, and that the domestic political impact of a successful general was a danger in itself.

The defining moment was the Battle of Fei River in 383, seen here from the southern side. The Former Qin ruler Fu Jian, who had unified much of northern China, assembled a large army to attack Eastern Jin. Sources record enormous numbers, which historians regard as exaggerated. The Jin defenders, under commanders from the Xie family including Xie Shi and Xie Xuan and relying heavily on the Northern Headquarters army, met the invading forces along the Fei River, a tributary in the Huai region. Accounts describe a Jin attack that threw Former Qin ranks into confusion, and the northern army collapsed. Whether or not the details reflect literary shaping, the outcome is clear: the invasion failed, the south survived, and Former Qin's unity dissolved into competing successor states (Chapter 20). For the south, the victory gave the Jin court a generation of relative security and enormously enhanced the prestige of the Xie family and the Northern Headquarters force. It confirmed the strategic wisdom of defending the Yangtze frontier with mobile infantry, river craft, and fortified towns.

In the early fifth century, Liu Yu conducted the last major southern campaigns to reconquer the north. He took Southern Yan in 410 and, in 416–417, attacked Later Qin, capturing Chang'an. Yet he could not remain, since his position at home required his return, and a northern regime soon retook the city. Later, Liu Song campaigns under Emperor Wen in 430 and 450 sought to regain the Yellow River lands but were repulsed. After the Northern Wei unified the north in the 430s, the balance changed: Northern Wei was a powerful, well-organized state, and the southern courts found themselves on the defensive. When the Wei emperor Taiwu led an army to the Yangtze in 450–451, Jiankang's leaders had to face the possibility of northern invasion of the capital region. The Wei withdrew, but the episode demonstrated the seriousness of the northern threat.

Diplomacy became routine. From the late fifth century, the southern courts and Northern Wei exchanged envoys, and these exchanges reveal both rivalry and mutual interest. Each side treated its envoys as representatives of a rival claim to universal rulership, and protocol disputes were common, but the visits allowed information, gifts, and cultural influence to pass. Some southern officials defected to the north. Wang Su, a member of an aristocratic southern family whose father had been killed by the Southern Qi court, fled to Northern Wei in 493 and became an adviser on ritual and institutional matters to Emperor Xiaowen (Chapter 22). Others moved in the opposite direction. Liang, for its part, sheltered northern nobles and claimants, and the expedition of 528–529 was designed to install one of them at Luoyang. Trade also crossed the frontier, sometimes officially and sometimes illicitly, and border markets and smuggling linked the economies. Prisoners of war, refugees, and merchants added to the flow, and monks and texts moved between southern and northern monasteries.

Claims to legitimacy shaped both sides. The southern courts claimed direct succession from Jin, and thus from the Han and Wei-Jin sequence, and they called northern rulers by derogatory names in official documents. Northern courts developed their own claims, which drew on Chinese dynastic precedent, on the authority of Inner Asian rulership, and, increasingly, on Buddhist ideas, and they, in turn, treated southern rulers as illegitimate. By the fifth and sixth centuries, "north" and "south" described durable political systems with different histories, elites, and institutions, and the very terms "Northern Dynasties" and "Southern Dynasties" are retrospective labels created by later historians. Contemporaries used other language, and the concept of a divided empire remained part of the political imagination of both sides.

The south was transformed by these interactions in several ways. Military pressure shaped the geography of power, concentrating armies along the Huai and Yangtze lines and in Jingzhou. Refugees and defectors supplied administrative and military talent. Diplomatic contact circulated ideas and material culture. The experience of defending against the north, together with the memory of Fei River, contributed to a sense of southern political identity, while the persistent claim to northern territory kept alive the legitimacy of the regime. At the same time, the south's growing internal cohesion, seen in its economy, its capital, and its culture, reduced dependence on the northern connection. As the next chapter shows, the north was undergoing its own transformation under Northern Wei and its successors, and the interplay between the two regions would set the stage for eventual reunification.

Section 21.10

Chapter Review and Southern Dynasties Checkpoint

This chapter has traced a chain of transformation. The collapse of Western Jin led a Jin prince, Sima Rui, to establish a court at Jiankang. Northern migrants and established southern society reached accommodations that made a working government possible. Aristocratic court politics, military commanders, and the throne contended for power, and a military figure, Liu Yu, replaced Jin in 420. Agricultural and commercial growth deepened the south's importance, and successive dynasties (Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, and Chen) governed from Jiankang until Sui conquest in 589. The question that the checkpoint poses is why dynastic instability did not prevent long-term regional development. The answer offered here is that the southern political world was larger than any dynasty: its capital, elite families, administrative expertise, villages, waterways, markets, and cultural institutions outlasted regime changes.

The most consequential achievement of the Eastern Jin and Southern Dynasties was not political reunification. It was the creation of a southern political world durable enough to survive repeated dynastic change. By 589, the Yangtze region was no longer a peripheral refuge from northern crisis. It had become one of the essential centers of Chinese political, economic, and cultural life, and any future reunified empire would have to incorporate both the northern and southern worlds created during the Age of Division.

Primary Source Window

Wang Xizhi, Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Gathering (353)

Creator: Wang Xizhi, official and calligrapher of the Langye Wang lineage · Date: 353 (the ninth year of the Yonghe era) · Region: Shanyin, near modern Shaoxing in the southeast · Type: Literary preface to a collection of poems composed at a social gathering

Context: On the third day of the third lunar month of 353, Wang Xizhi and a company of forty-one literati gathered at the Orchid Pavilion to perform a spring purification ritual and to compose poems while seated along a winding stream. The participants included members of prominent families. Wang wrote a preface to the resulting collection. The preface is celebrated both as prose and, in later tradition, as a masterpiece of calligraphy. It is essential to distinguish the text from the calligraphy: the original autograph manuscript does not survive. The famous visual tradition of the work is preserved through later copies and rubbings, and versions of the text have been transmitted in later works, so textual details and the authenticity of particular copies have been subjects of scholarly discussion.

Paraphrase (not a translated quotation):

The author describes assembling with fellow guests at the pavilion in the spring, amid high mountains, thick bamboo, and a clear stream that curved around the gathering, where cups of wine were floated along the water and each guest composed verses as a cup passed. There was no need for music; wine and poetry were enough for conversation and relaxed feeling. He reflects on how people, whatever their temperaments, find delight in what they encounter and forget that age is approaching, but that pleasures fade and life ends. He rejects the view that life and death are the same, and he anticipates that later readers will feel toward this gathering what he feels toward the writers of the past.

Analysis: The preface is an Eastern Jin text traditionally attributed to Wang Xizhi in 353 and transmitted through later textual and calligraphic copies; it belongs to the southern elite society this chapter examines. It reveals a culture of literary sociability, appreciation of landscape, ritual observance blended with leisure, and reflection on mortality. Its reference to the equating of life and death echoes debates drawn from Zhuangzi, showing the presence of Xuanxue themes in elite discourse. It illustrates the geographical reach of elite culture into the southeast, away from the capital. Its limits are equally significant. The preface represents the viewpoint of one educated man writing for a small audience. It says almost nothing about migrants, farmers, artisans, women, non-elite populations, or the political and military pressures of the time. It also reflects a chosen self-image: elite writers presented themselves as detached and refined, and such presentations should not be read as unmediated descriptions of a whole society. The gathering itself, moreover, later acquired symbolic significance that may color how historians read it.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source describe or reflect about the gathering?
  2. Who created it, when, and under what circumstances?
  3. What was the purpose of the preface and its intended audience?
  4. What can historians learn from it about Eastern Jin elite culture?
  5. What can it not establish about ordinary southern society, migrants, farmers, women, or non-elite populations?
Historical Debate

What Transformed Southern China Most: Northern Migration or Developments Already Underway in the South?

The question: Between the early fourth and late sixth centuries, the Yangtze region changed politically, demographically, and economically. Historians disagree about how much of this change should be attributed to the arrival of northern migrants and how much to processes already in motion.

Interpretation A — Migration was decisive: Some historians emphasize that the movement of northerners supplied new populations, administrative skills, elite networks, military manpower, and demand, and that these accelerated reclamation, urban growth, and political organization. On this view, the Eastern Jin and successor courts were, in large part, products of migration, and the south's rise was tied to the northern collapse.
Interpretation B — Prior southern development mattered more: Others argue that this exaggerates northern agency. The south had substantial populations, local elites, agricultural systems, trade, and political traditions going back through Wu and earlier, and migrants entered an already inhabited and developed world. Northern refugees were initially a minority. On this view, the courts of Jiankang built on southern foundations.
Interpretation C — Transformation through interaction: A third position holds that neither factor alone explains the change. Migrant institutions and populations encountered southern societies that constrained, absorbed, and reshaped them, producing a hybrid political and social order that was neither a northern transplant nor a continuation of earlier southern patterns.

Evidence: Supporters of A cite estimates of the size of migrant populations, the creation of émigré commanderies, the military role of migrant soldiers, and the prominence of northern families at court. Supporters of B cite the Wu-era foundations of Jianye, the persistence of southern families, the pre-existing agricultural base, and the evidence that most of the population lived in local communities untouched by court politics. Supporters of C point to evidence of negotiation, such as the accommodation of southern elites by Wang Dao, the territorial registration reforms, and the recruitment of southern magnates by later dynasties, especially Chen. All sides rely on incomplete quantitative evidence and on sources written by elites.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over the scale and timing of migration; how much migrants contributed to reclamation, urbanization, and commercialization; how much continuity should be assigned to Wu-era institutions and established southern elites; and whether the greatest changes resulted from demographic movement itself or from the political and fiscal arrangements created as migrants and existing southern communities interacted.

Why it matters: How historians weigh migration and prior development shapes how they understand agency, regional identity, and the origins of the later southern economic shift. It also shapes whether the south is seen as a passive recipient or an active participant in the history of division.

Student task: Using evidence from migration, politics, agriculture, and local society, evaluate which interpretation is best supported, or explain what additional evidence you would need to decide among them.

Multiple Perspectives

Arriving in the South

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical viewpoints synthesizing patterns in the sources, not quotations from named individuals. The groups did not have equal power, and the sources represent them very unequally.

A northern aristocratic refugee household: Such a household might carry genealogies, ritual knowledge, and political connections, and hold a strong sense of exile. Its members could obtain office and influence far out of proportion to their numbers, though they sometimes lacked landholdings and depended on court appointments, marriage alliances, or the acquisition of estates in the southeast.
An established Jiangnan elite family: Families such as those of the Wu region held land, local authority, and a history of service under Wu. They could view northern newcomers as both allies and competitors, since the émigrés often dominated the highest court posts. Their cooperation was essential to the regime, and some gained influence through it, but many resented northern political dominance.
An ordinary southern farming household: Existing villagers experienced migration as new neighbors, competition for land and water, tax burdens that differed from those of exempt newcomers, and, potentially, demands for labor and service. Their voices survive mainly through the state's records of taxation, registration, and rebellion.
A migrant soldier in the Northern Headquarters forces: For a displaced man, military service offered livelihood, status, and a chance at recovery of a homeland. Soldiers formed communities of shared origin and were sometimes fiercely loyal to their commanders, which made them both the defenders of the dynasty and, potentially, the means of its overthrow.
A merchant on the Yangtze waterways: Growing population, urban demand, and coastal trade created opportunities for boatmen, traders, and artisans. Merchants benefited from expanding markets but also faced taxation, official exactions, and the risks of warfare and river piracy.
A hill or frontier community encountering expanding state authority: Communities labeled in official sources with terms such as Man, Shanyue, or Dan (categories that were fluid and should not be assumed to match later ethnic or occupational identities) might experience the expansion of commanderies as pressure to register, pay taxes, and provide labor, or as opportunities for trade and intermarriage. Some resisted, some negotiated, and some became incorporated into the population that later sources call Han. Our sources reflect the outsider's view.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Refugee Regime to Southern Center

What Changed

  • The capital of the Chinese imperial state shifted south and remained there for nearly three centuries
  • Migration altered the distribution of population, and Jiankang expanded into a great metropolis
  • Political elites changed: northern émigré families joined established southern ones, and later military magnates from the south gained influence
  • The Yangtze's fiscal and commercial role grew, and military geography increasingly centered on Yangtze and Huai defenses

What Stayed the Same

  • Imperial titles, court ritual, and classical education
  • Counties and commanderies as the basic units of administration
  • Aristocratic family strategies of office, marriage, and learning
  • Farming as the economic foundation and rural households as the main taxpayers
  • The aspiration, at least in rhetoric, to reunify the realm, and many Jin administrative practices

Who experienced change most directly? Migrants, who lost homes and re-established lives; southern elites, whose relationship to the court was reordered; and residents of the lower Yangtze, whose landscape and society were reshaped. Who experienced continuity? Many rural households, whose work and obligations followed familiar rhythms, and the great families, who preserved status across dynasties. Timing: political relocation was rapid (311–318), migration continued through the fourth and early fifth centuries, and economic growth was gradual across the period. Why the pattern matters: a temporary refugee regime helped create an enduring southern center, an outcome no one at the time could have predicted.

Cause and Effect

How Did Western Jin's Collapse Transform Southern China?

Long-term causes

Wu-era development of Jianye and the lower Yangtze; existing southern populations, agriculture, elite families, and river networks; the earlier concentration of imperial power in the north.

Short-term causes

The War of the Eight Princes, the Yongjia crisis, and the fall of Luoyang and Chang'an, which destroyed northern government and set refugees in motion.

Immediate trigger and context

The fall of Chang'an in 316 ended the remaining Western Jin imperial center in the north while Sima Rui already possessed an established political base at Jianye/Jiankang.

Event or transformation

Sima Rui assumed the title King of Jin in 317 and the imperial title in 318, establishing Eastern Jin at Jiankang as both a continuation of Jin legitimacy and a new southern regional regime.

Immediate effects

A new court, émigré commanderies and registration problems, competition and bargaining with local elites, and a military dependent on migrant soldiers.

Long-term consequences

Demographic growth, the political centrality of Jiankang, agricultural and commercial expansion, a sequence of southern dynasties, and an enduring Yangtze political center.

Historical complexity

Migration accelerated change but did not create southern society from nothing. Outcomes depended on local elites, geography, military contingencies, and choices by rulers and commanders.

Avoiding simple explanations: It would be inadequate to say that the south flourished because northerners "brought civilization," or that southern dynasties fell because they became "decadent." Fiscal leakage, military decentralization, succession disputes, geography, and regional social structures explain both the resilience of the southern world and the frequent fall of its dynasties.

China and the Wider World

The South in Eurasian and Maritime Networks

The southern courts were not isolated from the wider world. Sea routes linked Guangzhou and other ports to Southeast Asian polities such as Funan and Linyi and, further, to the Indian Ocean, and envoys and merchants arrived in Jiankang, bringing goods and religious texts. Sources record embassies from South and Southeast Asian states, including Sri Lanka, to southern courts. Buddhist monks traveled these sea routes as well as land routes: Faxian returned by sea in 412, and Paramartha reached Guangzhou in the sixth century. In the northwest, the Tuyuhun, a state based in the upper Yellow River and Qinghai region, connected Sichuan and the Liang court with the routes leading toward Central Asia, allowing the south to participate in overland exchange without direct control of the Gansu corridor.

Relations with the Korean peninsula and Japan were also active. Southern courts exchanged envoys with the Korean kingdoms, particularly Baekje, whose tombs and culture show clear influence from southern Chinese material culture, and rulers of the Japanese islands sent envoys to the Liu Song court in the fifth century seeking recognition and titles. These contacts were shaped by the political interests of all sides: southern courts sought allies and prestige against the north, while Korean and Japanese rulers sought legitimacy and access to technology, texts, and craftsmen. Chinese diplomacy, trade, and cultural influence were therefore active processes, not merely responses to external pressure, and the southern dynasties played an important role in transmitting Chinese script, Buddhism, and material culture to East Asia.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Eastern Jin and the Southern Dynasties were not merely weakened remnants of northern empires. Beginning with Sima Rui's establishment of a Jin court at Jiankang in 317–318, northern refugee elites, established southern families, military commanders, farmers, merchants, religious communities, and local populations constructed a durable southern political system. The chapter's central distinction is between dynastic instability and regional weakness: five dynasties rose and fell, but the southern political world continued to develop.

The Eastern Jin court was built through bargains. Sima Rui and Wang Dao worked with northern émigré families and southern elites, using Jiankang, the former Wu capital, as a base. The regime claimed continuity with Western Jin while operating in a new environment. It was not a transplant of Luoyang. Jiankang inherited Wu's political landscape and, over time, grew into one of the great cities of its age, with palaces, markets, temples, aristocratic residences, and river connections.

Migration was a demographic, administrative, military, and economic transformation, but it occurred in waves and among diverse groups: elite lineages, ordinary refugees, soldiers, dependents, and captives. Migrants entered a populated south where established families, farming communities, and non-Han populations already lived. The court responded with émigré commanderies, separate registers, temporary exemptions, and later territorial registration reforms, all of which were pragmatic and contested. Northern émigrés were a numerical minority but had disproportionate political influence.

Politics turned on the balance among the imperial house, great families, and military commanders. Wang Dao's conciliation and Wang Dun's rebellion, Huan Wen's conquests and ambitions, Xie An's leadership and Xie Xuan's Northern Headquarters army, and the Sun En rebellion illustrate the range of tensions. The Battle of Fei River in 383 preserved the southern state, but the same military forces that defended it produced Liu Yu, who replaced Jin in 420 after roughly sixteen years of de facto control. His success shows that military founders could seize the throne yet still needed aristocratic administrative networks.

Economic and social change proceeded beneath the political turnover. Wet-rice agriculture, reclamation, irrigation, river transport, handicrafts, and trade increased the south's demographic and fiscal importance, accelerating a long-term development that had begun under Wu. The chapter cautioned against claiming that the later Tang-Song economic shift was already complete and stressed that most people lived in villages, not in Jiankang. Everyday life revolved around farming, household labor, registration and tax obligations, local religious practice, and relations with estates, neighbors, and migrants.

Liu Song and Southern Qi experimented with strengthening imperial power through princes as governors, secretariat officials, and registration reforms, while suffering from succession disputes and the vulnerability of the capital to commanders on the upper river. Liang, under Emperor Wu, presided over prosperity and cultural achievement but confronted structural limits, and the Hou Jing rebellion of 548–552 devastated Jiankang and fragmented the regime. Chen, based on southern military magnates and a smaller territory, showed the resilience of southern institutions until its conquest by Sui in 589.

Culture in the south was rich but socially narrow in its documentation. Wang Xizhi's calligraphy and the Orchid Pavilion gathering, Tao Yuanming's rural poetry, Xie Lingyun's landscape verse, the salons of Qi and Liang, and Emperor Wu's Buddhist patronage show a lively elite culture in which Confucian learning, Xuanxue, Buddhism, and Daoist currents coexisted. Students should remember that these sources represent a small stratum and that Chapter 23 will place them in a wider religious and social context.

North-south relations were both rivalrous and connected. Military campaigns, diplomacy, defections, trade, and the movement of monks and texts crossed the frontier, while each side maintained claims to legitimacy. The southern world also engaged with Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, Korea, and Japan, showing that Chinese diplomacy and cultural influence were active processes.

By 589 the Yangtze region was no longer a peripheral refuge from northern crisis. It had become one of the essential centers of Chinese political, economic, and cultural life. The most consequential achievement of these centuries was not political reunification but the creation of a southern political world durable enough to survive repeated dynastic change. Chapter 22 turns to the northern side of the story, where Northern Wei and its successors consolidated and transformed the north; Chapter 23 gathers the broader religious and cultural transformations of the Age of Division; and Chapter 24 shows how reunification would have to bring the two worlds together.

MGU Library Research Project

Jiankang Across Three Centuries

This project asks you to study Jiankang as a city across the period 317–589, combining written, archaeological, and map evidence.

  1. Frame a question. For example: how did Jiankang change from Eastern Jin to Chen, and what caused the changes?
  2. Gather sources. Use contemporary or near-contemporary textual evidence, such as inscriptions, letters, and selected fifth-century passages from the Shishuo xinyu, while identifying the date and literary character of each source. Treat archaeological objects, tombs, and structural remains as primary material evidence; treat modern excavation reports, scholarly books and articles, and museum catalogues as secondary analyses. Explain what each type of evidence can and cannot establish.
  3. Analyze maps. Locate the palace precinct, the Qinhuai River, elite residential districts, and temples on a reconstructed plan, noting which elements are uncertain.
  4. Consider multiple groups. Discuss what the city meant to emperors, aristocrats, monks, merchants, artisans, and rural visitors, noting whose voices the sources preserve.
  5. Assess events. Evaluate the effects of the Hou Jing rebellion on the city and its population, with caution about the numbers reported.
  6. Write. Produce a 5–7 page essay or exhibit that presents your argument and identifies the limits of the evidence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that political fragmentation could be creative. The loss of the northern capitals did not end Chinese imperial government; it relocated part of it to a region whose development would transform the shape of later history. In Jiankang, imperial ritual, classical learning, and aristocratic culture were combined with southern agriculture, waterways, and local societies to produce a durable political world.

The chapter also shaped what came afterward. The Sui and Tang states that reunified the realm inherited not one tradition but two, and the southern tradition brought a strong agricultural and commercial base, a literary and religious culture, and administrative experience. The long-run growth of the Yangtze region, which would culminate in the economic shift of the Song period, had important roots in these centuries, though it was neither inevitable nor complete by 589.

Larger themes appear here that recur throughout the book: the interplay of migration and local society, the tension between central authority and regional power, the importance of elite families and local networks alongside emperors, the role of environment and geography, and the necessity of reading sources with attention to who wrote them. The southern dynasties also connect China to wider Asian and maritime worlds through Buddhism, trade, and diplomacy with Southeast Asia, Korea, and Japan.

Above all, the chapter helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, and regionally varied. A refugee court became a center; short-lived dynasties presided over long-lived social change; and a region once thought peripheral became essential. As students move to Chapter 22, they should keep the southern story in mind, since the northern dynasties were engaged in their own transformation, and the meeting of the two would define the era of Sui and Tang.
