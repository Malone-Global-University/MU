Part II — Bronze Age China and the Classical Foundations

The Spring and Autumn Period

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In 651 BCE, at a place called Kuiqiu, rulers and envoys from several of the most powerful states of the Zhou world gathered to renew a solemn covenant. They swore oaths, exchanged pledges, and affirmed, in language that still echoed the vocabulary of Zhou royal ritual, their shared commitment to protect the reigning Zhou king, to refrain from certain kinds of warfare against one another, and to uphold a common political and moral order. The man who presided over this gathering was not the Zhou king. It was Duke Huan of Qi, ruler of a regional state on the eastern seaboard, acting in the position later chroniclers would call a "hegemon," or ba — a powerful regional lord who claimed to lead the Zhou political world on the king's behalf, precisely because the king could no longer lead it himself.

This scene captures, in miniature, the central historical problem of this chapter. A little more than a century earlier, in 771–770 BCE, the western Zhou royal capital had fallen to a coalition of rebellious allies and non-Zhou peoples, the reigning Zhou king had been killed, and his successor, King Ping, had relocated the royal court eastward to the city of Luoyi, near modern Luoyang. Chapter 8 examined that founding catastrophe and its immediate aftermath as the end of the period historians call the Western Zhou. This chapter examines what came next: nearly three centuries during which the Zhou royal house continued to exist, continued to be recognized, and continued to matter to the political imagination of the age, even as its practical military and administrative power shrank to a small fraction of what earlier Zhou kings had commanded.

Historians conventionally call this period, extending from roughly 770 BCE to somewhere in the vicinity of 476 or 475 BCE, the Spring and Autumn period, after the Chunqiu, or Spring and Autumn Annals, a terse chronicle, kept and added to over time by court scribes of the state of Lu, that records a bare skeleton of political and ritual events in that state and its neighbors. The title uses "spring and autumn" as a conventional shorthand for annals arranged year by year across the seasons; the chronicle itself in fact marks entries under all four seasons — spring, summer, autumn, and winter — of each ruler's year, and the name should not be read as implying anything about climate or about only two seasons being recorded. Later tradition, above all the much richer narrative commentary known as the Zuo Tradition, or Zuozhuan, filled the Annals' terse entries with vivid stories, speeches, and moral judgments — material of enormous value to historians, but material that this chapter will insist on treating carefully, since much of it reached its present written form well after many of the events it describes.

The temptation, when telling this story, is to reach immediately for a single dramatic word: collapse, decline, chaos, disintegration. Some later Chinese historical writing, looking back on the Spring and Autumn period from the vantage point of a unified empire, cast it as a tragic falling-away from an earlier age of unified, well-ordered Zhou rule under Heaven's mandate. This chapter asks students to resist that framing, or at least to hold it at arm's length long enough to examine what the evidence actually shows. What the evidence shows is not simply the erosion of a single center of power, but the construction of something new: an interstate political system, in which formally subordinate regional domains became increasingly autonomous actors, competing, allying, warring, negotiating, and — crucially — innovating, all within a shared political and ritual vocabulary inherited from the Western Zhou even as its practical content changed dramatically.

This chapter's central thesis, then, is that the Spring and Autumn period should be understood less as the failure of a political order and more as its transformation. Zhou kings lost most of their coercive power. They did not lose all of their significance. Regional lords who had once owed direct military service and tribute to an effective royal center increasingly ran their own courts, fielded their own armies, conducted their own diplomacy, and absorbed or annexed weaker neighbors, yet many of them continued, for centuries, to frame their ambitions in the language of defending, honoring, or restoring the Zhou order rather than replacing it outright. Only toward the end of the period, and more decisively in the Warring States era examined in Chapter 10, would rulers begin to abandon that inherited framework altogether and build the kind of centralized, bureaucratic, explicitly self-sufficient territorial states that would eventually make the old Zhou vocabulary irrelevant.

Several major historical developments unfold across this chapter. Regional states such as Jin, Qi, Chu, Qin, Lu, Song, and Zheng, among others, consolidated their internal authority and expanded, often violently, at the expense of smaller and weaker neighbors, producing a shifting map of interstate competition that students must learn to see as a genuine multi-state system rather than a single unified "China" temporarily disordered. Warfare itself changed in scale and character, moving gradually away from the smaller, more ritually bounded, chariot-centered combat of the aristocratic Western Zhou tradition toward larger, more sustained, more territorially consequential campaigns, although the full transformation toward mass infantry armies belongs to the Warring States period examined in the next chapter. Diplomacy grew increasingly sophisticated, producing covenants, alliances, marriage ties, and a recognized, if informal, position of interstate leadership — the hegemon, or ba — through which especially powerful rulers could claim to coordinate the Zhou world without formally displacing the Zhou king. Beneath these headline political developments, deeper social and economic changes were also under way: hereditary aristocratic lineages began, unevenly and gradually, to lose ground to men of lower birth who served rulers through demonstrated skill in administration, diplomacy, ritual, or war; agricultural production expanded; iron implements began a slow, uneven spread that would have much larger consequences in later centuries; and regional courts began to govern territory and population in ways that increasingly resembled administration rather than simple kinship-based lordship.

Finally, this chapter matters because it explains the political world in which some of the most consequential intellectual traditions in Chinese history began to take shape. Confucius, whose teachings and later influence are examined in detail in Chapter 11, lived and taught during the final century of the Spring and Autumn period, in the small state of Lu, amid precisely the kind of political uncertainty, aristocratic decline, and inherited-but-failing ritual order this chapter describes. Understanding the world Confucius inhabited — a world in which old institutions still commanded deep respect even as they visibly failed to produce lasting peace or stable government — is essential background for understanding why his teachings, and those of the thinkers who followed him, took the shape they did. This chapter does not attempt to explain classical Chinese philosophy in full; that task belongs to Chapters 11 and 12. But it lays the essential political and social groundwork, asking a question this chapter leaves for those later chapters to answer in depth: what happens to political thought when inherited institutions continue to command respect but increasingly fail to produce order?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the distinction between the end of Western Zhou royal supremacy in 771–770 BCE and the continued existence of the Zhou royal house throughout the Spring and Autumn period.
  2. Trace the gradual decline of effective Zhou royal military and administrative power and the survival of Zhou ritual prestige and legitimating authority.
  3. Identify the major regional states of the Spring and Autumn period and describe their differing origins, geographic positions, and trajectories.
  4. Compare early and late Spring and Autumn warfare, tracing the gradual shift from aristocratic, chariot-centered combat toward larger and more sustained campaigns.
  5. Analyze the diplomatic system of covenants, alliances, and hegemonic leadership through which regional rulers managed interstate relations without a strong Zhou king.
  6. Explain the concept of the hegemon, or ba, and assess why hegemonic leadership functioned as a political solution to weakened royal authority.
  7. Describe changes in aristocratic society, including the growing importance of the shi stratum and increasing, though still limited, social mobility based on service and skill.
  8. Evaluate the evidence for agricultural expansion, growing regional trade, and the gradual, uneven spread of iron technology during this period, while avoiding technological determinism.
  9. Distinguish the early movement toward territorial administration examined in this chapter from the more fully developed bureaucratic states of the Warring States period.
  10. Assess competing historical interpretations of the Spring and Autumn period as either political decline or a formative period of state-building.
  11. Distinguish the reliability of different kinds of historical evidence for this period, including bronze inscriptions, the terse Spring and Autumn Annals, and the much richer but later Zuo Tradition.
  12. Connect the political and social conditions examined in this chapter to the intellectual ferment of the classical philosophical traditions introduced in Chapters 11 and 12.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What is the difference between the collapse of a royal center's practical power and the collapse of a dynasty itself, and why does that distinction matter for understanding 771–770 BCE?
  2. Why might powerful regional rulers have continued to value recognition from a Zhou king who could no longer command their armies or compel their obedience?
  3. How did the diplomatic institution of hegemonic leadership, or ba, allow strong states to manage interstate order without formally replacing the Zhou king?
  4. In what ways did Spring and Autumn warfare remain connected to aristocratic tradition, and in what ways did it begin to change?
  5. What kinds of evidence allow historians to trace increasing social mobility among lower-ranking aristocrats and men of service during this period, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  6. Why should students avoid treating the gradual spread of iron technology as a simple, single cause of social or political change?
  7. How did regional courts begin to govern territory and population in ways that differed from the kinship-based lordship of the Western Zhou, and why was this shift incomplete during the Spring and Autumn period?
  8. Why do historians treat the Zuo Tradition's vivid speeches and dialogues with caution, even though the work provides an unusually rich narrative of the period?
  9. In what ways did political fragmentation coexist with, and perhaps even encourage, new forms of intellectual and cultural creativity?
  10. How does understanding the Spring and Autumn period as an interstate system, rather than simply a "disordered" phase between two unified periods, change the way students think about Chinese political history?

Historical Context

Chapter 8 traced the Zhou conquest of Shang in the eleventh century BCE, the articulation of the Mandate of Heaven as a doctrine of political legitimacy, and the construction of a Western Zhou political order built substantially on kinship: the Zhou king stood at the apex of a network of regional lords, many of them his own relatives or close allies, who governed their domains in his name, owed him military service and ritual deference, and, in principle at least, recognized his unique standing as the Son of Heaven. That chapter also traced the gradual erosion of this system over roughly two and a half centuries, as regional lords grew more entrenched in their own territories, royal authority weakened at the frontiers, and internal succession disputes and external pressures placed growing strain on the royal center. Chapter 8 closed with the traumatic events of 771–770 BCE, when a coalition that included the marquis of Shen, other disaffected nobles, and non-Zhou peoples identified in later sources as the Quanrong overran the western Zhou heartland in the Wei River valley, killed King You, and forced the relocation of the royal court eastward to Luoyi.

This chapter begins by insisting on a distinction that students must carry through the remainder of this textbook: the destruction of the western Zhou royal center and the killing of a Zhou king did not end the Zhou dynasty. King Ping, You's son, was installed as king and led the reconstituted court to its new eastern capital, and Zhou kings would continue to reign — with steadily diminishing practical power, but with real and lasting symbolic significance — for more than five additional centuries, until the Zhou royal dynasty conventionally ended in 256 BCE, when the expanding state of Qin extinguished the remaining Zhou royal domain. Historians therefore divide the Zhou dynasty into two major periods: the Western Zhou, examined in Chapter 8, centered on the royal capital in the Wei River valley in the west, and the Eastern Zhou, beginning with the move to Luoyi in 770 BCE and lasting until 256 BCE, centered on a much reduced royal domain around the new eastern capital. The Eastern Zhou period is itself conventionally divided into two unequal parts: the Spring and Autumn period, examined in this chapter, running from approximately 770 to 476 or 475 BCE, and the Warring States period, examined in Chapter 10, running from that point until the Qin unification of 221 BCE examined in Chapter 13.

Periodization of this kind is a scholarly convenience rather than a fact directly recorded by contemporaries, and students should understand its basis. The customary end date for the Spring and Autumn period derives largely from the chronological coverage of the Spring and Autumn Annals itself, which as a work associated with the state of Lu concludes its year-by-year entries in 481 BCE according to the Gongyang and Guliang textual traditions (479 BCE according to the Zuo-associated tradition), with some conventions extending the associated period slightly further to 476 or 475 BCE; the Warring States period is then dated from that point, or sometimes from the later, more clearly documented partition of the state of Jin in 453 or its formal recognition in 403 BCE, discussed further in Chapter 10. These boundary dates should not be mistaken for a single decisive historical rupture. Political, military, social, and economic change during this roughly five-century span of the Eastern Zhou was gradual and continuous in many respects, and the transition from Spring and Autumn patterns to Warring States patterns unfolded unevenly across different states and different spheres of life rather than occurring at one uniform moment across the entire Zhou world.

Geographically, the political world examined in this chapter was substantially larger and more differentiated than the Western Zhou heartland emphasized in Chapter 8. Regional states expanded outward from the middle and lower Yellow River core toward the Yangtze basin in the south, the Shandong peninsula in the east, the Wei River valley and lands further west, and the northern frontier zones bordering steppe and semi-steppe peoples. This chapter therefore ranges across a wider and more variegated geography than its predecessor, examining states whose populations, in some cases, included substantial numbers of people whose ancestral cultural traditions differed from those of the Zhou royal house and its closest allied lineages, and whose relationship to a shared "Zhou" or, later, "Chinese" cultural identity was itself historically variable and, in the case of a state such as Chu, actively contested and renegotiated over time rather than simply given.

Students should also carry forward from Chapter 8 an understanding of the ritual, religious, and ideological resources on which Eastern Zhou political actors continued to draw. The Mandate of Heaven, the practice of ancestor veneration, and an elaborate system of ritual distinctions marking rank and status did not disappear with the fall of the western capital; they persisted, were reinterpreted, and were increasingly invoked strategically by regional rulers seeking to justify their own growing power, their claims to leadership among their peers, or, in some cases, their eventual absorption of weaker neighboring states. This chapter examines how that inherited ideological vocabulary was stretched, reused, and gradually transformed to fit a political reality — an interstate system of competing regional powers — that the vocabulary itself had not originally been designed to describe.

Section 9.1

The Eastern Zhou Transition

The events of 771–770 BCE, examined at the close of Chapter 8, mark the conventional dividing line between the Western Zhou and Eastern Zhou periods, but students should approach that dividing line with care. What ended in 771–770 BCE was not the Zhou dynasty as such, but rather the specific western royal center, located in the Wei River valley, from which Zhou kings had governed since the conquest of Shang more than two and a half centuries earlier. According to the traditional account, King You's controversial decision to set aside his original queen and heir in favor of a favored consort and her son provoked the queen's father, the marquis of Shen, into alliance with other disaffected nobles and with non-Zhou peoples identified in later sources as the Quanrong. This coalition overran the Zhou heartland, sacked the capital, and killed King You in 771 BCE. Students should treat the specific personal and moral details of this narrative — a familiar pattern in which court intrigue and a ruler's poor judgment are blamed for political catastrophe — with the same caution urged throughout this textbook toward later texts describing earlier events, since the fullest surviving versions of this story were composed well after the events themselves and may reflect later moralizing conventions as much as, or more than, precise historical reconstruction. What is more securely established is the underlying political fact: the western capital was destroyed or rendered indefensible, and effective royal authority within the old Zhou heartland collapsed.

In the aftermath, a coalition of regional lords supported the installation of You's son as King Ping, and the new king, unable to hold or fully reconstitute the western capital, relocated the royal court eastward to Luoyi, near modern Luoyang, a secondary Zhou administrative center that had existed since early Western Zhou times. Students should know that the traditional account, which presents this succession as a comparatively direct transfer of authority to King Ping, has been complicated by recently recovered manuscript evidence. The Tsinghua University bamboo-strip text known as the Xinian preserves a more complicated account involving a rival claimant, King Hui of Xie, and suggests that recognition of King Ping was neither immediate nor uncontested. The precise political sequence between King You's death and King Ping's eventual consolidation as the recognized Zhou king therefore remains an active subject of scholarly debate, and the traditional narrative followed in the remainder of this section should be understood as the conventional account rather than a settled, uncontested reconstruction. This relocation is the event historians use to mark the beginning of the Eastern Zhou period. It is essential that students register the distinction between dynastic continuity and the loss of practical supremacy: the Zhou royal house survived the crisis, Zhou kings continued to reign, to perform ritual functions, to issue occasional pronouncements, and to be recognized, at least nominally, as sitting atop the political hierarchy inherited from the Western Zhou — but the territorial base, military capacity, and administrative reach available to support that position had been drastically and permanently reduced. The royal domain around Luoyi was a small fraction of the territory the Zhou kings had once commanded, and the abandoned western lands, including much of the old Zhou heartland, increasingly fell under the control of regional states, above all the rising state of Qin, examined further in Sections 9.3 and in Chapter 10.

The Spring and Autumn Annals were not composed at a single moment by a single author but accrued, entry by entry, from ongoing record-keeping by court scribes of the state of Lu across generations; later tradition associated the text with editorial work by Confucius, though the precise nature and extent of any such editorial involvement remains debated among scholars and should not be presented to students as an established fact. Because the Annals grew out of contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous record-keeping rather than later retrospective compilation, historians generally treat the chronicle as standing substantially closer to the events it records than the much richer, but later-compiled, Zuo Tradition examined further below. The Annals record, in a highly compressed, formulaic style, a bare sequence of political and ritual events — accessions, deaths, diplomatic meetings, military campaigns, natural anomalies such as eclipses and unusual weather — organized under the spring, summer, autumn, and winter seasons of each ruler's regnal year, without extended narrative explanation. It is this seasonal organizing structure, not any claim about the character or climate of the age, that gives the period its conventional English name.

Conventional dating places the Spring and Autumn period from 770 BCE, the year of the move to Luoyi, to somewhere in the vicinity of 476 or 475 BCE, a boundary derived chiefly from the fact that the Lu Annals' entries conclude in 481 BCE according to the Gongyang and Guliang textual traditions (479 BCE according to the Zuo-associated tradition), with some conventions extending the associated historical period a few years beyond the chronicle's own textual endpoint. Other scholarly traditions instead mark the transition to the Warring States period at 453 BCE, when the powerful state of Jin was effectively partitioned among three formerly subordinate ministerial lineages, an event examined in Chapter 10, or at 403 BCE, when the Zhou king formally recognized the resulting three states of Han, Zhao, and Wei as legitimate regional lords in their own right. Students should understand that these varying boundary dates reflect different scholarly conventions rather than a single, universally agreed historical rupture, and that the deeper political, social, and economic changes tracked across this chapter and the next occurred gradually and continuously, at different paces in different states, rather than switching abruptly from one clearly defined "period" to another on any single calendar date.

Section 9.2

Decline of Royal Authority

It would be a serious oversimplification to say that the Zhou kings of the Spring and Autumn period "became powerless." A more precise and more historically useful formulation is that Zhou royal authority became increasingly ritual, symbolic, and legitimating in character, while its coercive, military, and administrative dimensions steadily diminished relative to the growing capacities of the leading regional states. The Zhou king retained, throughout this period, the unique title of Son of Heaven and the associated ritual prerogatives that only the holder of that title could perform, including certain sacrifices understood to concern the welfare of the entire Zhou political world rather than any single state. Regional rulers, however powerful in their own domains, generally continued, for much of this period, to hold titles formally granted by or associated with the Zhou king's authority, a practice that testifies to the continuing symbolic centrality of the royal house even as its practical power waned. Students should not, however, overstate this continuing centrality: Eastern Zhou political and inscriptional culture became increasingly state-centered over time, and regional courts increasingly developed their own local political and ritual conventions, including, in many surviving inscriptions, dating practices anchored to a regional ruler's own reign rather than to the reigning Zhou king's regnal years.

The military dimension of this decline is documented with reasonable clarity. Where Western Zhou kings, as described in Chapter 8, could raise substantial royal armies and compel or strongly influence the military contributions of regional lords, Eastern Zhou kings, operating from a much-reduced royal domain around Luoyi, could field only modest forces of their own and increasingly depended on the goodwill, or the self-interested calculation, of powerful regional states for protection and support. This dependency is illustrated starkly by episodes in which Zhou kings required military assistance from regional rulers to resolve succession disputes within the royal house itself or to defend the royal domain from external threats — a striking reversal of the Western Zhou pattern in which royal military power had underwritten the entire regional order.

Yet this apparent weakness coexisted with, and indeed helps explain, a genuine and important form of continuing royal relevance. The Zhou king remained an unusually prestigious source of ritual recognition, retaining the capacity to confer or confirm titles, sanction successions, and lend symbolic weight to major interstate covenants, even as regional rulers increasingly acted, in practice, without effective royal control or compulsion. Ambitious regional rulers frequently found it advantageous to seek, cite, or claim such royal sanction for their own actions, even when they possessed more than sufficient military power to act without it. This produces the central paradox this chapter asks students to hold in mind: the weaker the Zhou king became in coercive, practical terms, the more valuable royal legitimacy could become as a diplomatic and political resource, precisely because it was scarce, prestigious, and, crucially, no longer backed by a royal military threat that might make regional rulers wary of invoking it. A state that could plausibly present itself as acting in the Zhou king's name, or in defense of the Zhou order, gained a form of political capital unavailable to a state that simply asserted naked self-interest.

This dynamic underlies the institution of hegemonic leadership examined in detail in Section 9.5: the most successful regional rulers of the Spring and Autumn period were not those who ignored or displaced the Zhou king, but those who most effectively positioned themselves as his defenders, protectors, and instruments, thereby converting military and economic strength into a form of political authority still partly borrowed from, and legitimated by, the very royal institution whose practical power they had eclipsed. Students should therefore distinguish carefully, throughout this chapter, between formal hierarchy — the nominal ranking of Zhou king above regional lords, inherited from Western Zhou practice and still ritually acknowledged — and effective power, which by the Spring and Autumn period rested overwhelmingly with a shifting cast of the strongest regional states.

Section 9.3

Regional States and Political Competition

At the outset of the Spring and Autumn period, the Zhou political world encompassed a substantial number of regional domains — later traditions sometimes cite figures in the range of well over a hundred nominally recognized states, though the precise count varies by source and by definition of what counted as a state. Over the following three centuries, this crowded political landscape gradually consolidated, through conquest, annexation, and absorption, into a much smaller number of dominant powers, a process that accelerated further during the Warring States period examined in Chapter 10. Understanding this consolidation, and the differing character of the states that drove it, is essential to understanding the period.

Jin, located in the Fen River valley of the northern Yellow River region, emerged as one of the most consistently powerful states of the period, its rulers descended from a Zhou royal relative enfeoffed at the time of the early Western Zhou settlement of the region. Jin's rulers built a large, well-organized state that would repeatedly claim hegemonic leadership and that, by the middle and later Spring and Autumn period, had absorbed numerous smaller neighboring polities. Jin's eventual internal fragmentation and partition among three great ministerial lineages in the fifth century BCE — an episode central to Chapter 10 — should not obscure the state's earlier importance as one of the two or three most consistently powerful actors in the interstate system.

Qi, situated on the fertile and economically productive Shandong peninsula with access to maritime resources and salt production, developed as another leading power, its wealth and administrative sophistication underlying the rise of Duke Huan as the period's first widely recognized hegemon, discussed further in Section 9.5. Qi's economic strength, resting substantially on trade, craft production, and access to coastal resources, illustrates that Spring and Autumn interstate power rested not solely on military capacity but on the underlying economic foundations examined further in Section 9.7.

Chu, centered in the middle Yangtze region far to the south of the traditional Zhou heartland, followed a distinctive trajectory that repays careful attention. Chu's rulers, according to both later tradition and some earlier evidence, claimed the royal title of "king" (wang) independently of Zhou grant or recognition, a claim that directly challenged the Zhou king's unique standing as the sole legitimate holder of that title within the Zhou political order. Northern states, and the transmitted historical tradition shaped substantially by northern perspectives, frequently characterized Chu as culturally distinct from, or even alien to, the core Zhou political and ritual world, sometimes using language suggesting Chu occupied an ambiguous or marginal position relative to a "civilized" Zhou-centered order. Students should treat such characterizations critically: Chu was, by any practical measure, one of the largest, wealthiest, and most militarily formidable states of the entire Eastern Zhou period, deeply engaged in the same diplomatic, ritual, and military systems examined throughout this chapter, and its relationship to Zhou cultural identity is better understood as historically complex, contested, and evolving than as a simple binary of "Zhou" versus "non-Zhou." Chu's repeated conflicts with Jin for regional supremacy, examined further in Section 9.4, form one of the defining rivalries of the period.

Qin, based in the Wei River valley in the far west — territory that had formed the core of the Western Zhou heartland before 771 BCE — began the Spring and Autumn period as a comparatively peripheral state, credited by tradition with having assisted King Ping's relocation and with gradually asserting control over former Zhou royal lands in the west. Qin's rise to the overwhelming dominance it would achieve by the Warring States and early imperial periods, examined in Chapters 10 and 13, lay still centuries in the future during most of the Spring and Autumn period, but the state's steady territorial consolidation in the west during this era laid important foundations for its later expansion.

Smaller and, in several cases, more ritually significant states also shaped the period's politics. Lu, home to the Spring and Autumn Annals and to Confucius, maintained close ties to core Zhou ritual tradition, its ruling house descended from a close relative and associate of the Zhou founders. Song, ruled by descendants of the defeated Shang royal house and granted a distinctive, honored place within the Zhou ritual order after the conquest described in Chapter 8, occupied an unusual position combining considerable ritual prestige with more modest military and territorial power. Zheng, located near the royal domain itself, played an outsized diplomatic role in the earlier Spring and Autumn period precisely because of its geographic proximity to Luoyi and its consequent entanglement in royal court politics.

Taken together, these states illustrate a central lesson of this section: there was no single, uniform "Chinese state" during the Spring and Autumn period, and no single trajectory of state development. Some states descended directly from close Zhou royal relatives; others, like Chu, developed political traditions and claims to authority substantially independent of Zhou royal grant. Some states built their power chiefly on agricultural and demographic strength in the Yellow River heartland; others, like Qi, drew heavily on trade and coastal resources, while Chu drew on the resources of the vast and, from a northern perspective, still only partially settled Yangtze region. Understanding this variation is essential preparation for Chapter 10, which examines how a small number of these states — chiefly the successors of Jin, along with Qi, Chu, Qin, and a few others — consolidated into the handful of large, mutually hostile territorial powers that would define the Warring States period.

Section 9.4

Warfare and Military Transformation

Warfare during the earlier Spring and Autumn period retained strong continuities with the aristocratic military culture of the Western Zhou examined in Chapter 8. Combat centered on the chariot, a vehicle whose construction, maintenance, and skilled operation required considerable resources and training, and whose use was correspondingly restricted largely to members of the hereditary aristocracy and their closest retainers. Later texts, particularly portions of the Zuo Tradition, describe early Spring and Autumn battles as governed by an array of ritual conventions: formal challenges preceding engagement, expectations of fair or honorable conduct between opposing nobles, and limits on the pursuit and treatment of defeated enemies that modern readers might associate with codes of chivalry in other historical contexts. Students should treat these depictions with appropriate caution. Some scholars regard them as reflecting genuine, if imperfectly and inconsistently observed, aristocratic military norms of the earlier period; others emphasize that such accounts, composed or compiled later, may reflect retrospective moralization by writers looking back on an idealized "age of ritual propriety" from the vantage point of a later, more openly instrumental and less ritually constrained style of warfare. The most defensible position is a cautious one: some genuine aristocratic military conventions likely did shape earlier Spring and Autumn combat, but their extent, consistency, and the degree to which later texts romanticized or exaggerated them cannot be established with full confidence from currently available evidence.

Over the roughly three centuries covered by this chapter, several converging trends gradually transformed the scale and character of warfare, even as the fullest expression of these trends — the large-scale, sustained, infantry-heavy, mass-conscription warfare characteristic of the Warring States period — belongs to Chapter 10 rather than to this one. Campaigns grew longer and more territorially consequential, as leading states increasingly fought not merely to punish or humiliate a rival but to annex territory, extinguish rival states outright, or compel lasting submission. The number of independently recognized states diminished steadily across the period, from many dozens of recorded polities at its outset — with some later traditions citing considerably higher totals, depending on what is counted as a state — to only a little over twenty surviving by the early fifth century BCE, a trend that continued and intensified in the Warring States period. Armies, while still built substantially around aristocratic chariot forces for much of the period, began in some states and in some contexts to incorporate larger numbers of infantry, drawn from a broader segment of the population than the narrow aristocratic warrior class of earlier practice, foreshadowing, without yet fully anticipating, the mass conscript armies of the following period.

Major interstate battles of the period, such as the Battle of Chengpu in 632 BCE, in which Jin decisively defeated Chu and confirmed Jin's position as a leading hegemonic power, illustrate the scale such conflicts had already reached by the middle Spring and Autumn period, involving substantial forces, careful strategic planning, and consequences that reshaped the interstate balance of power for a generation or more. At the same time, students should resist reading such battles as evidence that Spring and Autumn warfare had already become the totalizing, existentially destructive warfare of the Warring States period, in which entire states might be annihilated and their populations subjected to mass slaughter or forced resettlement on a scale not generally documented for the earlier period.

This section, in short, should be read as tracing a transition rather than presenting a finished transformation. Chariot-centered aristocratic combat did not vanish overnight, nor did mass infantry conscription arrive suddenly at the period's outset; rather, the roughly three centuries examined in this chapter show a gradual, uneven shift, proceeding at different paces in different states, away from the smaller-scale, more ritually bounded warfare associated with earlier Zhou aristocratic tradition and toward the larger, more territorially consequential, more broadly manned conflicts that would come to full maturity in the era examined in Chapter 10.

Section 9.5

Diplomacy and Interstate Alliances

If warfare provides one measure of Spring and Autumn political life, diplomacy provides another, equally important, and arguably even more historically distinctive. The period's political culture developed an elaborate and increasingly sophisticated set of diplomatic institutions and practices through which regional states, in the absence of an effective overarching royal authority capable of compelling their cooperation, nonetheless managed a substantial degree of interstate coordination, conflict limitation, and collective action.

Interstate meetings, at which rulers or their high-ranking representatives gathered to negotiate, renew alliances, settle disputes, or coordinate joint action, occurred with considerable frequency throughout the period and are recorded extensively in the Spring and Autumn Annals and elaborated at length in the Zuo Tradition. These meetings frequently culminated in formal covenants, solemn oaths sworn before witnesses and, in many cases, understood to invoke the sanction of ancestral spirits or other supernatural powers, by which participating rulers bound themselves and their states to specific commitments: mutual defense, non-aggression, support for a particular claimant in a succession dispute, or general adherence to a shared set of interstate norms. Marriage alliances between ruling houses served similar diplomatic functions, creating kinship ties intended to stabilize relationships between states, though, as the Zuo Tradition and other sources make clear, such ties by no means guaranteed lasting peace or loyalty. Hostage exchange, in which a ruler's son or other close relative resided, sometimes for extended periods, at the court of an allied or dominant state, functioned as a further mechanism for securing commitments and monitoring compliance, and also, notably, exposed young aristocrats to the political culture and personal networks of other states, with long-term consequences for their own later careers, as illustrated by the traditional account of Duke Wen of Jin's long exile among several different states before his eventual accession and rise to hegemonic power.

The most historically distinctive diplomatic institution of the period is hegemonic leadership, conventionally rendered in English as "hegemon" and corresponding to the Chinese term ba. A hegemon was a powerful regional ruler who, through a combination of military strength, diplomatic skill, and successful claims to be acting in defense of Zhou order, secured recognition — formal or informal, and never universal or uncontested — as the leading figure among the assembled states, empowered to convene interstate meetings, organize joint military action, and arbitrate disputes among lesser rulers, all while continuing, at least in official language and ritual gesture, to defer to the ultimate, if largely symbolic, authority of the Zhou king. Duke Huan of Qi, whose reign from 685 to 643 BCE included the celebrated covenant at Kuiqiu in 651 BCE described in this chapter's opening, is traditionally regarded as the first ruler to achieve widely recognized hegemonic standing. Duke Wen of Jin, whose rise to power in 636 BCE followed years of exile and whose victory over Chu at the Battle of Chengpu in 632 BCE confirmed his own hegemonic standing, is similarly celebrated in later tradition as an exemplary hegemon.

Later tradition sometimes organizes this history around a canonical list of "Five Hegemons," but students should be cautious about memorizing any single such list as though it were a fixed and universally agreed historical fact. Different transmitted sources name different rulers as belonging to this group, reflecting varying regional traditions, differing criteria for what counted as genuine hegemonic achievement, and the retrospective, sometimes moralizing purposes for which later writers compiled such lists. The more historically important lesson is not which five rulers belong on a canonical roster, but the underlying political logic the institution of hegemony reveals: hegemonic leadership functioned as a practical, workable political solution to the problem of a weakened royal center, allowing the strongest states to provide real interstate leadership, coordination, and conflict management while preserving, at least in form, the inherited Zhou-centered ritual and ideological order rather than discarding it outright. This is precisely the paradox introduced in Section 9.2: the less capable the Zhou king became of exercising direct power, the more valuable it became for the era's most ambitious rulers to be seen defending, rather than replacing, the framework of authority he still nominally represented.

Section 9.6

Social Mobility and Aristocratic Change

Beneath the interstate drama of covenants, hegemons, and battles, the Spring and Autumn period witnessed significant, if gradual and uneven, changes in the internal social structure of the states examined in this chapter. The hereditary aristocratic order inherited from the Western Zhou, in which political, military, and ritual authority were tightly bound to lineage and birth, began to show signs of strain and transformation, though it did not disappear during this period and would persist, in modified form, well into the Warring States era and beyond.

Some old, established noble houses lost power, wealth, or influence over the course of the period, whether through the misfortunes of factional politics at their home courts, defeat in war, extinction of a lineage's direct male line, or simply being outmaneuvered by rival families and rising new figures. At the same time, individuals and families of comparatively modest or lower aristocratic standing found increasing opportunity to gain influence, wealth, and standing at regional courts through demonstrated skill and service rather than through inherited high rank alone — as capable military commanders, as skilled negotiators and diplomats entrusted with delicate interstate missions, as ritual specialists whose expert knowledge of proper ceremonial conduct carried real political value in a world where ritual correctness still mattered enormously, and as administrators managing the growing, if still limited, governmental apparatus of expanding regional states.

This development is closely associated with the growing importance of a social stratum generally referred to using the term shi. Students should handle this term with particular care. In much later Chinese history, especially from the Han dynasty onward, examined in Chapters 14 through 18, shi came to refer to an educated scholar-official class whose status rested substantially on classical learning and examination success — a meaning explored further in Chapters 15, 26, and 34. Applying that later, more fully developed meaning to the Spring and Autumn period would be anachronistic. During the period examined in this chapter, shi is better understood as referring to a broad stratum of lower-ranking hereditary aristocrats and men of service — individuals who held some aristocratic standing, however modest, but who typically lacked the great landed domains, hereditary high office, or independent political base of the greatest noble lineages, and who consequently depended substantially on rendering valuable service to a ruler or to a more powerful noble house in order to secure their livelihood, standing, and advancement. Some among this stratum served as retainers, military officers, ritual specialists, or administrative aides; some traveled between states seeking employment with whichever ruler seemed most likely to value their particular skills, a pattern of mobility that itself testifies to a loosening, though by no means a dissolution, of older, more strictly hereditary and locally rooted forms of aristocratic service.

This section should be understood as preparing students for the classical philosophical traditions examined in Chapters 11 and 12 without pretending to explain those traditions in full. Confucius himself, examined in detail in Chapter 11, is best understood in relation to precisely this social milieu: a man of modest aristocratic background, without great inherited landed power, who built his reputation and following substantially through learning, ritual expertise, and a distinctive body of ethical and political teaching, and whose life illustrates, in a particularly well-documented individual case, the broader social pattern this section describes. The growing importance of service, skill, and demonstrated capability, alongside continuing, but no longer absolute, deference to hereditary rank, created social and political space in which new kinds of political and ethical thinking could develop and find an audience among rulers increasingly eager for capable advisers regardless of pedigree — a theme Section 9.9 develops further.

Section 9.7

Agriculture, Iron, and Economic Growth

The political and military developments examined in the preceding sections rested on, and in turn reinforced, significant economic changes across the Spring and Autumn period. Agricultural production expanded in many regions, supported by continued land clearance, the extension of settlement into previously less intensively farmed areas, and, in a number of regions, growing investment in irrigation and other water-management infrastructure intended to improve and stabilize crop yields. This agricultural expansion supported, and was in turn supported by, continued population growth across much of the Zhou world, providing an expanding base of labor, military manpower, and taxable resources on which the growing regional states examined in this chapter increasingly depended.

Regional and interregional trade also expanded during this period, connecting different areas of specialized production — states with strong agricultural surplus, states with access to salt, metal ores, or other valuable raw materials, and states positioned along important overland or riverine trade routes — into an increasingly interconnected, though still regionally variable, economic system. Qi's prosperity, discussed in Section 9.3, rested substantially on such trade and on access to coastal resources including salt production, illustrating that Spring and Autumn state power drew on economic as well as military foundations.

Craft specialization also continued to develop, building on patterns already well established in the Bronze Age societies examined in Chapters 6 through 8. Bronze production remained economically and ritually central throughout the period, continuing to supply the ritual vessels, weapons, and other elite goods whose possession and display, as in earlier periods, marked and reinforced social status.

The gradual emergence of iron technology during this period requires especially careful historical treatment, since this is a topic where students, and popular historical narratives generally, are prone to oversimplification. Current archaeological and textual evidence indicates that iron implements began to appear with increasing, though still geographically and socially uneven, frequency during the Spring and Autumn period, particularly from around the sixth century BCE onward, with dating for specific developments remaining a subject of ongoing archaeological research and some scholarly debate. It is essential that students not imagine a sudden, sweeping "Iron Age" arriving at the very outset of the period and abruptly displacing bronze, nor a tidy sequence in which iron began purely as a humble material for ordinary tools before later being adopted for weapons. Archaeological finds show a more complicated picture: early Spring and Autumn iron objects include prestige items such as swords, knives, dagger-axes, and spear components, particularly in some Qin-associated contexts, alongside utilitarian objects, and it is only across the middle and later Spring and Autumn period, continuing into the Warring States transition, that iron tools such as shovels and spades and, eventually, cast-iron implements become more broadly available. Bronze, meanwhile, continued for a considerable time to be used for the most prestigious ritual vessels and remained economically and symbolically important throughout the period, as discussed above. The much larger-scale economic, military, and social consequences commonly associated with iron technology — mass production of iron agricultural implements supporting substantially increased farm yields, and iron weaponry equipping much larger infantry armies — belong chiefly to the Warring States period examined in Chapter 10, when iron production and use expanded far more dramatically.

Students should also resist a second, related oversimplification: the temptation to explain the social and political changes examined elsewhere in this chapter, such as the erosion of strict hereditary aristocratic privilege discussed in Section 9.6, as having been directly and simply "caused" by the introduction of iron technology. Such technological determinism — the claim that a single technological innovation mechanically produced a specific social or political outcome — oversimplifies a far more complex historical picture. The gradual weakening of rigid hereditary status distinctions, the growing importance of the shi stratum, the intensification of warfare, and the beginnings of more territorially organized government, examined in Section 9.8, developed through the interaction of multiple factors: expanding agricultural production and population, growing military and administrative demands placed on regional states by their intensifying rivalries with one another, evolving political strategies by ambitious rulers seeking capable servants regardless of birth, and, only gradually and partially during this period, the expanding availability of iron tools and weapons. The most defensible historical explanation treats these factors as mutually reinforcing rather than searching for one master cause.

Section 9.8

Cities and Regional Administration

As regional states grew larger, wealthier, and more heavily engaged in sustained interstate competition over the course of the Spring and Autumn period, their internal governance began to change in ways that anticipate, without yet fully achieving, the more thoroughly bureaucratic territorial states of the Warring States period examined in Chapter 10. Walled urban centers, serving as the political, ritual, military, and economic hubs of their respective states, grew in size and administrative importance, housing expanding courts, storage facilities for tribute and tax revenue, workshops for craft and weapons production, and the residences of an increasingly differentiated body of officials and retainers serving the ruler.

Rulers of the larger and more successful states increasingly relied on administrative personnel — some drawn from established noble lineages, others, as discussed in Section 9.6, drawn from the broader shi stratum on the basis of demonstrated capability — to manage an expanding range of governmental functions: overseeing agricultural production and the collection of taxes or labor obligations from the population, administering justice and resolving disputes, organizing military levies and logistics for the increasingly large-scale campaigns discussed in Section 9.4, and conducting the diplomatic business examined in Section 9.5. This represents a meaningful, if still incomplete, shift away from the purely kinship-based lordship characteristic of the Western Zhou political order described in Chapter 8, in which regional governance had rested substantially on personal and familial bonds of obligation between the king, his relatives, and their own kin-based followers, and toward a model in which regional courts increasingly governed populations and territory through more impersonal administrative relationships and mechanisms. Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that some states, including Qin, Jin, and Chu, had already begun experimenting, particularly in newly annexed or frontier territory, with administrative units later generally called xian, a term conventionally translated as "county." These early Spring and Autumn xian were not yet the standardized, uniformly organized county-level units of the later Qin and Han territorial administration examined in Chapters 13 through 15; they instead appear to have varied considerably in size, function, and degree of central oversight from state to state and from case to case. Their appearance nonetheless offers students a concrete, if still limited, example of the broader shift toward territorial governance this section describes.

Students must be careful, however, not to project the fuller institutional apparatus of later periods backward onto the Spring and Autumn states examined in this chapter. This period did not yet see universal, systematic territorial administration organized into standardized commandery or county units across entire states, of the kind that would develop more fully during the Warring States period and reach its most thoroughgoing early expression under the Qin, examined in Chapter 13. It did not yet see comprehensive, standardized taxation systems applied uniformly across a state's territory, nor did it see the large-scale, systematic population registration that later Warring States governments would use to mobilize labor and military manpower with much greater precision and reach. Aristocratic lineages, hereditary landholding, and personal ties of loyalty and obligation between rulers, nobles, and their followers continued to structure much of political and social life throughout the Spring and Autumn period, even as the balance between these older, more personal forms of authority and newer, more impersonal administrative mechanisms began, gradually and unevenly, to shift.

The most defensible summary formulation, and one students should be able to state clearly, is this: over the course of the Spring and Autumn period, regional states increasingly governed territories and populations rather than operating solely through networks of hereditary aristocratic and kinship relationships, even as this shift remained partial, uneven across different states, and far from complete by the period's end. This represents an important bridge between the kinship-centered politics of the Western Zhou examined in Chapter 8 and the far more centralized, bureaucratic, and territorially organized states of the Warring States period examined in Chapter 10, but it should not be mistaken for the arrival of that later, more fully developed system.

Section 9.9

Political Fragmentation and Cultural Creativity

It would be a mistake to conclude, from the preceding sections' emphasis on warfare, declining royal authority, and shifting aristocratic fortunes, that the Spring and Autumn period was simply a story of loss, disorder, or cultural stagnation. On the contrary, the very conditions of political fragmentation and intensifying interstate competition examined throughout this chapter appear, on the available evidence, to have encouraged, rather than suppressed, significant forms of intellectual, diplomatic, and cultural creativity.

The elaborate diplomatic culture examined in Section 9.5 required, and rewarded, genuine expertise: skilled negotiators capable of crafting persuasive arguments, invoking precedent and ritual propriety effectively, and navigating the complex, shifting web of interstate obligations and rivalries. The Zuo Tradition preserves numerous accounts, however much later editorial shaping they may have undergone, of diplomatic speeches and exchanges whose sophistication testifies to a political culture that valued eloquence, historical knowledge, and skillful argumentation as genuine political assets. Interstate competition also encouraged historical reflection, as rulers, ministers, and advisers drew on real or purported precedent from earlier Zhou history, and from the recent history of their own and rival states, to justify present actions, warn against particular courses of conduct, or claim legitimacy for contested political positions.

Ritual debate, too, flourished in this environment. Because ritual correctness continued to carry real political and social weight throughout the period, as this chapter has emphasized repeatedly, disputes over the proper performance of specific ceremonies, the correct ritual treatment owed to particular relationships or occasions, and the deeper meaning and purpose of ritual practice itself became sites of genuine intellectual engagement rather than mere procedural formality. Administrative experimentation, examined in Section 9.8, likewise generated practical political and ethical questions about how rulers ought to govern, whom they ought to employ, and on what basis political authority could be justified when inherited hereditary status alone no longer seemed a fully sufficient answer — questions that the growing shi stratum, discussed in Section 9.6, had every practical incentive to consider carefully, since their own livelihoods and prospects for advancement depended on persuading rulers that service, skill, and virtue mattered as much as, or more than, birth.

This chapter does not attempt to teach the content of classical Chinese philosophy in full; that essential task belongs to Chapters 11 and 12, which examine Confucius, the Confucian tradition, and the broader intellectual ferment of the "Hundred Schools of Thought" in the depth they deserve. What this chapter can and should establish is the historical soil from which that later intellectual flowering grew. Confucius, born in the state of Lu in 551 BCE according to traditional dating and active for much of his life during the final century of the period examined in this chapter, lived amid precisely the conditions described throughout this section and this chapter more broadly: continuing deep respect for inherited Zhou ritual and political tradition, combined with unmistakable, often painful evidence that this inherited tradition was failing to produce the stable, well-ordered political world it claimed to describe and legitimate. This is the essential bridge this chapter offers to the chapters that follow: what happens to political and ethical thought when inherited institutions continue to command genuine respect and authority, but increasingly, visibly fail to produce lasting order? That question, posed with real historical urgency by the conditions examined throughout this chapter, is the question Chapters 11 and 12 will show Confucius, his early followers, and a range of rival thinkers beginning to answer, in different and often competing ways, during the final decades of the Spring and Autumn period and throughout the Warring States period that followed.

Section 9.10

Chapter Review and Spring and Autumn Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the transformation of the Western Zhou's kinship-centered regional order, examined in Chapter 8, into the increasingly autonomous, competitive, but still ritually and diplomatically interconnected interstate system of the Spring and Autumn period. Students completing this chapter should be able to explain, with confidence and precision, several key distinctions: the difference between the loss of Western Zhou royal supremacy in 771–770 BCE and the continued existence of the Zhou dynasty itself for centuries afterward; the difference between a regional state, such as Jin, Qi, Chu, or Qin, and the far more centralized empire that would eventually emerge under the Qin dynasty examined in Chapter 13; the difference between the aristocratic, chariot-centered warfare of the earlier period and the larger, more sustained warfare that developed gradually across the centuries examined in this chapter, without yet reaching the mass-conscription infantry armies of the Warring States period; the difference between formal ritual hierarchy, in which the Zhou king still nominally stood at the apex of the political order, and effective political power, which rested overwhelmingly with the leading regional states and their hegemonic leaders; and the difference between political fragmentation and cultural stagnation, since this chapter has shown that the multi-state competition of the Spring and Autumn period coincided with, and arguably encouraged, significant diplomatic, ritual, and intellectual creativity.

Students should also be able to distinguish the Spring and Autumn transformation examined in this chapter from the more thoroughgoing Warring States centralization examined in Chapter 10: this chapter has shown regional states beginning to govern territory and population in increasingly administrative, rather than purely kinship-based, terms, and beginning to rely more heavily on capable men of service rather than hereditary status alone, but it has been careful throughout to note that these developments remained partial and incomplete by the period's end, setting the stage for, rather than already constituting, the fuller bureaucratic, military, and economic transformations examined in the next chapter.

Finally, this chapter has asked students to evaluate historical evidence with particular care. The Spring and Autumn Annals provide a contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous, extremely terse and limited, chronological skeleton for the period. The Zuo Tradition provides an immensely richer narrative, filled with speeches, dialogues, and detailed anecdotes, but one whose received text reached its present form well after many of the events it describes, requiring students to treat its vivid particulars — especially extended speeches attributed to named historical figures — as valuable evidence of later historical and ethical thinking about the period, rather than as a reliable stenographic record of precisely what was said or done at the time. Bronze inscriptions from this period, though generally less abundant and less politically central than the great Western Zhou inscriptions examined in Chapter 8, provide additional, more directly contemporaneous evidence where they survive. Comparing and weighing these different kinds of evidence against one another is itself an important historical skill this chapter has sought to model and to ask students to practice.

Historical Evidence

Reading the Annals and the Zuo Tradition

Historians studying the Spring and Autumn period rely on several distinct kinds of evidence, each with its own strengths and limitations. Bronze inscriptions, where they survive from this period, offer evidence created close to the events or circumstances they describe, though they are generally less abundant and less centrally focused on interstate political narrative than the great Western Zhou inscriptions examined in Chapter 8. The Spring and Autumn Annals of Lu offer a chronologically reliable, contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous skeleton of major events — accessions, deaths, meetings, campaigns, notable natural occurrences — but in an extremely compressed, formulaic style that records bare facts without explanation, motive, or narrative detail.

The Zuo Tradition, by contrast, offers exactly the kind of rich narrative detail the Annals lack: extended speeches, dialogues, accounts of motive and strategy, and vivid characterization of historical figures. This richness makes the Zuo Tradition an invaluable source for understanding how later Zhou-era readers and compilers understood, moralized about, and made political and ethical sense of the Spring and Autumn period. It does not, however, make the work a straightforward, verbatim contemporary record. Scholars generally agree that the Zuo Tradition's received text reached something like its present form later than the events of the earlier Spring and Autumn period it describes, meaning that specific speeches and dialogues, however plausible and however consistent with other evidence in broad outline, cannot be treated as precise transcripts of what particular historical figures actually said.

The historically responsible approach, and the one this chapter has modeled throughout, is to use the Annals for chronological anchoring, to use bronze inscriptions and archaeological evidence where available for more directly contemporaneous detail, and to use the Zuo Tradition's rich narrative material cautiously — mining it for broad political and social patterns, and for insight into how the Spring and Autumn period was understood and interpreted by writers of a somewhat later era, while resisting the temptation to treat its most vivid and specific details, particularly extended speeches, as unquestionable fact.

Primary Source Window

The Covenant at Kuiqiu, 651 BCE: One Event, Several Sources

Context: The gathering at Kuiqiu, presided over by Duke Huan of Qi and described in this chapter's opening, is one of the best-documented interstate covenants of the Spring and Autumn period — but "best-documented" does not mean documented by a single, straightforward source. Surviving accounts come from at least three distinct textual layers, composed at different times for different purposes, and comparing them directly teaches an important lesson about how historians must handle evidence for this period. Source type: Three layered textual accounts of the same diplomatic event: a terse chronicle entry, a fuller narrative history, and a much later philosophical citation.

Layer one — the Spring and Autumn Annals: The Annals record the Kuiqiu meeting in their characteristically compressed style, noting simply that the assembled rulers met at Kuiqiu in the relevant year, without elaboration on the covenant's specific terms, ceremony, or significance.

Layer two — the Zuo Tradition: The Zuo Tradition supplies a considerably fuller narrative, describing the participating states, the general diplomatic context, and Duke Huan's standing as the assembly's leader, in language consistent with the broader pattern of covenant-making discussed throughout this chapter.

Layer three — the Mengzi: Several generations later, the philosophical text known as the Mengzi (Mencius 6B.7) cites the Kuiqiu covenant as an example of proper interstate conduct, paraphrasing what it presents as five specific injunctions agreed upon by the assembled rulers — covering matters such as the treatment of unfilial sons, the honoring of the worthy, respect for elders, and fair dealing between states. Notably, the Mengzi's account explicitly states that the participants did not seal their agreement by smearing blood on their lips, the ritual practice this chapter associates elsewhere with covenant ceremonies generally; the Mengzi instead frames Kuiqiu as a covenant sealed by binding words alone.

Analysis: No single one of these three sources gives historians full, direct access to "what actually happened" at Kuiqiu. The Annals confirms that a meeting occurred but tells us almost nothing about its content. The Zuo Tradition offers narrative detail but, as this chapter has emphasized throughout, reached its present form well after the event. The Mengzi is further removed still, composed generations later by a philosophical school with its own strong interest in presenting Kuiqiu as a model of virtuous interstate conduct — which may help explain why its version specifically denies the blood-oath ritual practice associated with covenants elsewhere, a detail that could reflect either a genuine historical memory or a later idealization designed to distance a praised historical episode from a ritual practice the Mengzi's compilers may have regarded as unrefined. Comparing these three layers side by side, rather than treating any single one as simply "the" account of Kuiqiu, models the careful, comparative use of evidence this chapter has asked students to practice throughout.

Questions for students:

  1. Why does the terse Annals entry, despite telling us so little, remain historically valuable alongside the richer Zuo Tradition and Mengzi accounts?
  2. What might explain the Mengzi's specific claim that no blood oath was used at Kuiqiu, when this chapter elsewhere associates covenant ceremonies with such rituals?
  3. Whose interests or purposes might have shaped the Zuo Tradition's and the Mengzi's differing levels of detail about the same event?
  4. What general lesson does comparing these three layered sources teach about using later Chinese historical and philosophical texts as evidence for Spring and Autumn events?
  5. How does this source comparison illustrate the broader paradox, discussed in Section 9.2, of political authority operating without dominant coercive power?
Historical Debate

Decline or State Formation?

The question: Should the Spring and Autumn period be understood chiefly as a story of political decline — the fragmentation and weakening of a once-unified Zhou order — or chiefly as a story of state formation, in which new, more consolidated, more administratively capable regional states gradually took shape?

Interpretation A — Political decline: Some historians and, more prominently, later traditional Chinese historiography emphasize the loss suffered by the Zhou political order: the destruction of the western capital, the sharp reduction of royal military and administrative power, the erosion of the kinship-based regional loyalty that had underpinned the Western Zhou system, and the intensifying warfare and interstate violence documented throughout this chapter. On this view, the period represents a falling-away from an earlier, more unified and more stable political order, a decline that would only deepen further during the Warring States period examined in Chapter 10.
Interpretation B — State formation: Other historians emphasize that the very developments associated with "decline" from the perspective of the Zhou royal house — the growing independence, administrative sophistication, and territorial consolidation of regional states examined in Sections 9.3 and 9.8 — represent genuine state-building achievements in their own right. On this view, the Spring and Autumn period should be understood as a formative period during which more capable, more territorially organized, and, in some respects, more administratively sophisticated regional states gradually developed, laying essential institutional groundwork for the far more centralized states of the Warring States period and, eventually, for the unified imperial state examined in Chapter 13.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on largely the same body of evidence — the Spring and Autumn Annals, the Zuo Tradition, and surviving inscriptions — but differ in which developments they treat as most historically significant: the erosion of the old Zhou-centered order, or the construction of new regional-state institutions and practices.

Why it matters: This debate shapes how students should understand the entire arc from Western Zhou through the Warring States to imperial unification: whether that arc is best told as a story of loss and fragmentation eventually reversed by unification, or as a story of gradual, cumulative institutional development in which the Spring and Autumn period played an essential, constructive role.

Student task: Using specific evidence from this chapter, particularly the developments examined in Sections 9.6 through 9.9, evaluate which interpretation better fits the evidence, or explain why elements of both interpretations may be necessary to understand the period accurately.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Within the Interstate Order

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns described in historical sources and modern scholarship, not verbatim quotations from specific named individuals.

From the perspective of the Zhou king at Luoyi: Ritual primacy and the unique title of Son of Heaven remained real and valuable assets, but maintaining influence increasingly required careful diplomacy with powerful regional rulers rather than reliance on independent royal military strength, and royal favor had to be extended selectively to those states most likely to honor, rather than simply exploit, the prestige it conferred.
From the perspective of a hegemon such as Duke Huan of Qi or Duke Wen of Jin: Claiming to act in defense of Zhou order provided a valuable framework for organizing interstate cooperation and justifying leadership among rival states, but sustaining that leadership required continual military strength, diplomatic skill, and the willingness to honor at least some genuine obligations to weaker allied states, since a hegemon's authority rested on recognition that could be withdrawn or contested by rivals.
From the perspective of a shi seeking employment at a regional court: Skill in ritual knowledge, diplomacy, administration, or military strategy offered a genuine, if uncertain, path toward advancement and influence not fully dependent on high hereditary birth, though success still required finding a ruler willing to value such skill and navigating the competitive, sometimes precarious politics of court service.
From the perspective of a ruler or elite figure in Chu: Chu's independent claim to royal title and its distinct regional political traditions reflected genuine local political development in the Yangtze region rather than mere defiance of a northern order, even as northern states and later northern-influenced historical writing often characterized Chu in terms that questioned its standing within the shared Zhou-descended political and ritual world.
From the perspective of an ordinary farming household in a growing regional state: Intensifying interstate warfare and expanding state administration, examined in Sections 9.4 and 9.8, likely meant increasing demands for agricultural surplus, labor, and, in some cases, military service, even as expanding agricultural production and regional trade, examined in Section 9.7, may also have offered some households new economic opportunities, with the overall balance of burden and opportunity varying considerably by region, state, and individual circumstance.
From the perspective of a soldier serving in a regional army: Early in the period, military service and the prestige of chariot combat remained largely the preserve of aristocratic households and their closest retainers, but as campaigns grew longer and more broadly manned across the period, examined in Section 9.4, a wider range of men, including some of common birth, were increasingly drawn into military service, exposed to the dangers of expanding, more sustained warfare without necessarily sharing in the status or rewards available to aristocratic commanders.
From the perspective of a community newly incorporated into an expanding regional state: As states such as Jin, Chu, and Qin absorbed smaller neighboring polities and extended administrative reach into frontier or newly annexed territory, discussed in Sections 9.3 and 9.8, communities that had previously enjoyed their own local rulers or a looser relationship to any single regional power increasingly found themselves subject to a new court's taxation, labor demands, and, in some cases, experimental territorial administration such as the early xian discussed in Section 9.8, a transition that could bring new forms of order and protection as readily as new burdens, and whose consequences likely varied considerably depending on the manner and circumstances of incorporation.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Western Zhou to Spring and Autumn

What Changed

  • Effective royal military and administrative power shrank dramatically, replaced in practice by the growing power of leading regional states
  • Political authority among regional states shifted, unevenly and gradually, from kinship-based lordship toward more territorial, administrative forms of governance
  • Warfare grew in scale and territorial consequence, with a gradual, still-incomplete shift from predominantly aristocratic, chariot-centered combat toward larger, more broadly manned campaigns incorporating growing numbers of infantry
  • Social mobility increased, unevenly, as the shi stratum gained growing importance relative to strict hereditary aristocratic privilege
  • The number of independently recognized states diminished steadily through conquest and annexation

What Stayed the Same

  • The Zhou royal house continued to reign, retaining unique ritual prerogatives and continuing symbolic centrality
  • Ritual correctness and inherited Zhou ceremonial and political vocabulary continued to carry real political weight and to shape how rulers justified their actions
  • Hereditary aristocratic lineages continued to structure much of political and social life, even as their relative dominance gradually eroded
  • Agriculture remained the fundamental economic foundation of every state examined in this chapter
  • Ancestor veneration and related ritual practices, examined in Chapters 7 and 8, continued to shape elite religious and political life

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the real transformations and the underlying continuities of the Spring and Autumn period helps students understand this era as a genuine, gradual transition rather than either a simple continuation of Western Zhou practice or a complete, sudden break from it.

Cause and Effect

From Weakened Kingship to Interstate Competition

Political causes

The destruction of the western Zhou capital and the sharp reduction of royal military capacity, examined in Section 9.1, removed the coercive foundation that had underpinned the Western Zhou regional order, while the continuing existence and ritual prestige of the Zhou king, examined in Section 9.2, created ongoing incentive for regional rulers to seek royal-adjacent legitimacy rather than discard the old framework entirely.

Economic causes

Expanding agricultural production, population growth, and growing regional trade, examined in Section 9.7, provided the resource base that allowed the largest and best-positioned regional states to sustain larger courts, larger armies, and more elaborate administration.

Social causes

The growing importance of the shi stratum and increasing, if uneven, social mobility, examined in Section 9.6, gave ambitious rulers access to a wider pool of administrative, diplomatic, and military talent than a purely hereditary aristocratic system alone could supply.

Effects

The cumulative result was the interstate system examined throughout this chapter: a shrinking number of increasingly powerful, increasingly administratively capable regional states, competing and cooperating through the diplomatic and military mechanisms examined in Sections 9.4 and 9.5, within a shared but steadily reinterpreted Zhou ritual and political framework — developments that established essential preconditions for, without directly guaranteeing, the more centralized territorial states of the Warring States period examined in Chapter 10.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains the transformation from Western Zhou kinship politics to the Spring and Autumn interstate system. Political, economic, social, and military factors developed together and reinforced one another, and the process unfolded at different paces in different states rather than as one uniform, empire-wide transformation.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Farming Household in a Growing Regional State

Most people living within the states examined in this chapter were farmers, and the great interstate dramas of covenant, hegemony, and battle examined throughout this chapter reached most households chiefly through their practical consequences: demands for agricultural surplus to support expanding courts and armies, demands for labor to build and maintain walled towns, roads, and irrigation works, and, at times, demands for military service or exposure to the dangers of warfare passing through or near one's home region.

At the same time, the gradual agricultural expansion and growing regional trade examined in Section 9.7 likely offered some farming households new opportunities: access to a wider range of goods through expanding markets and trade networks, the possibility, however limited, of improved tools as iron implements began their slow spread later in the period, and, for a small number of especially capable individuals, the possibility of advancement into the expanding administrative or military apparatus of a regional state as a member of the broadening shi stratum discussed in Section 9.6, though such advancement remained the exception rather than the rule for most people of common birth.

Village and household organization for most farming families likely continued to resemble the patterns established in earlier centuries, examined in Chapters 4 through 8, with agricultural labor organized around household and extended kin groups, and with ancestor veneration and locally rooted ritual practice continuing to structure much of community and family life. Students should recognize that the interstate political history emphasized throughout this chapter, while historically significant and well documented, represents primarily the experience and concerns of rulers, aristocrats, and the growing shi stratum, and that reconstructing the fuller texture of ordinary agricultural life during this period depends heavily on archaeological evidence and cautious inference, since surviving textual sources overwhelmingly reflect elite political, diplomatic, and ritual concerns rather than the daily experience of the great majority of the population.

Regional Context

Chu, Qin, and the Boundaries of the Zhou World

This chapter has emphasized, in Section 9.3 and elsewhere, that the Spring and Autumn interstate system was not culturally or politically uniform, and two states in particular reward closer regional attention: Chu, in the south, and Qin, in the west.

Chu's rulers claimed the royal title of wang independently of Zhou grant, a claim northern states generally reserved for the Zhou king alone. Later transmitted sources, composed substantially by writers working within a northern, Zhou-descended textual tradition, sometimes describe Chu using language that casts the state as culturally distinct from, or peripheral to, a "civilized" Zhou-centered order. Students should treat such characterizations as evidence of how northern elites perceived and rhetorically positioned Chu, rather than as a neutral description of Chu's actual cultural or political development. In practical terms, Chu was fully engaged in the diplomatic, ritual, and military systems examined throughout this chapter: its rulers participated in interstate covenants, its armies fought major battles such as Chengpu on terms recognizable within the broader Spring and Autumn military culture, and its court drew on ritual and administrative practices with deep roots in the wider Zhou-descended political world, even as Chu also developed its own distinct regional traditions, drawing on the resources, peoples, and cultural currents of the middle Yangtze region introduced in Chapter 2. Chu's relationship to "Zhou" identity is best understood as historically contested and actively negotiated — by Chu's own rulers as much as by their northern rivals — rather than as a fixed, self-evident fact of ethnic or cultural classification.

Qin, based in the Wei River valley, presents a different kind of regional complexity. Occupying much of the former Western Zhou heartland after 771–770 BCE, Qin stood at a frontier where the settled agricultural world of the Zhou political tradition met steppe and semi-steppe peoples to the north and west, including groups later sources associate with the Rong and Di. Qin's early Spring and Autumn rulers governed a state shaped by this frontier position, incorporating population, military practices, and administrative experiments — including some of the early xian discussed in Section 9.8 — developed partly in response to the specific demands of frontier consolidation and defense. Later, unified imperial historiography, examined in Chapter 13, would sometimes portray Qin's distinct frontier development as a mark of cultural difference from the Zhou heartland states, in ways that echo, though do not exactly mirror, the rhetorical distancing sometimes applied to Chu.

The comparison between Chu and Qin illustrates a broader methodological lesson for this textbook: frontier and southern states were not simply passive recipients of a single, already-formed "Chinese" or "Zhou" culture radiating outward from a northern core. They were active participants in shaping the political, military, and cultural world of the Eastern Zhou period, developing their own regional trajectories that would prove decisively important for the unification examined in Chapter 13 — since it would be Qin, the state northern tradition sometimes cast as peripheral, that would ultimately conquer and absorb every other state examined in this chapter, Chu included.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Spring and Autumn period, conventionally dated from 770 to approximately 476 or 475 BCE, as the era in which the kinship-centered regional order of the Western Zhou, examined in Chapter 8, transformed into an increasingly autonomous, competitive, yet still ritually and diplomatically interconnected interstate political system. The chapter began with the traumatic events of 771–770 BCE, in which the destruction of the western Zhou capital and the killing of King You forced King Ping's relocation of the royal court to Luoyi, and it insisted throughout on a crucial distinction: this catastrophe ended Western Zhou royal supremacy, but it did not end the Zhou dynasty, which continued to exist, and to matter, for centuries afterward.

Students have learned that Zhou royal authority during this period should be understood as increasingly ritual, symbolic, and legitimating rather than coercive, and that this apparent weakness paradoxically enhanced, rather than eliminated, the political value of royal recognition, since ambitious regional rulers found it advantageous to claim they were defending, rather than replacing, the inherited Zhou order. This dynamic underlies the chapter's central political concept, hegemonic leadership, or ba, through which powerful rulers such as Duke Huan of Qi and Duke Wen of Jin organized interstate cooperation, limited (though never eliminated) conflict, and provided practical leadership the weakened Zhou king could no longer supply.

The chapter examined the major regional states of the period — Jin, Qi, Chu, Qin, Lu, Song, and Zheng, among others — emphasizing their differing origins, geographic positions, economic foundations, and political trajectories, and warning against treating any of them as representative of a single uniform "Chinese state" of the period. Particular attention was given to Chu, whose independent claim to royal title and distinct regional political tradition illustrate the complexity, rather than any simple uniformity, of Zhou-era cultural and political identity.

Students examined the gradual transformation of warfare across the period, from aristocratic, chariot-centered combat governed by contested ritual conventions toward larger, more sustained, more territorially consequential campaigns, while learning to distinguish this Spring and Autumn transition from the fuller mass-infantry warfare of the Warring States period examined in the next chapter. The chapter examined Spring and Autumn diplomacy in detail, covering interstate meetings, covenants, marriage alliances, and hostage exchange as mechanisms for managing an interstate system that lacked an effective overarching enforcement authority.

The chapter also traced significant social and economic change beneath these headline political and military developments: the gradual, uneven erosion of strict hereditary aristocratic privilege and the growing importance of the shi stratum, whose members advanced through demonstrated skill and service; expanding agricultural production, population growth, and regional trade; and the early, gradual, and geographically uneven spread of iron technology, whose fuller consequences the chapter carefully reserved for Chapter 10 rather than overstating for this period. Students learned to resist technological determinism, understanding these economic, social, and political changes as mutually reinforcing rather than reducible to any single cause.

The chapter examined the beginnings of more territorial, administrative forms of regional governance, while cautioning students against projecting the fuller bureaucratic apparatus of later periods backward onto this era. And it argued that political fragmentation coincided with, and likely encouraged, significant diplomatic, ritual, and intellectual creativity, situating the traditional birth of Confucius in 551 BCE and the political conditions of the late Spring and Autumn period as essential background for the classical philosophical traditions examined in Chapters 11 and 12.

Throughout, this chapter has modeled careful attention to historical evidence, distinguishing the terse Spring and Autumn Annals, which accrued through contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous Lu court record-keeping, from the much richer, but later-compiled, Zuo Tradition, and asking students to weigh the value and the limits of each. It has also modeled a broader interpretive stance this textbook will return to repeatedly: resisting simple narratives of decline in favor of a more precise account of what changed, what continued, who was affected, and what new political, social, and cultural forms emerged from a period of genuine transformation.

Armed with this understanding of the Spring and Autumn interstate system, students are now prepared to move into Chapter 10's examination of the Warring States period, where they will see the trends traced in this chapter — territorial consolidation, administrative development, intensifying warfare, and the erosion of old aristocratic loyalty — accelerate dramatically, transforming the competitive but still Zhou-oriented world of this chapter into a smaller number of centralized, militarized, explicitly self-sufficient territorial states that would ultimately produce the first unified Chinese empire examined in Chapter 13.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a pivotal transformation in early Chinese political history: the shift from the kinship-centered regional order of the Western Zhou to the increasingly autonomous, competitive, yet still ritually and diplomatically interconnected interstate world of the Spring and Autumn period. Understanding this transformation matters because it establishes a pattern this textbook will encounter again in later periods of Chinese history — moments when formal political unity weakens or fractures, yet shared cultural, ritual, and ideological frameworks persist and continue to shape how political actors understand legitimacy, authority, and their relationships with one another.

The developments traced in this chapter did not remain confined to the Spring and Autumn period in their historical significance. The gradual erosion of rigid hereditary aristocratic privilege and the growing importance of service and skill, examined in Section 9.6, anticipate the more fully developed meritocratic and bureaucratic ideals of later imperial government examined in Chapters 14 through 76. The early movement toward territorial, administrative governance, examined in Section 9.8, anticipates the much more thoroughly bureaucratic states of the Warring States period and the unified imperial administration first achieved under the Qin, examined in Chapter 13. And the political and social conditions examined throughout this chapter directly set the stage for the classical philosophical traditions examined in Chapters 11 and 12, traditions that would shape Chinese ethical, political, and intellectual life for more than two thousand years.

This chapter has also modeled an important historical thinking skill: resisting the temptation to read the Spring and Autumn period simply as an age of decline or disorder, and instead asking what specifically changed, what specifically continued, and what new political and social forms emerged from a genuine, multi-causal historical transformation. This same interpretive discipline — distinguishing decline from transformation, continuity from stagnation, and fragmentation from failure — will serve students well throughout the remainder of this textbook, particularly in later chapters examining other periods of Chinese political division, such as the Three Kingdoms, the Age of Division, and the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms.

As students move forward into Chapter 10's examination of the Warring States period, they should carry with them a clear understanding of the trends this chapter has traced: a weakened but persistent Zhou royal house; an interstate system of competing regional states managed through hegemonic leadership and diplomatic covenant; a gradually changing military and social order; and the beginnings of a more territorial, more administratively sophisticated form of regional governance. Chapter 10 will show these trends accelerating dramatically, as the more moderate competitive order examined in this chapter gives way to a smaller number of centralized, militarized, mutually hostile territorial states whose rulers would increasingly abandon the old Zhou-centered political vocabulary altogether — setting the stage, in turn, for the first unified Chinese empire examined in Chapter 13.
